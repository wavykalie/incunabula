A bedtime routine does its real work hours before anyone mentions sleep. Children read the evening not by the clock but by the order of things. The sequence itself becomes the signal, and the signal becomes the sleep. Most families already run such a sequence without naming it. Naming it is the first act of designing it. Design comes later. What matters first is that the steps happen in the same order every night.

Parents sometimes expect the finished routine to look elaborate. It does not need to. A bath. A book. A dim lamp. The order is the whole lesson. Due to the fact that young children cannot yet read a clock, they read the sequence instead. The routine tells them what comes next, and knowing what comes next is what lets a body relax. Uncertainty is the enemy of drowsiness.

It is worth noting that the routine must survive tired parents, skipped naps and long car rides. A routine that collapses whenever the household wobbles is not a routine; it is an aspiration. So build the short version first: one book instead of three, one song instead of a concert. The short version is the one that runs at nine at night.

It bears mentioning that praise lands better than threats at this hour. A child who chose the book owns a small piece of the evening. Ownership changes the whole negotiation. None of this is permissive; the boundary still holds, fixed and calm. What changes is who appears to be enforcing it.

At its core, the method is attention delivered on schedule. Sleep follows attention the way shadows follow furniture. No timer teaches a child to trust the evening. Only a pattern can do that. Build the pattern, and the pattern does the work.

Tonight is as good a night as any to start.

# The Day Behind

### Chapter 1: The Ash and the Lagging

The boiler room did not belong to the school; it belonged to the pipes.

Robin Mercer had learned this during the third week of September, when the rain had turned the perimeter playing fields into gray grease and the girls from Form 8C had taken over the dry space beneath the library fire escape. If you sat on the low dwarf-wall by the bicycle sheds, Tanya Siddle would kick your plimsolls until the rubber peeled away from the canvas toe-caps, and if you tried to eat your sandwiches in the lower corridor outside the home economics kitchens, Mrs. Garside would confiscate the plastic box because of the borough crumbs policy.

"Litter is a choice, Robin," Mrs. Garside had said, her thumb leaving an oily print on the lid of Robin’s Tupperware. "And wandering is a choice. You have a designated tutor base."

Her designated tutor base was Room 14, where Kacey Vane sat on the low cupboard by the window, swinging her heels against the veneered plywood with a rhythmic, hollow *thump... thump... thump* that could be heard all the way to the staff room door. Whenever Robin walked past the cupboard to reach the coat pegs, Kacey would reach out with the flat of her school shoe—the heavy black patent kind with the thick tread that her mother bought from the boutique behind the bus station—and plant the sole squarely against Robin’s shin. She did not do it with malice; she did it with the casual, absent-minded regularity of someone knocking a dry scab off their own elbow.

"Watch it, Skimmer," Kacey would murmur, not even looking up from her phone.

The word meant nothing. It was not even a proper name. It had started in October of Year 7, when Robin had dropped a plastic tub of margarine in the domestic science pantry and had tried to scrape the yellow fat off the linoleum with the edge of a six-inch steel ruler. Kacey had seen her from the doorway. By fourth period, every radiator in the C-Block had *SKIMMER* scratched into the dull silver paint with the point of a geometry compass.

The boiler room was different. It lay down sixteen concrete steps behind the defunct CDT metal-turning shop, down where the original 1968 foundations met the limestone shelf that ran beneath the canal. The door was made of sheet steel, painted the color of tinned peas, with an old brass padlock that hung through an iron hasp. The hasp had been sheared through with a cold chisel twenty years ago, probably during the miners’ strike when the caretaker had needed to force the coal chutes, and whoever had replaced it had merely jammed a bent six-inch nail through the eyelet to keep the latch from swinging in the draught.

Robin pulled the nail out with two fingers. It was greasy with cold machine oil, and she wiped her thumb down the seam of her maroon school skirt, where the dark stain would not show against the synthetic pleats.

She pulled the pea-green door toward her. It gave a long, rusted shriek that ended in a dull *plink* as the bottom edge scraped across the grit on the threshold, and then the smell came out to meet her.

It was not a bad smell, but it was heavy. It smelled of hot paraffin, dust that had been baked three times over on the ribbed surfaces of iron radiators, damp brick, dead wasps, and the sour, chemical tang of the green powder the council spread across the coal-bunkers to keep the cellar-rats from nesting. The warmth hit Robin’s face like an old woolen blanket pulled out of a damp drawer: dry, tickling the nostrils, carrying the faint, rhythmic shudder of the three great cast-iron heat exchangers that sat in the middle of the pit like sleeping cows.

Robin pulled the door shut behind her. She did not replace the nail; she let the latch rest against the frame, leaving a pencil-thin slit of gray October daylight that showed the wet tarmac of the service yard and the bottom six inches of the dustbin-lorry tyres.

She sat down on an overturned plastic milk crate between the primary circulation pump and the lagged copper riser that carried hot water up to the boys’ changing rooms.

The pump went *thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug*.

It was the only sound in the school that did not have a human voice attached to it. If you sat very still, with your knees pulled up inside the hem of your coat—the navy parka her mother had bought from the charity shop behind the railway arches, the one with the broken toggle and the hood that smelled faintly of someone else’s spaniel—you could feel the rhythm coming through the concrete floor into the bones of your pelvis. It was four beats slower than her own heart, but after ten minutes, her ribs seemed to adjust themselves to the iron machinery.

She opened her lunchbox.

Two slices of white bread with processed cheese slices that had turned translucent at the edges where the grease had sweated into the crumb; half an apple that her mother had cut with a paring knife before her six-o’clock shift at the packing sheds, the white flesh already browning into the color of cider; and a penguin biscuit that had broken into three pieces during the morning rush through the bus barriers.

Robin ate the apple first, chewing through the core until the black pips tasted of bitter almond between her back teeth. She liked the bitterness. It stayed in the corners of her mouth longer than the processed cheese.

To her right, half-buried behind a stack of redundant iron bed-frames that had been cleared out of the old residential wing when the comprehensive became an academy trust, was the back wall of the chamber.

It was older than the rest of the building. The school above was CLASP construction—pre-cast concrete panels, square aluminum window frames with dry black mastic that fell out in crumbs when you picked at it, and interior walls made of straw-board that punched through if a Year 10 boy fell against them in the corridor. But down here, the wall was made of heavy, dressed millstone grit, three feet thick, the mortar between the blocks soft and dark like wet gingerbread. It had belonged to the flax mill that had occupied the site before the council bought the meadows in 1964.

In the middle of the stone wall was a timber door.

Robin had looked at it every day for three weeks, but she had never touched it. It was narrow—barely twenty inches wide, the sort of door they put into chimney breasts to allow the sweep’s boy to reach the flue-cleaners—and it had no knob. There was only an iron ring, three inches across, set into a square plate that was held to the gray oak by four hammered rivets.

Above the ring, someone had painted a number in white lead: *17*.

The white lead was cracking like dried fish-scales, but the *1* and the *7* were clear enough. They were drawn with that stiff, Victorian sign-writer’s flourish, with a little hook on the top of the seven that looked like an iron gaff.

Robin licked the chocolate off her thumb.

Outside, far up in the rain, the two-tone electronic buzzer sounded for the end of the lunch hour. It went *ding-dong-dell, ding-dong-dell*, a sour, flattened electronic chime that died instantly in the wet air. In forty seconds, three hundred pairs of rubber-soled shoes would begin thumping down the fire-stairs toward the lower gym for double Games. Mr. Craddock would be standing at the door of the changing room with his whistle between his lips, his clipboard tucked under his arm, smelling of menthol cigarettes and damp tracksuit fleece, shouting at the boys who hadn't brought their clean white socks.

Robin did not have Games on Mondays. On Mondays she had Library Studies with Mr. Finch, which meant sitting at the circular table near the microfiche cabinet while Mr. Finch marked second-year spelling tests with a purple felt-tip pen that left wet rings on his knuckles.

She stood up from the crate. Her hip was stiff from the cold concrete, and her cardigan—the thin gray acrylic one that had shrunk in the wash until the cuffs stopped three inches above her wrists—had gathered a gray beard of boiler-dust along the hem.

She walked over to the stone wall.

The iron ring was cold. It was colder than the water-pipes, colder even than the concrete under her plimsolls. When her skin touched the metal, she felt a tiny, sharp spark of static jump from the rivet to the ball of her forefinger, like a flea-bite.

She turned the ring.

It did not screech. It did not grate against the lintel like the sheet-steel fire door. It turned with a heavy, oiled *clack* that had the weight of a grandfather clock’s weight dropping into its case. The gray oak came away from the millstone grit smoothly, without a draft, revealing a narrow opening twelve inches wide.

Robin peered through.

She had expected the coal-chute: a diagonal drop of black slime, broken bottles, and eighty years of damp slack that would run down into the culvert beneath the old mill-race.

There was no coal.

There was a corridor.

It was lit by four-foot fluorescent tubes encased in wire-mesh cradles, the plastic diffusers yellowed by thirty years of tobacco smoke and municipal floor-wax. The floor was laid with square thermoplastic tiles of mottled beige and green—the exact same pattern that covered the corridor outside the school nurse’s office on the ground floor of A-Block.

And on the wall opposite the opening, pinned to a strip of brown cork tile with four red plastic map-pins, was an A4 notice printed on blue sugar-paper:

*NOTICE TO ALL PUPILS*  
*The fire doors at the north end of the Humanities corridor will remain secured between the hours of 08:30 and 15:30 during the installation of the new perimeter security fence. Pupils requiring access to the Drama Studio must use the exterior path via the bicycle sheds.*  
*Signed: M. Teasdale (Deputy Headmaster, Pastoral)*  
*Monday, 14th October.*

Robin stared at the date.

Today was Tuesday, the fifteenth.

### Chapter 2: The Hallway of the Dead Cabbage

The air that came through the gap was five degrees cooler than the boiler room, but it had none of the boiler room’s machine heat. It smelled of floor-seal, damp coats that had been dried too fast against low-pressure radiators, and that faint, sour vinegar-and-tin smell that always hung around the canteen hatch after the third-year lunch sitting had cleared the fish fingers.

Robin did not step through immediately. She stayed on her knees on the coal-dust of the cellar floor, her chin resting against the greasy oak frame of Door 17, her fingers hooked over the millstone grit lintel.

She knew the blue notice. She had seen it yesterday morning.

Yesterday morning, at twenty past eight, she had walked past the Humanities fire doors with her satchel held across her chest like a breastplate to keep the older boys from grabbing the nylon strap. Mr. Teasdale had been standing on an aluminum stepladder with a staple gun between his teeth, his gray nylon trousers pulled up so high that three inches of gray sock showed above his brown slip-on brogues. He had dropped two staples onto the linoleum, and Robin had watched them bounce—*snick, snick*—into the corner by the fire extinguisher.

She looked down at the floor of the corridor through the door.

There they were.

Two silver wire staples, their ends bent into shallow right angles, lying half-in and half-out of the dust-fur that gathered beneath the skirt of the radiator.

Robin pulled her legs through the gap.

The threshold was eighteen inches deep, solid ashlar blocks that had been squared off with an adze a hundred years before the first plastic tile had been laid in the county. As her plimsolls hit the linoleum, the soles made a sharp, clean *chirp* against the wax.

It was a real sound. It was not the muffled, woolly sound of an empty cellar; it had the long, flat slap of a corridor with plasterboard walls and twenty-eight closed timber doors.

To her left, the corridor ran thirty yards toward the double fire doors that led to the central hall. Through the wired glass in the upper panels, the light was yellow and flat—the dull, greasy daylight of yesterday’s rain, which had stayed thick against the hills until nearly three in the afternoon.

To her right, the passage led past the three small practice rooms attached to the music block.

From Practice Room 2 came the sound of a clarinet.

It was not playing a tune. It was someone trying, five times in succession, to produce the low G-sharp in the fourth bar of the *Petite Suite*. The note came out four times as a hollow, spitting wooden clatter, like an empty thread-spool dropped on a carpet, and then on the fifth attempt it broke into a piercing, bird-like shriek that made the fillings in Robin’s back teeth ache.

Robin stopped dead. Her hand flew to her cardigan pocket, her fingers closing around her plastic bus pass as if the laminated card could anchor her to the floorboards.

That clarinet was Leo Henderson.

Leo was in 8B. Yesterday, during fifth period, Leo had been sent out of Mr. Finch’s English class for having an unsharpened pencil, and Robin had heard him being marched down the corridor by the head of year, who had told him that if he couldn't keep his mouth shut during the silent reading he could spend the rest of the afternoon doing his scales in the soundproof annex.

She had heard that exact same shriek—the high, spitting G-sharp that sounded like a wet finger dragged across a dinner-plate—yesterday at twenty past one.

She looked at her watch.

It was a cheap electronic Casio with a cracked acrylic face that her uncle had found in the glove-box of a Ford Cortina he had broken for scrap in July. The digital numbers said: *13:22*.

Robin walked five paces toward Practice Room 2.

The doors were modern—hollow-core birch ply, painted with three coats of borough-spec gloss the color of dried marigolds. In the center of each door was an observation panel: a twelve-inch square of thick plate glass with a grid of hexagonal chicken wire embedded in the middle.

She looked through.

Leo Henderson was sitting on an orange polypropylene stacking chair with his knees splayed wide, his blucher boots unlaced and the tongues hanging out like two black leather dogs’ tongues. He had his tie off; it was lying on the top of the upright piano beside an empty packet of salt-and-vinegar crisps and an iron key on a red plastic fob.

His cheeks were puffed out until the small brown freckles on his cheekbones looked like nutmeg grated over lard. His thumb was crooked over the register key, his lower lip tucked back over his teeth in that ugly, grimacing snarl that Mr. Braithwaite was always trying to correct with a wooden ruler.

He did not look at the door.

He blew: *squawk... squawk... ch-gug... wheeeee*.

Robin stood so close that the tip of her nose almost touched the cold surface of the glass. She could smell the orange oil they used to polish the piano keys; she could see the gray smudge on Leo’s white collar where he had rubbed his neck with an ink-stained thumb.

He was not a photograph. He was not a television show. He was sweating. A tiny drop of moisture had gathered in the hollow above his collarbone, right where the top button of his shirt had come off, leaving three frayed threads of white cotton that twitched every time his ribcage expanded.

He took the mouthpiece out of his mouth, wiped the reed against the sleeve of his blazer, and swore softly.

"Bugger it," he said.

The voice was clear through the hollow-core ply. It was thin, slightly cracked, with that high, whistling lisp he had had ever since he lost his left canine to a cricket ball behind the gasworks.

Robin stepped back.

Her left heel came down on the edge of a floor tile that had lifted from the screed, producing a dry, clicking *tick*.

Leo’s head jerked up.

He did not look at the glass generally, the way people look when a shadow crosses the window. He looked directly at her eyes.

His pupils were wide, black, reflecting the cold white light of the fluorescent tube behind her head. His eyebrows drew down into a hard, ugly V that pulled the freckles across the bridge of his nose until they formed a solid brown bar.

"Who’s that?" he said.

He did not speak loudly. He spoke in that sharp, suspicious snarl that boys use when they think someone has been watching them do something stupid through a crack in the toilet doors.

Robin did not answer. She could not. Her tongue felt broad and dry, like a piece of salted ox-tongue that had been left in the sun until the skin curled.

Leo stood up. He did not put the clarinet down on the piano; he held it by the barrel like a club, his fingers white around the key-rods. He took one step toward the door, his unlaced boot making a heavy, dragging *flump* against the acoustic matting.

"What you looking at, Skimmer?" he asked.

The name.

He had said it with the exact same flat, bored cruelty as Kacey Vane, but there was something else in his voice now—something that had not been there yesterday when he had passed her on the fire-stairs.

It was confusion.

His eyes flickered down from her face to the front of her clothes.

Robin was wearing her navy parka.

Yesterday—on Monday the fourteenth—Robin had not worn the navy parka. On Monday the fourteenth, the rain had stopped for two hours between breakfast and lunch, and her mother had made her wear the gray macintosh with the plastic belt because the parka had been hanging over the clothes-airer in the scullery to dry after Sunday’s downpour.

Leo stopped two feet from the glass. He tilted his head to one side, like a dog looking through a gap in a fence at a cat it wasn't sure was dead yet.

"Why you got that coat on?" he asked through the wood. "You were wearing the other one in French. I saw you."

Robin did not wait.

She spun on the balls of her feet, her plimsolls squealing on the wax, and ran the five paces back to the opening in the wall.

Door 17 was still standing twelve inches open. Behind the gray oak, the boiler room was dark, orange-bellied from the furnace glow, smelling of hot iron and dust.

She dropped to her knees, drove her head and shoulders through the ashlar blocks, and scrambled across the millstone grit. The rough stone tore at the sleeve of her parka, the sharp edge of an old mortar-joint catching the fabric with a loud, tearing *zip*, but she did not stop until her shins hit the cold iron rim of the milk crate.

She grabbed the iron ring of Door 17 and hauled it shut.

The door went *clack*.

The sound was small, dry, completely final.

Robin lay on the concrete with her cheek pressed against the cold dust, her chest heaving against her ribs like a bird in a shoebox.

Above her head, through twelve feet of pre-cast concrete and three inches of timber floorboards, came the distant, muffled sound of three hundred pairs of rubber shoes returning from double Games: *thud... thud... thud... thud*.

It was Tuesday. It was twenty-five past one in the afternoon.

And through the floor, from the Humanities corridor three levels down and twenty-four hours behind, she could hear—faint as a pin dropped into an empty milk-bottle—the slow, rhythmic tapping of a wooden clarinet barrel against the wired glass of an observation panel.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

### Chapter 3: The Geometry of the Apple Core

Robin did not go home by the main road.

She went by the canal bank, where the towpath had been paved with red clinker that stained the sides of her plimsolls with a fine, brick-colored powder. The town was called High Beck, though the beck had been diverted through two six-foot Armco pipes beneath the shopping precinct in 1974, leaving only the canal and the marsh-drains that ran between the railway line and the chemical works.

The town did not look old, and it did not look new. It looked like something that had been built in a hurry out of gray breeze-blocks and corrugated asbestos, and then forgotten in the rain until the iron screws had rusted into orange halos around the washers.

Her house was in Duke Street.

Duke Street was sixty houses long, built of narrow, wire-cut brick that had turned the color of cold bacon-grease from eighty years of soot from the shunting yards. It had no front gardens; the stone doorsteps came straight down onto the pavement, two inches from the asphalt, so that when the heavy lorries from the container depot hit the drain-cover at the corner, the willow-pattern plates on the Welsh dresser in Robin’s kitchen would chatter against their wire stands like teeth in a draft.

Her mother was sitting at the table in her white nylon coat, the one with the blue trim on the collar that showed she was an auxiliary on the evening shift at the St. Jude’s Lodge residential home.

She had an enameled basin between her knees, full of cold water and small, yellowish potatoes that she was peeling with an old horn-handled knife whose blade had been ground down until it was no wider than a willow leaf.

*Scritch... plop.*

The peelings came off in thin, curling strips that floated on the dark water like dead leaves.

"You’re late," her mother said. She did not take her eyes off the potato in her thumb. "The five-past bus must have gone right past the end of the lane. I told you, Robin, if you don't stand right by the lamp-post where the driver can see you, he won't stop for a single girl. They don't like the stop on the hill; it takes the lining off the brakes."

"I walked," Robin said.

She took off her parka and hung it on the back of the scullery door. The tear on the left sleeve was three inches long—a clean, triangular rent where the ashlar had caught the outer nylon. Through the hole, the gray polyester wadding looked like greasy sheep’s wool.

Her mother stopped her knife.

"What’s that?"

Robin kept her back turned. She took her school shoes out of her satchel—she had changed into them at the bus shelter to save her plimsolls from the clinker—and set them under the copper boiler.

"Wire," she said. "On the fence by the tennis courts."

"You’re careless, Robin," her mother said, and her voice did not have anger in it; it had that thin, flat fatigue that was worse than anger, the kind that sounded as if she were reading the items off an old grocery receipt that had already been paid by someone else. "That coat was six pound fifty from the church hall. I haven't got six pound fifty to give to Mrs. Corley for another one before the frost. You’ll have to wear the mac."

"The mac’s thin," Robin whispered.

"The mac has got no holes in it," her mother said. *Scritch... plop.* "Wash your hands. There’s drippings in the jar if you want a crust before I go."

Robin went to the stone sink. The cold tap had a piece of rubber tubing slipped over the nozzle to stop the spray, but the rubber had perished at the neck, and a fine, needle-like jet of water squirted sideways every time the valve was opened, hitting the yellow distemper on the wall with a soft *pssst*.

She washed her hands with the brown carbolic soap.

Under the skin of her right palm, right in the fleshy pad beneath the thumb, was a tiny black speck.

It was not coal-dust. She scrubbed it with the stiff bristles of the nail-brush until the skin was pink and raw, but the speck did not come out. It was a flake of white lead paint from the number *17* on the boiler-room door, driven deep under the dermis when she had hauled the timber shut against Leo Henderson’s clarinet.

She dried her hands on the roller towel behind the door.

"Mum?"

"Aye."

"If you leave something somewhere... like, in a room that’s locked up... does it stay there?"

Her mother dropped the last potato into the basin. The water had turned gray and cloudy from the starch, with a greasy yellow foam gathering around the rim.

"What sort of question is that?" she said, wiping her thumbs on her apron. "Of course it stays there. Unless someone takes it. If people didn't take things, the world would be ten feet deep in old boots and tea-leaves. Put the kettle on. I’ve twenty minutes before the relief van comes."

Robin filled the kettle.

She stood by the gas-stove, watching the blue points of flame lick around the blackened aluminum base, her thumb rubbing the tiny lead splinter in her palm.

*If people didn't take things.*

Tomorrow was Wednesday.

In the real school—the school where Mrs. Garside confiscated Tupperware and Kacey Vane swung her patent heels against the plywood—it would be the sixteenth of October.

In the room behind Door 17, it would be Tuesday the fifteenth.

It would be today.

### Chapter 4: The Notch in the Bench

On Wednesday morning, Robin arrived at school at ten past eight, forty minutes before the warning bell.

The service yard behind the kitchens was empty except for two council refuse lorries that were backing up to the swill-bins with their diesel engines idling: *rattle-rattle-clonk... rattle-rattle-clonk*. The drivers were inside the boiler-house office, having a mug of tea with Mr. Gowan, the head caretaker. Through the high, wire-meshed windows of the workshop, Robin could see the yellow light of their paraffin heater and the blue smoke of their rolling tobacco drifting across the rafters.

She did not use the pea-green sheet-steel door.

She went around by the coal-bunkers, where the retaining wall had dropped six inches into the clay, leaving a gap between the concrete coping and the chain-link fence just wide enough for an eight-stone twelve-year-old to slide through sideways. Her skirt caught on a blackberry runner, the dry thorns pulling three loops of purple wool out of the pleats, but she did not stop to untangle them; she dropped into the well of the old coal-chute, her plimsolls sinking two inches into the wet slack.

The sheet-steel door was unlatched. The bent six-inch nail lay on the greasy flagstone where she had left it on Tuesday.

Robin slipped inside.

The boiler room was hotter than it had been the day before. The three heat exchangers were working at full capacity, their secondary valves whistling with a high, thin steam-leak that sounded like someone blowing across the top of an empty ink-bottle.

*Thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug.*

Robin did not sit on the milk crate.

She took an apple out of her pocket. It was not a fresh one; it was the core she had saved from her Tuesday tea—a dry, shriveled gray spindle of flesh with three black pips still clinging to the fibers and a brown bruise where her thumb had pressed into the skin.

She walked to the millstone grit wall.

Door 17 looked smaller in the dim, orange furnace-light. The white lead *17* seemed to have receded into the grain of the oak, the edges of the numbers blurred by sixty years of coal-smoke.

She took the iron ring in both hands, turned it until the heavy bolt clacked, and pulled the door toward her sixteen inches.

The cool air came out.

It was not yesterday’s air.

Yesterday’s air had smelled of Leo Henderson’s salt-and-vinegar crisps and the dry, burnt-felt smell of the Humanities radiator.

This air smelled of wet floor-mops and cold, boiled eggs.

Tuesday.

Robin crawled through the ashlar opening.

The Humanities corridor was dark. The four-foot fluorescent tubes were off, but the emergency bulkheads—small, rectangular boxes of green plastic with two low-wattage bulbs inside—cast a sickly, lime-colored glow across the thermoplastic tiles.

She checked her Casio.

*08:14.*

On Tuesday morning at 08:14, Robin had been standing in the bus shelter at the bottom of Duke Street, waiting for the No. 4 bus that had never arrived because the driver had had a fit in the depot at High Beck. She had stood there for twenty-five minutes in the freezing mist, her knees turning the color of corned beef, while the older boys from the grammar school threw gravel at the corrugated iron roof of the shelter.

She was here now.

The corridor was empty, but it was not silent.

From the far end, beyond the double doors that led to the library, came the sound of a rubber bucket being set down on stone: *clonk... slosh*.

Then the long, wet *hiss-swish* of a squeegee being dragged across wet linoleum.

It was Mrs. Noakes, the morning cleaner.

Mrs. Noakes was seventy-two. She had a hip that clicked every time she put her weight on her left foot, and she wore a blue nylon tabard over a brown cardigan that had safety pins instead of buttons. She had cleaned the Humanities corridor every school morning since 1978.

*Swish... click... slosh.*

Robin pressed her back against the cork noticeboard.

The blue sugar-paper notice was still there, pinned with the four red pins. Beside it, someone had pinned a handwritten card on lined index paper:

*FOUND: One silver-colored Parker pen with blue ink-stains on the grip. Owner may claim from the Pastoral Office on payment of a 10p library fine.*

Robin remembered that card. Tanya Siddle had found that pen on Monday afternoon in the girls’ toilets, and had handed it in to Mr. Teasdale on Tuesday morning to show how honest she was, after she had used it to write *ROBIN MERCER IS A DOG* across the back of the toilet door in the third-year block.

Robin looked down at the apple core in her hand.

She walked four paces down the corridor to the small recess where the water-cooler stood.

The cooler was an old English Electric model with a white enameled cowl and a heavy brass pedal that had to be kicked with your heel to make the jet rise two inches above the porcelain drain.

Beneath the cooler was an eight-inch space between the motor housing and the wall, covered by a black iron grille that was held in place by two slotted screws.

Robin knelt down.

She took the apple core by its dry stem, reached her arm through the two-inch gap between the iron bars, and pushed the shriveled core into the dark space behind the compressor.

It sat on the greasy zinc tray, right between the condenser coils, surrounded by three dead blowflies and a ball of gray carpet-fluff.

She stood up.

She took a four-inch wire nail from her skirt pocket—the one she had picked up from the broken packing crate in the boiler room—and walked to the yellow-gloss door of Practice Room 1.

The door-frame was made of softwood, painted five times with the same thick marigold gloss.

Robin took the point of the nail and pressed it into the timber, right at the height of her own shoulder, four inches below the top hinge.

She did not carve a word.

She carved a single, horizontal line, three-quarters of an inch long, and then drew two vertical ticks across it, like the marks they make on the timbers of a ship to show where the bulkhead meets the ribs.

*---||---*

The dry paint resisted for a second, then cracked with a tiny, sharp *tick* that sounded like a fingernail snapping. A small curl of white lead undercoat came away on the point of the nail, leaving the yellow gloss scarred with a raw, gray furrow that smelled of dead linseed oil.

From the far end of the corridor, the bucket went *clonk*.

The squeegee stopped.

"Who’s down there?"

Mrs. Noakes’s voice was like two gravel-bags being rubbed together in an alley.

"That you, Gary? If you’ve come for them folding chairs, they’re in the boiler-house. Gowan took the keys home with him last night on account of the latch being loose."

Robin did not move. She flattened herself into the recess beside the water-cooler, her heels pressed hard against the skirting board.

A shadow fell across the green emergency light at the corner of the library wing.

It was not a tall shadow; it was wide, round-shouldered, with the distinct, triangular outline of a plastic dustpan held against a hip.

The footsteps came on: *scuff... click... scuff... click*.

The click was the bad hip. It had a hollow, mechanical sound, like an old bicycle pedal that had lost its ball-bearings.

Mrs. Noakes turned the corner.

She was twenty yards away. She was not looking at the floor; she was looking at the yellow doors of the practice rooms, her spectacles catching the green light until her eyes looked like two round, phosphorescent pond-snails.

She stopped.

She did not see Robin in the shadow of the cooler.

She was looking at the door of Practice Room 1.

Her head went forward, her neck stretching out of the blue tabard like an old hen looking through the wire of a coop.

"What’s that?" she muttered.

She took three dragging steps toward the marigold door, her felt slippers making a wet, whispering sound on the tiles she had just mopped.

She stopped eighteen inches from the mark.

Her hand—a large, red, swollen hand with fingers that looked like five Cumberland sausages that had been boiled until the skins split—went up to the softwood frame.

Her thumb touched the gray furrow: *---||---*.

Robin watched her.

Mrs. Noakes did not curse. She did not say, *Them bloody kids.*

She drew her thumb back as if the gray paint had been hot.

She looked at her thumb-pad. A tiny crumb of dried yellow gloss was stuck to her skin, right in the whorl of her print.

Then she looked down the corridor toward the water-cooler.

She did not look generally.

She looked directly into the dark recess where Robin was standing.

Her spectacles were crooked, one arm mended with a piece of pink Elastoplast that had turned black at the edges from dishwater. Behind the thick lenses, her eyes were small, wet, and pale blue—the color of skimmed milk that had been watered down to stretch the jug.

"You," she said.

The word was not loud, but it had no question in it.

It sounded as if she had been waiting for Robin to stand in that recess since five o’clock in the morning.

"You’re the Mercer girl," Mrs. Noakes whispered. "From Duke Street."

Robin did not breathe. Her ribs were locked against the casing of the water-cooler; she could feel the vibration of the little fan inside the motor buzzing against her left hip-bone.

"You weren't here," Mrs. Noakes said.

She took one more step. The bad hip gave a loud, wet *tock* that sounded like a dry bone being split with an iron cleaver.

"I mopped this floor at twenty past seven," she said, her voice rising into that thin, querulous whistle that old women use when their tea has gone cold. "There wasn't no one through the turnstiles. Gowan hasn't unbolted the north gate yet. How did you get in here with them dirty shoes?"

Her hand went out.

The red sausages reached into the recess.

Robin saw the dark dirt under the old woman’s fingernails—not school dirt, but the greasy, carbonized dust from the grate of an old coal-fired range that hadn't been black-leaded since the winter of sixty-three.

Robin ducked.

She dropped beneath the sweeping arm, her shoulder striking Mrs. Noakes’s thigh. The blue nylon tabard smelled of bleach and damp dog-biscuit, so thick that Robin had to swallow her own spit to keep from retching.

The old woman gave a sharp, wet grunt: *oof*.

Robin scrambled across the wet tiles on all fours. Her plimsolls found no grip on the fresh floor-seal; she skidded two feet into the opposite skirting board, her knee striking the iron radiator bracket with a dull, ringing shock that went straight to her teeth.

Behind her, Mrs. Noakes did not shout for help.

She turned around with that slow, heavy, three-stage rotation of people whose joints have calcified into solid bone.

"You aren't on the register," she said.

Her voice was different now. The querulousness had gone out of it; it was flat, heavy, like the sound of a shovel-blade striking wet clay at the bottom of a trench.

"You aren't on the register for today, Robin Mercer. Your name’s down for tomorrow."

Robin reached Door 17.

She did not look back. She threw herself through the opening head-first, her satchel catching on the lower hinge and tearing the strap away from the buckle with a loud *thwack*.

She fell four feet onto the coal-dust of the boiler room, landing on her elbows and knees in the cold slack.

The sheet-steel fire door behind her went *bang*.

No. Not the steel door.

Door 17.

It had shut itself.

The iron ring had rotated sixty degrees in its plate, the heavy bolt falling into the keeper with the sound of an iron gate dropping into place on an anchor-chain: *thluck*.

Robin sat in the coal-dust, holding her bleeding knee between her palms.

The boiler room was empty.

The three heat exchangers went *thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug*.

Above her head, through the ceiling, the eight-thirty bell began to ring for the start of Wednesday morning.

It was Wednesday.

The sixteenth.

Robin stood up, her legs shaking so violently that she had to brace her forearms against the greasy casing of the primary pump to keep from falling back into the slack.

She walked out through the pea-green door, across the service yard, and into the main school through the boys’ entrance by the gym.

### Chapter 5: The Evidence of Wednesday

The Humanities corridor was full of people.

It was eight-forty. Two hundred second- and third-year pupils were milling outside the classrooms, shoving each other against the lockers, their wet anoraks filling the air with the smell of steamed wool and salt-grit.

Robin walked slowly down the center of the tiles.

Her knee was stiff; a dark, circular patch of blood, two inches across, had soaked through her gray wool tights and was drying into a stiff, black crust against the fabric of her skirt.

She stopped at the water-cooler.

Three first-year boys were taking turns trying to squirt water into each other’s blazer pockets by jamming their thumbs over the nozzle.

"Get out of the way, Skimmer," one of them said, not even looking at her as he elbowed her aside to reach the brass pedal.

Robin dropped to her knees on the linoleum.

She did not care that thirty people were watching her; she did not care that Tanya Siddle was standing ten feet away by the display cabinet, whispering something to Kacey Vane that made both of them look over their shoulders and snigger into their pencil cases.

She peered through the black iron grille beneath the compressor.

There, on the greasy zinc tray between the condenser coils, sat the apple core.

It was not gray anymore.

It had been sitting there for twenty-four hours in the warm draft of the little electric fan. The flesh had shrunk into a hard, black, leather-like knob, the size of a walnut, crusted with the gray dust that came off the bottom of the pupils’ trousers.

Two small black ants were crawling over the withered skin.

Robin stood up.

Her heart was beating in her throat, a high, fluttering panic that felt like a moth trapped inside an enameled mug.

She walked four paces to the door of Practice Room 1.

The door was closed. Through the wired glass, she could see Miss Finch sitting at the small upright piano, her cardigan draped over the back of her chair, her pencil held between her teeth while she corrected a sheet of manuscript paper.

Robin looked at the softwood frame, four inches below the top hinge.

There it was.

The mark: *---||---*.

It was not raw anymore.

The exposed wood in the center of the furrow had turned a dull, dirty yellow-gray, darkened by twenty-four hours of corridor-dust and the greasy smoke from the school kitchen chimneys. The little curl of white lead undercoat that Robin had peeled away with the point of the nail was gone—knocked off, probably, by someone’s sports bag as they pushed past during the three-o’clock rush on Tuesday afternoon.

It was old.

It looked as if it had been carved ten years ago, during the term when they had built the new library extension and the painters had left their ladders in the Humanities porch.

"Robin Mercer."

The voice came from behind her right shoulder.

It was not Mrs. Noakes.

It was Mr. Teasdale.

The deputy head was holding a sheaf of buff-colored disciplinary cards in his left hand, his thumb hooked into the pocket of his gray waistcoat. His face was long, pale, with that faint, purple network of broken veins across the bridge of his nose that came from thirty-two years of shouting at boys in the rain on the lower playing fields.

He looked down at Robin’s knee.

"You’re bleeding on the council’s floor-seal, Mercer," he said. His voice was not loud, but it had that dry, administrative weight that carried across forty yards of shouting children without rising half a tone. "Go to the nurse."

"Yes, sir," Robin whispered.

She began to turn away, but Mr. Teasdale’s hand came down on her shoulder.

His fingers were broad, flat-tipped, smelling of tobacco and the carbolic soap they put in the staff lavatories. His grip was not violent, but it had the steady, immovable pressure of an iron clamp.

"Just a moment," he said.

He leaned forward, his spectacles sliding down the sweat on his nose, his eyes fixed on the door-frame of Practice Room 1.

He looked at the mark: *---||---*.

Then he looked at Robin’s right hand.

Robin had her fingers curled into her palm, but she had not washed them since she crawled out of the coal-slack. The nails were black with furnace-grit, and across the knuckle of her right forefinger was a fresh, white scratch where the corner of the ashlar block had taken the skin off as she fell.

Mr. Teasdale reached down.

He took her right wrist between his forefinger and thumb. He did not pinch; he simply held the joint so firmly that she could feel her own pulse thumping against his calloused skin.

He turned her hand over.

In the soft meat beneath her thumb, the white lead splinter from Door 17 shone like a tiny, milky eye under the red skin.

"Mrs. Noakes spoke to me this morning," Mr. Teasdale said.

He spoke very quietly, almost into the collar of his own shirt, so that the children passing them in the corridor could only see a master reprimanding a pupil for a torn skirt.

"She came to my office at twenty past seven," he continued. "She was quite distressed, Mercer. She claimed that someone had been in this corridor before the gates were unlocked. She said someone had vandalized the woodwork outside the practice rooms."

Robin tried to pull her wrist back, but Mr. Teasdale’s thumb did not move.

"I told her she was mistaken," he said. "I told her that Mrs. Noakes is seventy-two years of age and that her cataracts are not improving with the winter light. I told her that no pupil could possibly have been in the Humanities wing at seven in the morning, because the keys to the master-padlocks are in my desk drawer."

He paused.

His eyes left her hand and traveled slowly up the front of her uniform.

He looked at her gray cardigan with the missing buttons; he looked at the maroon skirt with the pulled threads; he looked at her face, where the cold had left two bright, feverish spots of red on her cheekbones.

"Why were you in the boiler room yesterday afternoon, Robin?"

The question was so simple, so flat, that Robin felt her knees give way an inch.

"I wasn't," she lied.

"Mr. Gowan found your satchel," Mr. Teasdale said.

He reached into the deep pocket of his tweed jacket and pulled out a narrow strap of dark blue nylon, the buckle bent into a shallow U-shape where it had caught on the iron hinge of Door 17.

"He found it at twenty past three yesterday," Mr. Teasdale said. "Lying in the coal-slack behind the secondary heat exchanger. He brought it to me before he locked up."

Robin stared at the strap.

*Yesterday.*

Tuesday at twenty past three.

At twenty past three on Tuesday, Robin had been sitting in Room 14, watching Kacey Vane swing her patent shoes against the plywood while Mr. Finch read aloud from *The Mayor of Casterbridge*.

Her satchel had been under her desk.

She had carried it home at twenty to four. Her mother had looked at it when she took out her homework diary.

"You’re confused, Mercer," Mr. Teasdale said softly.

He let go of her wrist.

He did not give her the strap; he dropped it back into his pocket with the buff disciplinary cards.

"You have been wandering," he said. "Your mother has enough difficulties without having to come to this school on a Friday afternoon to explain why her daughter is crawling through the coal-bunkers like an animal. Go to the nurse. Have that knee cleaned. And if I see you south of the CDT workshops again this term, I will place you on an official Headmaster’s Report."

He turned and walked down the corridor toward the main hall, his brogues making a rhythmic, heavy *clack... clack... clack* on the tiles.

Robin stood by the water-cooler.

Across the corridor, Tanya Siddle was watching her.

Tanya was not smiling. She had her arms folded across her chest, her fingers digging into the maroon wool of her blazer sleeves, her head tilted slightly forward like a sheep that had spotted an open gate in a drystone wall.

When Robin looked at her, Tanya did not look away.

She took three steps across the corridor, her shoes making no sound at all on the linoleum, until she was standing eighteen inches from Robin’s ear.

"I saw you," Tanya whispered.

The smell of her was sweet and sour—cheap strawberry lip-gloss from the chemist and the cold, fatty bacon she had eaten for breakfast.

"I saw you in the cellar yesterday," Tanya said. "You had that little door open. You were talking to someone."

Robin’s skin went cold.

"I wasn't," she said.

"You were," Tanya whispered. Her eyes were small, brown, wet like two drowned raisins. "You were talking to the old woman. I heard you through the grate by the bins. You said her name."

She leaned closer, until her breath was hot against the shell of Robin’s ear.

"You’re going to get it, Skimmer," she said. "Kacey knows. She knows what you did to her locker."

Robin blinked.

"What locker?"

Tanya smiled. It was a tiny, sharp movement of her upper lip that showed her two front teeth, which were slightly crossed at the center.

"You’ll find out tomorrow," she said.

### Chapter 6: The Paper Mask

The horror did not come all at once. It came like the salt-damp through the kitchen wallpaper: first a faint gray discoloration in the corners, then a soft, spongy swelling under the thumb, and then, weeks later, the whole sheet coming away from the plaster in a single, cold, stinking flap.

On Thursday morning, Robin did not go to registration.

She waited in the girls’ toilets on the ground floor of C-Block until the nine-o’clock bell had finished ringing and the sound of two hundred voices singing "Lord of All Hopefulness" came through the floor-vents from the central hall.

The toilet smelled of carbolic, damp sanitary towels, and the menthol cigarettes that the fifth-year girls smoked behind the cubicle partitions. On the back of the third door, written in purple felt-tip pen, was the graffiti Tanya had mentioned:

*ROBIN MERCER IS A DOG*  
*SHE EATS COAL*  
*ASK HER ABOUT THE CELLAR*

Robin did not look at it. She went out through the fire-exit behind the boiler-house, slipped through the gap in the chain-link fence, and ran across the wet slack to the pea-green door.

She was not afraid of Mr. Gowan now. She was not even afraid of Mr. Teasdale.

She had to know what she had done.

*You’ll find out tomorrow.*

Wednesday had been yesterday.

If Wednesday was yesterday, then behind Door 17, it was Wednesday now.

The boiler room was empty. The pumps were working with that low, heavy *thrum-chug*, the sound filling the room like water in a well.

Robin went to the millstone grit wall.

Door 17 stood four inches open.

She did not have to turn the iron ring; the heavy bolt had not fallen into the keeper. Someone had wedged an old wooden blackboard rubber into the gap at the bottom of the oak, its gray felt surface crusted with white chalk-dust.

Robin dropped to her knees.

She did not crawl through immediately. She listened.

From the other side of the stone came the sound of seventy people singing.

It was faint, muffled by three feet of ashlar and sixty feet of plasterboard, but the tune was clear:

*Be thou my vision, O Lord of my heart...*  
*Naught be all else to me, save that thou art...*

Wednesday’s assembly.

Yesterday’s assembly.

Robin pulled the blackboard rubber out of the door with her thumb and forefinger. The chalk-dust puffed into her face, smelling of dry gypsum and cold classrooms, and then she squeezed her ribs through the eighteen-inch opening.

The Humanities corridor was bright.

The sun was out.

For the first time that week, the clouds had broken over the fells, and long, diagonal shafts of yellow October light were cutting through the high hopper-windows of the corridor, turning the dust-motes into swarms of golden midges.

Robin stood on the beige and green tiles.

Her watch said: *09:12.*

Yesterday at 09:12, she had been sitting in the central hall, second row from the back, her head bowed while Mr. Teasdale read the announcements about the cross-country trials.

She walked quickly down the corridor toward the Art Block.

The Art Block lay at the far end of the south wing, in what had once been the school laundry when the building was a home for orphan boys. It had high, north-facing windows made of ribbed glass, two large ceramic sinks with lead traps that were always clogged with clay-slip, and sixteen heavy deal tables that were scarred with thirty years of lino-cuts and dried poster-paint.

The door was unlocked.

Robin pushed it open.

The room smelled of wet papier-mâché, linseed oil, and the sour vinegar they used to clean the printing plates.

On the drying racks along the back wall, forty papier-mâché masks were sitting on sheets of old newspaper, their gray glue-soaked layers drying in the draft from the open hopper.

They were Year 8 projects. They had spent three weeks making them out of strips of the *Westmorland Gazette* and wallpaper paste, molding the wet pulp over inflated balloons to form the shapes of Greek theater masks.

Robin knew Kacey Vane’s mask.

It was on the third rack from the left, sitting on a double page of the property section.

Kacey had spent four periods painting it with gold acrylic paint she had brought from home in a glass marmalade jar. She had given it high, arched eyebrows drawn with black permanent marker, and two long, narrow eye-slits that she had cut out with a scalpel from the medical cabinet. Around the mouth—a wide, cruel, smiling oval that showed six teeth cut from white card—she had glued thirty tiny plastic pearls that she had stripped off an old hairband.

It was beautiful. It was the only thing Kacey had ever made in school that had not been thrown into the waste-bin at the end of the lesson.

Robin stood by the table.

She did not touch it.

Her hands were deep in the pockets of her gray macintosh—she had worn the macintosh today because the navy parka was still hanging in the Duke Street scullery with the tear in the sleeve.

She looked at the gold paint. It was dry to the touch, but it still smelled of ammonia and cheap varnish.

Then she heard the footsteps.

They were not coming from the corridor.

They were coming from the small storeroom at the back of the art room—the dark cupboard where they kept the rolls of cartridge paper and the five-gallon tubs of PVA glue.

The timber door of the storeroom was painted gray.

It opened three inches.

A girl was standing in the dark behind the paper-racks.

She was wearing a maroon school blazer, two sizes too large, with the sleeves rolled back twice at the cuffs to show the grubby white lining. Her skirt was pleated, thin, with three loops of purple wool pulled out of the hem by a blackberry thorn. Her knee was covered by a square of lint held in place by four strips of pink adhesive tape that had already begun to turn black from boiler-dust.

Robin stopped breathing.

The girl in the cupboard was Robin Mercer.

She was looking at Robin through the three-inch gap between the door and the frame.

Her face was dead-white, the color of skimmed milk, with two bright, greasy circles of red on her cheekbones where the fever had come up. Her hair—the mousy, shoulder-length hair that her mother had cut with the kitchen shears on the first of September—hung in greasy rats’-tails across her forehead.

In her right hand, the girl held a pair of ten-inch dressmaking shears—the heavy, iron-handled ones from the fabric drawer that were only supposed to be used by the teacher for cutting buckram.

Robin could not move.

The floor seemed to drop twelve inches beneath her soles, as if the ashlar blocks of the flax mill had suddenly dissolved into the black grease of the canal.

"Don't," Robin whispered.

The word was so small it did not even disturb the dust-motes in the yellow light.

The girl in the cupboard did not answer.

She did not blink.

Her eyes were wide, flat, completely black in the shadow of the paper-racks, reflecting nothing at all—not the yellow window, not the gold mask on the drying rack, not the girl standing four feet away from her in the gray macintosh.

She brought the shears out of the dark.

The blades were eight inches long, cold, gray carbon-steel with an oily thumb-print near the screw.

She did not look at Robin.

She stepped out of the cupboard with that slow, heavy, dragging lurch that Robin recognized—the stiff, halting walk of someone whose right knee had been bruised against an iron radiator bracket twenty-four hours ago.

She walked to the third drying rack.

She raised the shears in both hands, her knuckles turning white around the black iron loops.

"No," Robin said.

She lunged forward, her hand reaching out for the sleeve of the maroon blazer—her own sleeve, her own woolen hem, the torn lining she had pinned with a safety pin on Tuesday night.

Her fingers closed on the wool.

It was not solid.

It did not feel like cloth. It felt like cold, wet grease—the thick, gelatinous fat that sits on the top of an old beef-stew after it has spent three days in the pantry. Her fingers slipped through the fabric without resistance, her knuckles striking the wooden edge of the drying rack with a sharp, hollow *tock*.

The girl with the shears did not turn her head.

She brought the blades down.

*Crunch.*

The sound was terrible. It was the sound of twelve layers of dry, glue-soaked newspaper being crushed between two blunt edges of iron.

The gold mask split from the forehead to the chin.

The thirty plastic pearls popped off the mouth like peas from a dry pod, bouncing across the deal tables and into the zinc sinks: *tic... tic... tic-tic-tic*.

The girl did not stop.

She drove the point of the shears through the right eye-slit, twisting the iron blades until the cardboard teeth tore away in a jagged, hairy flap of gray fiber.

Then she picked up a six-ounce bottle of scarlet Quink ink from the teacher’s desk.

She did not take the stopper out.

She smashed the neck of the bottle against the corner of the iron sink.

The blue glass shattered into fifty pieces, and the dark red ink—thick, oily, smelling of iron-filings and dry phenol—poured across the two halves of the gold mask, soaking into the torn newspaper until the Greek face looked like something that had been pulled out from under the wheels of a tractor.

The girl dropped the broken bottle into the sink.

She turned around.

She looked directly at Robin.

Her mouth was open. Not in a scream; not in a laugh.

Her lower jaw was hanging down two inches, as if the muscles had been cut through with a razor, her pale tongue lying flat and dry against her lower teeth like a dead slug.

She raised her right hand.

The skin was black with coal-dust, the palm crusted with dried blood from the knee-fall, and the white lead splinter from Door 17 was glowing in her thumb-pad like a pearl under glass.

She pointed her forefinger at the gray macintosh.

"Tomorrow," she whispered.

The voice was not Robin’s voice.

It was Leo Henderson’s voice. It was Mrs. Noakes’s voice. It was the sound of the primary pump in the cellar going *thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug*.

"Tomorrow, Skimmer."

Robin screamed.

She did not know she had screamed until her throat felt raw and salty, like she had swallowed three mouthfuls of canal-water.

She turned and ran.

She ran down the Humanities corridor, past the water-cooler where the apple core was shrinking into black leather, past the cork board with the four red pins, and threw herself through Door 17 into the dark.

### Chapter 7: The Interrogation

"She’s not a normal girl, Claire."

Mr. Teasdale was sitting behind his desk in the Pastoral Office.

The desk was made of steel-framed laminate, the edges finished with black PVC trim that had been chewed away near the telephone by thirty years of nervous fingers. On the blotter lay the two halves of Kacey Vane’s gold mask, wrapped in a sheet of clean greaseproof paper from the canteen, and a transparent polythene evidence bag containing a ten-inch pair of iron dressmaking shears.

The shears had red ink on the blades.

Robin’s mother was sitting on the low vinyl armchair by the filing cabinet. She had not had time to take off her auxiliary’s coat; the white nylon was gray at the cuffs from the disinfectant they used in the laundry at St. Jude’s, and her plastic identity badge—*MRS. C. MERCER, CARE ASSISTANT*—was pinned crookedly across her left breast.

She had her hands in her lap, her fingers twisting her imitation-leather purse until the plastic seams showed white.

"She was in registration," her mother said. Her voice was thin, small, carrying that terrifying, defensive whine that Robin had heard her use with the gas-man and the rent-collector. "Her tutor said she was there. Mr. Finch said she was sitting by the radiator."

"She was in registration on Wednesday morning," Mr. Teasdale said. He spoke with that terrifying, patient gentleness that headmasters use when they are about to phone the borough social services department. "We are not talking about Wednesday morning, Mrs. Mercer. We are talking about Wednesday afternoon."

"She comes straight home," her mother whispered. "She has her tea at four. Two eggs and a slice. She never stays for the clubs."

Mr. Teasdale sighed.

He took his spectacles off, folded the wire arms with a dry *click*, and rubbed the bridge of his nose where the red pads had left two circular dents in his flesh.

"Mrs. Mercer," he said. "The art room was locked at three-thirty on Wednesday afternoon by Miss Finch. When she arrived at eight o'clock this morning—Thursday morning—the door was still locked from the outside. The mortice bolt had not been thrown."

He leaned forward, pointing a blunt forefinger at the greaseproof paper.

"This was found inside the sink. The ink was still wet, Mrs. Mercer. The solvent had not evaporated from the paper."

"Then it wasn't Robin," her mother said, and there was a sudden, sharp flare of hope in her eyes that made Robin’s ribs ache with shame. "If it was done last night, Robin was in her bed. I sleep with the scullery door unlatched, Mr. Teasdale; if the latch goes, I hear it from the top of the stairs. She was in her bed from nine o’clock."

"It was not done last night," Mr. Teasdale said.

He opened a manila folder with the name *MERCER, ROBIN (8C)* written across the tab in purple felt-tip pen.

"The ink was wet because the bottle had been smashed under the running tap," he said. "The tap was dripping. But the shears... these shears, Mrs. Mercer... were taken from the needlework cupboard on Tuesday morning. We know that because Miss Finch noted their absence in the stock register at eleven o'clock on Tuesday."

He looked at Robin.

Robin was standing by the door, her shoulders braced against the wall-bars of the radiator. She had her hands behind her back, her fingers hooked into the hem of her cardigan.

"Show your mother your hands, Robin," Mr. Teasdale said.

Robin did not move.

"Robin," her mother whispered. "Show him your hands. Show him there’s nothing on 'em."

Robin brought her hands out.

She held them out with the palms down, the fingers straight.

Across the back of her right wrist was a dark, purple-red smear, three inches long, where the Quink ink had soaked into the pores of her skin. It was not fresh; it had been washed with carbolic soap until the skin was raw and shining, but the red pigment had gone deep into the creases of her knuckles, outlining every tiny rhomboid of her skin with a fine, scarlet thread.

Her mother made a small, dry sound in her throat—*huk*—like someone who had swallowed a bone.

"Where did you get that?" her mother asked.

Robin looked at the gold mask on the desk.

The split ran right through the nose. The cardboard teeth were still hanging by two threads of gray papier-mâché, crusted with the dried red ink until they looked like the teeth of a sheep that had been worried by dogs in a ditch.

"I didn't do it," Robin said.

"Robin," Mr. Teasdale said, his voice dropping another semi-tone into that dreadful, administrative quiet. "Tanya Siddle saw you."

"Tanya lies," Robin said.

"Tanya Siddle was in the library corridor at three-fifteen on Wednesday," Mr. Teasdale said. "She was returning a dictionary to the staff room. She saw you coming out of the Art Block. She said you were wearing your gray macintosh."

He looked down at the floor by Robin’s feet.

"You are wearing the gray macintosh today, Robin. You were not wearing it on Wednesday morning. On Wednesday morning, according to your tutor, you were wearing your navy parka."

"The parka has a hole in it," Robin whispered.

"Yes," Mr. Teasdale said. "A hole that you told your mother was caused by the tennis-court wire. But Mr. Gowan found a scrap of navy nylon caught in the lower hinge of the service door in the old boiler room. A three-inch triangular scrap, Robin. With gray polyester wadding adhering to the seam."

He closed the manila folder.

The sound was very small, but it filled the room like the dropping of a trapdoor.

"Kacey Vane’s father is a member of the Board of Governors," Mr. Teasdale said, not looking at Robin now, but looking directly at her mother’s white nylon badge. "He has already spoken to the chairman this morning. He is... understandably... extremely concerned. Not merely by the destruction of the child’s work, which represents several weeks of effort, but by the... the vindictive character of the incident."

"She’s twelve," her mother pleaded. "She’s only twelve, Mr. Teasdale. She’s never had a report. Her father... her father wasn't right in his head before he went, but Robin’s never had a bad word from the police."

"This is not a matter for the police, Mrs. Mercer," Mr. Teasdale said, though the way he said the word *police* made it clear that the telephone was already sitting on the corner of his desk for that very purpose. "Not at this stage. But I must consider the safety of the other pupils. A child who carries ten-inch dressmaking shears through the school corridors during the lunch hour... a child who hides in the boiler-houses and defaces property with iron nails..."

He stopped.

He looked at the window.

Outside, in the gray drizzle that had returned with the midday wind, the boys from the upper fifth were lining up by the gymnasium for the bus to the playing fields.

"I am suspending Robin for five days," he said. "Beginning tomorrow morning. She will not attend the school premises for any reason until the Board has reviewed the incident on Tuesday next."

"Five days," her mother whispered. "I have to work the weekend, Mr. Teasdale. Who’s to look after her? She can't sit in the house by herself with the gas-fire on."

"That," Mr. Teasdale said, standing up from his desk and smoothing the front of his waistcoat with both hands, "is a domestic matter for you, Mrs. Mercer. Robin will leave the site now. She will take her belongings with her."

He looked at Robin.

His eyes were not angry. They were tired, cold, carrying that complete, immovable indifference of a civil servant who has successfully moved an awkward file from the *Current* tray into the *Pending* box.

"Go to your locker, Mercer," he said. "Clear your books. And do not let me see you on the perimeter path after three o'clock."

### Chapter 8: The Boy in the Changing Room

Robin did not go to her locker.

She did not go to Room 14 to collect her geometry set, her English exercise book with the blue paper cover, or her spare pair of gray woolen socks from the bottom of the wire basket.

She walked out of the Pastoral Office, down the central stairs where the portraits of the former headmasters hung in their brown oak frames, and straight through the fire-exit behind the boiler-house.

The rain was falling hard now. It was that cold, northern rain that came off the Irish Sea and hit the Pennines, turning into a fine, gray sleet that stung the bare skin of her legs like thrown salt.

She did not use the gap in the chain-link fence.

She walked straight to the pea-green sheet-steel door.

The six-inch nail was lying on the flagstone. She kicked it into the drain with the toe of her plimsoll, listening to the small *clink* as it hit the black sludge six feet below the yard.

She pulled the door open and went in.

The boiler room was cold.

The three heat exchangers had cut out; their automatic thermostats had tripped when the pressure in the town mains dropped at noon, and the great iron bodies were clicking as the metal cooled: *tic... tic... tic*.

The smell was different now.

The machine smell had gone. The paraffin and the hot machine oil had faded into the background, replaced by a cold, sour, earthy stench that smelled of the canal-bottom—of dead weed, rotting timber, and the gray silt that sat beneath the stone piers of the railway bridge.

Door 17 was wide open.

It was not twelve inches open; it was standing forty-five degrees into the chamber, the heavy oak frame resting against the rusted side of the secondary heat exchanger.

Robin stood on the concrete.

She did not have her satchel. She did not have her lunchbox. She had only her gray macintosh, her thin school skirt, and the four-inch wire nail she had kept in her pocket since Tuesday morning.

She walked to the opening.

The threshold was dry. The ashlar blocks were the color of lead, crusted with a fine, powdery frost that had formed along the mortar-lines, though the temperature in the cellar was well above freezing.

She looked through into the Humanities corridor.

It was not bright.

The lights were out.

Not just the fluorescent tubes; the green emergency bulkheads were dead too, their plastic covers filled with three inches of dark, oily water that had leaked through the ceiling-boards from the flat roof above.

The floor was covered in two inches of gray water.

It was not clean tap-water. It was the greasy, soap-filmed water that came out of the school kitchens when the grease-traps overflowed, carrying small scraps of cabbage-leaf, orange-peel, and the white, disintegrating mush of wet toilet-paper.

Robin stepped through.

Her plimsolls went under immediately: *slosh*.

The cold was agonizing. It went through the thin canvas and the wool of her socks like twenty needles driven into her arches, making her draw her breath in with a sharp, whistling hiss.

Her Casio said: *00:00.*

The liquid crystal display was dead. The little black segments had scrambled into a meaningless knot of lines, like a spider that had been crushed between two sheets of mica.

She looked down the corridor.

At the far end, outside the music practice rooms, a light was burning.

It was not an electric light.

It was the yellow, smoky glare of a hurricane lamp—an old tin lamp with a cracked glass chimney that was sitting on the floor outside Practice Room 3.

Beside the lamp, sitting on an orange stacking chair that had been placed in the middle of the flooded corridor, was a boy.

It was Leo Henderson.

He was not playing the clarinet.

He was sitting with his elbows on his knees, his hands hanging between his shins, his fingers trailing in the gray water that covered the tiles.

He was wearing his full school uniform: the maroon blazer, the gray trousers, the black blucher boots.

But the blazer was wrong.

It was not the modern polyester blazer that the academy trust had introduced in 2004, with the machine-embroidered crest on the pocket that showed an open book and an iron gear-wheel.

It was an old wool-flannel blazer—heavy, thick, the color of dried liver—with the brass buttons of the old High Beck Grammar School, each button stamped with the county arms: three red roses on a chevron between three silver crescents.

Leo did not look up when Robin’s plimsolls splashed through the water.

He was looking down at his own reflection in the grease-filmed flood.

"Leo," Robin whispered.

Her voice sounded huge in the dark passage, echoing off the wet plasterboard walls with a hollow, booming ring that sounded like an empty cistern filling in the night.

The boy did not move his head.

"You’re late, Skimmer," he said.

The voice was his, but it had no crack in it. It was deep, slow, vibrating with that dreadful, rhythmic certainty that Robin had heard in the boiler pumps.

"The bell went twenty minutes ago," Leo said. "Craddock’s got the book out. He’s putting the red crosses down for the boys who didn't show for the run."

Robin took two steps toward him, the water rising to her ankles: *slosh... slosh*.

"Leo," she said, her teeth chattering so hard she had to bite her own lower lip to stop the sound. "Where is this?"

Leo raised his face.

The skin was not freckled anymore.

It was the color of a turnip that had spent six weeks in a dark clamp, soft and gray-white, with two hollow, purple sockets where his eyes should have been.

In the center of each socket, floating in the dark grease of the fluid, was a tiny, brass watch-wheel that turned three teeth at a time: *tick... tick... tick*.

"It’s yesterday," Leo said.

He did not smile. His jaw did not move when he spoke; the words seemed to come out of the collar of his shirt, through the wool of the liver-colored blazer, like the sound of an iron water-pipe vibrating in an empty room.

"It’s always yesterday in here, Skimmer," he said. "That’s why you like it, isn't it? Because nobody can give you a bad mark for what you haven't done yet."

He pointed a finger toward the double doors of the central hall.

"They’re waiting for you," he said.

"Who?" Robin whispered.

"The ones who are going to be blamed," Leo said. "The ones who were here before they took the roof off the mill. The ones who had their names on the brass plates by the vestry."

He stood up from the orange chair.

He was six inches taller than Leo Henderson had been on Tuesday. His arms were long, thin, hanging down past his knees like the arms of an old iron crane that had lost its jib.

"You shouldn't have opened the door, Robin," he said softly.

He took one step toward her through the flood.

The water did not splash around his boots. It parted with a long, tearing hiss, like grease dropped into a red-hot frying pan.

"When you open the door," he whispered, "yesterday gets into today. And when yesterday gets into today, someone has to pay for the salt."

Robin did not scream.

There was no breath left in her ribs for a scream.

She backed away, her heels catching on the submerged door-frame of Practice Room 1, her knees buckling as she hit the water.

She fell on her back in the two inches of greasy slime.

The cold hit the base of her skull like an iron hammer, driving the air out of her lungs in a single, bubbling gasp: *huh*.

Above her, the ceiling was moving.

It was not plasterboard anymore.

The acoustic tiles had rotted away, revealing the great oak floor-beams of the flax mill—twelve-inch baulks of dark, English timber, black with sixty years of machine-oil and soot, studded with thousands of iron hooks where the drying-lines had hung in 1840.

And from every hook, hanging by its heels like a salted herring in a smoke-house, was an empty school uniform.

Maroon blazers with the brass grammar-school buttons; gray pleated skirts with the hems rotted into rags; white shirts with the collars stiff with yellow starch; and long, black woolen stockings that swayed in the cold draft from the culvert with a dry, papery whisper:

*Swish... swish... swish.*

"Robin."

The voice was not Leo’s.

It was coming from Door 17.

Robin turned her head in the water.

Standing on the ashlar threshold, her head bent low to clear the lintel, was her mother.

Not the mother from Duke Street.

Not the woman in the white nylon auxiliary’s coat with the plastic badge and the enameled basin of potatoes between her knees.

This woman was thirty years younger.

She was twelve years old.

She was wearing the gray gym-slip of the High Beck Secondary Modern School for Girls, Class of 1974, with the square white collar and the three-inch black belt with the two-prong iron buckle.

Her hair was tied back with a piece of red parcel-string, and her face—the thin, pale, sharp-chinned face that Robin saw every morning in the scullery shaving-mirror—was covered with boiler-dust.

In her lap, held between her two thin wrists like an offering, was a tin lunchbox with the name *CLAIRE HOLLAND* scratched into the blue enamel lid with a compass-point.

The girl looked down at Robin in the water.

Her eyes were not hollow; they were wide, clear, terrified, filled with that dreadful, ancient despair of a twelve-year-old child who has spent six months hiding in the dark because she cannot bear the sound of the girls laughing behind the gym.

"Robin," the girl whispered.

Her voice was Robin’s voice.

It was the voice Robin used when she talked to herself in the pantry while the kettle was coming to the boil.

"Don't go through," the girl said.

She pointed her thin, white thumb back over her shoulder, into the dark of the boiler room where the three heat exchangers were clicking in the cold.

"If you go back out there," the girl said, "they’ll tell you it was you. They’ll tell you that you did everything that happened in the dark. They’ll make you sign the book, Robin. They’ll make you sit in the small room by the office until the van comes from the county home."

Robin sat up in the water.

The gray slime was running down her face, into her mouth, tasting of salt, lead-paint, and old horsehair.

"Who are you?" Robin asked.

The girl on the threshold did not answer.

She opened the blue enamel lunchbox.

Inside, sitting on a bed of dry wood-shavings, was an apple core.

It was not black. It was not withered.

It was fresh, white, the juice still shining on the cut flesh, the five black pips gleaming like beetle-backs in the yellow light of the hurricane lamp.

"It’s tomorrow, Robin," the girl whispered.

She picked up the apple core with two fingers.

"And tomorrow," she said, her voice rising into that thin, terrible shriek that the clarinet had made through the birch-ply door, "is where they keep the ones who have nowhere else to go."

### Chapter 9: The Blame

At four o’clock on Thursday afternoon, Mr. Gowan found the pea-green door standing open.

The rain had stopped, but the fog was coming up from the canal, a thick, white, tallow-smelling mist that laid a greasy coat of grease over the concrete of the service yard and turned the yellow sodium lights of the security fence into fuzzy orange blurs.

Mr. Gowan had his oilcan in his hand.

He was sixty-four, had three fingers missing from his left hand from an accident on the coal-elevators at the docks in sixty-eight, and wore an old brown dust-coat with leather patches on the elbows.

He stopped at the top of the sixteen steps.

"Who’s down there?" he shouted.

His voice was like an iron bucket dropped into a dry well: *clonk... clonk*.

No one answered.

The boiler room was dead silent. The pumps had shut down completely; the low-water alarm was buzzing with a dry, insect-like rattle that sounded like a cicada in an empty jar.

Mr. Gowan went down the steps, his iron-shod boots ringing on the concrete: *clink... clink... clink*.

He stepped into the pit.

The floor was covered with six inches of black coal-slack that had been washed out of the bunkers by the rain.

In the middle of the floor, between the secondary heat exchanger and the millstone grit wall, lay a school satchel.

It was made of dark blue nylon, the strap torn away at the buckle, the brass zip half-open to reveal three HB pencils, an unsharpened geometry compass with an oily steel point, and a clean exercise book with a red cover that had the name *MERCER, ROBIN* written across the front in blue ballpoint.

Beside the satchel lay a pair of black plimsolls, size 4, the canvas rotted through at the toes, the rubber soles worn down until the canvas showed through the diamond tread.

Mr. Gowan picked up the satchel.

It was heavy.

It was not full of books; it was full of small, square chunks of millstone grit that had been chipped away from the lintel of the back wall with an iron chisel.

He walked to the stone wall.

There was no door.

There was only a solid, six-foot face of dressed ashlar blocks, three feet thick, the gray lime-mortar between them hard and dry as flint, crusted with forty years of boiler-soot and the white, powdery bloom of rising damp.

In the center of the wall, carved six inches deep into the stone with an iron point that had broken three times during the work, was a number:

*17*

And below the seventeen, scratched into the lime with the point of a geometry compass, were three words:

*TOMORROW IS MINE*

Mr. Gowan reached out his thumb.

He touched the stone.

The limestone was cold—colder than the well-water from the canal, colder even than the salt-grit they put on the school steps in January.

Then, from the other side of the three feet of solid millstone grit—from deep within the earth where the old flax mill had dropped its foundations into the limestone shelf eighty years before the first schoolmaster had blown his whistle in the valley—came a sound.

It was not a pump. It was not a steam-pipe.

It was the slow, rhythmic, hollow tapping of a ten-inch pair of iron dressmaking shears against a lead pipe:

*Clink.*  
*Clink.*  
*Clink.*

And then, faint as a dead child’s voice through three inches of wet turf, a girl spoke:

"She did it, Mr. Gowan. She did it in the dark. And she’s coming back for her coat tomorrow."

### Chapter 10: The Register of the Left-Behind

The school did not close.

A comprehensive school with nine hundred children and sixty-two teachers cannot close because an eight-stone girl from Duke Street has failed to return her dinner-money envelope.

On Friday morning, the register was called at nine o'clock in Room 14.

Mr. Finch sat at his desk by the radiator, his purple felt-tip pen held between his knuckles, his register-book open to the page for Form 8C.

"Henderson, Leo."

"Present, sir."

Leo was sitting in the third row, his tie pulled up to his collar, his clarinet case sitting on the floor between his blucher boots. His face was pale, and he kept his thumb pressed into the corner of his eye as if he had a cinder under the lid, but when he answered, his voice was the thin, whistling treble of a twelve-year-old boy who had spent twenty minutes practicing his scales before breakfast.

"Hirst, Chloe."

"Present, sir."

"Mercer, Robin."

Mr. Finch stopped.

The purple felt-tip pen hovered two inches above the white paper.

The space beside Robin Mercer’s name was blank. On Monday, there was a black slash for present; on Tuesday, a black slash; on Wednesday, a black slash; on Thursday, a red *S* for suspended.

The room was very quiet.

Outside, the rain had turned the playing fields into a gray lake that reflected the low, dirty sky like a sheet of unpolished zinc.

In the back row, Kacey Vane was sitting on her low cupboard.

She was not swinging her patent shoes against the plywood.

Her hands were folded in her lap, her fingers tucked inside the sleeves of her maroon blazer, her head turned toward the window that looked down onto the service yard and the pea-green door of the boiler room.

"Mercer, Robin," Mr. Finch repeated, his voice slightly louder, slightly thinner, carrying that small, irritable edge of a man who had left his reading glasses in the staff lavatory.

Kacey Vane did not look around.

"She’s not here, sir," Kacey said.

The voice was not loud, but it had no mockery in it. It was flat, dry, carrying the quiet, terrible certainty of someone who had seen an iron ring turn three inches in an oak plate while the world was sleeping.

Mr. Finch looked up over his spectacles.

"Do you know where she is, Kacey?"

Kacey brought her right hand out of her blazer pocket.

She did not look at the master.

She opened her palm.

In the center of her hand, resting on the clean, white skin of her palm, was an iron nail—four inches long, bent into a shallow curve, its point blackened by sixty years of coal-smoke.

"She’s in yesterday, sir," Kacey whispered.

She looked down at the nail, her thumb tracing the rusted head with a slow, rhythmic motion that went three beats to the second: *rub... rub... rub*.

"And she says... she says if we don't leave her coat on the peg by three o'clock... she’s going to let tomorrow in."

Mr. Finch stared at her for three seconds.

Then he dipped his purple pen into the ink-pot, brought the point down against the white paper of the county register, and drew a red cross through the name of Robin Mercer.

A red cross.

The mark they make for the ones who have gone into the dark, and left their names behind them on the wall.
### Chapter 11: The Scullery Clock

The alarm in the kitchen went off at five past six, but it did not ring with a bell. It gave a dry, rattling buzz like a hornet caught behind a net curtain, vibrating against the zinc top of the meat-safe until Robin reached out from beneath the horsehair blanket and struck the brass shut-off lever with the heel of her hand.

The house was cold. It was the special cold of Duke Street in the late autumn, when the brick cavities had drunk three weeks of canal-fog and held it in the mortar like a wet sponge against the wallpaper.

Robin lay still for ten minutes, her chin tucked into the neck of her flannel nightdress.

Through the floorboards, she could hear her mother moving in the scullery.

The rhythm never varied. First the double strike of a match against the rough side of the Swan Vestas box—*scritch, scritch*—then the hollow, soft *pluff* of the front gas-ring catching, followed immediately by the dull, tinny clatter of the enameled kettle being set on the trivet. Then her mother’s shoes: the flat, black rubber-soled nurse’s shoes that squeaked against the greasy linoleum by the pantry door with a sound like two wet mice being squeezed in an oily cloth.

*Squeak... scuff. Squeak... scuff.*

Her mother did not come up the stairs. She never came up the stairs before the half-past-six buzzer at the chemical works, because the wood of the fourth tread had split along the grain in the winter of eighty-two and made a sound like an iron shovel striking a gravestone if you put your full weight on it.

Instead, her voice came up through the register-grate in the chimney-breast—thin, sour, carrying the smell of boiled water and cheap tea-dust.

"You’re to stay inside the doors, Robin."

Robin did not answer. She stared at the ceiling, where the water-stain from the ninety-six blizzard had dried into the shape of a pig’s lung, the edges yellow and crusted with lime-bloom.

"I’ve spoken to Mrs. Corley next door," her mother’s voice continued, rattling the iron louvers of the grate. "She’ll be at her window with her mending. If you set foot on the pavement before the five-o’clock siren, she’ll have the word down to the lodge before my shift is half-done. Mr. Teasdale said if there’s any wandering during the suspension, the committee won't even look at your books on Tuesday. They’ll send the officer from the County Hall with the blue van."

A pause. The spoon scraped against the bottom of the earthenware mug: *ting, ting, ting*.

"There’s dripping on the blue plate," her mother said. "Don't touch the gas. If you’re cold, pull the old army coat off the box in the attic. And keep the latch down on the yard door. The men from the scrap-wharf are coming down the lane for the lead-piping from number forty."

Then the back door opened.

The latch gave its long, brass groan, followed by the wet slap of the rubber draft-excluder dragging across the doorstep, and then the iron gate in the yard-wall went *clack*.

Silence.

Not real silence; the house had its own small, dry noises that lived behind the skirting boards. A beetle ticking in the wainscot like an old watch; the water-pipe in the scullery sweating two drops a minute into the lead trough; and the slow, heavy thrum of the coal-trains four miles away on the viaduct, their diesel engines sending a low, three-note vibration through the limestone foundations that made the teaspoon in Robin’s tooth-mug rattle against the rim.

Robin stood up.

Her feet hit the oilcloth. It was like stepping onto wet slate.

She did not light the gas. She went down the stairs in her bare feet, skipping the fourth tread by hooking her fingers into the banister rail and swinging her ribs through the dark like a monkey on an iron gate.

In the scullery, the blue plate sat on the bread-board.

The dripping was pale, the color of tallow, with a dark, jelly-like crust of brown beef-meat at the bottom that smelled of pepper and cold suet. Beside it lay half a loaf of store-bread, wrapped in a tea-towel that had the name of an insurance company printed in faded red letters across the hem.

Robin did not touch the bread.

She walked to the copper boiler in the corner.

Beneath the wooden lid, where the clothes were boiled on Mondays with two cakes of carbolic soap and an old washing-soda packet, lay her satchel.

She had retrieved it from the yard the night before, while her mother was at the sink.

The strap was still broken; the buckle hung by two threads of greasy nylon, the iron frame bent into that shallow, ugly U-shape where it had caught on the bottom hinge of Door 17.

Robin reached her hand into the bag.

Her fingers did not find her geometry set. They did not find her exercise book.

They closed around the chunks of millstone grit that Mr. Gowan had tipped out onto the boiler-house floor.

She had gathered four of them from the coal-slack before she ran.

They were small—none of them larger than a hen’s egg—but they had the extraordinary, freezing weight of stone that has spent eighty years four feet below the groundwater level. The lime-mortar still clung to the flat sides, gray, crumbly, smelling of damp horsehair and dead well-water.

And on the smallest chunk, right across the dressed face where the stonemason’s punch had left four parallel grooves, was a tiny smudge of dried scarlet Quink ink.

It was not on the surface; it had soaked into the porous grit, spreading along the micro-fractures in the sandstone like red moss growing on a wet gravestone.

Robin took the stone to the window.

The mist was so thick in Duke Street that she could not see the lamp-post outside number twelve. The brick facades opposite were only dark, flat planes, like sheets of unpainted tin set on end in the mud.

Down in the lane, the scrap-cart was already moving.

She heard the horse’s hooves first: *clop... clop... clop*, the iron shoes slipping on the greasy cobbles where the night-soil dray had leaked its wash. Then the high, dry rattle of the cart’s wooden axles, and the voice of old man Huddleston—the one who had had his jaw broken by an iron winch on the grain-staithes in sixty-six—shouting his long, wordless cry into the mist:

*Raaa-ag... booo-one... any old ir-on...*

The cry sounded like a gull being strangled in a wet sack.

Robin pulled her skirt on over her nightdress.

It was Friday.

In the school on the hill—in Room 14, where the radiator was already hammering with air in the pipes—Mr. Finch was opening his black leather register.

He would call Leo Henderson. He would call Chloe Hirst.

And then he would call her name.

And Kacey Vane, sitting on her low cupboard with her patent shoes swinging against the plywood, would open her white palm and show the master the iron nail.

*She’s in yesterday, sir.*

Robin put the stone into her pocket.

The cold weight of the millstone grit pressed against her thigh through the thin cotton of her pocket, like a lump of river-ice that refused to melt.

She did not put on her school shoes; she put on her old leather ankle-boots—the ones her mother’s sister had sent from Rochdale four years ago, with the metal eyelets that had rusted into green verdigris and the heels that had been built up on one side with two layers of horse-leather to correct a turn in the ankle she had never actually had.

She went to the back door, lifted the heavy drop-latch with both thumbs to keep the spring from snapping, and stepped out into the yard.

### Chapter 12: The Perimeter Fence

The school did not look like an academy from the canal path.

It looked like an old municipal hospital that had been extended four times during a typhus epidemic and then abandoned when the money ran out.

The low brick wings of the 1968 blocks lay along the crest of the ridge like gray packing crates that had been dropped off the back of a train into the wet gorse. Between the perimeter fence and the railway line lay fifty yards of sour pasture, where two piebald ponies belonging to the tinkers at the lime-kilns stood up to their hocks in yellow water, their heads hanging down toward the sodden rushes with that total, motionless misery of animals that have forgotten what dry hay smells like.

Robin kept to the ditch.

The ditch was three feet deep, filled with brambles, rotting plastic sacks from the silage heaps, and the blue, oily water that seeped out of the old gasworks tip on the other side of the embankment.

Her boots made a heavy, sucking sound in the gray clay: *schloop... schloop... schloop*.

Every time her heel came up, the smell came with it: coal-tar, dead nettles, and that faint, sweetish stench of municipal landfill that never entirely left the air in High Beck between October and April.

She stopped at the north-west corner of the playing fields.

Here, the chain-link fence had been cut through three years ago by the boys who wanted to fetch their footballs back from the railway cutting. Someone had tried to mend the gap with four strands of barbed wire, but the cattle had rubbed against the wooden posts until the staples pulled out, leaving a loose, sagging loop of wire that swayed in the wind like an iron spider-web.

Robin dropped to her stomach.

The mud went through the front of her skirt immediately, cold and greasy, soaking into the wool until it smelled like a wet sheep.

She slid under the lower strand of wire.

A barb caught the shoulder of her macintosh—not the fabric, but the small plastic button that held the storm-flap down—and tore it off with a sharp *snick*.

She did not look for the button. She scrambled through into the dead gorse behind the cricket pavilion.

The pavilion was made of green tongue-and-groove timber, set on four concrete piles to keep the floorboards off the swamp. The windows had been covered with sheets of perforated zinc after the break-in of ninety-two, and the small veranda was littered with broken cider flagons, dry crisp-packets, and three damp, mold-covered gym-mats that smelled of ancient sweat and mouse-droppings.

From the veranda, she had a clear view of the service yard.

The nine-o’clock bell had finished ringing ten minutes ago.

The yard was empty.

The two refuse lorries had gone; their greasy wheel-tracks in the wet gravel were already filling with gray rainwater that reflected the blank white sky like narrow strips of tin.

The pea-green door of the boiler room was shut.

Not latched; shut.

Someone had driven three four-inch wire nails through the timber frame, right across the rebate where the sheet-steel met the jamb, their round heads hammered flush with the wet paint so that the wood around them had split in three clean, yellow splinters.

Robin watched the door for five minutes.

No smoke was coming from the short square chimney of the boiler-house.

That was wrong.

Even on mild days in October, the boilers kept a three-pound head of steam to run the hot-water calorifiers for the kitchen sinks. The chimney always showed a thin, greasy dribble of blue coal-smoke that smelled of wet slack and sulfur, drifting across the gymnasium roof like an old woman’s gray shawl.

Today, the chimney-top was dead.

The brick coping was black, wet, with two crows sitting on the iron spark-arrestor, their feathers ruffled against the drizzle, their beaks tucked under their wings like two lumps of coal.

Robin left the pavilion.

She ran across the wet gravel of the service yard on the balls of her feet, her boots making almost no sound on the stones until she hit the concrete well of the sixteen steps.

She went down.

The air in the well was colder than the yard.

It was not the damp, living cold of the rain; it was the dead, stagnant cold of an empty cellar where the water has risen above the floorboards and sat there for forty years in the dark.

She stopped at the pea-green door.

The nails were real.

She touched the head of the center nail with her thumb. It was cold as an icicle, crusted with a greasy film of machine-oil from the carpenter’s bag.

Above the handle, tacked to the sheet-steel with four brass drawing-pins, was a yellow cardboard notice:

*DANGER*  
*UNAUTHORISED ENTRY STRICTLY PROHIBITED*  
*BOILER PLANT DECOMMISSIONED FOR EMERGENCY MAINTENANCE BY ORDER OF THE DISTRICT WORKS DEPARTMENT*  
*M. Teasdale (Deputy Headmaster)*  
*Friday, 18th October.*

Robin looked at the date.

*Friday, 18th October.*

Her head began to swim.

Today was Friday.

She looked at the yellow card again. The ink was dry, but the edges of the cardboard were already buckling from the damp air of the stairwell, the corners curling forward like dried orange-peel.

Behind the sheet-steel, something moved.

It was not the pumps. The pumps were dead.

It was a sound like someone dragging an empty coal-sack across a dry flagstone: *hiss... shrr-up... hiss*.

Then a breath.

A long, slow, whistling inhalation through the nose, carrying the rattle of a dry throat that had swallowed two ounces of boiler-dust and never found the water to wash it down.

"Robin."

The voice came through the keyhole.

It was not her mother’s voice. It was not Leo Henderson’s voice.

It was Mr. Gowan’s voice.

Not the voice he used when he shouted at the boys for skidding their bicycles across the clean asphalt of the car park.

It was the voice he had used thirty years ago, when he had stood at the pit-head after the explosion at the Moor-Colliery and read the names of the fifteen men who were still behind the ventilation doors on the four-foot seam.

"You’re on the wrong side, girl," the caretaker whispered through the steel.

"Mr. Gowan?" Robin said.

She leaned her forehead against the cold pea-green paint. The metal smelled of sour lead and rust.

"Go round by the coal-chute," he said.

His voice didn't rise; it had that dreadful, flat rhythm of an old man talking in his sleep.

"They’ve put the timber across the top, but the fourth batten’s loose where the wet got into the mortice. You can slip through if you don't take your coat off."

"Is the door open?" Robin asked.

"Door 17?"

A pause. Through the keyhole, Robin heard the dry, metallic chink of his iron keys—forty brass and steel keys on a twelve-inch iron ring that he had carried on his belt since the comprehensive opened in sixty-eight.

*Chink... chink... clink.*

"It’s not open, Robin," he whispered. "It doesn't open from this side anymore. Once the red cross goes into the book, the latch only lifts from the day behind."

"Then how do I get in?"

"You don't get in," Mr. Gowan said.

His breath hit the keyhole, smelling of cold pipe-tobacco and peppermint lozenges that had gone soft in a greasy pocket.

"You get dragged in, girl. Same as the rest of us."

### Chapter 13: Thursday in the Flesh

The coal-chute was narrower than it had been on Wednesday.

The rain had brought down thirty tons of wet slack from the railway embankment, filling the brick channel with a black, greasy slurry that reached to Robin’s knees as she squeezed between the split batten and the concrete retaining wall.

The slack was cold—colder than the ditch, colder than the rain. It got inside the tops of her Rochdale boots, packing itself around her ankles like wet lead-shot until each foot felt as if it weighed twenty pounds.

She dropped through into the darkness of the chamber.

There was no sound.

No hum from the three heat exchangers; no steam whistling from the secondary valves; no low-water buzzer.

The boiler room was completely dark except for a single shaft of gray daylight that came down through the coal-chute behind her, illuminating a square yard of wet slack and the rusted iron base of the primary pump.

Robin stood on the concrete.

The air was warm.

Not machine-warm; body-warm.

It smelled of ninety people who had been sitting in an unventilated room for three hours with their wet coats on, breathing into their handkerchiefs and eating cold paste-sandwiches out of greaseproof paper.

She looked toward the back wall.

The millstone grit was glowing.

Not with light; with a faint, greasy phosphorescence that looked like the belly of a dead haddock that had spent three days in the bottom of a fish-box.

Door 17 stood eighteen inches open.

Robin walked toward it. Her boots made no sound on the floor; the slack was so thick that it deadened her steps into soft, muffled thuds that sounded like a mallet striking a mattress.

She reached the threshold.

The ashlar blocks were not frosty now. They were sweating.

Long, greasy drops of moisture were running down the dressed sandstone, collecting in the stonemason’s punch-marks and dripping onto the linoleum beyond with a slow, rhythmic *plink... plink... plink*.

Robin stepped through.

The corridor was bright.

It was blindingly bright.

The four-foot fluorescent tubes were humming with that high, sixty-cycle buzz that made her eyes water, and the floor was clean—bone-dry, polished with that yellow, pine-scented wax that the council bought in forty-gallon drums from the chemical works at the mouth of the bay.

Her Casio was working.

The cracked face showed the black numbers with absolute, terrifying clarity:

*11:14.*

*THU.*

Thursday.

Robin Mercer stood in the middle of the Humanities corridor, twenty-four hours behind herself.

Down the passage, toward the library, a door opened.

Room 12.

The geography room.

Mr. Craddock came out first. He was wearing his blue nylon tracksuit with the white stripe down the leg, carrying an armful of contour-maps that were rolled up into tight cylinders and tied with red rubber bands.

He did not look at Robin.

He walked past her—so close that the rough nylon of his sleeve brushed against the shoulder of her macintosh with a dry, static hiss: *zzt*.

Robin flinched. She drew her shoulders in, waiting for his hand to drop onto her collar, waiting for the sharp, barked question: *What are you doing out of your base, Mercer? Where is your corridor-slip?*

Mr. Craddock did not stop.

He did not turn his head.

He walked with that heavy, heel-first stride that PE masters have, his eyes fixed on the double doors of the staff room thirty yards away.

Behind him came the class.

Form 8C.

Robin stepped back into the shadow of the water-cooler.

Her knees were trembling so hard that her shins banged against the black iron grille: *tock, tock*.

They came in a pack, shoving each other with their elbows, their satchels swinging against their thighs, their voices loud, raw, filled with that chaotic, mid-morning energy that smelled of salt-and-vinegar crisps and wet hair.

There was Chloe Hirst, wearing her sister’s maroon jumper that had the neck stretched out so far that her white collar-band stood up like a horse-collar.

There was Tanya Siddle, carrying her transparent plastic pencil case between her teeth while she tried to tie her shoelace without dropping her geometry compass.

And in the center of the group, walking with that slow, rolling swagger that she had learned from her older brothers who worked the crane-barges at the dock, was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was laughing.

She had her hand on Chloe’s shoulder, her fingers digging into the stretched wool of the maroon jumper, her head thrown back so that the long tendon in the side of her neck stood out like a white cord.

"And then he said... he said she had to have the report signed by her mother!" Kacey shouted, her voice cutting through the noise of the corridor like a butcher’s saw through brisket. "Her mother! Her mother doesn't even know what day it is! My dad said she was found in the ditch behind the cattle-market in seventy-nine with two bottles of cider and no knickers!"

The girls shrieked.

Tanya Siddle dropped her pencil case; it hit the tiles with a hollow, plastic clatter, fifty yellow wooden pencils rolling across the floor toward the water-cooler: *clack-clack-clack*.

Robin pressed her spine against the compressor.

A yellow pencil rolled between her boots.

It was an HB pencil, its lead sharpened to a fine, needle-like point, with the name *TANYA* carved into the yellow paint with the point of a pin.

Robin looked down at it.

She did not touch it.

Then, at the end of the line of children—ten yards behind the rest, walking with her chin buried in the collar of her maroon blazer—came the other girl.

The Thursday Robin.

She was twelve years old.

Her skirt was pinned at the waistband with a rusty safety pin because the zipper had broken during Tuesday’s scuffle by the bicycle sheds. Her face was gray-white, her eyes fixed on the line of the mortar between the floor-tiles, her satchel held in both hands across her belly like a shield.

She looked small.

Robin, watching from the recess of the cooler, felt a cold, sickening jolt in her throat.

She had never seen herself from the outside.

She had seen her face in the scullery mirror—the thin, sharp chin, the crooked tooth at the side, the two brown moles near the hairline—but she had never seen the way her shoulders curved forward like the ribs of an old basket, or the way her right foot dragged half an inch on the tiles every third step, as if she were trying to pull a dead dog behind her on a string.

The Thursday Robin walked past the water-cooler.

She did not look at the recess.

She did not see Robin standing three feet away in the shadow.

She was crying.

She was not crying with tears; she was crying with that dry, silent shudder of the ribs that children use when they have spent two hours being pinched under the desk during double Geography and know that if they make a sound, the master will give them fifty lines for disturbing the class.

A tiny drop of clear spit was hanging from her lower lip, swaying with every dragging step: *tick... tick*.

Robin reached her hand out from the recess.

She did not mean to. Her arm moved of its own accord, her fingers extending through the light toward the sleeve of the maroon blazer—her own sleeve, her own frayed cuff, the gray acrylic thread that she had chewed during the spelling test on Tuesday morning.

"Robin," she whispered.

The Thursday Robin stopped.

She did not turn her head.

Her whole body went rigid, like a hare that has heard the click of a lurcher’s teeth behind its tail in the long grass.

Her right hand went to her blazer pocket.

She pulled out her fingers.

They were clean.

There was no Quink ink on her knuckles; there was no white lead splinter in her thumb-pad; there was no scrap of millstone grit in her skirt.

She was clean.

She was the girl who had not done it yet.

Then, from forty yards down the corridor, outside the Pastoral Office, Mr. Teasdale’s door went *bang*.

The sound was like an iron plate dropped onto concrete.

Mr. Teasdale stepped out into the light.

He was not carrying his buff disciplinary cards; he was carrying a sheet of yellow paper—the yellow notice that Robin had seen tacked to the pea-green door in Friday’s rain.

He did not walk toward the staff room.

He walked toward the two girls by the water-cooler.

His shoes made no sound on the dry wax. None at all.

His brogues rose and fell with that rhythmic, heavy stride, but when the brown leather struck the mottled tiles, there was only a dead, sucking silence, like a rubber plunger being pulled out of a dry pipe.

He was looking at Robin.

Not the Thursday Robin with the clean hands.

He was looking directly into the shadow of the water-cooler, his spectacles catching the white fluorescent tubes until his eyes were two round, empty holes of chalk-white glare.

"Mercer," he said.

The voice was not in the corridor.

It was inside the bones of Robin’s ears, vibrating behind her eardrums like an iron needle being scraped across a slate.

"You have twenty-four hours to explain the damage to the boiler-house," he said.

He took three steps forward.

The Thursday Robin did not move. She stood between them like a pillar of cold salt, her eyes fixed on the floor, her chin buried in her collar, completely unaware that the man was speaking to someone who was standing twenty-four inches behind her left elbow.

"And if you cannot explain it," Mr. Teasdale whispered, his hand reaching out toward the shadow, "we will have to take the name off the register permanently."

His fingers touched Robin’s sleeve.

They were not warm. They were not cold.

They had no substance at all; they were like two streams of freezing air that went through the nylon of her macintosh, through the wool of her cardigan, and settled into the meat of her shoulder like two lead balls.

Robin screamed.

This time, the sound came out.

It filled the Humanities corridor, bouncing off the yellow-gloss doors of the practice rooms with a high, tearing shriek that made the fluorescent tubes flicker and buzz: *bzzzt-click*.

The Thursday Robin turned her head.

She looked into the shadow.

She saw Robin.

She saw the gray macintosh with the missing button; she saw the Rochdale boots with the rusted eyelets; she saw the red Quink ink that outlined the bones of her own right hand.

The two girls stared at each other across the twelve inches of dry linoleum.

And then the Thursday Robin spoke.

She did not use her mother’s voice. She did not use Leo Henderson’s voice.

She used the voice of the girl who had sat in the Duke Street attic for three years, listening to the rain on the slates:

"You shouldn't have come back for the coat, Robin. We’ve already burned it."

### Chapter 14: The Stares in the Cloakroom

The change began with the eyes.

When Robin broke away from the water-cooler and ran toward the south stairs, the school was no longer moving with the chaotic, irregular tumble of a Thursday lunch-hour.

The nine hundred children had stopped.

They were standing along the walls of the C-Block corridor in two straight lines, their backs pressed against the brown cork tiles, their hands hanging straight down by their seams like the soldiers in the print of the Battle of the Alma that hung in the school library.

None of them were talking.

There was no sound of crisp-packets being torn; no sound of rubber soles squeaking; no laughter from the toilets.

There was only the breathing.

Nine hundred twelve- and thirteen-year-olds breathing through their noses at the exact same moment: *in... out... in... out*.

The sound was like the bellows of a great forge three levels below the earth, pumping air into a fire that had gone out sixty years ago.

Robin ran down the center of the passage.

The space between the two lines was four feet wide—just wide enough for an eight-stone girl in a gray macintosh to pass without her elbows brushing the maroon wool of their blazers.

Every head turned as she passed.

It was not a quick movement; it was the slow, mechanical swivel of ninety brass gun-barrels tracking a barge across a river.

As she passed Leo Henderson, his eyes were not watch-wheels anymore.

They were gray-white marbles of unpolished limestone, their surfaces dry, dusty, with tiny veins of red clay running through the sclera like cracks in a dried puddle.

"She’s got the stone in her pocket," Leo said.

His lips did not move. The voice came from the empty air between his chin and his breast-pocket, flat, dead, carrying the dry rattle of the salt-marsh.

"She’s got the stone from the lintel," Chloe Hirst said from the opposite wall.

Her eyes followed Robin’s boots.

"She’s going to bring the frost into the classrooms," Tanya Siddle whispered. "She’s going to let the canal into the cellar."

Robin did not stop.

Her knee was screaming now; the lint had torn away from her skin during her crawl through the coal-chute, and the wet wool of her tights was rubbing against the raw meat of her patella with every stride.

She turned the corner into the girls’ cloakroom.

The cloakroom was thirty feet long, lit by three bare sixty-watt bulbs that hung from green twisted flex between the rows of iron coat-pegs.

The pegs were full.

Two hundred maroon blazers, forty blue parkas, and twenty gray macintoshes hung in the dark, swaying slightly in the draft from the broken hopper-window with that dry, whispering rustle: *swish... swish*.

In the center of the room, standing beneath the middle bulb, was Kacey Vane.

She was alone.

She had her school satchel open on the low wooden bench where the girls changed their shoes for gym.

She was not looking at her books.

She was holding Robin’s navy parka.

The parka that was supposed to be hanging in the Duke Street scullery, behind the back door, drying over the clothes-airer.

It was here.

Kacey had it spread out across her knees.

In her right hand, she held the four-inch wire nail that she had shown to Mr. Finch in Friday’s registration.

She was driving the rusted point through the fabric.

Not quickly; slowly, with that steady, patient, rhythmic pressure that a shoemaker uses when he drives an awl through three layers of sole-leather.

*Punch... scritch.*

She drove the nail through the left breast, right where the academy trust crest had been unpicked by Robin’s mother to save the blazer for the junior school.

She pulled the nail out.

A small puff of gray polyester wadding came with the iron point, looking like a tuft of moldy cotton-wool.

*Punch... scritch.*

She drove it through the collar.

Robin stopped in the doorway. Her hands were braced against the timber frame, her fingernails digging into the yellow gloss until her thumbs went white.

"Kacey," she said.

The word was thick, greasy with spit.

Kacey did not look up.

Her fingers—long, thin, with the cuticles bitten down until the skin was pink and raw—continued to guide the point of the nail into the blue nylon.

"You’re dead, Skimmer," Kacey said.

Her voice was normal. It was the only voice in the school that sounded like a twelve-year-old girl from the council estate behind the bus station.

It was sharp, nasty, small.

"My dad says your mother’s on the list," Kacey said. "He says the council’s clearing the whole terrace before Christmas. He says they’re going to put a car park where your kitchen is."

She pulled the nail out again.

She held the point up to the bare bulb.

A single drop of thick, dark fluid was clinging to the rusted iron.

It was not machine-oil. It was not Quink ink.

It was dark red, the color of liver-blood that has sat in a cold butcher’s tray until the grease forms a skin over the surface.

"And he says," Kacey whispered, her thumb touching the wet iron, "that if a girl hasn't got an address in the borough, she doesn't get a dinner-ticket. She doesn't get a desk. She gets put in the shed behind the cemetery with the old desks that have got the ink-wells broken out of the corners."

She looked at Robin then.

Her eyes were not limestone.

They were her own eyes—small, brown, wet, vicious, filled with that pure, untouchable satisfaction of a child who has finally found a stone that can break another child’s teeth without making a mark on the skin.

"Take your coat, Robin," Kacey said.

She held the navy parka out by its broken hood.

The tear in the left sleeve was not three inches long anymore.

It ran from the shoulder to the cuff—a jagged, gaping wound that showed the whole gray polyester lining, soaked through with the dark, liver-colored grease that was dripping onto the wooden bench: *plop... plop... plop*.

"Take it," Kacey said. "You’ll need it for tomorrow. It’s going to be very cold on the marsh."

Robin took two steps toward her.

She did not reach for the coat.

She reached for the iron nail in Kacey’s right hand.

Her fingers closed around Kacey’s wrist.

The skin was real.

It was hot—fever-hot, sticky with the cheap strawberry lip-gloss she had smeared across her knuckles during the morning break.

Kacey’s eyes went wide.

The viciousness did not leave her face; it turned into a sudden, piercing shock, like an animal that has stepped into a trap it set for something else.

"Let go," Kacey gasped.

Her wrist twisted in Robin’s grip, the small bones clicking under Robin’s thumb like walnut-shells: *snick, snick*.

"Give me the nail," Robin said.

"It’s mine," Kacey spat. "I found it in the coal! I found it where you were hiding!"

Robin tightened her fingers.

She felt the tiny lead splinter in her own palm—the white flake from Door 17—press deep into Kacey’s skin, right over the radial artery where the blood was jumping like a small, frightened frog.

Kacey screamed.

It was not a school scream; it was a thin, high, animal shriek that brought the smell of burning hair into the room.

From Kacey’s wrist, right where the two skins met, a thin curl of white smoke began to rise.

It smelled of sulphur. It smelled of old lime-mortar and wet coal-slack.

Kacey’s fingers opened.

The four-inch wire nail fell onto the wooden bench: *clack*.

Robin snatched it up.

The iron was so cold it burned her palm like dry ice, but she did not drop it; she shoved it into her skirt pocket beside the millstone grit.

Then she looked at the navy parka on the bench.

She picked it up.

It was heavy—it felt as if it had been soaked in forty gallons of canal-water—and as she pulled it toward her chest, she saw the name-tape sewn into the collar.

It was not *ROBIN MERCER*.

It had been sewn with red stranded cotton, in the large, irregular cross-stitch that girls were taught at the parish church school in 1952:

*CLAIRE HOLLAND*

Her mother’s name.

The coat was thirty years older than Robin.

The wadding inside the sleeves was not polyester; it was shredded wool-shoddy from the old mills at Dewsbury, gray and rotten, smelling of seventy years of wet sheep and sulfur smoke.

"Robin."

The voice came from the cloakroom door.

Mr. Gowan was standing in the opening.

He had his brown dust-coat on, but the three missing fingers on his left hand were not stumps anymore.

They were three long, tapered chisels of rusted iron, held to his knuckles by bands of brass wire that had cut deep into the yellow flesh of his hand.

He raised the left hand.

The iron points clicked together with the sound of three butcher’s knives being sharpened on a steel:

*Chink.*  
*Chink.*  
*Chink.*

"The shift is over, Mercer," he said. "The bell’s going for the lock-up."

### Chapter 15: The Geography of the Mill

Robin did not run for the stairs.

She knew the stairs were dead; she had heard the sound of nine hundred pairs of blucher boots coming down the winders from the upper floors—a slow, rhythmic, synchronized march that shook the light-pendants in the ceiling until the bare bulbs swung in three-foot arcs, throwing long, lunging shadows across the rows of empty blazers.

She ran for the back of the cloakroom.

Behind the third row of pegs was the old drying cupboard—a narrow recess where the wet macintoshes had been hung in the days when the school had its own coke-fired heating tubes.

The door was made of vertical pine match-boarding, painted five times with the same dark chocolate gloss that the county had used on all its institutional woodwork between the wars.

In the center was an iron latch with a thumb-piece that had been worn down to silver by sixty years of caretakers’ mittens.

Robin lifted the latch.

The door opened inward.

There were no pipes.

There was a stone staircase.

It did not lead up to the classrooms; it led down.

The steps were straight, narrow, eighteen inches wide, cut out of the solid millstone grit with an adze that had left long, diagonal tooth-marks across every riser.

The air that came up the shaft was warmer than the cloakroom, but it was not the machine-heat of the boilers.

It was the air of the lower works.

It smelled of raw flax—that sweet, grassy, rotting smell of wet hemp that has been left to ferment in stagnant ponds until the fibers separate from the wood.

And beneath the flax smell was the sound.

It was not the pump.

It was the wheel.

A forty-foot breast-shot waterwheel, forty tons of green oak and iron plate, turning three revolutions a minute in the black race fifty feet below the floorboards of the comprehensive school.

*Slosh-chunk... slosh-chunk... slosh-chunk.*

Every time the buckets hit the tail-water, the stone beneath Robin’s boots trembled with a dull, hydraulic shudder that made her jaw-teeth chatter together.

She went down.

She did not look behind her. She did not need to; she could hear Mr. Gowan’s three iron fingers scraping against the match-boarding at the top of the stair: *scritch... scritch... scritch*.

The steps ended in a vaulted chamber of dressed gritstone.

The ceiling was low—barely six feet high—supported by three rows of square stone pillars that were black with grease from the line-shafts.

This had been the scutching room of the High Beck Mill.

Here, in 1845, sixty children had stood for fourteen hours a day between the revolving iron flails, feeding the wet flax into the rollers until their fingers were the color of peeled parsnips and their lungs were filled with the white, dry dust that the doctors called the "mill-fever."

The flails were gone.

The rollers were gone.

In their place, arranged in three neat rows between the stone pillars, were forty modern school desks.

The double-seater desks from the old 1968 classrooms—the ones made of gray tubular steel with lids of beech-faced plywood that had the ink-wells recessed into the upper right corner.

Every desk was occupied.

The children were sitting with their backs straight, their forearms flat on the beech plywood, their heads facing the far wall where an old blackboard of slate-stone was set into the masonry.

They were not wearing modern blazers.

They were wearing the gray flannel jackets of the union workhouse, with the brass badges on the collars that showed the letters *H.B.U.*—High Beck Union.

Their hair was cropped close to the scalp, gray with lime-dust, and their ears stuck out like small, pink dried apricots from the sides of their narrow heads.

None of them turned around when Robin’s boots hit the flagstones.

At the slate blackboard stood Mr. Teasdale.

He was not wearing his tweed jacket.

He was wearing a black frock-coat of heavy broadcloth that had turned green at the seams from eighty years of coal-smoke, with twelve bone buttons down the front that were fastened to the throat.

In his right hand he held an iron school-slate with a brass frame.

In his left, a piece of white pipe-clay.

He was writing names on the slate.

*1844: Martha Garside (Drowned in the tail-race).*  
*1858: William Henderson (Caught in the bevel-gears).*  
*1872: Thomas Vane (Died of the lung-rot).*  
*1891: Sarah Holland (Fell from the staging).*

He stopped his hand.

The pipe-clay made a long, agonizing squeal against the slate: *screee-ch*.

He did not look around.

"You’re late for the muster, Robin Mercer," he said.

His voice was like two millstones grinding six grains of wheat between their faces.

"The union paid four pound ten for your indenture," he said. "The overseer signed the book at nine o'clock. We don't keep girls who spend the morning hiding in the coal-chutes."

Robin stood by the last pillar.

She held the navy parka against her belly with both hands, her thumbs hooked into the torn wool of the lining.

"I’m not in the union," she said.

Her voice sounded small, thin, like a sparrow chirping inside an iron boiler.

"My mother lives in Duke Street. She pays ninety pence a week to the council for the rent."

Mr. Teasdale turned around.

His spectacles were gone.

His face was smooth, flat, made of that same gray, unpolished limestone that she had seen in Leo Henderson’s eyes.

There were no nostrils; there were no eyebrows.

There was only a horizontal slit three inches long where his mouth should have been, through which Robin could see the small, rhythmic movements of an iron escapement-wheel turning three teeth at a time: *tick... tick... tick*.

"There is no Duke Street, Robin," he whispered through the slit.

"The council sold the bricks in seventy-two. The houses were pulled down to make the bypass for the chemical works. You have been living in the day behind for three years."

Robin backed away.

Her shoulder hit the stone pillar.

The gritstone was wet, covered in a cold, slippery film of algae that smelled of canal-bottom and old grease.

"No," she said.

"Look at your boots, girl," Mr. Teasdale said.

Robin looked down.

The Rochdale boots were gone.

She was wearing two wooden clogs with iron irons nailed to the soles, the black leather uppers fastened across her instep with two brass clasps that had turned green with verdigris.

The clogs were wet.

A dark, oily water was rising between the flagstones, bubbling up through the lime-mortar in fifty small, yellow fountains that smelled of sulfur and dead fish.

The forty workhouse children stood up from their desks.

They did not move like children; they moved like four-foot puppets whose iron wires were being pulled by a single crank on the ceiling.

They turned their gray, cropped heads toward the pillar.

Their mouths opened.

Forty horizontal slits in forty limestone faces.

And then they spoke.

The sound was not forty voices.

It was the whistle of the three-o’clock shift at the chemical works, rising through the earth like a steam-vent opening in the marsh:

*She did it in the dark.*  
*She did it in the coal.*  
*She took the four-inch nail.*  
*And she left her name behind.*

### Chapter 16: The Gate on the Water

Robin did not go back up the stone steps.

She knew Mr. Gowan was sitting on the fourth tread with his three iron chisels spread across his knees like a cobbler’s tools.

She ran along the wall of the scutching room, toward the low arch where the tail-race disappeared into the black limestone shelf beneath the hill.

The water was up to her knees now.

It was not cold anymore; it was so freezing that her legs had lost all sensation below the patella, moving through the flood like two wooden fence-posts driven into the silt.

The arch was four feet high.

Above the keystone, carved into the gritstone with that same stiff, Victorian flourish she had seen on the boiler-room door, was an inscription:

*THE HIGH BECK DRAINAGE AND NAVIGATION TRUST*  
*FOUNDED 1838*  
*FOR THE PRESERVATION OF ORDER AND THE RELIEF OF THE DESERVING POOR*  
*OUTLET NO. 17*

Door 17 was not made of oak here.

It was an iron sluice-gate—a four-inch slab of cast iron that hung in two vertical stone grooves, its lower edge resting six inches above the water-level to allow the drainage from the mill-race to escape into the canal.

Robin did not have to lift it.

The four-inch wire nail was still in her hand.

She took the nail, reached her arm into the dark space beneath the iron plate, and jammed the rusted point into the teeth of the rack-and-pinion gear that held the sluice in its frame.

*Crunch.*

The iron nail did not bend.

It bit into the cast-iron teeth with a sharp, screaming spark that lit the vaulted ceiling with a blue, electric flash.

The winch-drum above her head began to scream.

The chains—two inches thick, each link forged by an iron-puddler in sixty-two—began to run through the pulleys with a sound like a locomotive skidding on wet rails: *screee-ch-clonk*.

The iron gate rose.

It did not stop at four feet; it rose twelve feet into the masonry, disappearing into the dark heart of the school foundations.

And behind it lay the canal.

Not the modern canal with the red clinker path and the plastic bottles floating among the reeds.

The canal of 1880.

The water was black, thick as treacle, carrying thirty flat-bottomed timber barges loaded with coal-slack from the pits at Wigan.

On the opposite bank, under the yellow flare of six open tar-barrels, three hundred men in moleskin trousers were unloading iron-ore with wooden shovels, their bare shoulders black with soot, their voices rising through the freezing mist in a slow, monotonous chanty that had no words, only a rhythm:

*Heave... and go.*  
*Heave... and go.*  
*One for the master.*  
*And none for the crow.*

Robin scrambled through the arch.

Her clogs hit the mud of the towing-path: *flump*.

She fell forward on her hands and knees, her face sinking three inches into the cold horse-manure and coal-dust that covered the verge.

Behind her, the sluice-gate did not fall.

She looked back.

Through the archway, sixty feet away in the lighted chamber of the scutching room, the forty workhouse children were still standing by their desks.

They were not looking at the slate blackboard anymore.

They were looking out through the archway at the canal.

And at the front of the line, her hands folded in the lap of her gray union apron, was Kacey Vane.

Her face was not limestone.

It was her own face, but it was twelve years old and had been dead for ninety years.

Her teeth were black with scurvy; her hair had been sheared to the scalp by the parish barber; and across her right wrist was a long, dark scar where an iron nail had burned its way through to the bone.

"We’ll see you tomorrow, Robin," Kacey whispered.

The voice crossed sixty feet of black canal-water without losing a fraction of its malice.

"We’ll see you when the five-o’clock bell goes for the shift."

Robin stood up.

She did not look at the barges. She did not look at the men with the shovels.

She ran along the mud of the bank, her clogs slipping on the greasy cobbles, her hands clutching the navy parka to her ribs like a stolen joint of meat.

She ran toward the place where Duke Street should have been.

### Chapter 17: The House on the Embankment

Duke Street was not there.

Where the sixty red-brick houses had stood, there was only the embankment of the London and North Western Railway—twenty feet of gray limestone ballast that had been dumped across the salt-marsh in 1876, carrying two lines of steel rails that ran straight toward the fells like two silver pencil-lines drawn across a wet slate.

At the bottom of the embankment, sitting in the yellow rushes by the drain, was a single building.

It was not a house; it was a line-keeper’s cabin.

Eight feet square, built of creosoted railway sleepers set on end, with a roof made of corrugated iron that had been coated with black coal-tar to keep the rain out.

A chimney made of a three-foot length of cast-iron soil-pipe projected through the roof, sending a thin, greasy dribble of blue coal-smoke into the fog.

Above the door, painted in white lead on an old signal-board, was a number:

*4*

Robin’s number.

Her mother’s house was number 4, Duke Street.

Robin walked through the rushes.

The water was up to the brass clasps of her clogs; each step produced a hollow, sucking *glop* as the bog-iron mud tried to pull the wooden soles away from her feet.

She reached the door.

There was no Yale lock. There was no iron rim-lock.

There was only a latch-string made of greasy hemp that hung through a gimlet-hole above the center rail.

Robin pulled the string.

The latch lifted: *clack*.

She pushed the door open.

The cabin was twelve feet square.

In the center was a small, round cast-iron stove—an old "pot-bellied" stove from a guard’s van—its belly glowing a dull, dark red in the gloom, smelling of burning slack and fried onions.

Sitting on an upturned grease-cask by the stove was a woman.

She was wearing a black serge skirt that had been patched at the knees with gray sacking, and a woolen shawl the color of cold oatmeal that was crossed over her breast and pinned at the back with a brass safety pin.

She had an enameled basin between her knees.

She was peeling potatoes.

*Scritch... plop.*

The peelings came off in thin, curling strips that floated on the dark water like dead leaves.

"You’re late, Robin," the woman said.

She did not look up.

Her fingers—the long, swollen, red-knuckled fingers that Robin had known since she was three years old—were moving the horn-handled knife with that same slow, rhythmic certainty that had no anger in it, only fatigue.

"The four-fifteen from Carnforth went past ten minutes ago," she said. "The driver gave the whistle at the cattle-creep. He said there was a girl walking the ballast with no hat on."

Robin stood in the doorway.

The navy parka was heavy in her arms, dripping black ditch-water onto the timber floorboards: *drip... drip... drip*.

"Mum?" Robin whispered.

The woman stopped her knife.

She looked up.

Her face was not thirty years younger.

It was sixty years older.

Her hair was dead-white, thin, pulled back into a small, hard knob at the base of her skull with four iron hairpins that had rusted into her hair. Her skin was the color of yellow parchment that had been kept in an oily drawer behind the kitchen range, covered with thousands of tiny, microscopic wrinkles that looked like the crazing on an old tea-plate.

Her eyes were clear, pale blue—the color of the sky over the fells on a freezing morning in January.

"You’ve got the coat," she said.

Her voice had no surprise in it.

It was the voice she had used when Robin had brought home the silver certificate for neatness in Year 4—pleased, tired, slightly suspicious that someone had made a mistake in the office.

"I found it in the cloakroom," Robin said.

"It wasn't in the cloakroom," her mother said. *Scritch... plop.* "It was in the box under the coal-staithe. I put it there in forty-seven, when the frost broke the pipes in the wash-house. I told your father... I told him if we kept the wool dry, someone would come for it when the new road was made."

She set the knife on the rim of the basin.

She reached out her hand.

The skin of her palm was broad, flat-tipped, crusted with forty years of carbolic soap and the sharp grease from the railway axles.

"Give it here," she said.

Robin took two steps toward the stove.

She laid the navy parka across her mother’s knees.

The wet nylon touched the black serge skirt with a soft, hissing sound, as if the fabric were hot.

Her mother’s fingers went to the tear in the left sleeve.

She did not look at the red Quink ink; she did not look at the black ditch-slime that covered the hem.

Her thumb went straight to the small, white lead splinter that was still embedded in the seam where the ashlar had caught it on Tuesday morning.

She touched the flake of paint.

"Seventeen," her mother whispered.

"Aye," Robin said.

"It’s an old number, Robin," her mother said. Her voice dropped into that rhythmic, storytelling murmur that she only used when the house was dark and the storm was coming off the Irish Sea. "My grandfather had it on his tally when he worked the low level at the iron-mine. If you had seventeen, you didn't go down the main shaft with the horses. You went down the ladder-way behind the pump. It took two hours to reach the four-foot, and when you got there, you were up to your middle in red water."

She looked at Robin’s clogs.

"Who gave you them?"

"Mr. Teasdale," Robin said.

Her mother nodded once, slowly.

"He was an overseer’s man," she said. "His father had the lime-kilns on the wharf. They always liked the clogs with the iron clasps; they said if a child had iron on her feet, she couldn't jump the fence into the turnip-field without the watchman hearing the irons on the wall."

She picked up the knife again.

"He’s marked the book, Robin."

"I know," Robin said. "I saw the red cross."

"A red cross is three years," her mother said, her thumb driving the knife through the yellow skin of a potato: *scritch*.

"Three years in the second class at the union. You sleep twelve in a room on the flock-beds, and on Saturdays they take you down to the chapel on the quay to hear the curate read about the barren fig-tree."

She looked up at Robin then, her pale blue eyes boring through the gloom of the cabin like two iron drills.

"Do you want to go back to the school, Robin?"

Robin looked at the pot-bellied stove.

The iron was cooling; the dark red was turning back to the color of cold lead, and the smell of the fried onions was fading into the sour, damp stench of the salt-marsh.

"They hate me," Robin whispered.

"Of course they hate you," her mother said. Her voice was not cruel; it was just stating a fact, like saying the tide would be low at twenty past four.

"You’re the one who doesn't fit the desk. When a girl doesn't fit the desk, they have to plane the corners off her until she goes into the slot. If she won't plane, they put her in the coal-hole."

She stood up from the grease-cask.

She was short—shorter than Robin now—her back curved into a permanent C by sixty years of carrying laundry-tubs across the yard.

She walked to the small corner cupboard behind the stove.

From the top shelf, behind a stone marmalade jar and a box of dry matches, she took an object.

It was an iron key.

Six inches long, forged by hand from a single bar of square iron, its bit cut into three deep, irregular steps that looked like the teeth of an old cross-cut saw.

The bow of the key was round, stamped with that same number: *17*.

She held it out to Robin.

The iron was cold—colder than the millstone grit, colder than the canal.

"What is it?" Robin asked.

"It’s the shift-key," her mother said.

"The caretaker has the key for today. The headmaster has the key for tomorrow. But this... this is the one they dropped down the well when the mill stopped in eighty-six."

She pressed the heavy iron into Robin’s right palm, closing Robin’s fingers over the cold metal with her own broad, calloused hand.

"If you turn it once," her mother whispered, "you go back to Thursday. If you turn it twice, you go back to Wednesday, before the shears were taken from the box."

"And if I turn it three times?" Robin asked.

Her mother stopped.

Her eyes went to the single small window of the cabin, where the mist was pressing against the glass like a gray, wet fleece.

"If you turn it three times, Robin," she said, her voice dropping into a whisper so faint that Robin had to lean her cheek against her mother’s shawl to hear the words, "you go back to the day before they built the school. You go back to the water-meadows, where the sheep were, before anyone had ever written a name in a book or marked a red cross on a clean page."

"And what’s there?"

Her mother looked at her with a smile that was not a smile—a small, terrible tightening of her pale lips that showed the yellow stumps of her teeth.

"Nothing, Robin," she said.

"Just the rain. And the grass. And nobody to tell you that you’re five minutes late for the register."

### Chapter 18: The Strike of the Key

The school was waiting for her.

When Robin came up the sixteen steps into the service yard, it was four o'clock on Friday afternoon.

The modern four o'clock.

The rain had stopped, but the light had gone out of the sky; the valley was filled with a cold, blue twilight that smelled of diesel smoke, wet slag, and the sulfur-fumes from the fertilizer plant on the coast.

The yellow sodium lights along the security fence were humming: *hum-m-m-m*, their long, orange beams cutting through the mist to illuminate the pea-green door of the boiler room.

The three nails had been drawn.

They lay on the concrete threshold, their shanks bent into shallow curves where the carpenter’s crowbar had levered them out of the pine frame.

The sheet-steel door was standing three inches open.

Robin stood at the top of the steps.

She had her own clothes on again.

The wooden clogs were gone; her Rochdale boots were back on her feet, the wet leather stiff with dried canal-mud, the rusted eyelets digging into the meat of her ankles.

The navy parka was zipped to her chin, its torn sleeve pinned with the rusty safety pin, its pockets heavy with the two objects:

In her left pocket, the four-inch wire nail.

In her right pocket, the six-inch iron shift-key.

She went down the steps.

Each boot-heel made a sharp, clean *clack* against the concrete: *one... two... three... sixteen*.

She pushed the pea-green door open.

The boiler room was full of people.

They were not ghosts. They were not workhouse children with limestone faces.

They were the people from the school.

Mr. Teasdale was there, standing by the primary pump with his tweed jacket unbuttoned, his spectacles hanging from a black cord around his neck, his hands deep in his pockets.

Mr. Finch was there, sitting on the upturned milk-crate with his register-book open across his knees, his purple felt-tip pen held between his teeth like a pipe.

Mr. Gowan was there, leaning against the cold iron of the secondary heat exchanger with his oilcan in his right hand, his left hand tucked into the pocket of his brown dust-coat to hide the three missing fingers.

And standing in the center of the chamber, between the lagged copper pipes and the ashlar wall, was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was wearing her maroon school blazer.

Her patent shoes were clean, polished with that bright, liquid shine that reflected the orange light of the furnace-doors.

Across her right wrist was a broad band of white surgical lint, held in place by two strips of brown zinc-oxide tape that had been applied by the school nurse.

When Robin stepped onto the concrete, none of them moved.

The pumps were still dead, but the room had that heavy, vibrating silence of a church before the organ starts—a thick, electric quiet that made the skin on Robin’s forearms prickle with cold sweat.

Mr. Teasdale took his spectacles from his chest and put them on his nose.

"You’re late, Mercer," he said.

His voice was his own voice—the voice of the deputy headmaster who had thirty-two years of municipal service and a house with a garage in the new development on the fell-road.

"The Board concluded its review at three-thirty," he said.

He did not look angry; he looked satisfied, carrying that calm, impenetrable authority of a magistrate who has just signed a five-year committal order for a boy who had stolen three turnips from an allotment.

"They have accepted the evidence of Miss Vane," he said. "They have examined the damage to the Art Block. They have reviewed the... the unfortunate history of your family’s tenancy in the district."

He pointed a finger toward the stone wall.

Door 17 was shut.

The gray oak was dark, wet, the millstone grit around the frame crusted with a fresh layer of gray lime-mortar that had been pointed with a trowel within the last two hours.

The mortar was still soft; three long, greasy tears of lime-water were running down the ashlar blocks into the coal-slack.

In the center of the door, the iron ring was held fast by a heavy, modern padlock of hardened steel—a four-lever Chubb lock with a yellow brass shackle that had been bolted through the oak with two six-inch coach screws.

"Mr. Gowan has secured the access," Mr. Teasdale said. "The district surveyor has inspected the foundations. The old mill-race will be filled with concrete on Monday morning by the contractors from the bypass."

He stepped forward, his brogues crunching on the coal-slack: *scritch, scritch*.

He held out his hand.

"Give me the key, Robin."

Robin did not move.

"What key, sir?"

"The key your mother gave you," Mr. Teasdale said softly.

His face was very close to hers now. She could smell his afternoon tea—stale milk, peppermint, and the dry, paper-dust that lives inside the registers of the county education office.

"Your mother was an Holland," he said. "The Hollands were line-keepers here for three generations. They always kept the shift-keys in the tea-caddy behind the scullery door. They thought because they had an iron key they could walk between the days without paying the rate."

He reached out his fingers.

They were not limestone. They were warm, fleshy, slightly moist at the tips.

"It belongs to the Trust, Robin," he whispered. "It was scheduled under the Education Act of nineteen forty-four. If you surrender it now, the committee may consider a transfer to the residential school at Kirkby Lonsdale. You would have your own bed, Robin. You would have two skirts and a clean collar on Sundays."

Robin looked past him.

She looked at Kacey Vane.

Kacey was watching her.

Her eyes were not vicious now.

They were wide, terrified, staring at Robin’s right pocket where the heavy shape of the six-inch iron key was dragging the wool of the macintosh down three inches below the hem.

Kacey raised her right wrist—the one with the white lint and the zinc-oxide tape.

She touched the tape with her left thumb.

Under the white cloth, something was moving.

A tiny, rhythmic pulsation that went three beats to the second: *tick... tick... tick*.

"Don't give it to him, Skimmer," Kacey whispered.

The voice was so small it barely crossed the six feet of boiler-room air, but it struck Mr. Teasdale like an iron rod across the shoulder.

The deputy head stopped.

He did not look around at Kacey.

His eyebrows drew down, his lips tightening into that thin, white, bloodless line that meant someone had dropped their dinner-tray in the middle of the headmaster’s speech.

"Be quiet, Kacey," he said without turning his head.

"She’s got the whole week in her pocket," Kacey said, her voice rising into that thin, hysterical shriek that girls use when they see an adder in the gorse.

"She’s got Monday! She’s got Tuesday! If she turns the key in the lime, tomorrow won't come at all! We’ll stay in Friday forever! We’ll be in the cloakroom with the blazers!"

Mr. Teasdale lunged forward.

His hands went for Robin’s pockets.

He did not move with dignity now; he moved with the frantic, scrambling violence of a man who has seen a spark fall into an open keg of blasting-powder in a stone-quarry.

Robin was faster.

She was twelve years old, eight stone, and had spent four years dodging the heavy boots of the older boys in the narrow alleys behind the fish-market.

She ducked beneath his arm.

Her shoulder struck his hip, driving him back against the iron casing of the primary pump with a dull, ringing *clang* that shook the soot from the flue-cleaners.

She ran to the millstone grit wall.

The lime-mortar was soft as butter.

She did not use the Chubb padlock; she did not touch the yellow brass shackle.

She took the six-inch iron shift-key from her right pocket.

She drove the bit—the three irregular, saw-toothed steps of hand-forged iron—straight into the soft lime-mortar between the seventeenth ashlar block and the oak door-frame.

The mortar gave way with a long, wet *squelch*.

The key sank four inches into the wall, right to the round bow with the number *17*.

Robin took the bow in both hands.

Her boots braced against the coal-slack, her teeth clenched until her gums tasted of salt, she turned the key.

Once.

A sound like an iron safe being dropped onto a stone pier: *CLONK*.

The boiler room vanished.

The yellow sodium lights disappeared; the pea-green door disappeared; Mr. Finch’s purple pen disappeared.

The air went cold—freezing, five degrees below zero, smelling of frost on dead heather and the sharp, iron tang of a mountain beck.

It was Thursday.

The corridor was dark. The workhouse desks were gone; the four-foot fluorescent tubes were flickering with a faint, dying orange glow like the embers of a coal-fire in the rain.

Mr. Teasdale was still there.

He was standing ten feet away, but his tweed jacket was covered in sixty years of white lime-dust, and his spectacles had cracked across the right lens in a fine, starry web that reflected the dying light like an insect’s wing.

"Robin," he whispered.

His voice was thin, old, ninety years old, rattling in his throat like three dry peas in a tin whistle.

"Don't turn it again, girl."

Robin did not answer.

She took the iron bow in both hands.

Her thumbs were bleeding; the sharp edge of the stamped *17* had cut through the meat of her thumb-pad, her blood running down the cold iron and into the lime-joint.

She turned it twice.

*CLONK.*

The sound went through the foundations of High Beck.

Four miles away, on the viaduct, the coal-train stopped with a screaming shriek of iron brake-shoes that echoed across the salt-marsh like a dying horse.

Wednesday.

The Art Block was there.

The forty papier-mâché masks were sitting on the drying racks, their gold acrylic paint shining in the yellow light of an oil-lamp that was hanging from the center rafter.

Kacey Vane was standing by the deal table.

She had the shears in her hand.

She was crying.

She was twelve years old, wearing her clean maroon blazer with no torn lining and no safety pins, her face red with that pure, innocent misery of a child who has spent three hours trying to make something beautiful and knows that the other girls will spit on it before the three-o'clock bell.

"Robin," Kacey wept.

She dropped the shears into the ceramic sink: *clatter*.

"I didn't mean it about your mother," she said.

"I said it because Tanya told me... Tanya told me that if I wasn't mean to you, the girls wouldn't let me sit by the window."

Robin looked at her.

The shears were clean.

The scarlet Quink ink was sitting on the teacher’s desk in its blue glass bottle, the cork sealed with red wax that had never been broken by a thumbnail.

"It doesn't matter, Kacey," Robin said.

"It does," Kacey said, her tears running down her cheeks into the corners of her mouth, tasting of salt and bread-and-butter.

"They’ll blame you anyway, Robin. They always blame the girl from Duke Street. They’ve been blaming you since sixty-four."

Robin took her hands off the key.

The iron was hot now.

It was white-hot, glowing in the lime-mortar like a bar of iron being drawn from a blacksmith’s hearth.

She had only to turn it once more.

One more turn to the left.

Three times.

The water-meadows.

The sheep in the gorse; the beck running through the stones without an Armco pipe; the silence of sixty square miles of wet heather where no master had ever blown a whistle and no head of year had ever marked a red cross in a black leather book.

She reached her fingers toward the bow.

"Robin."

The voice was not behind her.

It was in front of her.

Standing in the doorway of the Art Block, her satchel held across her chest, her maroon blazer two sizes too large, was the Thursday Robin.

The clean Robin.

The girl who had not touched the Quink ink; the girl who had not crawled through the coal-chute; the girl who was still afraid of the five-penny bus-fare and the girls laughing behind the gym.

She looked at Robin.

She did not look afraid.

She looked tired—terribly, anciently tired, with that quiet, immovable patience of children who know that tomorrow will be exactly like today, and that the only thing to do is to pull your socks up, put your head down against the rain, and walk through the school gates before the bell stops ringing.

"If you turn it three times, Robin," the clean girl said, "who’s going to make her tea?"

Robin stopped her hand.

Her fingers were two inches from the white-hot iron.

"What?" she whispered.

"Her tea," the girl said.

"She comes off the shift at half-past five. Her ankles are swollen from the laundry-floor. If there isn't a crust on the plate and the kettle on the gas, she’ll sit in the dark with her coat on until the six-o'clock news."

Robin stared at her.

The Art Block was fading.

The forty Greek masks were turning back into rolls of wet newspaper; the oil-lamp was dying; the smell of the raw flax was coming up through the floor-tiles like a cold mist from an open cellar.

"We have to go home, Robin," the clean girl said.

She held out her hand.

Her fingers were thin, red-knuckled, the nails bitten to the quick, with a single brown freckle in the hollow between the thumb and the forefinger.

Robin looked down at her own hand.

The red Quink ink had faded.

The white lead splinter had gone.

Her hand was just a hand—a twelve-year-old girl’s hand, dirty from the coal-slack, smelling of carbolic soap and the damp wool of an old macintosh.

She took the other girl’s fingers.

They were warm.

They were real.

Robin turned her back on the iron key.

She did not turn it three times.

She pulled it out of the mortar.

The iron came away with a dry, papery *snick*, leaving the seventeenth ashlar block solid, gray, impenetrable—three feet of dressed millstone grit holding up fifty thousand tons of municipal comprehensive school.

The wall went dark.

The floor went solid.

And above their heads, through three levels of pre-cast concrete and sixty yards of linoleum corridor, the four-fifteen buzzer went for the end of the school week.

*Ding-dong-dell.*  
*Ding-dong-dell.*

A cheap, flattened electronic chime that died instantly in the wet air of the valley.

Robin walked out through the pea-green door.

The service yard was empty.

The rain had started again—a fine, soft drizzle that had no weight to it, the sort of rain that did not fall so much as hang in the air until your eyelashes were gray with it.

She walked across the gravel, through the gap in the chain-link fence, and down the red-clinker path toward the canal.

She had her satchel.

The broken strap was tied with a piece of red parcel-string, the buckle resting against her hip with a quiet, rhythmic *clink* that went three beats to the second.

In Duke Street, the lamps were not lit yet.

The street was dark, gray, the narrow brick houses huddled together against the railway embankment like sheep behind a dyke.

At number 4, the scullery window was yellow.

Through the net curtain, Robin could see the shape of the kettle on the gas-ring, the blue crown of flame flaring softly in the dusk: *pluff*.

She walked up the two stone steps.

She put her thumb to the brass latch, lifted the lever with both hands to spare the spring, and pushed the door open.

"Robin?" her mother’s voice called from the pantry.

"Aye, Mum," Robin said.

She hung her macintosh behind the door.

She took off her boots and set them under the copper boiler, right where the cold water dripped two drops a minute into the lead trough.

"You’re late," her mother said, coming out with the enameled basin between her thumbs. "The four-o’clock bus must have been full from the works."

"I walked," Robin said.

She went to the table, pulled the Windsor chair out from the oilcloth, and sat down.

On the bread-board lay the blue plate with the dripping, the crust of store-bread, and two clean knives with bone handles.

Robin picked up her knife.

She looked at her wrist.

The skin was clean.

There was no mark on the bone; there was no number on the wall; there was only the sound of the kettle coming to the boil, and outside, on the wet slates of the terrace, the steady, patient, endless falling of tomorrow’s rain.
### Chapter 11: The Bread on the Ledge

The kettle did not whistle; it had a tin lid with three small punch-holes that hissed when the water rose against the rim, spitting tiny grey beads of lime onto the black iron top of the gas-ring.

Robin sat at the oilcloth table in Duke Street, her boots unlaced, her knees pressed tight against the cold wooden drawer where her mother kept the insurance books and the gas-cards.

It was half-past seven on Friday morning.

Her mother was standing by the stone sink, scraping the bottom of an aluminium pan with the dull edge of an old tablespoon. The scrape went *tink-shuck... tink-shuck*, regular as an engine, vibrating through the cold linoleum under Robin’s heels.

"You’ll sit by the grate," her mother said without turning. Her voice had the flat, hard edge of a woman who had spent four hours on the night-shift turning wet sheets in the steam-calender at St. Jude’s Lodge. "You’ll not put the gas-poker in the coals until twelve, and you’ll not open the street door if the postman rattles the flap. If Mrs. Corley sees you on the step, she’ll be down at the corner shop before the kettle’s on the boil, telling the women from the terrace that the Mercer girl’s been put out of the gates for thieving."

"I didn't thieve," Robin said. Her voice sounded thin, dry, like paper being rubbed between two fingers.

"The master said shears," her mother replied, and the spoon struck the tin bottom with a sharp, heavy *clank*. "Big dressmaking shears from the needlework cupboard. He said you were seen with 'em in your macintosh pocket, Robin. Ten inches of iron, he said. A girl don't carry ten inches of iron in her pocket unless she’s minded to do mischief with it."

"I was in English," Robin whispered. "On Thursday morning at eleven, I was in Room 14. Mr. Finch was reading about the sheep-shearing in the book."

Her mother turned then. Her eyes were small, red-rimmed from the chlorine steam, the yellow bags beneath them swollen like two dough-balls that had risen too fast in the tin. She held the wet spoon against her apron, leaving a greasy grey streak of oatmeal across the bib.

"Don't lie to me, Robin," she said, and her voice did not rise; it sank into that terrifying, exhausted hush that was colder than a slap across the cheek. "Mr. Teasdale doesn't invent stories to tell the governors. He’s had thirty years in that school. He knew your uncle Billy when Billy was taken to the court for the lead on the church roof. He said to me, *Mrs. Mercer, we want to help the child, but she’s wandering. She’s in the corridors when she ought to be at her desk, and she looks at the masters like she doesn't know what month it is.*"

She dropped the spoon into the zinc basin. It gave a hollow *thump*.

"I’ve to go back for the relief shift at ten," her mother said, taking her black serge coat off the peg behind the door. "Old Mr. Garside’s bad with his chest; they’re having to turn him every twenty minutes to keep the fluid off his lungs. There’s three slices of bread on the pantry ledge. Eat 'em dry if you like, or spread the grease from the pot. But you stay in this room, Robin. If I come back down Duke Street at five and find the latch off, I’ll take the strap off your father’s trunk and give you what Billy got."

The door closed.

The latch dropped with a dull, oiled *thud*, and then the gate in the brick yard-wall rattled once against its iron catch as her mother’s boots turned into the back lane.

Robin did not move for five minutes.

The house was empty now, but it was not quiet. The cold came through the lime-mortar between the brickwork, carrying the low, grinding whine of the coal-screenings from the railway siding half a mile away. On the wall above the gas-meter, the cheap electric clock—the one with the brown plastic rim that her father had brought from the radio shop in seventy-nine—hummed with a dry, insect-like vibration: *zzzz-click... zzzz-click*.

Robin stood up.

Her shins were stiff from where Kacey Vane’s shoe had struck them on Tuesday, the bruise now turning a mottled, greenish-yellow like the rind of an old melon. She went to the scullery window and looked out into the yard.

The yard was eight feet square, paved with greasy blue engineering bricks that sloped toward a central gulley-grate. Against the coal-bunker sat her school satchel, where she had dropped it when she came through the door on Thursday night.

The blue nylon was dark with damp. The strap hung in two frayed ribbons, the brass buckle missing entirely.

Robin walked out into the cold air.

The mist was low over the roofs of Duke Street, a thick, yellow-grey blanket that smelled of damp slack, fried lard, and the sulfur-wash from the paper mill down on the estuary.

She picked up the satchel.

It was heavy, but not with books.

When she unzipped the main pocket, she found two things lying on top of her torn geometry exercise book.

The first was a flat, round pebble of grey limestone, about the size of a tea-saucer, smooth on one side and rough on the other where it had been pried out of a foundation wall.

The second was a small wooden pencil-box with a sliding lid—the cheap cedar kind that first-year pupils were given on their first day of term.

Robin had never owned that pencil-box. Hers had been a blue vinyl pouch with a broken zip that she had found under a bench in the public library.

She pushed the cedar lid back with her thumb.

Inside was a single slip of pink paper, folded three times into a tight, narrow square.

She unfolded it.

It was an official corridor-pass from Riddings Comprehensive School, stamped with the blue rubber seal of the Deputy Headmaster’s office.

The name written on the line for *PUPIL* was:

*MERCER, R. (FORM 8C)*

The line for *DESTINATION* read:

*Boiler House Sump (Authorized Clearance)*

And below, in the stiff, slanted hand of Mr. Teasdale, written in black Indian ink that had bled slightly into the soft wood-pulp of the paper:

*Date: Friday, 18th October.*  
*Time: 10:45 AM.*  
*Signed: M. Teasdale.*

Robin looked at the slip.

Her hands began to shake, the paper fluttering between her dirty thumbs like a moth’s wing.

Today was Friday, the eighteenth.

The clock on the kitchen wall had said twenty to eight when her mother walked out the door.

Ten-forty-five had not happened yet.

Mr. Teasdale could not have written the pass. He was sitting in the school hall right now, listening to the third-year boys stumbling through the responses to the morning psalm while Mr. Finch played the upright piano with his stiff, purple-stained fingers.

Robin turned the pink slip over.

On the back, written with an ordinary graphite pencil that had been pressed so hard into the paper that the lead had torn through the grain in three places, was a single sentence:

*Bring the key before the bell goes for the milk.*

The handwriting was not Mr. Teasdale’s.

It was her own.

### Chapter 12: The Ventilation Shaft

The red-clinker path along the canal was empty except for three mallards sitting on a half-submerged railway sleeper, their heads tucked beneath their wings against the drizzle.

Robin kept low against the sodden hawthorn hedge that separated the towing path from the railway embankment.

She had her navy parka on, the torn sleeve safety-pinned to the wrist so the grey lining wouldn't drag in the mud. In her right pocket was the six-inch iron key her mother had taken from the tea-caddy; in her left was the cedar pencil-box with the pink slip.

The comprehensive school sat on the ridge like an old grey coal-bunker that had been abandoned by the board of works.

From the football pitch, she could hear the faint, hollow *thump* of a wet leather ball being booted against the timber goal-posts, followed by the high, sharp whistle of Mr. Craddock: *peep... peep-peep*.

"Get your knees up, Henderson! You run like an old woman with two bags of sprouts!"

The voice drifted through the fog, thin and wet, dying almost before it reached the gorse bushes where Robin was crouching.

She did not go near the bicycle sheds or the library fire escape.

She circled wide around the back of the CDT workshops, where the long grass was littered with rusted off-cuts of angle-iron, broken carboys of sulphuric acid from the chemistry labs, and four cast-iron radiator sections that had cracked in the great frost of eighty-seven.

The pea-green sheet-steel door to the boiler room was visible at the bottom of the sixteen concrete steps.

It was locked.

A heavy iron bar, two inches wide, had been bolted across the jamb with two coach-screws, and a new brass padlock—bright, yellow, smelling of machine-oil—hung through the center staple.

Robin did not go down the steps.

She knew the bar. Mr. Gowan kept it in the oil-store behind the gym, for when the river overflowed into the coal-cellars during the November spring-tides.

She moved around the side of the stone foundation wall, where the Victorian mill-base projected three feet beyond the modern CLASP panels of the upper school.

There, almost hidden behind an overgrown clump of elderberry bushes whose black berries had turned to rotten jelly in the frost, was the low ventilation opening.

It was two feet square, covered by a heavy cast-iron grating with vertical bars three inches apart.

Years ago, the boys had tried to pry the grating off with an iron crowbar to get at the spare footballs Mr. Craddock kept in the cellar, and they had broken the lower stone lug away from the millstone grit. The grating now hung by its two upper lead-plugs, swinging three inches free at the bottom like an unfastened cellar-flap.

Robin dropped to her stomach in the wet nettles.

The earth smelled of leaf-rot, coal-tar, and the stale urine of the stray dogs that hunted the rabbits along the railway embankment.

She pulled the bottom of the iron grating outward.

It gave a dull, greasy groan: *errr-k*.

A puff of warm, dry air came out through the gap.

It was not the cold, sour air of Duke Street.

It smelled of hot cylinder-oil, baked dust, dry lime-wash, and the peculiar, sharp iron tang of three hundred gallons of boiling water moving through cast-iron pipes.

Robin pushed her satchel through first.

It fell with a soft, muffled *flump* onto the coal-slack six feet below.

Then she took a deep breath, hooked her elbows over the stone sill, and slid her legs into the dark.

Her boots found no purchase on the smooth, dressed ashlar of the mill-wall; she slid down the masonry like an empty sack, her knees scraping through her wool tights as she hit the floor of the boiler room.

The coal-slack was six inches deep where she landed.

She sat in the darkness for a moment, her breath coming in short, painful gasps that tasted of iron-filings and dust.

The room was not silent.

The three great heat exchangers were working.

*Thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug.*

The sound was deep, heavy, vibrating through the concrete floor into the soles of her boots. Above her head, the four-inch copper mains ticked as they expanded in their iron hangers: *clink... clink... clink*.

Robin stood up and picked up her satchel.

The boiler room was lit by a single bare sixty-watt bulb hanging from a black rubber cord above the primary pump.

Beneath the bulb, sitting on the upturned milk-crate where Robin had eaten her cheese sandwiches on Tuesday, was a girl.

The girl was wearing a maroon school blazer.

She had her back to the room, her head bent over an exercise book that lay open on her knees, her pencil moving across the paper with a slow, scratching rhythm that Robin could hear between the beats of the pump: *scritch... scritch... scritch*.

Robin did not move.

Her boots were frozen in the coal-slack, the iron key in her right pocket pressing against her thigh like an icicle.

"You’re ten minutes late," the girl said.

She did not turn her head.

The voice was Robin’s voice, but it had none of the tremor that Robin had carried in her throat since Tuesday morning. It was flat, hard, carrying the cold, quiet certainty of an old teacher who had spent forty years watching the children fail their spelling tests.

"I had to wait for the four-o’clock shift to clear the yard," the girl on the crate said.

She turned around.

It was Robin.

Not the Robin of Friday morning with the torn stockings and the red Quink ink washed out of her knuckles.

This girl was neat.

Her maroon blazer had all six of its black plastic buttons. Her white collar was stiff with starch, without a smudge of grease at the neck. Her skirt was pressed, the pleats sharp as knives, and her school shoes were clean, polished with black nugget until the leather looked like patent.

Across her knees lay a clean, new exercise book with a red card cover:

*CALDBECK COMPREHENSIVE SCHOOL*  
*NAME: Robin Mercer*  
*FORM: 8C*  
*SUBJECT: English Composition*

The other Robin looked at Robin’s Rochdale boots.

Her mouth twisted into a small, dry smile that didn't reach her eyes—eyes that were clear, bright, and completely empty, like two clean marbles dropped into a bowl of rainwater.

"Look at you," the girl said softly. "You look like something the council pulled out of the culvert with an iron rake."

"Who are you?" Robin whispered.

The girl closed the red exercise book with a sharp *clack*.

"I’m tomorrow," she said.

### Chapter 13: The Arithmetic of the Day

"You can't be tomorrow," Robin said, her fingers digging through the pocket of her parka to find the iron key. "Today is Friday. Tomorrow is Saturday. There isn't any school on Saturday. The gates are padlocked and Mr. Gowan goes to the pigeon-lofts on the canal."

The neat girl stood up from the milk-crate.

She was the same height as Robin, but she stood straighter; her neck was long, her chin held high above the clean white collar, as if she were waiting for the Headmaster to pin a silver prefect’s badge to her lapel.

"You think like an eight-stone girl from Duke Street," the neat girl said, walking two paces toward the secondary heat exchanger. Her shoes made a clean, sharp *tap... tap* on the concrete that sounded completely different from the dull scuff of Robin’s broken heels. "You think because the parish council puts a name on a brass plate at the corner of the lane, the days have to follow each other like sheep through a hurdle."

She pointed to Door 17 in the millstone grit wall.

The oak door was shut.

The white lead *17* was not peeling anymore. It looked as if it had been painted twenty minutes ago, the edges of the numbers crisp, sharp, smelling of fresh white-lead and raw linseed oil.

"What do you think is in there, Robin?" the neat girl asked.

"Yesterday," Robin said. "The school from yesterday. Where Leo Henderson was playing his scales."

The neat girl laughed. It was a short, dry, clicking sound like two bone knitting-needles being struck together in an empty room.

"It’s not yesterday," she said. "It’s the school they didn't finish."

She took one step closer.

Robin could smell her.

She did not smell of carbolic soap or damp wool.

She smelled of new exercise books, clean blotting paper, and the sharp, chemical lemon-oil they used to polish the desks in the Headmaster’s study before the governors’ annual tea.

"In nineteen sixty-four," the neat girl said, her voice dropping into that rhythmic, recitation tone that teachers use for the history syllabus, "the Ministry of Education drew up the plan for the South Westmorland Central High School. Six hundred desks, twenty-four classrooms, two gymnasiums, and a domestic science suite with four gas-ovens and two electric ranges."

She tapped the red exercise book against her thumb: *tap... tap*.

"They built the classrooms. They put the plastic tiles on the floor. They hung the yellow-gloss doors on the music rooms. But they didn't have the money for the second wing. The borough went into the deficit on the sewage-works, and the Ministry said the children from the canal-terraces would have to stay in the old secondary modern until the rates came up."

She pointed her thumb toward the floor.

"This is the school they planned, Robin. The one that was supposed to come after the old mill was cleared. But it never arrived. It’s been sitting twelve feet below the gravel since sixty-four, waiting for someone to sign the requisition for the timber."

"Then why are the people in it?" Robin asked, her throat aching with cold. "Why is Mr. Teasdale there? Why is Kacey Vane there?"

The neat girl smiled again.

"Because they don't want to be up there," she whispered.

She gestured toward the ceiling, where the faint, rhythmic thrumming of three hundred pairs of rubber-soled plimsolls was coming through the pre-cast concrete panels from the boys’ gym above.

"You think Kacey Vane likes her father?" the neat girl asked. "You think she likes going back to that house on the bypass where he drinks three bottles of gin and throws the tea-plates at the dresser? You think Mr. Teasdale likes sitting in that little plywood office with the pink slips, listening to the inspectors tell him that forty per cent of the third-year boys can't read the warnings on the fire-extinguishers?"

She leaned forward until her face was barely six inches from Robin’s.

Her skin was flawless—so smooth and white that it looked like the lard her mother kept in the earthenware jar by the stove, without a single freckle, without a scratch, without a pore.

"Down here," the neat girl whispered, "they do what they’re told. They sit in the rows. They don't scream behind the bicycle sheds. And when the bell rings, they don't go home to Duke Street. They go into the cupboards."

"The cupboards?" Robin said.

"The drying cupboards," the neat girl said. "Behind the cloakrooms. Where the coats hang. It’s warm in there, Robin. There’s no wind from the salt-marsh, and the gas-meter never runs out of shillings."

She held out her hand.

Her fingers were long, pale, the nails clean and trimmed into perfect, rounded crescents that looked like mother-of-pearl.

"Give me the key," she said.

Robin stepped back.

Her left heel caught the rim of the milk-crate, and she nearly fell into the coal-slack, her hand flying out to catch the cold copper riser to steady herself.

"No," Robin said.

"You have to," the neat girl said. Her voice didn't rise; it took on that heavy, mechanical weight that Robin had heard in the waterwheel beneath the mill. "Mr. Teasdale has already signed the card. Look at your pocket."

Robin reached her hand into her left pocket.

The cedar pencil-box was gone.

In its place was a heavy brass identity disc, three inches across, stamped with five capital letters and four numerals:

*C.C.S. 1964*  
*PUPIL NO. 17*

The edges of the brass were notched with twenty-four small, triangular cuts, like the teeth on a circular-saw blade.

"If you don't use the key," the neat girl said, "you have to wear the disc. And if you wear the disc, Mr. Gowan will find you by the water-cooler before the second bell."

"What does he do when he finds you?" Robin asked.

The neat girl didn't answer with words.

She reached up to the collar of her clean maroon blazer.

She unbuttoned the top button.

Then the second.

Then she pulled the lapels apart.

Beneath the clean white blouse, where her ribs should have been, there was no skin.

There was only a dark, rectangular cavity, six inches wide and eight inches deep, lined with rusted sheet-iron that had been secured to her collarbones with four brass rivets.

Inside the cavity, sitting in a nest of greasy wood-shavings and dry boiler-dust, was an electric meter.

A black Bakelite meter with a round glass dial, through which Robin could see an aluminum disc spinning with a high, faint, insect-like hiss: *zzzzz-snick... zzzzz-snick*.

Above the disc, a row of five white plastic rollers showed a number in black print:

*00017*

And below the rollers, cast into the black Bakelite in raised block letters:

*BOROUGH OF CALDBECK ELECTRICITY UNDERTAKING*  
*PREPAYMENT MECHANISM*  
*INSERT ONE SHILLING*  
*TIME REMAINING: 42 MINUTES*

The neat girl buttoned her blouse.

The white linen met across the sheet-iron with a crisp, clean rustle that sounded like fresh typing paper being smoothed down on an office desk.

"You see?" she said. "The shift is nearly up. If the coin doesn't drop before the forty-two minutes are gone, the door locks from both sides."

"And then?" Robin whispered.

"Then you stay in the wall," the neat girl said. "With the ones from the flax mill. Until the council knocks the foundations down for the bypass."

### Chapter 14: The Stalker in the Glass

Robin did not give her the key.

She turned and threw her weight against the pea-green door at the top of the sixteen steps, but the iron bar outside was solid; it did not give an eighth of an inch, the coach-screws holding the timber to the concrete with the immovable grip of rock-bolts.

Behind her, the neat girl did not give chase.

She sat back down on the milk-crate, opened her red exercise book, and resumed writing with her graphite pencil: *scritch... scritch... scritch*.

"Door 17 is open, Robin," her voice called out over the hum of the pumps. "It’s been open since nineteen sixty-four. You can't go back up the steps; Mr. Gowan’s got his bucket of grease on the third landing, and he’s waiting with the wire-pliers."

Robin walked down the sixteen steps again.

Her legs felt hollow, like dry elder-twigs that would snap if she tried to run.

She reached the ashlar wall.

Door 17 stood four inches ajar.

She did not use the brass ring; she set her shoulder against the gray oak and shoved.

The door swung back without a sound.

The Humanities corridor was there.

It was not flooded now.

The water was gone, but the thermoplastic tiles were damp, covered with a thin, greasy film of disinfectant that smelled of pine-oil and cold hospital corridors.

The fluorescent tubes overhead were flickering with a sick, yellow-green light, humming at sixty cycles with a sound that seemed to set Robin’s eye-sockets vibrating behind her cheekbones.

The date on the blue sugar-paper notice by the fire-exit read:

*Thursday, 17th October.*

Yesterday.

Robin stepped through the opening.

The air was cooler than the boiler room, but it was thick—so thick that each breath felt like swallowing cold, wet fleece.

Down the corridor, forty yards away toward the library, the double doors were open.

She could hear Mr. Finch.

He was teaching English to Form 8C.

His voice was clear, carrying that dry, monotonous drone that had put three generations of small-town boys to sleep between morning play and the dinner-sitting:

"Page forty-four, Henderson. Begin at line twelve. *The sheep were gathered into the fold before the frost could take the fleece...*"

Robin moved down the tiles, keeping her back hard against the cork noticeboard.

She passed the water-cooler.

The black iron grille beneath the compressor was empty. There was no apple core; there was only the greasy zinc tray and the dead blowflies.

She stopped outside Room 14.

The door was birch-ply, with the twelve-inch square of wired plate-glass in the upper panel.

Robin looked through.

The room was warm.

The iron radiators beneath the windows were clicking as the steam passed through the joints, their silver paint blistered into tiny scales near the valves.

Thirty children were sitting at the double-seater desks.

In the second row from the window, Leo Henderson was standing with his book held before his face with both hands, his thumb hooked into the corner of the page to hide the place where he had chewed the paper.

"Read on, Henderson," Mr. Finch said from his desk by the blackboard.

Mr. Finch had his purple felt-tip pen between his teeth like an unlit cigar, his cardigan unbuttoned to reveal a waistcoat of green tweed with leather buttons.

Leo took a deep, whistling breath through his nose:

" *The shearers came from the valley at dawn,* " he read, his voice cracking on the word *dawn* like a dry stick. " *They had their iron irons wrapped in greasy cloths to keep the wet off the blades...* "

Robin’s eyes moved past him.

To the back row.

There, sitting on the low plywood cupboard by the radiator, was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was not reading.

She had her school satchel open on her lap, her fingers moving inside the dark pocket with a slow, secretive rhythm that looked like someone handling a small, live animal.

She looked up.

She did not look at Leo Henderson. She did not look at Mr. Finch.

She looked directly through the wired glass of the door.

At Robin.

Her face was not the cruel, laughing face of Thursday morning.

It was pale, waxen, the skin around her mouth pulled tight until her lips were almost invisible, leaving only her teeth—long, yellow, slightly crossed in the center—showing in a rigid, mirthless grin.

She raised her right hand from the satchel.

She was holding an iron object.

It was not the four-inch wire nail.

It was a pair of sheep-shears.

Not modern dressmaking shears with black-painted handles; these were old, hand-forged spring-shears, twelve inches long, the two curved iron blades joined by a single loop of spring-steel at the butt, the metal pitted with orange rust and crusted with dried sheep-grease.

Kacey squeezed her palm.

The two blades came together with a flat, screaming *snick-tock* that Robin could hear through the birch-ply door:

*Snick.*

Then Kacey pointed the blades at the middle of the classroom.

At the second desk in the center row.

Robin looked.

Sitting at the desk, with her satchel between her ankles and her maroon blazer buttoned crookedly over her gray cardigan, was Robin Mercer.

The Thursday Robin.

The girl had her head down over her rough-book, her pencil moving across the grey paper in frantic, jerky strokes.

Robin stepped closer to the glass.

Her nose touched the cold surface.

She could see what the girl was writing.

It was not the notes on *The Mayor of Casterbridge*.

It was not the list of spelling words for the Friday test.

The girl was filling the page with a single word, written over and over again until the paper was torn through by the pencil point:

*TOMORROW*  
*TOMORROW*  
*TOMORROW*  
*TOMORROW*

And beside the girl, sitting in the other half of the double-seater desk, was a woman.

It was not a pupil.

It was Robin’s mother.

Not the tired woman in the white nylon auxiliary’s coat who worked the night-shift at the home.

She was twelve years old.

She was wearing the blue serge gym-slip of the parish school, her hair tied with the red parcel-string, her knees covered with dried mud from the canal-bank.

She was looking at the Thursday Robin with an expression of dreadful, hungry pity—the look that people give to a dog that has been hit by a lorry on the bypass and is dragging its hind-quarters into the ditch to die.

The mother reached out her hand.

Her fingers touched the shoulder of the Thursday Robin’s blazer.

"It’s no good writing it down, Claire," the mother whispered through the glass.

The voice was Robin’s mother’s voice, but it had that high, thin treble of a child who had never seen Duke Street, never seen the gas-meter, never seen the three slices of bread on the pantry ledge.

"If you don't take the shears to the Art Block before the three-o'clock bell, they’ll put your name in the parish book. They’ll say you took the fleece off the line."

The Thursday Robin did not stop her pencil.

*TOMORROW.*  
*TOMORROW.*

"I didn't take the fleece," the Thursday Robin wept, her voice muffled by the heavy timber of the door. "It was Kacey. Kacey took the iron from the smithy."

Mr. Finch looked up from his desk.

His spectacles slipped down his nose, catching the yellow light from the high hoppers.

He did not look at the two girls in the back row.

He looked straight past them.

Through the wired glass.

At Robin.

His purple pen came out of his mouth.

He stood up from his chair.

He did not rise like an ordinary man; he rose in three distinct, jerky movements, his knees clicking like an old umbrella being forced open in a gale: *snick... snick... snick*.

He took the wooden blackboard ruler off the ledge.

It was three feet long, made of yellow boxwood with brass-reinforced ends, stamped with the seal of the Westmorland County Council.

He walked toward the door.

His shoes made no sound on the floorboards. None at all.

He moved across the room like a shadow thrown by a lantern across a wet sheet, his eyes fixed on Robin’s face with a flat, cold, immovable hatred that made her ribs ache with terror.

"Mercer," his voice whispered through the keyhole.

"You have been wandering outside your assigned period."

Robin stepped back from the glass.

Behind her, down the Humanities corridor, the fire doors began to swing.

*Creak... slam.*  
*Creak... slam.*

From both directions—from the library wing and from the music practice rooms—they were coming.

The teachers.

Mrs. Garside, holding a wooden rolling pin crusted with dry flour from the domestic science kitchens; Mr. Craddock, his whistle hanging from his teeth like an iron fang, his tracksuit stained with sixty years of wet cinders from the track; and Miss Finch, her needlework scissors clicking against her thumb with that terrible, rhythmic *snick-tock*.

None of them were running.

They were walking with that slow, synchronized, military tread that Robin had heard in the waterwheel.

Their faces were not angry.

They were empty.

They were the faces of people who had been hired to clear an old drainage ditch, and had found a bundle of dirty rags that had to be hauled out with an iron hook before the water could flow toward the sea.

Robin turned.

She ran for Door 17.

### Chapter 15: The Predestination of the Shears

The door was not there.

Where the millstone grit wall had been, there was only a long, flat run of green plasterboard, newly nailed to three-by-two timber studs that smelled of fresh Swedish pine and white lime-dust.

Nailed to the middle stud with four wire tacks was a cardboard sign:

*NOTICE OF EXPULSION*  
*BY ORDER OF THE MANAGERS*  
*The pupil ROBIN MERCER (8C) having this day been found guilty of malicious mischief, trespass, and the possession of edged tools within the precincts of the foundation, her name is hereby deleted from the rolls of this school in perpetuity.*  
*Signed: M. Teasdale (Deputy Headmaster)*  
*Witness: K. Vane (Prefect)*

Robin threw herself against the plasterboard.

Her shoulder went through the thin material with a loud, tearing *crunch*, the dry gypsum crumbling into grey powder that filled her mouth and nose with the taste of chalk and dead sea-shells.

Behind the plasterboard was not the boiler room.

It was the Art Block.

Thursday afternoon.

Two o’clock.

The room was silent, lit by that cold, northern light that comes through ribbed glass when the fells are covered in low snow-cloud.

On the sixteen deal tables, the forty Greek masks were waiting.

They were dry now.

The gold acrylic paint was hard, shining like brass foil under the skylights.

Robin fell onto her knees between the printing press and the clay-troughs.

Her Rochdale boots were gone.

Her grey macintosh was gone.

She was wearing her maroon school blazer—the clean one, with all six black buttons, the stiff white collar, and the sharp pleats in her skirt.

In her right hand, she was holding the twelve-inch sheep-shears.

The iron was cold—so freezing that the skin of her palm was sticking to the rusted spring-loop, the rough metal biting into the meat beneath her thumb with a dull, throbbing pain.

"No," Robin whispered.

She tried to open her fingers.

She tried to drop the iron onto the floor-tiles.

Her hand would not obey.

Her tendons were locked, stiff as wire ropes that had been wound around an iron capstan and pulled taut by forty tons of canal-water.

A shadow crossed the north-facing skylight.

Someone was standing outside the window, on the flat asphalt roof of the laundry.

It was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was wearing her navy winter coat with the fur-trimmed hood—the expensive one her mother had bought from the department store in Lancaster.

She was leaning her forehead against the ribbed glass, her breath leaving a round, white circle of steam on the pane: *huuu-uh*.

She was looking down into the Art Block.

She saw Robin.

She saw the sheep-shears in Robin’s right hand.

Kacey did not tap on the glass. She did not shout.

She raised her right hand and pressed her palm against the outside of the window.

In the center of her palm was an ink-stain.

A dark, circular smear of scarlet Quink ink, two inches across, that looked like an open, bleeding wound against the cold glass.

Kacey nodded once.

Slowly.

Like an executioner giving the sign to the man at the winch.

Robin’s right arm rose.

It did not move with her own will; it rose with that heavy, hydraulic jerk that she had seen in the mechanical coal-shovels at the docks.

Her knuckles were white.

The point of the sheep-shears moved through the air toward the third rack from the left.

Toward the gold mask.

"Don't," Robin pleaded.

She was talking to her own shoulder, to her own wrist, to the twelve-year-old bones that had lived in Duke Street and eaten processed cheese off an oilcloth table.

"Please. It’s Kacey’s. She made it with the pearls from her hairband."

Her shoulder did not listen.

The iron shears came down.

*Crunch.*

The sound was not paper.

It was the sound of dry bone being split with a butcher’s saw.

The gold mask tore from the forehead to the chin, the thirty plastic pearls flying off the mouth like shot from a cartridge, rattling against the ceramic sinks and the timber cupboards: *tic-tic-tic-tic*.

The blades twisted.

The cardboard teeth tore away in a long, hairy strip of grey newspaper pulp.

Then her left hand—moving with that same horrifying, independent precision—reached out to the teacher’s desk.

Her fingers closed around the blue glass bottle of scarlet Quink.

She did not draw the cork.

She smashed the bottle against the lead rim of the sink.

*Smash.*

The glass shattered into forty sharp, blue splinters, and the thick, oily ink poured across the two halves of the gold mask, soaking into the torn edges of the *Westmorland Gazette* until the Greek face was buried in a pool of dark, smelling liver-blood.

Robin stood by the sink.

The iron shears were still in her hand, dripping red ink onto her clean white school socks: *plop... plop... plop*.

The door of the Art Block clicked.

It did not open wide.

It opened two inches.

Standing in the gap was Tanya Siddle.

Tanya was holding an empty plastic milk-crate by its wire handles.

Her eyes were wide, round, filled with that pure, triumphant malice that Robin had seen on the faces of the girls behind the bicycle sheds.

"I saw you," Tanya whispered.

Her voice had no question in it.

It sounded as if she had been standing behind that door since nine o’clock on Monday morning, waiting for the blue glass to hit the lead.

"I saw you with the shears, Skimmer."

Tanya did not run to the office.

She reached into her pocket, pulled out an iron whistle—the three-tone whistle that the prefects used for corridor duty—and put it to her lips.

She blew.

The sound was not an iron whistle.

It was the low, roaring blast of the five-o’clock steam-siren at the chemical works, vibrating through the school foundations until the ink-bottles on the teacher’s desk began to dance across the blotting paper:

*HOOOOO-OOOO-OOOO.*

### Chapter 16: The Court in the Laundry

They did not take her to the Pastoral Office.

They took her to the old laundry in the basement of B-Block, where the orphan boys had scrubbed the wool-fleeces with lye and river-water before the Great War.

The floor was made of large, square flagstones of Carboniferous limestone, black with sixty years of damp grease, sloping toward a central drain that was covered by an iron grating shaped like a cartwheel.

In the center of the room sat a long deal table, bleached white by ninety years of soda-wash.

Behind the table sat the three managers.

In the center was Mr. Teasdale.

He was wearing his black frock-coat, but the fabric was rotting at the armpits, showing patches of yellow horsehair padding that twitched every time he drew a breath. His spectacles had no glass in the frames; they were two empty brass circles through which Robin could see the yellow skin of his temples and the small, red-rimmed hollows where his eyes should have been.

To his left was Mrs. Garside, her hands folded over a thick, leather-bound volume entitled *THE REGISTER OF THE UNFORTUNATE AND THE INSUBORDINATE (CALDBECK PARISH, 1888)*.

To his right was Mr. Gowan.

The caretaker was not wearing his dust-coat. He was wearing an old navy bridge-coat with the anchors cut off the buttons, his three iron chisels resting flat on the bleached wood of the table like three carpenter’s gouges.

Robin stood before the table.

She was still holding the sheep-shears.

Her hands had not opened; the iron had rusted into her skin, the orange corrosion forming a hard, scaly collar around her knuckles that smelled of old blood and ditch-water.

Beside her, holding her left arm with two fingers that were as cold and hard as iron pipe-clips, was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was crying.

She had her gold mask in her lap—the two halves tied together with a piece of dirty red parcel-string, the scarlet ink dripping through the knots onto her patent shoes.

"She did it because I have a father with two motor-lorries," Kacey wept into her sleeve. "She did it because my mother buys me the butter-pasties from the bakery on the High Street. She lives in the ditch behind the railway, Mr. Teasdale. She has no sheets on her bed; she sleeps in an old army coat with the buttons torn off."

Mr. Teasdale did not move his head.

The empty brass frames of his spectacles turned toward Robin with that slow, heavy swivel of a searchlight.

"What do you say to the charge, Robin Mercer?"

His voice was like an iron bucket being dragged over the gravel of a dried-up river-bed.

"I didn't want to," Robin whispered. "My hand did it. The other girl... the girl on the crate... she had the meter in her ribs."

Mrs. Garside opened the leather-bound book.

The yellow sheep-parchment pages crackled like dry hay: *scritch-crackle*.

"There is no other girl, Robin," Mrs. Garside read from the page.

Her voice had the dry, whistling rattle of a woman who had spent forty years reading the burial services over the paupers’ graves at the south corner of the churchyard.

"There is only the girl who was enrolled on the first of September, nineteen sixty-four. There is only the girl who sat at Desk Seventeen in the second class. There is only the girl who took the four-inch wire nail from the boiler-casing and drove it into the eye of the school-clock."

She pointed a yellow fingernail at the ledger.

"Look at the entry, Mercer."

Robin was forced forward by Kacey’s iron grip.

Her chin was pressed down against the cold wood of the table until her teeth clicked together.

She looked at the page.

The ink was black, faded to brown at the edges, written in that stiff copperplate hand that masters used before the invention of the typewriter:

*OCTOBER 18, 1891: Mercer, Robin. Aged 12 yrs. Admitted from the Duke Street Tenements without boots or linen. Found this morning in the lower wool-drying cellar with twelve inches of iron in her sleeve. The child shows an obstinate and unregenerate spirit, refusing to name the persons who instructed her to tamper with the mill-sluice.*

*SENTENCE: To be returned to the day behind. To be kept within the stone walls until the borough shall have need of the ground for the widening of the coal-railway.*

*Signed: M. Teasdale (Chairman of the Committee).*

Robin stared at the date.

*1891.*

"It’s not me," she cried out, her voice breaking into a high, animal shriek that brought the smell of cold ditch-mud into her nostrils. "I have a Casio watch! I have a red library card! My mother has the blue uniform with the plastic badge!"

Mr. Teasdale stood up.

He reached his hand across the table.

His fingers were not flesh.

They were five round bars of square iron, two inches long, forged by hand and welded to an iron wrist-plate stamped with the letters *H.B.U.*

He did not touch her face.

He touched the front of her maroon blazer.

His iron fingers hooked into the neck of her collar and pulled.

The wool tore with a long, screaming *ri-i-ip*.

The black buttons flew across the flagstones like iron nuts: *ting... ting... clatter*.

Robin looked down at her own chest.

There was no blouse.

There was no skin.

There was only the black Bakelite meter, its glass dial glowing with a faint, greenish phosphorescence in the gloom of the laundry.

The aluminum disc was turning.

*Zzzzz-snick.*  
*Zzzzz-snick.*

The five white plastic rollers were spinning:

*00003*  
*00002*  
*00001*  
*00000*

A small brass hammer inside the mechanism rose three inches, hovered for a second against a coiled steel spring, and then fell against the internal chime-bar with a single, clear, hollow *CLANG*.

The light went out.

Not the electric light; the light of the day.

The high windows of the laundry went black, as if someone had dumped sixty tons of wet coal-slack against the glass from the top of the embankment.

The air went dead.

The smell of the pine-disinfectant vanished; the smell of the ink vanished; the sound of Leo Henderson’s scales vanished.

There was only the cold.

The terrifying, absolute cold of a stone chamber twelve feet below the mud of a northern estuary, where no water had moved and no bell had rung since the last coal-barge cleared the basin in eighteen ninety-six.

### Chapter 17: The Inventory of the Left-Behind

In the dark, Robin Mercer could hear her own heart.

It was not beating in her ribs; it was beating in the flagstones beneath her boots.

*Thump... thump... thump.*

Four beats slower than it had been in Duke Street.

A match was struck.

*Scritch.*

A small, yellow flower of flame bloomed three feet away in the darkness, smelling of sulfur and damp pine-wood.

Holding the match was Mr. Gowan.

He was sitting on an upturned wooden salt-tub, his brown dust-coat buttoned to his chin with pieces of copper wire. In his right hand he held an old oil-lamp—the square, tin kind that the shunters used on the mineral lines, with a bullseye lens of thick, green glass that threw a round, moss-colored circle of light across the limestone floor.

He set the match to the wick.

The lamp flared with a hollow *pluff*, and the green light crawled across the walls.

The laundry was gone.

The deal table was gone.

The managers were gone.

Robin was standing in a narrow tunnel of dressed millstone grit, four feet wide and six feet high, the roof supported by low, segmental arches of red brick that were white with lime-fur.

Along both walls, sitting on the stone floor with their backs pressed against the damp ashlar, were the coats.

Not twenty coats.

Hundreds.

They were arranged in a single, unbroken line that stretched away into the green darkness of the tunnel until the shapes were lost in the fog.

There were the blue nylon parkas from the nineteen-eighties, their orange synthetic linings rotting into oily grey fluff; there were the heavy black wool-nap overcoats from the nineteen-fifties, with the brass buttons of the railway board; there were the thin, grey fustian jackets of the flax-boys, their cuffs stiff with seventy-year-old linseed oil; and there were the little plaid shawls of the girls from the dame-school, their fringes tangled with dried burrs and sheep-grease.

Every coat had a label pinned to the collar.

A rectangular piece of zinc, two inches by one, stamped with a name and a year:

*1874: Arthur Holland (Line-boy).*  
*1882: Martha Vane (Carder).*  
*1911: George Cleary (Riveter’s lad).*  
*1939: Thomas Siddle (Evacuee).*  
*1972: Claire Holland (Pupil).*

Robin walked down the line of coats.

Her boots made a wet, sucking sound on the stone: *glop... glop*.

She stopped at the zinc label for *1972*.

The coat was her mother’s.

The blue serge school coat that had been bought from the church-hall bazaar when Claire Holland was twelve years old.

The hem had been let down three inches, showing a dark, unfaded band of navy wool along the bottom where the original stitches had been picked out with a pin.

Robin reached her hand out.

Her fingers were her own again; the sheep-shears were gone, the iron rust washed away by the cold moisture that hung in the tunnel air.

She touched the navy wool.

The cloth was not cold.

It was warm—warm as a rabbit pulled out of a burrow in the gorse, carrying that faint, sweet smell of lavender-soap and the bread-and-dripping her mother had eaten before the five-o’clock shift.

Inside the right pocket was a hard, round object.

Robin reached her fingers in.

She pulled it out into the green light of the shunter’s lamp.

It was an apple.

A small, round, red crab-apple from the wild trees that grew along the canal-bank behind the gasworks.

It was not withered. It was not black.

The skin was tight, shining with a faint, oily polish, without a bruise, without a worm-hole.

On the skin, scratched into the red wax with the point of a safety pin, was a date:

*Monday, 14th October, 1974.*

"She left it there," Mr. Gowan’s voice said from the salt-tub.

The caretaker had his clay pipe between his teeth, but he had no tobacco in the bowl; he was merely sucking on the dry reed, making a hollow, clicking sound that went with the dripping of the lime-water: *clack-tick... clack-tick*.

"She sat on the mounting-block by the north gate for two hours," he said. "Waiting for her father to come with the wheelbarrow for her tin box. He didn't come, Robin. He was at the station tavern with three men from the timber-wharf, drinking the four-shilling ale until the police took him to the lock-up."

"She went home," Robin said. "She lives in Duke Street."

Mr. Gowan took the pipe out of his mouth.

He pointed the dry stem down the tunnel, toward the dark end where the segmental arches disappeared into the black silt.

"She went to Duke Street," he said. "And she left the coat here. You can't take two coats through the gate, girl. The rule’s been on the board since sixty-four. If you take the new one out into the rain, you leave the old one on the hook."

"I don't want the new one," Robin whispered.

She looked down at her own chest.

Her maroon blazer was gone.

She was wearing her thin gray acrylic cardigan—the one that had shrunk in the wash until the wrists were three inches short, with the two missing buttons replaced by rusty brass safety pins.

Her hands were red, raw, the skin between the fingers cracked by the winter salt-damp.

"Then what are you doing with the key, Robin?" Mr. Gowan asked.

He stood up from the salt-tub.

He was not tall; he was an old man, five feet four, with a curvature of the spine that made his head poke forward like an old turtle looking for water in a dry ditch.

He held up his left hand.

The three iron chisels were gone.

He had his own three fingers back—short, broad, scarred with the blue marks of old coal-cuts, the fingernails thick and yellow as horn.

In his right hand he held his twelve-inch iron ring with the forty keys.

"The key in your pocket," he said. "The six-inch one. It doesn't belong to the school, Robin. The school belongs to the key."

"My mother gave it to me," Robin said.

"She didn't give it to you," the old man said, and his voice had no anger in it; it had that soft, terrible sorrow of a man who has had to tell sixty mothers that their boys were at the bottom of the sump. "She dropped it in the coal-chute when she was twelve years old, so she wouldn't have to go back to the desk on Tuesday."

He took two dragging steps toward her, his iron-shod boots scraping the gritstone flagstones.

"Every girl who comes down these steps drops the key," he said. "They drop it because the boys throw stones at their stockings; they drop it because the girls laugh at their dripping-sandwiches; they drop it because the master has the boxwood ruler with the brass ends."

He reached out his hand and touched Robin’s cheek.

His skin was rough, smelling of dry peat and engine-oil, but it was warm.

It was the first warm thing Robin had felt since Tuesday morning.

"And when they drop it," he whispered, "yesterday catches it. And yesterday never gives an iron thing back without a name to go with it."

### Chapter 18: The Strike of the Three Bells

"How do I get out?" Robin asked.

The tunnel was narrowing.

The green light from the shunter’s lamp was shrinking, the oil in the reservoir burning down to the last greasy inch of paraffin, the wick spitting blue sparks against the blackened glass.

"There’s only one way out of a day that’s already been lived, Robin," Mr. Gowan said.

He turned the bullseye lamp toward the wall behind the line of coats.

There, set into the dressed ashlar, was an iron plate.

Two feet square, secured by four large, hexagonal nuts that had been rusted into solid lumps of red oxide.

In the center of the plate was a keyhole.

Not a modern keyhole for a flat, brass cylinder-key.

A vertical slit, six inches high, shaped like the cross-section of an iron T-rail, with three stepped notches cut into the left side.

The shape of the six-inch shift-key.

"If you turn it to the right," Mr. Gowan said, "you go to Monday. You sit at the circular table in the library with Mr. Finch. You eat your processed cheese behind the secondary pump. And at three-fifteen, Kacey Vane kicks your plimsolls until the canvas peels off the toes."

"And if I turn it to the left?"

The old man looked down at his boots.

"If you turn it to the left, Robin, you go to tomorrow."

"Saturday?"

"No," he said softly. "The tomorrow that comes after the school."

"What school?"

"All of it," he said, waving his hand toward the segmental arches above their heads. "The desks. The red books. The registers with the purple crosses. The masters with the boxwood rulers. The whole ninety years of it, Robin."

"What’s there?" she whispered.

"The ground," he said. "Just the ground. Where the sheep were before the flax-mill had a chimney. Where nobody has a number, and nobody has an address in Duke Street, and nobody has to stand in the rain waiting for the relief van from the workhouse."

Robin took the six-inch iron key from her pocket.

It was heavy—so heavy that she had to use both hands to lift the round bow to the level of the keyhole.

The iron was cold, but it did not burn her now.

It felt like the stone of the mountain-becks—ancient, clean, hard, having nothing to do with schools or councils or the price of coal at the railway staithe.

She looked at Mr. Gowan.

"Will you come?" she asked.

The old man shook his head.

His eyes were very bright in the dying green light of the lamp, two small, clear drops of water resting in the red corners of his lids like dew on a dead leaf.

"I have to sweep the yard, Robin," he said. "The frost is coming down from the fells tonight. If the sixteen steps aren't salted before seven in the morning, someone’ll break their neck on the turn, and then the borough will be down with the clipboards asking why the sand-box was empty."

He pulled his clay pipe out of his pocket and jammed the stem between his gums.

"Put the key in the iron, girl," he said. "Before the oil goes."

Robin stepped to the plate.

The keyhole seemed to draw the iron bit toward itself; as the three saw-toothed steps entered the slot, the masonry around the plate gave a long, deep, hydraulic sigh: *h-u-u-u-u-sh*.

The key sank to the bow.

Robin took the iron ring in both hands.

Her boots were planted on the limestone flagstone; her teeth were set; her shoulder-blades braced against the damp ashlar of the arch.

She did not turn it to the right.

She did not want Monday.

She did not want the library table, she did not want the processed cheese, she did not want Kacey Vane’s patent shoes swinging against the plywood with that hollow *thump... thump... thump*.

She turned it to the left.

The key moved.

It did not grate; it turned with a heavy, oiled, silent rotation that had the weight of the earth behind it.

Once.

*CLONK.*

The sound went through the foundation walls of Caldbeck.

In the classrooms above, thirty miles of copper heating pipes contracted an eighth of an inch in their hangers, dropping twenty tons of soot from the chimney-breasts into the empty grates.

The green light went out.

The tunnel disappeared.

The coats disappeared.

Robin was standing in the dark.

She turned it twice.

*CLONK.*

The sound was louder now—it was the sound of a great oak beam snapping under the weight of three hundred tons of wet slate.

A wind hit her face.

It was not the wind from the boiler-room louver.

It was the wind from the sea.

A cold, clean, salt-laden gale that had traveled forty miles across the low-water sands of Morecambe Bay, carrying the smell of bladder-wrack, wet cockle-shells, and the freezing rain that falls upon the limestone scars of the high fells.

She turned it the third time.

There was no sound.

None at all.

No *clonk*; no snap; no chime from the electric meter.

The iron key simply dissolved in her hands, turning into a handful of cold, red mud that ran through her fingers like wet clay from the bottom of a beck.

The stone wall beneath her palms fell away.

It did not collapse; it ceased to be there.

Robin stepped forward.

Her boots did not hit linoleum.

They did not hit flagstones.

They sank three inches into the soft, springy turf of a mountain water-meadow.

### Chapter 19: The Meadow on the High Scar

The sky was white.

Not the dirty, greasy white of the school playground on a Tuesday afternoon; it was that vast, high, silver-grey vault of the northern winter, clear to the edges of the world, where the clouds were long, thin ribbons of mare’s-tail pulled out by the upper gale.

Robin Mercer stood in the middle of the field.

The grass was short, cropped close by six hundred years of mountain sheep, brown with the autumn frost, dotted with the pale grey boulders of limestone that lay upon the slope like sleeping cattle.

Below her, two miles down the valley, lay the estuary.

The tide was out.

The bay was seven miles of shining, silver mud, intersected by the dark, winding channels of the three rivers that came down from the mountains, their waters reflecting the white sky like ribbons of mercury drawn across a lead sheet.

There was no school.

Where the three CLASP blocks of Caldbeck Comprehensive had stood, with their yellow-gloss doors, their leaking roofs, and their sixty-cycle fluorescent tubes, there was only the low green mound of an old sheep-fold, its drystone walls half-tumbled into the nettles, the grey limestone blocks crusted with yellow lichen and the green velvet of wild moss.

There was no railway.

There was no Duke Street.

Where the sixty red-brick terraces had huddled against the embankment, there was only the broad, gravelly bed of the high beck, its clear, peat-brown water running between the round grey stones with a soft, endless, laughing chatter:

*Ch-ch-ch-ch... babble... ch-ch.*

Robin stood still for ten minutes, her arms hanging by her sides, the cold sea-wind blowing through the thin wool of her cardigan.

She looked down at her hands.

They were clean.

The red Quink ink was gone. The coal-slack was gone. The white lead splinter was gone.

Her fingers were just fingers—red at the knuckles from the wind, with two bitten nails on the left hand and the small brown mole she had had since she was three years old.

She reached into her pocket.

Her fingers found the crab-apple.

The small, round, red apple from Monday, the fourteenth of October, nineteen seventy-four.

The skin was cold, hard, smelling of autumn frost and wild wood.

Robin lifted the apple to her mouth.

She bit through the red skin.

The flesh was white, crisp, extraordinarily sour, the sharp juice filling her mouth with the taste of wild rain, bitter tannin, and the clean peat of the high fells.

She chewed it slowly, feeling the juice run down her throat like cold well-water.

Behind her, on the slope of the scar, someone was walking.

The boots made no sound on the turf, but the dry seed-heads of the hardhead grasses gave a faint, whispering rustle as the skirt brushed through them: *swish... swish*.

Robin did not turn around immediately.

She finished the mouthful of apple.

She spat the three black pips into the palm of her hand, looked at them for a second as they lay against her skin like three shiny beetle-backs, and then dropped them into the wild turf between her boots.

Then she looked back.

Standing on the limestone outcrop five yards away, her hands tucked into the pockets of an old navy wool coat whose let-down hem showed three inches of darker blue, was a girl.

She was twelve years old.

Her hair was tied back with a piece of red parcel-string, her knees were bare and blue with the cold, and her face—the thin, sharp-chinned face that Robin had seen in the scullery shaving-mirror—was turned toward the wide reach of the bay.

The girl looked at Robin.

She did not ask for a pass. She did not ask for a dinner-ticket.

She did not say, *What are you doing out of your base, Mercer?*

She pulled her right hand out of her coat pocket.

In her palm lay a piece of store-bread, spread thick with pale beef-dripping that had been salted with coarse grey salt from the stone jar.

"Do you want half?" the girl asked.

Her voice had no echo in it.

It did not vibrate like the pump; it did not rattle like the boxwood ruler.

It was just a girl’s voice, small and clear in the middle of sixty square miles of wet heather and salt-grass.

Robin walked across the stones.

Her boots found the centers of the grey limestone slabs without slipping, her Rochdale heels clicking against the rock with a clean, sharp *tock* that was lost instantly in the crying of the three curlews that were wheeling over the low-water channel.

"Aye," Robin said.

She took the half-slice of bread.

The dripping was cold, greasy, tasting of salt, pepper, and the dark suet from the bottom of the tin.

She sat down on the flat stone beside the other girl, her knees pulled up inside the hem of her cardigan, her back against the drystone wall of the old sheep-fold.

Below them, the tide was beginning to turn in the outer gut.

A long, thin line of white foam, no wider than a surveyor’s chain, was creeping across the dark mud of the bay, bringing the cold salt-water back to the channels where the cockles were buried in the silt.

In the valley, where the school had been, there was only the wind.

It blew through the gorse; it blew through the rushes; it blew through the empty stone sheep-fold without leaving a mark on the wall.

Robin ate the bread.

The crust was hard, but she chewed it until it was soft in her cheek, watching the white line of the water come on across the sand.

"What time is it?" Robin asked after a while.

The other girl chewed her crust, her eyes fixed on the distant hills across the water, where the winter snow was beginning to settle on the high screes.

"It isn't any time, Robin," she said softly.

She brushed a crumb of store-bread from her knee with her flat palm.

"It’s just today. And nobody’s marked the book yet."
### Chapter 20: The Edge of the Water-Meadow

The silence on the high scar was not like the silence in the library; it was not made of carpets and closed doors. It was made of wind moving through three miles of dead bent-grass, so wide and cold that after twenty minutes Robin’s ears stopped straining for the bell.

The other girl—the twelve-year-old Claire—sat with her back against the tumbled limestone boulders of the sheep-fold, chewing her crust with slow, rhythmic movements of her jaw.

"Where do the sheep go?" Robin asked.

Her voice sounded strange in the vast open basin of the bay: small, thin, dry as a sparrow’s feather dropped onto wet slate.

"Down to the low saltings when the frost sharpens," Claire said. She did not point; she merely lifted her chin toward the grey reaches of the estuary, where the channels of the Kent and the Leven snaked through thirty square miles of wet silt like lead-coloured veins on the back of an old drover’s hand. "Old man Postlethwaite brings his dogs up the lane from Grange on Thursdays. But that was before they put the embankment across the gut. Before the iron rails came down from Carnforth."

Robin looked down at her own feet.

Her boots were still the Rochdale ankle-boots, the leather cracked across the toe-caps where the salt-mud of the canal had dried into pale grey rings. But the mud was falling away now in dry, brittle flakes, revealing the brown grease of the horse-hide beneath.

"Claire?"

"Aye."

"If we stay here... does Friday come?"

The other girl stopped chewing.

She held the remaining corner of her bread-and-dripping between her forefinger and thumb, her fingernail bitten down to the pink meat just like Robin’s, with the same tiny white half-moon showing at the base of the nail.

"Nothing comes here, Robin," she said.

Her voice had no fear in it, but it had a terrible, flat weight, like an iron sinker that had dropped forty fathoms into the dark water outside the lightship.

"That’s why they call it the scar," Claire continued, looking out across the sands. "A scar is where something got cut open and grew together thick, without any hair on it and without any sweat. My grand-dad said that in sixty-eight, when the cholera came up the canal on the potato-boats from Ireland, the parish overseer brought twelve girls from the workhouse up to this fold. He gave 'em each a wool blanket, a bag of oatmeal, and an iron pot, and he told 'em they weren't to come down the cart-track until the lime-wash on the churchyard wall was dry."

"Did they go back?" Robin asked.

Claire looked down at the short, brown turf between her clogs.

"Two of 'em did," she whispered. "My aunt Martha and the girl from the rope-walk. The rest of 'em... they just sat behind the stones until the frost got into their stays. In the spring, when the drovers came for the ewes, there wasn't anything left except the brass buttons off their shifts and twelve iron spoons lying in the nettles."

She reached out and laid her hand on Robin’s knee.

The touch was not like the touch of the teachers.

It was not cold; it was not hot.

It was the touch of her own hand when she woke up in the Duke Street bedroom with her fingers tucked beneath her armpit to keep them from the draft that came through the sash-weights.

"You think you’ve got away from 'em, don't you?" Claire said softly.

"I pulled the key out," Robin said, her fingers tightening around the pocket where the iron had been. "I turned it to the left. Mr. Gowan told me to."

"Mr. Gowan’s an old man whose lungs are full of boiler-dust," Claire said. Her voice had that sharp, irritable edge that Robin’s mother used when the gas-card wouldn't register in the slot. "Mr. Gowan thinks because he’s got forty keys on a ring, he knows where the doors go. He doesn't know anything except how to shovel slack and put salt on the steps so the masters don't tumble on their grease."

She stood up from the limestone rock.

She was small—barely four feet eight—her skirt hanging in stiff, greasy folds around her thin shins, her black stockings patched at both knees with rough wool from an old railway muffler.

She walked to the edge of the scar, where the turf broke off into a sixty-foot drop of grey limestone scree that sloped down toward the valley floor.

"Look down there, Robin," she called against the wind.

Robin rose and stood beside her.

The wind from the Irish Sea came up the cliff-face like a wall of cold salt-spray, whipping her hair across her eyes until she had to hold it back with her red-knuckled fingers.

At first, she saw only the valley.

The wide, yellow-green expanse of the water-meadows, intersected by the dark ditches and the lines of stunted willows that marked the old parish drains.

Then the light shifted.

The high, silver-grey clouds parted for a second, and a long, pale needle of winter sunlight struck the floor of the valley, two miles south of the estuary.

The grass flickered.

It was not grass.

Under the sunlight, the green turf became transparent, like a sheet of dirty tracing paper held over an architect’s blueprint.

Beneath the turf, thirty feet down in the limestone gravel, lay the school.

Not ruined; not dead.

It was living.

Robin could see the three CLASP blocks laid out in an irregular H-shape: the long, flat roofs covered with grey mineral-felt; the square, yellow-lit windows of the science laboratories; the high, corrugated asbestos ridge of the gymnasium; and the tarmac service yard where the two council dustbin-lorries were still idling their diesel engines in the drizzle.

And inside the rooms, visible through the roofs as if the building were a doll’s house made of thin glass, were the people.

In Room 14, Mr. Finch was still sitting by the radiator, his purple felt-tip pen hovering two inches above the register-book.

In the Art Block, the forty Greek masks were still sitting on the drying racks, their gold paint shining under the north skylights.

And down in the Humanities corridor, standing by the water-cooler with his disciplinary cards held against his waistcoat, was Mr. Teasdale.

He was looking up.

Through the three ceilings. Through twelve feet of gravel. Through the sixty-foot limestone face of the scar.

His spectacles caught the light, flashing twice with a cold, white, mechanical glare: *blink... blink*.

"They aren't in the past, Robin," Claire whispered, her small fingers digging into Robin’s cardigan sleeve until the seam strained. "And they aren't in yesterday. They’re down there, waiting for the four-o’clock bus. And if you aren't at the stop when the bell goes, they’ll write the letter to the magistrate."

"What letter?"

"The one with the red seal," Claire said. Her mouth twitched, her eyes wide with a terror that was fifty years old and as fresh as fresh-spilled blood. "The one that says the child Robin Mercer is beyond the control of her natural guardian, having no fixed abode and showing an habitual tendency to vagrancy and the destruction of public property. They don't put you in the coal-hole for that, Robin. They put you in the girls’ home at Kendal, where you wear an unbleached linen smock and scrub the chapel flagstones with sandstone grit until your knees turn to water."

Robin looked down through the earth.

She saw herself.

Not the Thursday Robin; not the neat Robin with the electric meter in her chest.

She saw the girl who was sitting in the Duke Street scullery right now, in the cold dusk of Friday afternoon, waiting for her mother’s boots to come up the back lane.

The girl was twelve years old.

She had her head on the oilcloth table, her arms wrapped around her satchel, her shoulders shaking with that dry, silent shudder that no one ever heard because the coal-trains on the viaduct were always louder than a child’s ribs.

"She’s there," Robin said.

"Aye," Claire whispered, leaning her forehead against Robin’s shoulder until Robin could feel the cold damp of the girl’s wool cap through her own sleeve. "She’s there. And she’s got no one to light the ring."

### Chapter 21: The Seam in the Wall

"How do I get back down?" Robin asked.

The white light of the high scar was changing.

The silver was draining out of the clouds, replaced by a dull, bruised purple that came up from the south-east—the heavy, smoke-laden belly of a snow-sky that had gathered over the industrial towns of Lancashire and was rolling north along the limestone shelf.

Claire Holland did not look at the sky.

She turned back toward the old sheep-fold.

The three stone walls—built without mortar, the flat slabs of limestone laid one upon another with that ancient, interlocking geometry that northern wallers call "the through-and-through"—stood three feet high against the bent-grass.

At the western corner, where two walls met at an obtuse angle, was a stone that did not match the rest.

It was not grey limestone.

It was a dressed block of dark red millstone grit, sixteen inches long and eight inches thick, its surface smoothed by an iron mason’s chisel that had left five parallel gouges across the face.

In the center of the red block was a single round hole, two inches wide, where an iron hinge-gudgeon had been set with molten lead in eighteen forty-four.

The lead was still there, white with oxide, but someone had driven an iron nail into the center of the soft metal.

A four-inch wire nail, bent into a shallow curve.

Robin walked to the corner.

Her shadow was long now, stretching twenty yards across the cropped turf toward the edge of the scree like a black finger pointing at the valley below.

"That’s the lug," Claire said, standing two paces behind her with her hands tucked inside her sleeves. "The one from the sluice. My grand-dad said that when they cleared the flax-mill to make the foundations for the new grammar-school wing, the contractor sold twelve loads of the dressed stone to the waller on the scar for two shillings a cart. He thought because he’d moved the rock two miles up the fell, it wouldn't know the way back to the water."

She reached out her thin, bare foot and tapped the red stone with the brass-nailed heel of her clog: *tock*.

"It knows, Robin," she said.

Robin knelt down in the frost-bitten grass.

The red millstone grit was cold, but it did not have the dry, mineral cold of the limestone boulders; it had that deep, wet, cellar-chill that stays inside stone that has lived next to running water for a hundred years.

She touched the bent nail.

A tiny, sharp shock went through her thumb-pad—not static, but the cold vibration of iron under tension, like a fence-wire that has been plucked by a sheep’s horn in the dark.

And from deep within the red stone—from twenty feet below the turf, where the roots of the gorse-bushes were clinging to the fissures in the rock—came a sound.

It was not the wind.

It was the water-pump.

*Thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug.*

The sound was faint, muffled by two miles of earth, but it was there: the three cast-iron heat exchangers of Caldbeck Comprehensive, beating their four-beat measure beneath the concrete floor of the boiler-house.

"If I go back," Robin said, looking up at the other girl, "Mr. Teasdale will have the police at the door."

"Aye," Claire said. "He will."

"And Kacey Vane will tell the whole third year that I took the big shears out of the needlework drawer."

"She will," Claire agreed, her pale face completely still in the gathering dusk. "She’ll tell 'em you ate coal-dust out of the bunker, and she’ll tell 'em your mother goes to the public-house with the dray-men from the brewery."

"Then why should I go back?" Robin cried out, her voice breaking into that high, furious sob that had stayed behind her teeth for three days. "Why shouldn't I stay here with you? We could gather the sheep-wool off the brambles. We could sleep in the dry corner behind the wall. Nobody could mark a red cross against us here!"

Claire looked down at her.

Her eyes were not the eyes of a twelve-year-old girl now; they were the eyes of the woman who had spent forty years washing the floor-cloths in Duke Street, who had watched her husband’s lungs go black with the iron-dust, who had sat by the grate with an empty purse on Saturday nights and counted the copper pennies to see if there was fourpence for the milk.

"Because there isn't any dripping here, Robin," she said softly.

She opened her hands.

Her palms were empty.

The bread was gone; the apple was gone; there was only the pale, dry skin of her hands, lined with the grey dirt of eighteen seventy-four.

"You can't eat the wind, girl," Claire said. "And you can't warm your shins at an old wall. If you stay here, you’ll just turn into a name that someone finds on a zinc label when they clean the drains out in thirty years. You’ll be like me: something that lives in an old coat behind the door, waiting for someone to come down the steps with a candle."

She bent down.

Her fingers closed around Robin’s right wrist.

The grip was surprisingly strong—not with the heavy, administrative weight of Mr. Teasdale, but with the desperate, wiry strength of a girl who had spent her childhood carrying two four-gallon wooden pails of wash-water from the canal to the scullery copper.

She pulled Robin toward the red stone.

"Take the nail," Claire commanded.

"What?"

"The nail in the lead," she said, her voice rising above the roar of the sea-gale that was now tearing across the ridge from the north-west. "Take it with both hands. Pull it out of the gudgeon. It’s what’s holding the seam open, Robin! If you don't take it before the five-o’clock siren goes down at the works, the stone’ll set into the lime, and you’ll have to wait fifty years for the next girl to drop her dinner-tin in the sump!"

Robin reached out.

Her hands were shaking, her teeth chattering with a cold that went through her skin into the marrow of her shin-bones.

She took the bent four-inch wire nail in both hands.

The iron was rusted, rough as a rasp, with two small curls of white lead still adhering to the shank.

She braced her boots against the limestone scree.

"Pull, Robin!" Claire shouted into her ear. "Pull for Duke Street! Pull for the tea in the pot!"

Robin pulled.

The iron resisted for a second, the lead in the hole screaming with a high, wet, tearing groan: *scrrr-ee-ch*.

Then it gave.

The nail came out of the stone with a dull, heavy *plop* that sounded like an iron bar being pulled from deep mud.

The scar vanished.

The white sky went out like a snuffed tallow-dip.

The sixty miles of salt-meadow and bay dissolved into a roaring vortex of cold, black water, wet coal-slack, and the stench of burning engine-oil.

Robin fell.

She did not fall onto turf; she fell through sixty feet of air that smelled of boiled cabbage, carbolic disinfectant, and old geography exercise books, her shins striking an iron ladder with a ringing, bone-jarring *CLANG* that knocked the breath from her lungs in a single, salt-tasting gasp.

### Chapter 22: The Floor of the Sump

She was at the bottom of the boiler-house pit.

Not in the room with the milk-crate; she was twelve feet lower, in the square, concrete drainage-sump that sat beneath the primary circulation pumps.

The sump was three feet deep in oily, warm water—the black, greasy bilge that leaked from the stuffing-boxes of the three calorifiers, carrying a thick scum of fuel-oil, boiler-scale, and floating matchsticks.

Robin sat in the muck, her elbows braced against the rusted bottom rungs of the iron ladder.

Above her head, through the square opening in the floor-plates, the yellow sodium light of the modern school was burning.

It was Friday afternoon.

She looked at her Casio watch.

The acrylic face was cracked, but the liquid crystal display was alive, the small grey segments pulsing with steady, mechanical precision:

*15:42.*

*FRI.*

Three-forty-two on Friday, the eighteenth of October.

The school day was over.

Above her head, through twelve inches of reinforced concrete, the silence of Caldbeck Comprehensive was that heavy, hollow, echoing quiet that comes to a building of nine hundred children ten minutes after the final buses have pulled away from the gates.

The lockers were shut. The classroom doors were morticed. The cleaners were in the south block, the distant *hiss-swish* of their industrial floor-buffers vibrating through the ceiling-hangers like a swarm of brass beetles.

Robin climbed the ladder.

Her boots were heavy with water; each step up the cold iron rungs produced a wet, squelching *slosh* as the bilge-water poured through the lace-holes of her Rochdale boots.

She pushed her head through the hatch-cover.

The boiler room was empty.

The three heat exchangers were working, their great cast-iron bellies humming with that steady *thrum-chug... thrum-chug* that she had known since September.

The single sixty-watt bulb was burning above the pump.

On the milk-crate, where the neat girl had sat with the red exercise book, lay an object.

It was Robin’s satchel.

The blue nylon was dry now, though it had a dark, greasy ring around the bottom from the coal-slack. The strap had been mended—not with red parcel-string, but with a neat, double-row of black cobbler’s stitches that had been pulled tight through the nylon with an iron awl.

Robin climbed out onto the concrete floor.

Her knees were bruised, the skin on her shins scraped raw from the iron rungs, but the pain was clean; it was her own pain, belonging to an eight-stone twelve-year-old girl who had tripped in the coal-cellar.

She walked to the millstone grit wall.

Door 17 was there.

It was shut.

The gray oak was dry, hard, unyielding.

The white lead number *17* was cracking again, the paint scaling off in little triangular flakes that revealed the dark, ancient timber beneath.

And through the center of the oak—through the square iron plate where the ring had hung—someone had driven a three-inch steel wedge.

A quarryman’s splitting-wedge, four pounds of solid cast-steel, driven into the rebate between the door and the ashlar with a sixteen-pound sledgehammer until the head of the wedge was flush with the wood.

The door could not open.

It could not open an eighth of an inch, not for sixty keys, not for an iron crowbar, not for the high-water tide of a hundred winters.

Robin touched the cold steel of the wedge.

There was no vibration.

No sound of the clarinet. No sound of the waterwheel. No sound of the workhouse children chanting their lesson from the slate blackboard.

There was only the quiet, domestic ticking of the secondary steam-pipes: *clink... clink... clink*.

Robin turned around.

Standing at the top of the sixteen concrete steps, his brown dust-coat unbuttoned to reveal his blue flannel working-shirt, was Mr. Gowan.

He was holding a galvanized bucket full of red salt-grit in his right hand, a small zinc shovel tucked beneath his left arm.

He looked down into the pit.

He saw Robin standing by the primary pump, her hair plastered to her skull by the sump-water, her skirt dripping black bilge onto the concrete.

He did not drop the shovel. He did not blow an iron whistle.

He set the bucket down on the top tread with a dull *clatter*.

"You’re out of hours, Mercer," he said.

His voice was his own voice—the dry, gravelly bark of an old navy stoker who had spent thirty years yelling through voice-pipes against the noise of triple-expansion engines.

"Yes, Mr. Gowan," Robin said.

"The Headmaster’s car went out through the south gate ten minutes ago," he said, taking his short clay pipe from his pocket and tapping the bowl against the iron handrail. "And the police car went with him."

Robin took two steps toward the stairs.

"Did they... did they take my mother?"

Mr. Gowan looked at the bowl of his pipe.

"Your mother was at the gate at three o'clock," he said. "With her apron in a paper carrier-bag. She stood by the pillar until Mr. Teasdale came out with his leather briefcase."

He paused, sticking the pipe-stem between his yellow teeth.

"What did she say?" Robin whispered.

"She told him that if he didn't take the report off your name, she’d go to the borough surveyor and show him the crack in the foundation wall under the domestic science block," Mr. Gowan said.

A faint, dry twitch moved the corner of his grey mustache.

"She told him she had two uncles who helped dig the cellars in sixty-four, and she knew which three pillars were sitting on the old mill-race without any concrete footings under 'em."

Robin stared up at him.

"She said that?"

"She did," the caretaker said. "She was quite loud about it, Mercer. Mrs. Vane was sitting in her motor-car by the library, and she had to wind the window up because your mother’s language wasn't suitable for a member of the Education Committee."

He picked up his bucket of red grit.

"Go wash your face at the tap in the yard," he said, turning his back on the pit. "And don't let me catch you using the milk-crate for an ottoman again. It belongs to the primary school across the lane, and they’re two short on the crate-tally already."

### Chapter 23: The Bridge on the Canal

Robin came out through the pea-green door into the service yard.

The rain had stopped, but the twilight had that deep, wet blue of a northern October, the sky over the fells stained with the orange glare of the sodium lights from the motorway junction five miles to the east.

She did not run.

She walked across the gravel, her Rochdale boots making their heavy, rhythmic crunch on the stones: *scritch... scritch... scritch*.

She passed the CDT block.

She passed the bicycle sheds.

At the gap in the chain-link fence by the cricket pavilion, someone was waiting.

It was Kacey Vane.

Kacey was not wearing her navy coat with the fur hood; she was wearing her maroon school blazer, her arms crossed over her chest, her hands tucked inside her sleeves to keep them from the freezing mist that was rising from the drainage ditch.

She had her satchel on the ground between her shoes.

Robin stopped five feet away.

The two girls looked at each other in the blue dusk.

Across Kacey’s right wrist was a clean, white strip of adhesive tape—ordinary chemist’s tape, with no lint and no blood showing through the cotton weave.

"My dad’s furious with you," Kacey said.

Her voice was small, thin, lacking the swagger that it had carried in the corridor outside Room 14. It sounded like the voice of a girl who had spent forty minutes sitting in the back of an expensive motor-car while her parents shouted at each other across the gear-lever.

"I know," Robin said.

"He said your family was scum," Kacey continued, her eyes dropping to the mud around Robin’s boots. "He said you lived on parish relief and that your house ought to have been knocked down when the gasworks closed."

"It’s not parish relief," Robin said. "My mum works the night-calender at St. Jude’s."

Kacey did not answer that.

She reached her toe out and kicked a clump of dead gorse with the side of her patent shoe.

The patent was ruined; the grey mud from the service yard had crusted over the black shine, and the leather near the welt was cracked where she had stepped into a puddle by the cricket pavilion.

"I didn't break the mask, Robin," Kacey whispered.

The words came out in a hurry, tumbling over each other like water through a burst lock.

"I know you didn't," Robin said.

"Tanya said you did," Kacey said, her chin trembling slightly in the cold air. "Tanya told Mr. Teasdale she saw you coming out of the room with the ink on your cuffs. But I was there at lunch-time... I went in to get my pearls off the rack... and the mask was already on the floor. It was split right through the nose, Robin. Like someone had taken an axe to it."

She looked up at Robin then.

Her eyes were wide, brown, wet, but they did not have the malice of Thursday morning.

They had that hollow, frightened look that Robin had seen in her own mirror—the look of someone who had realized that the world was larger, older, and far more careless than the rules in the school planner.

"There was an old woman in the room, Robin," Kacey whispered, her voice dropping so low that Robin had to take a step closer to hear her against the distant roar of the bypass.

"What old woman?"

"She had a blue tabard on," Kacey said. "Like Mrs. Noakes, but it wasn't Mrs. Noakes. Her eyes were... they were like two pieces of grey slate with no holes in 'em. She was standing by the clay-bins, Robin. And she had an iron key in her hand. A big, long one, like they use for the sluice-gates."

Robin reached into her satchel.

Her fingers touched the cedar pencil-box.

The box was empty now; the pink corridor-pass had gone, the brass identity disc had gone. There was only the smell of old cedar-wood and the faint, dry dust of dried lead-pencil shavings.

"She’s gone, Kacey," Robin said.

"Where?"

"Back into the wall," Robin said. "Where she belongs."

Kacey looked at her for a long moment.

A lorry went past on the link-road, its heavy diesel engine throwing a long, roaring wave of sound across the playing fields, its white headlights sweeping through the mist to illuminate the two girls standing by the broken fence.

"You’ve got coal-dust in your hair, Skimmer," Kacey said softly.

It was the old name, but it had no poison in it now; it sounded like an old coat that had been worn so many times that the elbows had gone soft.

"Aye," Robin said. "The chute’s dirty."

Kacey picked up her satchel by the handle.

"I’m going to Grange on Monday," she said. "My mother’s transferring me to the convent school behind the station. She said she couldn't have me mixing with the children from the canal-side anymore."

She turned toward the gravel path that led to the car park.

She took three steps, her ruined patent shoes crunching in the dirt, and then she stopped.

She did not look back over her shoulder.

"Robin?"

"Aye."

"Don't go down those steps again," Kacey said.

"I won't," Robin said. "The door’s wedged."

Kacey nodded once, a quick, jerky drop of her head, and then she was gone into the blue fog of the service path, her maroon blazer disappearing into the shadow of the gymnasium wall like a dark leaf drifting down an empty gutter.

### Chapter 24: The Kettle on the Trivet

Duke Street was dark when Robin reached the railway bridge.

The council had not replaced the gas-lamp outside number twelve, and the street was illuminated only by the faint, yellow rectangles of the parlor windows, where the lace curtains showed the blue flicker of the television sets through the glass.

The smell was the same as it had always been.

Cold slack; boiled potatoes; the greasy steam from the fish-and-chip van at the corner of the lane; and the sharp, iron tang of the canal-water that rose from the culvert whenever the wind was from the south.

At number 4, the scullery light was on.

Through the net curtain, Robin could see her mother standing at the stove.

She was not wearing her auxiliary’s uniform; she was wearing her old grey cardigan with the mismatched bone buttons, her hair pinned back with two aluminum clips that had lost their teeth.

Robin walked up the two stone steps.

The sandstone was wet, greasy with the river-mist, but her boots did not slip; her heels found the center of the hollow where three generations of Mercer boots had worn the stone down three-eighths of an inch toward the scraper.

She lifted the brass latch.

The door went *clack*.

The kitchen was warm.

The front gas-ring was on, the small crown of blue points flaring softly beneath the enameled kettle: *pluff... pluff... pluff*.

On the oilcloth table sat the blue plate, with three thick slices of store-bread spread with the pale beef-dripping, and two earthenware mugs with the brown willow-pattern on the handles.

Her mother did not look around immediately.

She had an iron spoon in her hand, stirring two spoons of condensed milk into the bottom of one of the mugs with a steady, circular motion that went *ting... ting... ting* against the glaze.

"You’re late," her mother said.

Her voice was tired—terribly, anciently tired—carrying that dry, flat northern rhythm that sounded like a shovel-blade scraping across a gravel walk.

"I had to walk by the canal," Robin said.

She hung her navy parka on the peg behind the door.

The torn sleeve hung straight down, the clean black cobbler’s stitches holding the seam together so tightly that the grey polyester wadding couldn't show an eighth of an inch.

She took off her Rochdale boots and set them under the copper boiler, right where the cold tap dripped its two drops a minute into the lead trough: *plink... plink*.

"Your socks are wet," her mother said, setting the spoon on the oilcloth.

"I went through the sump," Robin said.

Her mother turned then.

Her face was long, pale, the skin around her eyes yellowed by thirty years of hospital disinfectant, but her mouth was straight—not bitter, not frightened, but set with that stubborn, immovable endurance of people who have lived behind the railway embankment for ninety years and never missed a rent-day.

She looked at Robin’s hands.

Robin held them out.

They were clean.

The red Quink ink had gone; the white lead flake had gone; the skin was raw and red from the north wind, but it was whole.

Her mother took Robin’s right hand in her own.

Her thumb—broad, flat-tipped, calloused by the laundry-rollers—rubbed the ball of Robin’s thumb, right where the number *17* had left its mark on Tuesday.

There was no mark.

There was only the ordinary skin of an eight-stone twelve-year-old girl, with the small brown mole near the base of the thumb that her mother had kissed when Robin was three years old and had fallen off the coal-cart into the dock-mud.

"He signed the paper, Robin," her mother said softly.

"Mr. Teasdale?"

"Aye," her mother said. "He had his golden pen out of his waistcoat. He crossed the *S* off the register, and he put the black mark down for Friday morning."

She released Robin’s hand.

She turned to the stove, took the enameled kettle by its wire handle, and poured the boiling water into the two mugs.

The steam came up, smelling of sweet condensed milk, roasted chicory, and the iron of the reservoir mains.

"He said you were to have your desk back on Monday," her mother continued, setting the kettle back on the iron trivet with a hollow *thump*. "In the second row by the radiator. He said they’d found the shears in the scrap-bin behind the metalwork shop, where one of the fifth-year boys had dropped 'em after the bell."

Robin sat down at the table.

She pulled the Windsor chair in until her knees touched the wooden drawer where the insurance books lived.

She picked up a slice of the bread-and-dripping.

The grease was cold, solid, tasting of salt, coarse pepper, and the dark suet from the bottom of the tin.

"Mum?"

"Aye."

"What did you do with the key?"

Her mother sat down opposite her, her two hands wrapped around her earthenware mug to take the heat into her swollen knuckles.

She looked at the small window that looked out onto the yard, where the darkness was pressing against the glass like a cold, wet fleece.

"What key’s that, then?" her mother asked.

Her voice had no question in it; it had that calm, flat innocence that women in Duke Street used when the school-board officer asked who owned the grey whippet that lived in the coal-hole.

Robin looked at her mother’s eyes.

They were clear, pale blue—the color of the sky over the high scar when the mare’s-tail clouds were running before the gale.

"The one from the tea-caddy," Robin whispered.

Her mother took a sip of her tea, the hot liquid making a small, comfortable *slurp* in the quiet kitchen.

"There wasn't never a key in the caddy, Robin," she said, setting the mug back on the oilcloth. "Only two half-crowns from the coronation year and your father’s medal from the canal-service."

She pushed the blue plate an inch closer to Robin’s elbow.

"Eat your crust, girl," her mother said. "The five-o’clock siren’s gone, and the milk-van comes early on Saturdays."

Robin took a bite of the bread.

The crust was hard, but she chewed it slowly, feeling the grease warm against her tongue, listening to the small, dry noises of the house in the autumn night:

The clock ticking above the gas-meter; the water dripping in the lead trough; and four miles away, across the marsh and the limestone shelf, the long, low whistle of the night-freight heading north toward the high fells, its iron wheels ringing on the cold steel rails as it carried the valley into tomorrow.
### Chapter 25: The Saturday Milk

The milk-float had three wheels and an electric motor that sounded like a wet grindstone turning against an axe-head.

It came down Duke Street at twenty past six on Saturday morning, its yellow headlamps cutting two fuzzy, butter-coloured tunnels through the river-mist, the glass pint-bottles in their zinc crates chattering together with a dull, hollow *clink-clonk... clink-clonk*.

Robin sat on the bottom step of the stairs in the dark, her knees pulled tight to her chin, her hands tucked inside the sleeves of her flannel nightdress.

Through the frosted glass of the fanlight, she watched the shadow of the milkman—old Mr. Winstanley, who had a wooden leg from the crossing at the Rhine in forty-five—move past the brick piers.

He set two bottles on the doorstep of number 4 with a sharp, heavy *thud*.

Then he stopped.

His iron ferrule scraped against the flagstone: *tock*.

He did not walk on to number 6.

He stood on the pavement for nearly two minutes, his breath puffing through his woollen balaclava in short, grey clouds that Robin could see through the letter-slot.

He reached down.

The brass flap of the letter-box gave its long, oiled creak: *errrk*.

Something fell through the aperture and landed on the coconut matting with a soft, papery *slap*.

Then the iron ferrule turned: *tock... tock... tock*. The electric float whined, its rubber tyres making a wet, tearing hiss on the greasy cobbles, and the milkman was gone toward the railway arches.

Robin did not move for five minutes.

Above her, in the front bedroom, her mother’s bed-springs gave their regular, rhythmic groan as she turned in her sleep. Her mother was sleeping the heavy, dead sleep of a woman who had worked thirty-two hours in three days, whose heels were so swollen with the laundry-water that she had to rub them with mutton-tallow before she could draw her stockings on.

Robin crept down the remaining three treads.

The coconut matting was cold and prickled the soles of her bare feet like dry gorse.

She picked up the object.

It was not a circular for the church jumble-sale. It was not the blue coal-voucher from the union.

It was an envelope of heavy buff paper, nine inches by four, stamped with the purple rubber crest of the borough education department:

*WESTMORLAND COUNTY COUNCIL*  
*DIVISIONAL EXECUTIVE NO. 3 (CALDBECK)*  
*PUPILS’ ATTENDANCE AND SPECIAL EXAMINATIONS BRANCH*

The address was typed on an old manual machine with an uneven *e* that sat slightly above the line:

*To the Parent or Guardian of Mercer, Robin (8C)*  
*4 Duke Street, Caldbeck*  
*BY HAND: URGENT*

Robin took the envelope to the scullery window.

The morning light was coming over the roofs of the coal-sidings now—not yellow, but that thin, bruised lilac of autumn frost that made the chimney-pots look like rows of broken teeth along the ridge.

She slid her thumbnail under the gummed flap.

The paper inside was not a letter.

It was an attendance card, printed on thick pink pasteboard with ruled red columns:

*CALDBECK COMPREHENSIVE SCHOOL*  
*SATURDAY MORNING REMEDIAL AND DISCIPLINARY SESSION*  
*Date: Saturday, 19th October*  
*Time of Assembly: 09:00 Hours*  
*Room Assigned: Boiler Annex (Old Foundations)*  
*Presiding Officer: M. Teasdale (Deputy Headmaster)*

And below the columns, in the section reserved for *SPECIAL INSTRUCTIONS FOR DETENTION*:

*The pupil Mercer, R., having been restored to the register on the undertaking of the parent, is required to attend the annex at nine o’clock precisely for the purpose of assisting the caretaker with the clearance of the fuel-chutes prior to the inspection of the district surveyor. Failure to attend will result in the immediate forfeiture of the pupil’s desk and the cancellation of the county travel-grant.*

*Signed: M. Teasdale.*

Robin turned the card over.

Her heart was thumping against her collarbones like a bird caught in a chimney-flue.

On the back was a single handwritten line, written in purple felt-tip ink:

*Henderson is already here. He has brought his box.*

Robin dropped the pink card onto the scullery table.

Saturday.

There were no Saturday detentions at Caldbeck Comprehensive.

There had not been a Saturday session since nineteen seventy-one, when the teachers’ union had passed the resolution against weekend duty at the county conference in Kendal. The gates were always chained at four-thirty on Friday by Mr. Gowan; the padlocks were three inches thick, covered with grease-caps to keep the boys from jamming matchsticks into the key-ways.

Robin looked at the copper boiler.

Beneath the wooden lid, where her Rochdale boots were sitting on the lead trough, was a fresh smudge of grey coal-slack.

Not dry dust.

Wet slack.

A small, dark heap of black sludge, the size of a hen’s egg, had dropped from the heel of her right boot during the night.

It was still sweating.

Tiny drops of black, oily water were running down the lead into the waste-pipe with that slow, rhythmic sound: *plink... plink... plink*.

And embedded in the top of the sludge, like a coin pressed into wet cement, was a small brass button.

Not a modern school button.

An old grammar-school button, stamped with the three red roses of Lancaster on a chevron between three silver crescents.

Leo Henderson’s button.

The one that had been torn from his blazer when he was dragged down the corridor on Thursday morning.

### Chapter 26: The Smoke from the Chimney

The town of Caldbeck on a Saturday morning was half-asleep and half-dead.

The market-square was empty except for two farmers from the high mosses who were setting up their timber trestles for the egg-trade, their horse-boxes smelling of sour straw and wet manure. Along the canal, the iron cranes on the timber-wharves were stationary, their jibs pointing into the grey fog like the bones of dead herons.

Robin walked with her head down, her hands deep in the pockets of her navy parka.

She had left two slices of bread on the plate for her mother, covered with the clean tea-towel, and she had drawn the kettle three inches to the side of the gas-ring so the water wouldn't boil dry if her mother didn't wake until ten.

She took the low path behind the gasworks.

The brick wall of the retort-house was warm to the touch, the lime-mortar smelling of ammonia and dead coal-gas, but as she reached the railway bridge where the track branched off toward the chemical works, the air went cold again.

She stopped at the bottom of the embankment.

A quarter of a mile away, on the crest of the limestone ridge, sat the school.

The three CLASP blocks were grey, silent, their long rows of hopper-windows blank as unsilvered mirrors.

The main gates were chained.

Two six-foot lengths of heavy ship’s cable had been passed through the ornamental wrought iron, fastened with an old brass mooring-padlock that Mr. Gowan had greased with yellow tallow.

But from the short, square brick chimney of the boiler-house, smoke was rising.

It was not the thin blue haze of the banked fires.

It was a thick, greasy, rolling column of yellow-black coal-smoke, so heavy that it did not rise into the sky; it rolled down the slate roof of the gymnasium in a sluggish, undulating wave, dropping across the playing fields like a wet woollen blanket.

It smelled of sulfur.

It smelled of the four-foot seam—the dirty, pyritic coal that they had brought up from the drowned levels at the pit-head in sixty-two, the coal that the corporation had banned because it ate the mortar out of the chimney-pots within three winters.

Robin crawled through the gorse by the cricket pavilion.

Her hands were stinging from the nettles, but she did not feel the smart; her eyes were fixed on the second floor of the humanities block.

There, behind the plate glass of Room 14, something was moving.

It was not a shadow.

It was a girl.

She was wearing a maroon school blazer, two sizes too large, with the sleeves rolled back twice at the wrists to show the dirty white cotton lining.

She was walking between the rows of empty desks.

Every third step, she stopped, bent down, and picked something up from the floorboards, dropping it into a tin pail that she carried in her left hand.

*Clink.*

The sound came across the four hundred yards of frozen turf, clear through the fog as if Robin were standing in the doorway of the classroom.

*Clink.*

Then the girl stopped at the window.

She pressed her face against the glass.

Even at four hundred yards, Robin could see the pale, triangular shape of the chin, the greasy strands of mousy hair hanging across the forehead, and the two bright, feverish spots of red on the cheekbones.

The girl raised her right hand.

She did not wave.

She pressed her flat palm against the pane, spreading her fingers wide.

In her palm lay an iron nail.

Four inches long, bent into a shallow curve, its rusted head catching the pale morning light with a cold, orange glint.

The girl looked directly at the gorse bushes where Robin was crouching.

Her mouth opened.

She did not scream.

She formed three words with her lips, speaking them against the glass with that slow, deliberate articulation that deaf children use:

*YOU LEFT ME.*

Then she stepped back into the gloom of Room 14, and the window went blank.

### Chapter 27: The Sunday Shift

Robin did not go through the gates.

She did not use the sixteen concrete steps.

She stayed in the ditch behind the cricket pavilion until the five-o’clock siren went down at the chemical works, watching the smoke pour from the short chimney until the playing fields were covered in a foot of yellow sulfur-fog that burned the back of her throat like mustard-grease.

At dusk, the smoke stopped.

The chimney went dead.

The smell of the sulfur faded into the cold, damp stench of the salt-marsh, and the crows that had spent the afternoon sitting on the rugby posts flew off toward the high woods behind the hall.

Robin walked back to Duke Street.

Her mother was sitting at the table, her uniform coat unbuttoned, her swollen feet resting in an enameled basin of warm water and mustard-flour.

"Where have you been, Robin?" her mother asked.

Her voice had no anger in it; it was just empty, like an old earthenware jug that had been washed three times and left to dry on the scullery ledge.

"I walked to the canal," Robin said.

"They came," her mother said, looking at the grey water in the basin.

Robin stopped by the scullery door.

"Who came?"

"The young man with the briefcase," her mother whispered. "Mr. Teasdale’s clerk. The one who has the little motor-car with the yellow wheels."

She reached her hand to the mantelshelf, took down a long, white card, and laid it on the oilcloth between the tea-tin and the salt-cellar.

"He said you weren't at the annex this morning, Robin."

Robin did not touch the card.

"The school’s shut on Saturdays, Mum. You know it’s shut. The union doesn't let 'em teach on Saturdays."

"He said the school was open," her mother said, and for the first time her voice trembled with that terrible, ancient misery that Robin had heard in the water-meadow. "He said the caretaker had seven boys down in the coal-chutes, shifting the wet slack before the inspector comes on Monday. He said Leo Henderson was there. He said Leo’s father was there with his dray-cart."

She looked up at Robin then, her eyes small, watery, red-rimmed from the steam.

"He said that if you don't attend the morning muster at eight o’clock on Monday, the county will execute the warrant. He said your uncle Billy’s house was cleared under a Section Nine in seventy-six, and the law hasn't changed by a comma since the Queen was crowned."

"It’s not Billy’s house," Robin cried out. "It’s our house! You pay the rent-man ninety pence every Monday morning!"

"We pay eighty-four," her mother said, her chin sinking toward her breast. "The sixpence was for the wash-house, and they took the roof off the wash-house three years ago to make the yard wider for the refuse-vans."

She lifted one foot out of the basin, the yellow skin dripping mustard-water onto the linoleum with a heavy *plop*.

"You’ll have to go, Robin. You’ll have to put your clean collar on, take your books, and stand by the radiator until the master gives you your card. If they take the name off the book, we’ll have to go to the workhouse ward at Kendal. They won't let a woman with a bad knee stay in a council terrace if there isn't a child attending the district school. It’s on the lease, Robin. It’s in the small print at the bottom of the blue paper."

Robin went up the stairs.

She did not light the candle in her bedroom.

She lay on her horsehair mattress with her clothes on, her navy parka pulled over her chest like a blanket, listening to the canal-trains.

The house was colder than it had ever been.

The cold didn't come through the window; it came through the floorboards from the cellar below.

A smell of old coal-oil; damp lime; and the sweet, grassy, rotting stench of wet hemp that had been soaked in forty fathoms of black ditch-water.

And beneath the bed, right under the floorboards where her head lay, someone was tapping.

Not with a knuckle.

With an iron nail.

*Tap.*  
*Tap.*  
*Tap.*

Three beats to the second. Regular as the watch-wheel in the boy’s eye.

Then a girl’s voice, faint as the whistling of a draft through an empty keyhole:

"Tomorrow is Monday, Robin. And Monday is the longest day in the week."

### Chapter 28: The Gate at Eight

On Monday morning, the frost was so hard that the stones of Duke Street were covered with a thick, white crust of salt-rime that crackled under Robin’s boots like eggshells: *scritch... scritch... scritch*.

She had her clean collar on.

Her mother had ironed it at five in the morning with the heavy flat-iron from the stove, pressing the linen until the points were stiff as cardboard and smelled of scorching starch.

Her satchel was across her chest, the strap repaired with the black cobbler’s stitches, her red exercise book tucked inside the main pocket beside her two sharpened HB pencils.

She walked up the hill with fifty other children.

None of them were talking.

The Monday morning silence was different from the rest of the week; it had that sullen, dragging, resentful weight of boys whose boots were still damp from Sunday’s rain, whose mothers had given them sixpence for the dinner-ticket out of the child-benefit book.

Leo Henderson was walking ten yards ahead of her.

He was wearing his maroon blazer, but the right sleeve was missing three inches of seam at the elbow, showing his white shirt-sleeve beneath like an old bandage.

He did not have his clarinet case.

He was carrying an old wooden box by an iron rope-handle—the sort of box they kept the carriage-bolts in at the railway workshops, heavy, grey, banded with hoop-iron that had rusted into orange scales.

Robin caught up with him at the bicycle sheds.

"Leo," she whispered.

The boy did not stop.

He did not turn his head.

His neck was stiff, held rigid by the high, starched collar of his shirt, his boots coming down on the tarmac with that heavy, mechanical *flump... flump* that she had heard in the corridor behind Door 17.

"Leo," she repeated, reaching her hand out to touch the wool of his torn elbow. "Were you there on Saturday?"

Leo Henderson stopped.

He did not look at her face.

He looked down at his own wooden box.

His eyes were not watch-wheels anymore, but they were dull—flat and grey as two lead sixpences that had been smoothed down by thirty years in a beggar’s pocket.

"The master had the book," he said.

His voice was thin, high, whistling through the gap where his left canine had been knocked out.

"He called the muster at nine," Leo said. "We had to shift forty tons of the small coal from the lower chute to the bunker under the gym. My hands are full of splinters, Robin. Look."

He held up his right hand.

The skin was black with coal-grit, the pores filled with grey dust that no soap could ever reach, and across the palm were twelve deep, blue furrows where the rough iron hoops of the box had cut into the meat.

"He said you were absent, Robin," Leo whispered.

"I wasn't absent," she said. "The school was shut!"

"It wasn't shut for the ones who were on the card," Leo said.

He took three steps toward the boys’ entrance by the gymnasium, his wooden box swinging against his knee with a dull, hollow *clonk*.

"Mr. Teasdale was in his frock-coat," he said over his shoulder. "And he had the big iron scissors on his desk. He said that if you didn't answer to the third call this morning, he was going to cut your name out of the cloth."

"What cloth?" Robin asked, her throat going cold.

Leo stopped at the stone door-lintel.

Above his head, carved into the lintel in nineteen sixty-four, was the motto of the academy:

*WORK AND OBEY*

He pointed his blackened thumb toward the central hall.

"The cloth with the names on," he said. "The one that hangs behind the organ. Where they put the ones who died in the fever."

### Chapter 29: The Assembly of the Unforgiven

The hall smelled of damp gym-mats, wet plimsolls, and the sour vinegar they used to clean the linoleum after the morning milk had leaked from the crates.

Nine hundred children were sitting on the floorboards, their legs crossed, their satchels placed between their knees like small blue sandbags.

On the stage stood the staff.

Sixty-two teachers in their black academic gowns, the nylon fabric shining under the four-foot fluorescent tubes with that greasy, greenish sheen of cheap institutional cloth.

In the center stood Mr. Teasdale.

He was not wearing his frock-coat today; he was wearing his gray tweed jacket and his gray nylon trousers, his spectacles sitting on the bridge of his nose like two small glass windows.

In his right hand he held the county register.

The thick, black-leather book, three inches deep, with the brass corners and the two iron clasps that were fastened with a key.

Robin sat in the third row from the back, between Tanya Siddle and an eight-year-old girl from the primary annex whose nose was running with yellow mucus.

Tanya did not look at her.

She had her hands in her lap, her fingernails picked raw, her shoulders hunched forward as if she were waiting for someone to drop an iron weight between her shoulder-blades.

Across her forehead was a fresh, white scratch, three inches long, that ran from the hairline down to the bridge of her nose—a clean, straight furrow that looked as if it had been drawn with the point of a geometry compass.

The assembly did not begin with a hymn.

There was no piano.

Mr. Finch was sitting on the wooden bench behind the podium, his purple felt-tip pen held between his knuckles, his register-board resting across his knees.

Mr. Teasdale took one step forward to the microphone.

The public-address system gave a high, piercing electronic shriek that made every child in the hall draw their shoulders up: *screeee-ch*.

"Silence," Mr. Teasdale said.

His voice did not need the microphone; it had that heavy, flat municipal authority that carried through sixty feet of timber and plasterboard without rising half a tone.

"I have received the report of the district surveyor," he said.

He did not look at the children; he looked at the brass clock at the back of the hall, whose long black minute-hand was pointing to twenty past nine.

"The survey has confirmed that the structural movement in the south foundations has exceeded the statutory tolerance," he continued. "The subsidence beneath the Humanities block has compromised the secondary drainage channels, and the presence of unrecorded voids in the lower strata represents an unacceptable hazard to the school population."

He paused.

He took his spectacles off, wiped the lenses with the corner of his handkerchief, and put them back on his nose.

"The Board of Governors has accordingly determined that the south wing will be decommissioned with immediate effect. The classes assigned to Room 14 will report to the temporary huts on the lower playing fields until the end of the current term."

A whisper went through the nine hundred children—a soft, rustling sound like dead leaves being blown across asphalt.

"Silence," Mr. Teasdale said again.

He opened the black leather register.

The heavy, gilt-edged pages turned with a sound like an iron shovel striking wet sand: *clack-shuck*.

"Before the closure is executed," he said, "there is a matter of administrative rectification."

He looked down from the stage.

His eyes did not search the rows.

They traveled in a straight, unswerving line from the center aisle to the third row from the back.

To Robin Mercer.

"Robin Mercer," he called.

His voice was clear.

"Stand up."

Robin did not stand up immediately.

Her knees were locked against the floorboards; the cold from the timber seemed to have risen into her hip-joints, freezing her tendons into solid lead.

Beside her, Tanya Siddle pulled her skirt away with a quick, vicious jerk, as if Robin’s woolen hem were smeared with night-soil.

"Stand up, Mercer," Mr. Teasdale repeated.

Robin stood.

She was the only person standing in the hall of nine hundred children.

She felt the eyes—eighteen hundred eyes swiveling in their sockets with that slow, synchronized, mechanical click that she had heard in the cloakroom.

"Come to the stage," the deputy headmaster said.

Robin walked.

The aisle was four feet wide, laid with brown linoleum that had been worn down to the canvas backing by forty years of rubber shoes.

Each step she took produced a loud, echoing *clack* against the timber: *one... two... three... forty*.

She reached the wooden steps that led up to the stage.

The six steps were narrow, painted with black deck-paint, the nosings protected by strips of serrated brass that had been polished by thirty generations of prefects’ heels.

She went up.

She stood three feet from Mr. Teasdale’s desk.

The deputy head looked down at her.

He did not look angry. He looked tired—exhausted by thirty years of dealing with broken windows, stolen milk-tickets, and girls whose mothers had had their names in the police-gazette.

"You were seen on the school premises on Saturday morning, Mercer," he said.

"No, sir," Robin said. Her voice was steady now; it had that quiet, hard flatness that she had learned from her mother when the rent-man stood on the doorstep. "I was in Duke Street. I was with my mother."

Mr. Teasdale did not argue.

He reached into his jacket pocket and pulled out a small, transparent polythene evidence bag.

Inside the bag was a school exercise book.

It had a red card cover:

*CALDBECK COMPREHENSIVE SCHOOL*  
*NAME: Robin Mercer*  
*FORM: 8C*  
*SUBJECT: English Composition*

The red card was crusted with wet coal-slack, the corners torn away where it had been forced through an iron grating.

"This was found at seven o'clock this morning," Mr. Teasdale said. "By Mr. Gowan. It was lying on the inspection-plate of the primary boiler, Robin. In the chamber that was sealed by the works department on Friday afternoon."

He opened the bag.

He drew the book out with two fingers.

The paper was damp, swollen to twice its thickness by the steam from the calorifiers, smelling of sulfur and wet pulp.

He opened it to the first page.

"Read the entry, Mercer," he said.

Robin looked down at the paper.

The handwriting was not her own rough, penciled scrawl.

It was written in black Indian ink, with that stiff, beautiful copperplate hand that she had seen on the stone slate in the scutching room:

*I, Robin Mercer, being of sound mind and twelve years of age, do hereby confess and acknowledge that I have this day entered the forbidden works beneath the school, and that I have with my own hands broken the sluice, scattered the coal, and brought the water into the lower chambers.*

*And I do further declare that I have no home in this parish, having surrendered my claim to the council’s air and the council’s water in perpetuity.*

*Signed this nineteenth day of October, nineteen sixty-four.*

*Witness: C. Holland.*

Robin stared at the signature.

*C. Holland.*

Her mother’s name.

Written in that large, irregular, childish hand with the round *o* and the long, trailing tail on the *d*.

"My mother didn't sign that," Robin said.

"Your mother signed that card when she was twelve years old, Robin," Mr. Teasdale whispered.

He leaned forward until his spectacles were level with her forehead.

"She signed it so they wouldn't send her to the reformatory at Staveley," he said. "She signed it because she had taken three loaves from the baker’s dray on the quayside. The parish accepted her surrender, Robin. They gave her the house in Duke Street on condition that she kept her name off the rate-book for forty years."

He took his golden pen out of his pocket.

The gold nib was broad, wet with purple ink that smelled of copperas and vinegar.

"The forty years are up today, Mercer," he said.

He held the pen out to her.

"Sign the book."

### Chapter 30: The Strike of the Pen

The hall was so silent that Robin could hear the gas-burners in the domestic science kitchens two blocks away: *hiss-s-s-s*.

Behind Mr. Teasdale, the sixty-two teachers stood like sixty-two wooden pillars, their black gowns hanging straight, their arms folded inside their wide sleeves, their eyes fixed on the blank wall above the clock.

Robin looked down at the black register.

The page was white, lined with red rulings that were spaced an eighth of an inch apart.

Beside the name *MERCER, ROBIN* was a blank space three inches wide.

She did not take the golden pen.

Her hands stayed at her sides, her thumbs hooked into the hem of her cardigan, her fingernails digging through the wool into the skin of her hips.

"No," she said.

The word was very small.

It did not have the force of a shout; it had the dry, hard snap of a dry twigs under a boot on the high scar.

Mr. Teasdale’s hand did not shake.

"If you do not sign, Robin," he said, his voice dropping into that dreadful, administrative whisper, "the surveyor will condemn the house in Duke Street before noon. The notice is already prepared. It sits on the Town Clerk’s table in Kendal. They will bring the lorries for the furniture at one o’clock."

"The furniture is broken," Robin said.

"They will take your mother to the hospital at Lancaster," he continued, not listening to her, his voice flowing like cold lard over the words. "They will put her in the third ward on the terrace, where the women sit in the grey smocks and string the beans for the kitchen. You will not see her again, Robin. The county does not permit visits from uncertificated dependents."

Robin looked at the gold nib.

A single drop of purple ink was hanging from the split in the metal, swelling slowly until it looked like a small, dark grape: *tick... tick*.

She remembered the scullery in Duke Street.

She remembered the enameled kettle with the three punch-holes that hissed when the water rose; she remembered the three slices of bread with the pale dripping; she remembered the way her mother’s hands smelled of mutton-tallow and carbolic soap when she kissed her forehead in the dark.

She looked past Mr. Teasdale.

At the back of the stage, half-hidden behind the blue baize curtain of the organ-loft, someone was standing.

It was not a teacher.

It was Mr. Gowan.

He was wearing his brown dust-coat, but he had taken his flat cap off; his bald head was white, scarred with the blue marks of old coal-dust cuts that looked like ink-stains under the thin scalp.

He held his right hand up to his chest.

He was holding an iron object.

It was the four-inch wire nail.

The one Robin had pulled from the red stone on the scar.

He held it between his thumb and forefinger, point down, and made a small, distinct, horizontal cut in the air:

*---||---*

The mark she had carved into the door of Practice Room 1 on Wednesday morning.

The sign of the through-and-through.

Robin took a deep breath.

The air smelled of floor-wax and old clothes, but beneath the floor-wax, rising through the boards of the stage like a cold fountain from the limestone bed, was the smell of the sea.

The wild, clean, salt-gale that had blown across the high scar; the smell of the curlews; the smell of sixty square miles of wet heather that no surveyor had ever measured and no committee had ever entered in a book.

She reached out her right hand.

She did not take the pen.

She took the red exercise book from Mr. Teasdale’s fingers.

She did not tear it.

She laid it flat on the black leather of the county register, right over the name *MERCER, ROBIN*, right over the red rulings, right over the forty years of municipal grease.

She drew her four-inch wire nail from her pocket.

She did not know it was there; her fingers had simply found it in the seam of her skirt, cold, heavy, rusted into a hard iron crescent.

She raised her arm.

Before Mr. Teasdale could move his shoulder, before the sixty-two teachers could unfold their black sleeves, she drove the point of the nail straight through the red exercise book.

Through the red card.

Through the forty pages of swollen paper.

Through the black leather binding of the county register.

And two inches deep into the solid, heart-of-oak timber of the headmaster’s podium.

*THWACK.*

The sound was not loud.

It was the sound of a ship’s carpenter driving a trenail through a four-inch garboard-strake.

The stage trembled.

The sixty-two teachers gave a long, collective, shivering gasp that sounded like the wind dying in the rigging of a timber-ketch: *h-u-u-u-sh*.

Mr. Teasdale’s spectacles fell from his nose.

They hit the timber boards, the two glass circles shattering into forty tiny, sparkling diamonds that bounced across the linoleum: *tic-tic-tic-tic*.

The deputy headmaster did not bend to pick them up.

He stood with his empty face turned toward the nail, his hands hanging by his seams, his mouth open in that same horizontal slit that Robin had seen in the scutching room.

From beneath the red exercise book, where the iron nail had split the leather, a dark fluid began to weep.

It was not purple ink.

It was black, greasy, smelling of thirty years of cylinder-oil and river-silt—the water of the old mill-race that had lived beneath the school since eighteen forty-four.

It spread across the page.

It covered the name *MERCER*.

It covered the name *HENDERSON*.

It covered the name *VANE*.

It washed across the red rulings until the white paper was black as a slate, until there was not a word, not a number, not an *S* for suspended, not a red cross for the ones who had gone into the dark.

"The shift is up, Mr. Teasdale," Robin said.

Her voice was not loud, but it filled the hall from the front row to the fire-exits.

"The bell went twenty minutes ago."

### Chapter 31: The Decommissioning

The school did not fall down.

A building of pre-cast concrete panels and structural steel frames does not tumble into the mud because a twelve-year-old girl has driven an iron nail into an old desk.

It simply ceased to be an authority.

When Robin walked down the center aisle of the hall, the nine hundred children did not look at her with limestone eyes.

They were looking at their own shoes.

Leo Henderson was rubbing the grease off his knuckles with an old handkerchief; Chloe Hirst was whispering something to the girl behind her about the geography test; and Tanya Siddle had her head between her knees, crying with that quiet, miserable snuffle of a girl whose father had forgotten to give her the dinner-money before he went to the quarry.

Robin went out through the double doors into the Humanities corridor.

The yellow-gloss doors of the music rooms were standing open.

Inside Practice Room 1, the small upright piano sat in the corner, its lid raised, its white keys yellow with fifty years of cigarette smoke from the staff room.

The mark was still there on the softwood frame, four inches below the top hinge:

*---||---*

It was not raw. It was not old.

It was just three scratches in yellow gloss, made by a child who had an iron nail and ten minutes to wait before the bell.

Robin walked past the water-cooler.

The compressor was hum-m-m-ing with that low, domestic buzz, spitting its tiny drop of cold water into the porcelain drain whenever someone trod on the brass pedal.

She went down the sixteen concrete steps behind the CDT workshops.

The pea-green door was wide open.

The quarryman’s wedge had been pulled from the ashlar; it was lying on the flagstones by the oil-can, its steel face scarred with three hammer-marks that had begun to rust in the rain.

Inside the boiler room, the three heat exchangers were working.

*Thrum-chug... thrum-chug... thrum-chug.*

Mr. Gowan was standing by the primary pump, with an oily rag in his hand, wiping the brass grease-cups on the rocker-arms.

He did not look up when Robin’s boots hit the concrete.

"The surveyor’s in the office, Mercer," he said, his voice flat and steady through the noise of the machinery. "He’s got his measuring tapes out. He says the whole south wing’s coming down in the spring."

"Where will they go?" Robin asked.

"To the grammar school on the bypass," the old man said, turning the screw-cap on the oil-cup with two fingers. "They’re building twelve new classrooms out of red brick with plastic double-glazing. Electric heaters in every room, Robin. No boilers. No pumps. No coal-chutes for girls to hide their dinners in."

He dropped the rag into his bucket.

He looked at the millstone grit wall.

Door 17 was shut.

The gray oak was dry, dark, ordinary.

The white lead number *17* had been scraped away with a painter’s shave-hook, leaving only a pale, clean patch of bare pine that would turn grey in three winters.

There was no keyhole.

There was only a solid iron rim-lock that Mr. Gowan had screwed to the stiles with six modern wood-screws, the brass deadbolt thrown into the keeper with an oiled *thluck*.

"What’s behind it now?" Robin whispered.

Mr. Gowan picked up his zinc shovel.

"Nothing, girl," he said.

"Just sixty feet of limestone gravel and the drainage-pipe from the canal. The borough put forty tons of quick-setting slurry down the flue at nine o’clock. If you opened that oak now with an iron crowbar, you’d find nothing except three feet of grey cement that’d break your pick before you got an inch into the core."

He looked at her over his shoulder.

His small, pale eyes were clear, bright, carrying that dry, iron humor of an old sailor who had seen the water come through the plates and lived to smoke his pipe on the pier.

"Take your satchel, Robin," he said. "The gate’s unlocked. And don't come back down these steps unless you’ve got a requisition-card signed by the bursar."

Robin walked to the milk-crate.

Her satchel was there, sitting on the plastic ribs.

She picked it up by the mended strap, slung it across her shoulder, and walked up the sixteen steps into the daylight.

### Chapter 32: Monday Afternoon and the Real Light

The light over Caldbeck at three o’clock was not the lilac of the frost; it was the pale, clear gold of late October, when the sun drops below the high fells and shines straight down the estuary, turning the wet sands of the bay into a vast, shining sheet of yellow brass.

Robin walked down the red-clinker path along the canal.

The mallards were swimming in the clear water by the lock-gates, their green heads shining like emeralds in the low sun.

On the towpath, old man Huddleston was leading his grey horse with the cart-load of scrap-iron, the iron tyres ringing on the cobbles with a slow, rhythmic, peaceful music: *tock... tock... tock*.

"Afternoon, Artie’s girl," the old man said as she passed.

"Afternoon, Mr. Huddleston," Robin said.

She did not keep her head down.

She looked at the chimneys of the paper mill; she looked at the long, grey line of the railway viaduct; she looked at the smoke rising from the brick terraces of Duke Street, straight and blue in the cold, still air.

She reached number 4.

The doorstep had been scrubbed.

It was today.

### Chapter 33: The Huts on the Lower Field

### Chapter 34: The Geometry of Tanya Siddle

At morning break, the rain began again.

It was not the driving, salt-laden gale from the Irish Sea; it was that small, soft northern rain that falls without a sound, turning the playing fields into a yellow-green porridge of mud and grass-clippings that stuck to the soles of the pupils’ plimsolls like wet paste.

The two hundred children from Huts A, B, and C could not go up to the main school.

They were confined to the covered boardwalk—a sixty-yard strip of wet spruce four feet wide, sheltered by a corrugated plastic canopy that rattled under the drizzle with a sound like a sack of dry peas being emptied onto an iron shovel.

Robin stood by the end-stanchion, where the timber ramp sloped down into the sour rushes of the drainage ditch.

She had her apple in her pocket.

Not the crab-apple from 1974; this was a small, green cooking-apple that her mother had found in the bottom of the vegetable-crate at the corner shop, its skin wrinkled and sour, with a tiny brown worm-hole near the stalk that she had pared out with the penknife before breakfast.

She leaned against the wet fir post, her parka zipped to her chin, watching the mist roll along the railway embankment.

From the door of Hut A came Tanya Siddle.

Tanya was alone.

Her hair was wet, plastered to the side of her neck in thin, brown rat-tails, and her maroon blazer was missing its top button, the white thread hanging loose like an old maggot against the wool.

She walked down the boardwalk with her shoulders hunched, her head down, her hands thrust into the pockets of her skirt so hard that the hem was pulled up two inches above her knees.

When she reached the end-stanchion, she stopped.

She did not look at Robin’s face.

She looked at the corner of Robin’s satchel, where the black cobbler’s stitches formed two neat, straight lines across the dark blue nylon.

"You think you’re clever, don't you, Skimmer?" Tanya said.

Her voice had no power behind it; it was thin, raspy, carrying that raw, defensive shrillness of a child who has been left behind on the pier when the pleasure-boat has pulled away from the bollards.

"I’m eating my apple, Tanya," Robin said.

"My dad says your street’s coming down anyway," Tanya said, stepping six inches closer, until Robin could smell the cold, greasy lard on her blazer and the peppermint chew she had been rolling around her back teeth since registration. "He says the council’s got no money for the roofs. He says the wet’s in the rafters at number four, and the inspector’s going to put the red ticket on the knocker before the frost."

Robin took a bite of her apple.

The flesh was hard, sour, full of acid juice that made the glands under her jaw-bones ache, but she chewed it slowly, feeling the white pulp cold against her tongue.

"Your dad doesn't work for the council, Tanya," Robin said.

"He does!"

"He works the tipper-truck for the quarry," Robin said, looking at the scratch on Tanya’s forehead—the three-inch furrow that was already healing into a thin, pink thread across the bone. "He drives the lime-dust to the kiln at Carnforth. He doesn't know anything about the roofs in Duke Street."

Tanya’s face went white.

A dull, ugly red came up into her cheeks—not the fever-red of the girl behind the glass, but the hot, furious shame of a twelve-year-old whose father had spent his Sunday afternoon shouting at the television in his undershirt with a bottle of four-ale between his shins.

"You’re a dirty little thieving cow," Tanya spat.

She raised her right hand.

The hand was dirty; the knuckles were black with coal-dust from the stove at home, the cuticles bitten down to the raw flesh.

She reached out to grab the strap of Robin’s satchel—the old, practiced movement that she and Kacey had used behind the library fire-escape every Friday since September.

Robin did not duck.

She did not pull her shoulders in.

She took one step forward on the spruce boards, her Rochdale boots coming down with a clean, heavy *clack* that shook the handrail between them.

She looked Tanya straight in the eyes.

Her eyes were clear.

They were not wide with terror; they were not dark with the coal-slack; they were just the pale grey-blue of the limestone rocks under the rain, hard as the ashlar blocks that held fifty thousand tons of comprehensive school above the silt.

"Don't touch the bag, Tanya," Robin said.

She did not shout.

She spoke in that low, flat, northern voice that her mother used when the butcher tried to weigh the marrow-bone with the beef.

Tanya’s hand stopped three inches from the nylon strap.

Her fingers curled inward, the fingernails digging into her own palm, her thumb twitching against her forefinger like an insect with a broken leg.

She stared at Robin.

She looked at Robin’s eyes; she looked at the clean collar; she looked at the small, sharp chin that had spent three days fighting sixty years of dead coal-dust and had come back to the desk without a scratch on the skin.

Tanya’s mouth opened.

She looked for a word—the old, nasty words that Kacey had taught her behind the gym, the words about the parish relief and the boots from the church bazaar.

None of them came.

They seemed to dissolve in the wet air of the boardwalk, thin as the steam from the Calor heater, dying before they could reach her teeth.

Tanya took one step back.

Her heel caught the edge of a spruce plank that had warped in the damp, and she stumbled, her shoulder striking the corrugated mint-green side of Hut B with a hollow *thump*.

"You’re mad," Tanya whispered.

Her voice had no spit left in it; it was just a frightened, twelve-year-old whine.

"You’re right off your head, Robin Mercer. You talk to yourself in the ditch."

She turned and ran.

Her plimsolls went *slap... slap... slap* along the wet spruce boards, her maroon blazer flapping around her hips like an old sheep-netting caught on a wire fence, until she disappeared through the door of Hut A into the yellow gas-light.

Robin finished her apple.

She chewed it right to the core, bit through the five black pips until they tasted of bitter almond between her back teeth, and then dropped the fibrous stem into the rushes of the ditch.

The drizzle came down.

It did not hurt.

It was just water—water that had come off the Irish Sea, crossed the sands of the bay, and was heading toward the high fells where the sheep were standing behind the drystone walls, waiting for the snow.

### Chapter 35: The Drill-Rig in the Sump

At two o’clock on Tuesday afternoon, the noise began.

It was not the sound of the school; it was the heavy, rhythmic, diesel-throbbing roar of an exploratory drill-rig mounted on the back of an eight-wheeled Foden lorry that had backed down the service lane behind the condemned south wing.

*CHUG-a-chug... CHUG-a-chug... CLANK.*

The sound went through the clay of the playing fields, vibrating through the concrete breeze-blocks of Hut B until the red plastic pens on Mr. Finch’s desk began to chatter against the rim of their ceramic pot.

Mr. Finch stopped reading from the grammar syllabus.

He took his purple pen from his teeth, laid it across his register, and walked to the hopper-window.

"What in the name of Providence are they doing with that machine?" he muttered.

The thirty children in Hut B did not look at their exercise books.

They all stood up, their chairs scraping the new vinyl floor with a chorus of high, squeaking protests: *screee-ch*.

Robin looked through the hopper.

The Foden lorry was painted municipal yellow, with the county crest on the cab-door: *WESTMORLAND COUNTY COUNCIL HIGHWAYS AND BRIDGES DEPARTMENT*.

On the rear deck of the lorry, an eight-inch steel auger—a great, spiral drill-bit twelve feet long, black with grease and wet clay—was turning slowly, driven by an open roller-chain that went *clack-clack-clack* around a two-foot sprocket.

Standing by the drill-controls was an engineer in a white safety-helmet and a yellow waterproof coat, his clipboard tucked beneath his left arm, his right hand on the hydraulic lever.

Beside him stood Mr. Teasdale.

The deputy head was wearing an old grey mackintosh over his tweed jacket, his hands clasped behind his back, his head tilted back as he watched the steel bit bite into the tarmac of the service yard.

The drill went down.

First the six inches of black asphalt: *crunch... shrrr-up*.

Then the twelve inches of limestone hardcore from the quarry at Silverdale: *screee-ch-clonk*.

Then the red clay of the lower meadow.

The machine’s engine roared, its exhaust-pipe throwing a thick puff of blue-grey diesel smoke into the drizzle: *ROOO-AAR*.

Robin watched the auger.

It went down six feet.

Eight feet.

Ten feet.

Every three feet, the engineer reversed the hydraulic lever, and the auger came out of the ground like an enormous iron corkscrew, carrying three hundredweight of wet, shining subsoil that was dumped onto the tarmac by two men with wooden scrapers.

The spoil was not red clay anymore.

It was black.

It was the black, greasy silt of the old mill-race, mixed with thousands of fragments of blue Welsh slate, rotted oak-shavings, and the crushed shells of freshwater mussels that had lived beneath the flax-mill in eighteen forty.

The drill went down for the fourth pass.

Twelve feet.

Fourteen feet.

Then the sound changed.

The *chug-a-chug* stopped.

The heavy steel auger struck something that was not clay, not hardcore, and not gravel.

The sound was like an iron cannonball being fired into the face of a granite cliff:

*CLANG-G-G.*

The entire Foden lorry bounced four inches off its rear springs, its heavy steel chassis twisting with a loud, metallic shriek that echoed off the brick walls of the gymnasium like a rifle-shot.

The roller-chain snapped.

The two-inch iron links whipped through the air, striking the steel cab of the lorry with a sound like a scythe hitting an empty bucket: *whack-thump*.

The diesel engine died with a coughing, choking rattle that ended in a long, whistling hiss of steam from the blown radiator-hose: *psss-shhh-clack*.

Silence.

The engineer dropped his clipboard into the mud.

He took off his white helmet, looked at the smoking hole in the tarmac, and swore three times with that slow, heavy, Lancashire emphasis that men use when an expensive machine has broken its crank-shaft fifty miles from the depot.

Mr. Teasdale did not swear.

He took two steps toward the hole, his brogues crunching on the black silt that had been dumped across the yard.

He leaned forward, his hands on his knees, his spectacles sliding down his nose as he peered into the twelve-inch bore.

Robin, watching through the slit of the hopper-window three hundred yards away, knew what was down there.

It was not bedrock.

It was not a limestone boulder.

It was the seventeenth ashlar block.

The dressed millstone grit from the flax-mill sluice, three feet thick, holding up the dead weight of fifty thousand tons of comprehensive school against the mud of the estuary.

And through the stone, driven three inches deep into the heart of the masonry, was the quarryman’s splitting-wedge, holding the door shut against the world.

### Chapter 36: The Core Sample

At four o’clock, when the bell went for the dismissal, the children did not go down the boardwalk to the bus-gates.

They gathered along the chain-link fence that separated the lower pitch from the service yard, their satchels held against their chests, their faces pressed against the galvanized wire until the diamond pattern left red indentations in their cold cheeks.

The engineer had brought out a hand-operated core-extractor—a six-inch steel cylinder with a serrated diamond-crown that was worked by two men turning an iron T-bar with wooden handles.

*Scritch... tock.*  
*Scritch... tock.*

They were turning it by hand, their boots slipping in the black silt that had flooded the yard, their breath rising in white clouds that smelled of pipe-tobacco and hot tea.

Mr. Gowan was there.

He had brought his wheelbarrow from the potting-shed, its timber sides painted with old red lead, full of sawdust to soak up the radiator-water that had leaked from the lorry.

Robin stood by the fence, six feet away from the others.

She did not press her face against the wire; she stood with her hands in her pockets, her head tilted slightly forward, watching the two men on the T-bar.

The core came up.

The two men lifted the iron cylinder out of the bore with a small block-and-tackle hung from an A-frame of scaffolding poles.

*Creak... clank.*

The engineer laid the cylinder flat on a wooden trestle-table that had been brought out from the CDT workshops.

He took a brass drift and a copper mallet, tapped the side of the barrel three times—*ting, ting, ting*—and the sample slid out onto the wet pine boards.

It was a cylinder of solid matter, six inches in diameter and three feet long.

The children along the fence leaned forward, their coats rustling against the wire like dry leaves.

The top twelve inches were ordinary: the black asphalt, the crushed limestone, and four inches of red meadow-clay.

The middle twelve inches were black: the compressed silt of the flax-mill race, layered with thin, papery leaves of grey moss and two round pebbles of dark red iron-ore from the mines at Lindal.

Then came the bottom twelve inches.

It was not stone.

It was paper.

Hundreds of layers of white wood-pulp paper, compressed by fifty years of earth-pressure and water-soak into a solid, grey-white block that looked like an enormous piece of lard.

The engineer took out his pocket-knife—a heavy, horn-handled clasp-knife with a curved blade—and cut a thin, triangular wedge from the side of the paper block.

He held it up to the yellow sodium light of the yard.

The paper was damp, but the ink was clear.

It was not printed.

It was ruled with red margins an eighth of an inch apart, covered with thousands of small, neat names written in blue ballpoint and purple felt-tip ink:

*1968: Henderson, W.*  
*1971: Holland, C.*  
*1974: Vane, K.*  
*1979: Siddle, T.*  
*1984: Mercer, R.*

The engineer scratched his head through his thin, sandy hair.

"What is it, Mr. Teasdale?" he asked, his voice carrying clearly across the quiet yard. "Is it an old rubbish-tip? Did the council dump the stationery down here when they cleared the grammar school?"

Mr. Teasdale did not answer immediately.

He stood by the trestle-table, his hands deep in his mackintosh pockets, his eyes fixed on the white cylinder that lay between the two scrapers.

He did not touch the paper.

He looked at the names.

He looked at the name *HOLLAND, C.*

He looked at the name *MERCER, R.*

Then he reached out with his bare right hand—his fingers long, thin, spotted with the yellow liver-spots of seventy years of damp parish air—and picked up the small triangular wedge that the engineer had cut with his clasp-knife.

He did not put it in his pocket.

He walked across the wet tarmac of the service yard.

He did not walk toward the office; he walked straight toward the chain-link fence.

Toward Robin Mercer.

The other children shrank back, their boots squelching in the mud as they cleared a space five yards wide on either side of her, leaving Robin standing alone against the galvanized wire.

Mr. Teasdale stopped eighteen inches from the mesh.

The grey mist was hanging in his eyebrows, and his spectacles had steamed over from the heat of his breath, but behind the wet glass his eyes were clear—not the empty holes of the scutching room, but the tired, troubled, human eyes of a man who had lived in the same valley for sixty years and had watched the water come through the floorboards every winter.

He held the wedge of paper out through the diamond mesh.

"Take it, Robin," he said softly.

His voice had no microphone in it; it had no school-hall echo.

It was just an old man’s voice, speaking across an iron fence in the gathering dusk.

Robin looked at the paper.

The wood-pulp was cold, wet, smelling of old lime and river-silt, but in the center was her own name, written in the blue ink of the ballpoint pen she had lost in the third-year cloakroom three weeks ago.

She took it.

Her fingers closed around the damp cellulose.

It did not burn her; it did not shriek; it did not smell of sulfur.

It was just paper—an old, forgotten scrap of municipal records that had spent twenty years in the dark under the foundations and had finally been brought to the surface by an eight-inch drill-bit.

"Is the school coming down, sir?" Robin asked.

Mr. Teasdale looked up at the three-storey brick wings of the 1968 block, where the yellow lights of the staff room were beginning to glow in the twilight.

"The south block is, Robin," he said.

"They will clear the brickwork in the spring. They will fill the cellar with limestone gravel from the bypass cutting, and they will roll out the green turf for the junior football pitch."

"And the door?" she whispered.

The deputy headmaster reached into his pocket and pulled out his handkerchief.

He wiped his spectacles slowly, carefully, holding the wire frames up to the evening light before setting them back on his nose.

"There is no door in the plan, Mercer," he said.

He turned his back on the fence, his brogues crunching in the black silt as he walked toward the office, his mackintosh flapping around his knees in the rising wind.

"There never was a door on the county map."

### Chapter 37: The Library at Noon

On Wednesday, the rain stopped.

The sky cleared over the high fells, leaving the air thin, sharp, and transparent as window-glass, with that hard, cold scent of snow that comes down from the peaks when the screes are frozen into solid iron.

The lunch-hour at the huts was thirty-five minutes long.

Robin did not sit by the Calor heater.

She walked across the wooden boardwalk, through the open gate by the cricket pavilion, and went up the gravel track to the old stone pavilion by the bowling green.

The parish council had converted the pavilion into a temporary reading room while the central library on the High Street was being re-wired.

It was twelve feet square, built of dressed limestone with a hipped roof of dark green Westmorland slate, smelling of floor-oil, old calf-skin bindings, and the small coke-stove that sat in the corner behind an iron fire-guard.

Miss Finch was sitting at the table.

She was not wearing her school cardigan; she was wearing an old navy-blue overcoat with bone buttons, her spectacles tied to her ears with two loops of black round-elastic to keep them from falling into her tea-mug.

She looked up when Robin’s boots came over the stone sill.

"You’re from 8C," she said.

Her voice was not the voice from the assembly; it was the quiet, dry, rustling voice of a woman who had spent forty years cataloguing the shipping lists from the Lancaster customs house.

"Yes, Miss Finch," Robin said.

"Wipe your feet on the coir," the woman said, pointing her bone paper-knife at the coconut matting by the door. "The coal-slack from the lane is ruining the linoleum. What do you want?"

"The map," Robin said.

"Which map?"

"The one from sixty-four," Robin said. "Before they made the school."

Miss Finch did not ask for a library ticket.

She did not ask for tenpence for the fine.

She stood up from her Windsor chair, her joints making two small, dry cracks like firewood breaking in a cold grate, and walked to the shallow pine drawers that stood against the back wall beneath the cricket trophies.

She pulled out the third drawer: *MAPS: PARISH OF CALDBECK (RURAL DISTRICT)*.

She lifted out a large, linen-backed sheet that had been folded eight times into a square the size of a tea-tray.

She laid it flat on the baize table.

The paper was yellow, smelling of dry wood-oil and starch, the ink drawn by hand with an iron-gall pen that had turned the dark brown of walnut-juice:

*SURVEY OF THE DRAINAGE AND HABITATIONS OF THE PARISH OF CALDBECK*  
*PREPARED FOR THE LOCAL BOARD OF HEALTH, 1874*  
*SHEET IV: THE LOWER MILL-MEADOWS AND THE EMBANKMENT*

Robin leaned over the table.

Her breath left a tiny, clear circle of steam on the linen.

There was the canal.

Drawn with two parallel blue lines, showing the lock-gates, the horse-bridge, and the timber-staithes by the railway siding.

There was Duke Street.

Sixty tiny red rectangles, numbered from 1 to 60, arranged in two straight rows along the foot of the limestone embankment.

Number 4 was there.

Inside the red rectangle was a tiny name, written in that microscopic copperplate hand:

*Holland, A. (Line-repairer).*

And there, a quarter of a mile up the slope, where the comprehensive school sat today with its three CLASP blocks and its three hundred yards of mint-green huts, was the old mill.

The plan showed the square wheel-pit; the scutching shed; the forty-foot chimney of the boiler-house; and the long, diagonal tail-race that ran through the limestone shelf toward the canal.

And at the western corner of the wheel-pit, marked with a small red cross that had been drawn with the point of a fine quill, was an inscription:

*OUTLET NO. 17: Sluice-gate and Overflow to the Marsh. Maintained by the Trust for the Relief of the High Beck Level.*

Below the inscription, in the margin of the linen sheet, was a note written by the surveyor in pencil:

*MEMORANDUM: The ground here is subject to periodic subsidence owing to the presence of unrecorded culverts beneath the flax-stabling. In the event of heavy rainfall upon the high scar, the pressure of water behind the seventeenth ashlar block is considerable. Care must be taken that the oak be not strained beyond the four-pound limit, lest the day behind be let into the lower works.*

Robin read the words twice.

She touched the linen with the ball of her thumb.

The paper was dry, hard, dead.

It was sixty years older than she was; it was thirty years older than her mother; it was older even than the railway viaduct with its thirty-six stone arches.

"Who was the surveyor?" Robin asked.

Miss Finch did not look up from her catalog-cards.

"A man named Teasdale," she said, her bone knife moving with that steady, rhythmic *scritch... scritch*.

"His father was the curate at the parish church. He had three fingers missing from his left hand, and he died of the typhus in seventy-six while he was measuring the culverts behind the churchyard."

She stopped her knife.

She looked at Robin over the top of her round spectacles.

"Did you find what you were looking for, Mercer?"

Robin looked at the little red rectangle for Number 4, Duke Street.

"Yes, Miss Finch," she said.

"Then fold the linen," the woman said, turning back to her cards. "The dust off the fells gets into the folds if you leave it open, and the county doesn't give us the grant for the linen-mending until after the new year."

Robin folded the map.

The linen crackled softly: *scritch-crackle... scritch-crackle*.

She laid it back in the shallow pine drawer, pushed the drawer home until the brass handle clicked against the frame, and walked out into the cold sunlight.

### Chapter 38: The Last of the Slurry

On Thursday morning, the concrete-mixers arrived.

Four large, yellow trucks belonging to the bypass contractors, their six-yard rotating drums churning with a heavy, grinding roar that sounded like twenty iron coal-drays being dragged across a cobble-lane: *rumble-chug... rumble-chug*.

They parked in the service yard behind the condemned block.

Two men in blue overalls ran four-inch rubber delivery-hoses down the sixteen concrete steps into the boiler room.

Robin stood on the boardwalk by Hut C during the morning break, watching the grey slurry flow.

It was quick-setting sulphate-resisting cement, grey-green as river-mud, mixed with fine limestone dust from the quarries to make it hard as granite within three hours.

*Schloop... schloop... schloop.*

The slurry went down the pipes under seventy pounds of hydraulic pressure, filling the sump; filling the lower flues; filling the three-foot space behind the secondary calorifiers; filling the seventeen-foot ashlar chamber of the old sluice.

Mr. Gowan was standing at the top of the steps.

He had his brown dust-coat on, his arms crossed over his chest, his flat cap pulled down until the peak touched his eyebrows.

He was watching the delivery-gauge on the side of the lead truck.

The red needle was moving:

*TEN TONS.*  
*TWENTY TONS.*  
*THIRTY TONS.*  
*FORTY TONS.*

At forty-two tons, the pump gave a short, wet, choking *thump*.

The rubber hose twitched like a dying snake, its wire reinforcement creaking under the back-pressure: *errrk-clank*.

The engineer on the truck-cab throttled the diesel back.

"She’s full, mate," he shouted through the noise of the drum. "She won't take another shovel. The slurry’s right up to the underside of the floor-plates. You could run a forty-ton tank across that cellar now and you wouldn't get a half-inch deflection on the dial."

Mr. Gowan nodded once.

He took his clay pipe from his pocket, wiped the stem with the corner of his sleeve, and stuck it between his teeth.

He walked down the three steps to the landing.

He looked down into the pit.

Robin, watching through the chain-link fence, could see his shoulder through the gap in the louvered door.

The boiler room was gone.

There were no three heat exchangers; there were no lagged copper pipes; there was no milk-crate; there was no millstone grit wall.

There was only a solid, flat, grey-green floor of wet concrete, twelve feet deep and thirty feet square, smooth as a billiard-table, the surface sweating tiny beads of lime-water that smelled of wet cement, dead coal-oil, and cold river-gravel.

In the center of the wet slab, sitting three inches above the surface where the contractor had driven it into the wet screed, was a bronze reference-pin.

A round brass disc, three inches across, stamped with the mark of the Ordnance Survey:

*O.S. BENCH MARK*  
*DATUM LEVEL: 44.2 FEET ABOVE NEWLYN HIGH WATER*  
*STATION NO. 17*

Mr. Gowan reached out his foot.

He tapped the edge of the bronze pin with the iron toe-cap of his boot.

*Clink.*

The sound was solid.

It did not echo; it did not vibrate; it did not go through the earth to the water-meadows.

It was just iron striking bronze in an ordinary cellar in an ordinary county school in the north of England.

The old man turned and came up the sixteen steps.

He saw Robin standing by the fence.

He did not shout. He did not tell her to go back to the huts.

He stopped by the top handrail, took his pipe from his mouth, and tapped the bowl against the iron stanchion: *tock... tock*.

"The shift is filled, Robin," he said.

"Yes, Mr. Gowan," she said.

"There won't be no more wandering in the dark," he said, looking out over the mint-green huts toward the estuary, where the white gulls were following a tractor that was turning the stubble on the marsh-farms. "The council’s putting the contract out for the new classrooms on Monday. Red brick, Robin. With a flat roof and three radiators from the boiler at the gym."

He stuck his pipe back into his cheek.

"Go to your lesson, girl," he said. "Mr. Finch has got the dictation ready, and he doesn't like 'em coming in after the second comma."

Robin turned from the fence.

She walked along the boardwalk, her Rochdale boots making their clean, sharp, regular music on the spruce boards: *clack... clack... clack*.

She did not look back at the yellow Foden lorry.

She went through the door of Hut B into the warm, gas-lit room where the thirty children were sitting with their exercise books open to the clean red lines.

### Chapter 39: The Ledger of the Living

On Friday morning, the twenty-fifth of October, the rain stopped completely.

A light frost had touched the grass of the lower pitch, whitening the bent-stems until the football field looked like a grey lake of salt, but the sky was clear—a pale, washed-out blue that had no clouds from the fells to the sea.

In Hut B, the nine-o’clock bell did not chime with an electronic chime.

The temporary wire had not been connected to the master-clock in the main school, so Mr. Finch used a brass hand-bell that he had borrowed from the primary infants across the lane.

He stood by his desk, took the wooden handle in his right hand, and shook it three times:

*Clang... clang... clang.*

A small, domestic, cheerful sound that died instantly in the wood-frame room.

"Sit down," he said.

The thirty children sat.

Mr. Finch opened the black leather register.

It was not the old register with the iron clasps and the torn corners; it was a new book, bound in red cloth with the borough arms stamped in gold-foil on the front cover:

*WESTMORLAND COUNTY COUNCIL*  
*CLASS REGISTER: FORM 8C (ACADEMIC YEAR 1984–85)*

He took his purple felt-tip pen from his waistcoat pocket.

He dipped his thumb into his handkerchief, wiped his reading glasses, and set them on his nose.

"Cleary, Arthur," he called.

"Present, sir."

"Garside, Martha."

"Present, sir."

"Henderson, Leo."

Leo sat in the second row, his tie pulled straight, his clarinet case sitting between his boots, his clean white shirt-sleeve showing no tears at the elbow.

"Present, sir," he said.

His voice was clear—a twelve-year-old boy’s treble, whistling slightly through the missing tooth, but steady and ordinary as the clock on the scullery wall.

"Hirst, Chloe."

"Present, sir."

Mr. Finch’s pen moved down the red rulings.

*Black slash.*  
*Black slash.*  
*Black slash.*

He stopped.

The purple pen hovered half an inch above the clean white paper.

The thirty children sat in complete silence, their forearms flat on the grey laminate tables, the Calor heater hissing softly in the center of the room: *psss-shhh*.

"Mercer, Robin," Mr. Finch called.

He did not look up over his spectacles.

His voice had no threat in it; it had no administrative weight; it was just a master calling the name of a pupil from the third row.

Robin sat with her back straight, her hands resting on the edge of her table, her eyes looking through the rear hopper-window at the high limestone scar two miles away across the valley.

The scar was white with frost, shining in the morning sun like a clean bone laid upon the heather, but below it, the smoke was rising from the sixty chimneys of Duke Street, straight and blue into the cold air.

She drew her breath in.

Her ribs expanded against the wool of her cardigan—the thin grey one with the two brass safety pins holding the hem.

"Present, sir," Robin said.

Mr. Finch brought the point of his pen down against the page.

*Black slash.*

A clean, straight, dark line of purple ink, three-eighths of an inch long, set squarely between the red rulings for Friday the twenty-fifth.

"Siddle, Tanya," he called, not waiting a second, his pen moving on to the next line with the steady, indifferent rhythm of a county clerk who had forty names to call before the bell for arithmetic.

"Present, sir," Tanya mumbled from the front row.

Robin looked at her red exercise book.

On the first page, beneath the red card cover, she wrote the date:

*Friday, 25th October, 1984.*

Below the date, she wrote the title of the composition that Mr. Finch had chalked upon the small portable blackboard by the door:

*THE VALLEY WHERE WE LIVE*

She picked up her HB pencil.

The lead was sharp, smelling of cedar-wood and graphite.

She began to write:

*The town of Caldbeck is built where the limestone meets the marsh. In the old days, there was a flax-mill by the beck, and the people worked the looms until the water failed in the dry summers. Then they built the railway, and the houses in Duke Street were put up for the line-repairers who had to walk the tracks to Carnforth.*

*My mother lives at Number 4.*

*She works the steam-calender at the home, and on Saturdays she washes the flagstones with the brown stone from the shop.*

*The school is made of three blocks on the hill, but the south block has been condemned because the foundations were sitting on the old race. We are in the huts on the field now, where the floor is made of spruce boards that smell of resin when the gas-heater is on.*

*It is very cold in the mornings, but if you look out of the hopper-window when the mist clears, you can see the curlews on the sand, and the high scar where the sheep go when the winter comes down from the fells.*

She wrote to the bottom of the page.

Her handwriting was small, regular, slightly slanted toward the margin, the pencil moving with a steady, quiet rhythm that went with the ticking of the clock:

*Scritch.*  
*Scritch.*  
*Scritch.*

### Chapter 40: The High Scar and the Town Below

On Saturday morning, Robin and her mother walked up the cart-track to the high scar.

It was nine o'clock, but the frost had not given way; the ruts in the limestone track were filled with ice as thick as window-glass, through which the red mud of the fell-drain showed like dried blood beneath a lens.

Her mother was wearing her black serge coat with the woollen shawl crossed over her breast, her feet in her old leather walking-boots with the triple-leather soles that her father had made for her when she was fourteen.

She walked with a limp—her left hip clicking every twenty paces with that small, dry, familiar sound—but she did not stop at the cattle-grid.

She walked with that slow, rolling, unstoppable gait of women who had carried six stone of coal up the cellar-steps every Monday morning of their lives.

They reached the top of the ridge where the old sheep-fold sat among the gorse.

The three stone walls were white with rime, the drystone limestone blocks crusted with yellow lichen and the green frost-fur that looked like sugar on a cake.

Robin went to the western corner.

The red millstone grit block was there.

The round hole in the center was empty; the four-inch wire nail was gone; the lead-plug had been smoothed over by seventy years of sheep-grease and mountain rain until it was flush with the dressed sandstone.

She touched the stone with her bare palm.

It was cold.

Just cold limestone.

No vibration; no pump; no boy with watch-wheels in his eyes.

Her mother stopped beside her, her hands resting on the top coping-stone of the wall, her breath coming in short, white clouds that blew away toward the bay before the north wind.

"Your grand-dad brought me up here in fifty-three," her mother said.

Her voice was clear in the vast, open quiet of the fells.

"The day the King died. We walked all the way from the dock-gates with two dry pasties in a red handkerchief. He sat on that very stone, Robin, and he took his pocket-knife out and he cut a piece of hazel-wood to make a whistle for my brother Billy."

She looked down into the valley.

The mist had cleared completely.

Caldbeck lay laid out along the foot of the ridge like a grey stone map that had been dropped into the meadows.

There were the sixty houses of Duke Street, their slate roofs shining in the morning sun like the scales on a salmon’s back.

There was the railway viaduct, its thirty-six stone arches marching across the salt-marsh to the river-reach.

There was the canal, narrow and silver, with the blue smoke from the scrap-wharf drifting across the lock-gates.

And there, on the hill above the gorse, was the school.

The three CLASP blocks sat quiet in the sunlight.

The south block had been stripped; the yellow warning tape was gone, replaced by a six-foot fence of galvanized chain-link that surrounded the brick wings like an iron cage.

On the lower pitch, the three mint-green huts sat on their breeze-block piers, their tin chimneys sending three small, clean curls of blue wood-smoke into the air from the morning stoves.

Two tiny figures—two eighth-grade boys in maroon blazers—were kicking a white football across the frozen grass of the upper field, their small shouts drifting up the scree like the piping of plovers:

*Here!*  
*To me!*  
*Go on, Leo!*

Robin’s mother reached into her deep coat-pocket.

She pulled out a small brown paper bag from the bakery on the High Street.

Inside were two Eccles cakes—round, flaky pastries crusted with coarse white sugar, filled with dark currants and nutmeg that smelled of cinnamon and hot butter.

She gave one to Robin.

"Eat it while the sugar’s dry," her mother said.

She sat down on the flat top of the limestone boulder, her black skirt spread across the rock, her boots resting in the brown bent-grass.

Robin sat beside her.

The pastry was flaky, crumbling into sixty buttery scales as her teeth went through the crust, the dark currants sweet and hot against her tongue.

She chewed slowly, watching the tide come into the bay.

It was coming fast today—the great northern spring-tide that comes once every nineteen years, when the moon is full and the wind is out of the west.

A three-foot wall of brown, foam-crested water was sweeping up the channel of the Kent, running over the cockle-beds, drowning the sand-banks, filling the salt-gut with thirty square miles of cold, clean, living sea.

It did not stop.

It covered the mud where the old barges had rotted; it covered the stone piers where the flax-boats had tied their ropes; it washed the black coal-slack from the mouth of the culverts and carried it out to the deep water beyond the lightship.

Robin looked at her mother.

Her mother was eating her cake, her eyes half-closed against the cold glare of the white water, a crumb of brown sugar clinging to the corner of her lower lip.

"Mum?"

"Aye, Robin."

"What are we going to do on Monday?"

Her mother swallowed her mouthful, brushed her apron with her flat palm, and looked down at the town that had tried to take her name forty years ago and had failed.

"We’ll get up at five," her mother said softly.

"I’ll put the kettle on the ring, and you’ll lace your boots. Then I’ll go down to the lodge for the linen-shift, and you’ll go down the boardwalk to the hut."

She reached out and took Robin’s hand in her own—the broad, scarred, work-swollen hand closing over the twelve-year-old fingers with that firm, immovable, eternal warmth that no winter could ever freeze.

"And if Mr. Finch asks who’s at the desk," her mother said, "you tell him it’s Robin Mercer. From Number 4, Duke Street."

She stood up from the wall.

"Come on, girl," she said, turning her back on the high scar and setting her boots to the cart-track. "The dinner’s in the pot, and the butcher’s boy doesn't wait for anyone on Saturdays."

Robin stood up beside her.

She took the last bite of her cake, wiped the sugar from her fingers onto the wool of her cardigan, and walked down the hill behind her mother.

Her shadow went before her—not the shadow of the girl who had hidden in the coal-chute, not the shadow of the girl who had held the shears in the dark, but the long, clean shadow of a girl walking down into her own town in the midday light.

The wind was behind them.

It blew through the gorse; it blew through the drystone walls; it blew through the empty sheep-fold and down across the roofs of Duke Street, carrying the smoke of the chimneys, the crying of the gulls, and the sound of the bell ringing from the huts on the field, all the way to tomorrow.

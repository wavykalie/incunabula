The staff canteen in the hospital sub-basement smelled of stale beef dripping, wet dishcloths, and the sharp sulfur tang of commercial gas hobs. A row of four cast-iron steam tables stood behind a plastic cough guard, their stainless steel wells filled with cloudy hot water that bubbled gently beneath metal trays of tinned marrowfat peas and fried luncheon meat.

It was half past five on a damp Thursday afternoon. The sky visible through the high iron gratings at pavement level had turned the color of dirty dishwater.

Maureen sat at the corner table beneath the menu board. Her plate held two slices of white bread spread with yellow margarine and a bowl of clear tomato soup that had formed a thin, wrinkled skin around the rim.

Brenda Higgins dropped into the plastic chair opposite her. She tossed a crumpled blue tabard onto the table, then reached into her apron for an enamel tin holding her cigarette papers and three ounces of rolling shag.

"My feet are throbbing like steam valves," Brenda said, massaging the ball of her right foot through the thick black nylon of her support stockings. "Six hours stripping the wax off the outpatient fracture clinic. That new supervisor with the yellow pencil stood by the clock and timed every ten-yard run. Told me I was twenty seconds behind the regional efficiency baseline."

"You shouldn't let him rush you, Brenda," Maureen said, breaking off a piece of crust and dipping it into the soup.

"Rush me?" Brenda laughed, a dry, rattling cough that rattled deep in her chest. "If I go any faster at sixty-two, they will be wheeling me straight into the mortuary fridge. But that is the point, isn't it? They want us to walk out before the redundancy terms are posted on the board."

Maureen set her spoon down against the ceramic bowl. "What redundancy terms?"

Brenda took a pinch of dark brown tobacco from her tin, spread it along a rice paper, and licked the gummed strip with the tip of her tongue. Her fingers moved with the effortless economy of forty years of habit.

"I had a word with Tommy's lad who works in the payroll office up on the fourth floor," Brenda murmured, leaning across the Formica table until her head was three inches from Maureen's. "The trust board signed the private facilities management tender yesterday afternoon. Vanguard Healthcare Services. Starting the first of September, every domestic cleaner, porter, catering lass, and laundry worker is being transferred out of the NHS pension scheme."

The air in the basement canteen seemed to drop three degrees. Maureen looked past Brenda's shoulder toward the serving hatch, where two young kitchen assistants were scraping baked beans into a plastic waste bucket with wooden spatulas. The wet, rhythmic scrape echoed against the tiled walls.

"Sterling said TUPE regulations protect the pensions," Maureen said quietly.

"Sterling tells lies the way other men breathe," Brenda retorted, rolling the cigarette between her palms until it was as tight and round as a matchstick. "Tommy's lad saw the annex to the contract. Staff who accept the new private contracts will get fifty pence an hour more on their basic rate, but the sickness benefits are cut to statutory minimum, and the pension contributions go into a private fund in London. If you refuse the transfer, you get statutory redundancy based on twenty years' service, capped at three hundred pounds a year. That is six thousand pounds for thirty years of scrubbing their floors, Maureen. And you are out on the street with no job and no reference."

Maureen took a sip of her tomato soup. It was lukewarm, tasting of salt and metal from the institutional tin.

"And what happens to the older lasses?" Maureen asked.

"They want us gone," Brenda said flatly. "They want twenty-year-olds on zero-hours agency tickets who don't know what a trade union branch looks like and won't complain when the floor stripper takes the skin off their knuckles. That is how Vanguard makes their twelve percent profit margin. Cut the headcount by a third, double the ward coverage, and let the agency carry the turnover."

She took an old tin lid from her pocket, set it flat on the Formica, and struck a match against the side of the box. The sulfur flare illuminated the deep lines around her mouth before settling into a tiny yellow flame. She drew the smoke deep into her lungs, exhaling toward the extraction vent in the ceiling.

"Janice Bradley is putting her retirement papers in," Brenda added in a whisper.

Maureen looked up sharply. "Janice? She has two years left before her full pension."

"She is taking early retirement on a reduced annuity," Brenda said. "She had a stand-up row with Sterling outside the drug dispensary on Tuesday morning. Porter told me Janice threw her clinical audit keys onto his clipboard and told him she had not spent thirty-five years nursing sick people to become an inventory clerk for a London venture capital syndicate. Sterling didn't even raise his voice. Just noted the date in his iPad and said her resignation would be processed under expedited governance protocols."

Maureen touched the rough seam of her blue cotton trousers. Beneath the pocket lay her metal key ring, heavy and cold.

"Janice knows where the bodies are buried," Brenda said, tapping an ash cone into the tin lid. "Thirty-five years on Ward 4. She remembers every doctor who came onto the ward smelling of gin, and every medication slip that got shredded when an MP's nephew was on the board. But she is tired, Maureen. Her sister has a bungalow down in Bridlington. Janice wants to plant sweet peas and forget she ever saw an intravenous drip."

The canteen tea urn hissed loudly as an automatic float valve opened, flooding the element with cold mains water.

The canteen air grew heavier as the kitchen extractors shut down with a dying mechanical groan. On the far side of the room, two catering assistants in hairnets were packing leftover bread rolls into plastic sacks for the hospital pigs.

Maureen looked at the worn Formica between them. In 1992, during the winter when the old boilers failed and snow drifted through the ward fire escapes, the domestic staff had taken turns sleeping on these very vinyl benches between twelve-hour double shifts. She remembered Brenda bringing a jar of pickled eggs from her cellar, and Janice Bradley smuggling three pints of pasteurized milk from the children's ward so the cleaners could have sweet tea at four in the morning.

"Do you remember when the regional board tried to take our weekend tea allowance, Brenda?" Maureen asked.

"Nineteen ninety-four," Brenda replied, coughing softly into her fist. "Old Dr. Hepworth was medical director then. Stood by the time clock with his pocket watch, telling us ten minutes was ample time to drink a mug of tea and smoke half a Woodbine. We walked out onto Kirkgate in our slippers and stood in the rain until the union convener came down from Leeds. Within three hours, the board backed down and gave us an extra ten minutes on Sundays."

"There is no union convener coming from Leeds this time," Maureen said.

"No," Brenda agreed, looking at the grey tea dregs in her cup. "The union office on Park Row was turned into a discount shoe warehouse five years ago. Now you ring an eight-hundred number in Newcastle and speak to a recorded voice that tells you your enquiry has been logged on the customer service matrix. Everything is matrices now, Maureen. Matrices and risk algorithms. Nobody has a face you can shout at."

She reached into her pocket, fingering the small tin lid she used as an ashtray. The metal was scorched black from twenty years of cigarette ends.

"Tommy says I should take the statutory redundancy and sign on for the state pension," Brenda murmured. "Six thousand pounds. After forty years. It wouldn't buy a decent second-hand Ford Cortina. But what choice is there? If I stay on under Vanguard, they will have me cleaning four flights of stone fire stairs with an eight-pound backpack vacuum until my hip gives out completely."

Maureen set her soup spoon down. Her tea was cold, a dark skin forming across the surface. She thought of Kathleen's care home fees, the nine hundred and twenty pounds due on the first of November, and the green file resting beneath her floorboards.

Brenda stubs out her rolled cigarette in a tin lid and folds her cleaning tabard.

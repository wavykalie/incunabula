The side room at the far end of Ward 4 had a single high window that looked out onto the grey slate roofs of the laundry block. The room was twelve feet square, its walls painted with washable gloss paint in a shade the NHS catalog described as *Misty Meadow*, though thirty years of floor stripper and tobacco smoke had turned it the color of boiled tripe.

It was ten minutes past three on Thursday morning.

An elderly woman named Mrs. Cleary had died in the room at two o'clock from congestive heart failure. The undertakers had wheeled the body away twenty minutes ago, leaving the vinyl mattress bare, the steel bedframe stripped of linen, and the smell of lavender water and dying flesh hanging in the cold air.

Maureen wheeled her yellow cleaning cart into the room. She closed the door behind her, shutting out the murmur of the night nurses in bay one.

A young agency staff nurse named Chloe, wearing a light blue tunic with plastic disposable aprons pinned to her shoulders, stood by the stainless steel bedside locker. She was tapping the glass screen of an NHS electronic tablet with a plastic stylus, her mouth twisted in frustrated irritation.

"The damn thing has frozen again," Chloe said, not looking at Maureen. Her accent was South London, sharp and hurried. "I've been trying to log the time of death for twenty minutes. Every time I hit *Submit Clinical Verification*, the screen shows a red wheel and says *Network Gateway Timeout*. The day sister will be on my neck if the bed state isn't updated by four."

"The Wi-Fi does not reach this side room," Maureen said, pulling a spray bottle of surface sanitizer from her cart. "The walls are three bricks thick. Old Victorian masonry. You have to take the tablet out into the corridor by the fire extinguisher if you want the signal."

Chloe sighed loudly, dropping the stylus into her tunic pocket. "In my last hospital in Croydon, everything was automated. Barcodes on the patient wristbands, electronic drug dispensing carts, automated bed tracking. Here you have to walk twenty yards down a drafty hallway just to tell a computer someone stopped breathing."

"We used to write it in the daybook," Maureen remarked, spraying the metal rails of the bed with sanitizer. The cold, alcohol-scented mist settled over the painted steel. "The sister had a black fountain pen. She wrote the name, the time, the doctor who attended, and the name of the relative who was called. Then she closed the book and put it in the drawer. The book never had a gateway timeout."

Chloe looked at Maureen with a mixture of amusement and condescending pity. "Yeah, well, books can be burned, can't they? Anyone could tear a page out of an old paper book and nobody would ever know. With the cloud server, every keystroke is logged with an IP address and a timestamp. You can't rewrite history on a digital database."

Maureen wiped the bedframe with a clean microfibre cloth. The yellow duster glided smoothly over the cold metal tubing.

*You can't rewrite history on a digital database.*

The girl truly believed it. She had grown up in an era where data was treated as absolute, incorruptible truth. She did not understand that a digital system did not prevent history from being rewritten; it simply made the deletion effortless, invisible, and absolute.

When Dr. Rao had infused forty millimoles of potassium into Liam's arm in 2004, the hospital had to hide paper charts in basement vaults, forge coroner's certificates, and threaten nursing sisters with dismissal. The crime left physical traces: thumbprints on ink, paper fibers, purple rubber stamps, and cardboard folders tucked behind water pipes.

In Sterling's digital hospital, there would be no paper traces. With three clicks on a computer screen in London or Dublin, a clinical incident could be reclassified, an audit trail archived, and a young man's death erased from the institutional memory without leaving a single speck of sawdust or a scrap of torn paper. The digital record was not permanent; it was weightless, disposable, and entirely owned by whoever paid the server bills.

Chloe picked up her tablet and pushed through the door into the corridor, her rubber clogs squeaking against the polished floor.

Maureen was left alone in the room.

She walked to the window. Outside in the courtyard, the rain had stopped. A thin sliver of pale autumn moon hung above the chimneys of the boiler house, casting a cold, silver light across the slate roofs.

She unzipped the rubber mattress cover, stripped it from the foam core, and folded it into a tight bundle for the laundry chute. Then she picked up her spray bottle and swept the nozzle across the bare steel springs of the bed.

The vinyl mattress on the stripped hospital bed had a long, curved crease down the center where Mrs. Cleary's frail body had rested for three weeks. Maureen sprayed the blue waterproof fabric with disinfectant, working her yellow cloth in long, overlapping strokes from the head of the bed to the foot.

The smell of dying—that faint, sweetish odor of failing kidneys, stale sweat, and drying lung secretions—was slowly yielding to the sharp, clean sting of hospital alcohol and pine oil.

Maureen looked around the bare side room. On the bedside locker sat a small plastic drinking cup containing three dentures soaking in cloudy water, and an unopened get-well card featuring a photograph of a ginger kitten playing with a ball of red wool. Across the top of the card, someone had written in shaky blue ballpoint: *To Dearest Auntie Eileen, Get well soon and come home to tea, with love from Sharon and the boys.*

Sharon and the boys had not arrived in time. Mrs. Cleary had stopped breathing at ten past two, her thin chest giving a final, shallow flutter while the electronic tablet outside in the corridor was searching for a Wi-Fi gateway.

Maureen picked up the dentures, rinsed the plastic cup under the sluice tap, and placed the small plastic bag containing Mrs. Cleary's personal effects on the locker shelf for the bereavement officer.

She remembered her father's death on Ward 2 in 1968. In those days, when a patient died, the ward sister did not stare at an electronic screen. The sister went to the ward kitchen, brewed a pot of fresh tea, and sat with the widow in the side room for half an hour. The doctor came down from the mess, took off his spectacles, and spoke to the family with his own voice. The death was entered into the heavy bound register with black ink, signed by the sister and the house officer, and the book was laid in the drawer until the next shift.

There was dignity in that paper. It had weight. It smelled of ink and human hands. It could not be wiped out by a software update or encrypted behind a password that only a private billing company in Dublin possessed.

She looked through the glass observation window into bay one.

In the dim amber night-light, eighteen old men slept under their cellular blankets, their breath rising and falling like the quiet tide in an estuary. In six months, Vanguard would replace those cellular blankets with disposable synthetic throws, and replace the domestic cleaning staff with contract workers on eight pounds fifty an hour.

Maureen turned back to the stripped bed. She unclipped her spray bottle, setting the brass nozzle to a fine mist.

The smell of alcohol disinfectant in the side room was sharp enough to sting the back of Maureen's throat. She worked her yellow duster along the stainless steel bed rails, wiping away the faint grey film of dust that had settled since the morning shift.

Through the high window, the chimneys of the town's old textile mills stood like silent sentinels against the grey autumn sky. Most of those mills were empty now, their brick spinning sheds converted into self-storage units or left to rot behind corrugated iron fences.

Maureen paused with her cleaning cloth.

She thought of the young agency nurse Chloe, who had carried her electronic charting tablet into the hallway to find a Wi-Fi connection.

To Chloe and her generation, the past was an obsolete operating system. If an event was not recorded on an NHS cloud database with an encrypted timestamp and a digital signature, it had no administrative validity. They believed that by digitizing healthcare, society had eliminated human fallibility.

Maureen knew better.

She had watched the technology change for thirty years. In the 1990s, the management had brought in green-screen desktop terminals to track patient admissions. In 2005, they had brought in modular barcode scanners. In 2018, they had brought in touchscreen tablets.

With each new technology, the promises were identical: total transparency, zero errors, seamless coordination, and the complete elimination of administrative waste.

And with each new technology, the reality was identical: exhausted nurses spent half their shifts staring at loading screens, doctors clicked through clinical warning boxes without reading the text, and patients died in darkened side rooms while staff searched for missing Wi-Fi passwords.

Digital systems did not prevent institutional cruelty. They merely automated it.

When Dr. Rao had killed Liam in 2004, the hospital had required three days of frantic meetings, phone calls, and forged paper documents to conceal the negligence.

In 2025, an administrator like Alistair Sterling could reclassify an entire ward's mortality statistics with a single spreadsheet formula, export the variance to an offshore server in Dublin, and present a sanitized due diligence report to London venture capitalists without ever touching a sheet of paper or speaking to a bereaved mother.

She strips the rubber mattress cover and sprays the metal bedframe with cold disinfectant.

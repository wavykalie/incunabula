The mahogany table reflected the fluorescent ceiling lights like black water under street lamps. The four-page disciplinary charge sheet lay squarely between Maureen's folded hands and Alistair Sterling's gold cufflink.

"The formal disciplinary hearing is scheduled for Thursday at nine o'clock," Mrs. Crane said, her silver pen hovering above her notepad. "If the allegations of theft and gross data breach are upheld, the panel will have no alternative under trust governance but summary dismissal. Under NHS staffing protocols, a summary dismissal for record tampering carries an automatic red flag on the central electronic staff record, preventing any future employment within public healthcare or registered care facilities."

Maureen looked down at her hands. The yellow callus on her thumb was greyed with ingrained dust from thirty years of cleaning carts.

"I am sixty years old, Mrs. Crane," Maureen said. "I have worked in this building since the miners' strike. Do you think I am looking for a job as a sister in London?"

"It is not merely a question of future NHS employment, Miss Finch," Sterling interrupted, his voice dropping into that quiet, reasonable register that sounded like oil flowing over cold steel. "A criminal referral to the West Yorkshire Police involves formal interviews under caution, search warrants executed at your domestic residence, and public disclosure in the local magistrates' court. The local press in this town is very attentive to allegations of corruption and document theft within the infirmary."

He reached into his leather briefcase on the floor and brought out a second folder. This one was bound in stiff royal blue card with two brass split-pins through the margin. Across the cover was printed in embossed gilt lettering: *DEED OF COMPROMISE & MUTUAL SEPARATION UNDER SECTION 111A OF THE EMPLOYMENT RIGHTS ACT 1996.*

Sterling slid the blue folder across the mahogany table until it rested against Maureen's knuckles.

"There is an alternative pathway," Sterling said, leaning forward. "One that protects the dignity of all parties involved."

Maureen did not open the folder. "What is it?"

"A mutually agreed severance settlement," Sterling explained. "You submit an immediate voluntary resignation on grounds of early retirement. The trust drops all pending disciplinary inquiries and waives any criminal or civil referral regarding the archive incident. We provide an agreed neutral reference confirming thirty years of continuous domestic service with exemplary attendance."

He turned to page three of the agreement and tapped a typed clause with his clean finger.

"In consideration of your long service, the trust will pay a ex-gratia severance payment of three thousand five hundred pounds net of tax, payable within forty-eight hours of execution. In return, you execute a standard non-disclosure agreement regarding all historical clinical observations, and you deliver all original trust documentation currently in your possession to this office before noon on Friday."

Three thousand five hundred pounds.

The number hung in the chilled air of the boardroom like an electric wire.

It was enough to pay Kathleen's care home fees at Saint Anthony's for four months. Combined with Maureen's small state pension and what she had saved in her Post Office passbook, it would guarantee her mother's room in the memory-care unit through the coming winter, past the spring frost, all the way to June. It meant Kathleen would not be loaded into an ambulance and dumped in a municipal ward in Goole among strangers.

"And Liam?" Maureen asked, her voice scarcely louder than the hum of the air conditioner. "What happens to the boy's chart?"

Sterling's eyes remained completely steady. "The original file will be incorporated into the statutory destruction manifest and processed through the confidential on-site pulping unit on Friday afternoon, along with seventy tons of obsolete paper from the basement archives. The record will be permanently closed. No litigation, no newspaper articles, no public inquests."

He pulled a black fountain pen with a gold nib from his waistcoat pocket and unscrewed the cap, laying the instrument beside the signature line on page five.

"You have until Friday morning to consider the settlement, Miss Finch," Sterling said quietly. "If the signed agreement and the original file are delivered to my desk by nine o'clock on Friday, the settlement cheque will be handed to you across this table. If not, the disciplinary hearing proceeds, the police warrant is executed, and your employment terminates without notice."

Sterling turned to page four of the royal blue folder, where two schedules of covenants were printed in small, dense type beneath the heading *SCHEDULE B: CONFIDENTIALITY & REPUTATIONAL NON-DEROGATION*.

"The terms of the non-disclosure covenant are comprehensive, Miss Finch," Sterling explained, his voice maintaining that calm, therapeutic cadence that management consultants used when announcing redundancies. "Under Clause 9.2, you agree that you will not communicate with any print journalist, television broadcaster, digital publisher, or social media platform regarding any clinical event occurring at Saint Jude's between October 1995 and the date of execution. You further agree that you will not provide witness statements, affidavits, or informal recollections to any legal firm or advocacy group."

Mrs. Crane pointed her pen at the bottom paragraph. "Clause 9.4 provides for liquidated damages of ten thousand pounds in the event of an actionable breach, Miss Finch. That is a standard provision in executive and senior operational separations to ensure total post-settlement stability."

Maureen looked through the double-glazed windows at the chimney stacks of the boiler house.

Three thousand five hundred pounds.

In her head, she calculated the numbers she had lived with for six years:

Room 14 at Saint Anthony's: £920 per month.
Council flat rent: £310 per month.
Prepayment gas and electric: £95 per month.
Food and tea: £80 per month.

Three thousand five hundred pounds was not wealth. It was not justice. It was four months of breathing room. It was four months during which Kathleen would wake up in her warm room, eat her porridge with brown sugar, look out at the gravel garden, and not be moved to a county asylum ward sixty miles away where the nurses did not even know her name.

"And if I sign this paper," Maureen asked, "what does the reference say?"

Mrs. Crane turned to Schedule C. "The agreed reference confirms that Miss Maureen Finch was employed continuously as a domestic services assistant from 12 September 1997 to 24 October 2025. It states that your conduct, punctuality, and attendance were exemplary, and that you left the trust's service voluntarily on grounds of early retirement. It is signed by the Director of Human Resources on official trust stationery."

Maureen looked at the fountain pen lying on the pale oak desk. The gold nib gleamed in the recessed spotlight, sharp and polished like a scalpel.

Sterling turned the page of the deed of compromise, pointing to the payment authorization section at the foot of Schedule A.

"The three thousand five hundred pounds will be issued as a certified cashier's cheque drawn on National Westminster Bank," Sterling said, his voice completely level. "It will be handed to you in this office upon delivery of folder STJ-W4-04-OCT-18. You can deposit it immediately at the Commercial Street branch. The funds are cleared and available upon presentation."

Maureen looked at the document.

Three thousand five hundred pounds.

It was seventy weeks of her savings. It was four hundred hours of floor stripping and toilet scrubbing.

And to Alistair Sterling and the Vanguard Healthcare consortium, it was a rounding error: a tiny fractional adjustment on a legal invoice from their Leeds commercial solicitors.

"And if Vanguard's auditors ask why this payment was made?" Maureen asked.

Mrs. Crane answered, her tone crisp and practiced: "The payment is accounted for under Section 8 of the pre-acquisition restructuring budget as an ex-gratia long-service severance honorarium. It is fully compliant with trust financial standing orders and requires no board ratification."

Everything had been arranged. The legal machinery had accounted for every contingency, covered every audit trail, and smoothed every potential bump on the road to privatization.

Maureen folded her hands over her vinyl bag.

She was not selling her brother's memory. She was using their own corporate money to protect Liam's living mother.

The blue legal folder lies open between them, its white pages gleaming under the recessed spotlights.

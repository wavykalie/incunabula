The Citizens Advice bureau on Lowfield Road was located in an old prefabricated Portakabin parked behind the Methodist church hall. The interior smelled of damp chipboard, paraffin space heaters, and the sour wool of twenty wet overcoats hanging from a metal coat rack by the entrance.

It was eleven o'clock on Tuesday morning. Maureen sat on a folding plastic chair in Cubicle B, separated from the adjoining advisor by a thin partition of grey acoustic felt.

The legal volunteer was a young man named Martin, wearing a rumpled tweed jacket with leather elbow patches and wire-rimmed spectacles that kept sliding down his nose. He had two thick law reference manuals open on the laminate desk, their pages covered with yellow fluorescent highlighter marks.

"I have examined the documents you described, Miss Finch," Martin said, speaking in a low, measured voice so as not to carry through the partition. He pushed his spectacles up the bridge of his nose with his middle finger. "Under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998, an employee who discloses information regarding medical negligence or criminal concealment is theoretically protected against detrimental treatment and dismissal."

"Theoretically?" Maureen asked.

"In practice," Martin said, closing one of the reference books with a dry puff of dust, "whistleblower protection is an affirmative defence raised *after* you have been dismissed. If Mr. Sterling serves you with a summary dismissal for record theft on Thursday, your employment terminates immediately. Your salary stops. Your pension accrual freezes."

He pulled a blank notepad toward him and drew a straight horizontal line across the paper.

"To challenge the dismissal, we would have to file an ET1 claim form with the Employment Tribunal in Leeds," Martin continued. "Under current regional backlogs, the preliminary case management hearing would not be listed for eight to ten months. The full substantive liability hearing would take at least fourteen to eighteen months from the date of filing."

"Eighteen months," Maureen repeated. The words were flat, heavy.

"During those eighteen months, you would have zero income," Martin said gently. He looked at her worn coat, her rough hands. "Because you were dismissed for alleged gross misconduct involving theft of patient records, the Department for Work and Pensions would impose an automatic twenty-six-week sanction on your Universal Credit entitlements. You would receive no standard jobseeker's allowance. You would be disqualified from emergency local authority hardship grants. And because you have an open disciplinary flag on your electronic staff record, no registered care agency or cleaning contractor in Yorkshire would hire you."

Maureen looked at the grey acoustic felt partition. On the other side, an elderly man was weeping softly while an advisor explained the council tax arrears procedure.

"And the tribunal itself?" Maureen asked. "If we win?"

He took off his spectacles and rubbed the red marks on his nose.

"Miss Finch," Martin said softly, his voice dropping into a register of genuine, human sympathy. "I see cases like this every week. Big public bodies and private healthcare consortiums do not fight fair. They have legal retainers, media consultants, and unlimited time. They use delay as a weapon. They drag proceedings out until the claimant exhausts her savings, loses her home, and suffers a nervous collapse. You are sixty years old. You have an elderly mother in residential care who relies entirely on your monthly contributions."

He reached out and closed his manila file, pressing the cardboard edges together with both hands.

"The law is an instrument for allocating commercial risk," Martin said quietly. "It is not a machine for delivering justice to working-class families. If you fight this, the hospital will survive, Mr. Sterling will get his corporate promotion, and you will be left with nothing."

Martin the volunteer turned the pages of a heavy blue volume titled *Harvey on Industrial Relations and Employment Law*. His fingertips were grey with newsprint from the law digests he had been reading since dawn.

"I need to be very clear with you about the realities of the Employment Tribunal system, Miss Finch," Martin said, his voice dropping into a tone of quiet, unadorned honesty. "The government introduced mandatory ACAS early conciliation in 2014 specifically to filter out public interest claims. If you refuse Mr. Sterling's compromise agreement and he summarily dismisses you for theft on Thursday, ACAS will assign a conciliation officer in Sheffield. That officer's statutory remit is not to investigate medical negligence or uncover corporate fraud; his sole statutory remit is to broker a financial settlement between the parties."

"And if I refuse to settle?" Maureen asked.

"Then the trust's legal representatives—Capsticks or DAC Beachcroft, the major healthcare litigation firms in London—will lodge an ET3 response," Martin explained, pointing to a paragraph of small print. "Their first procedural application will be for an order striking out your claim on grounds that it has no reasonable prospect of success. They will argue that because the 2004 clinical incident occurred twenty-one years ago, any disclosure relating to it is time-barred under the Limitation Act 1980. They will also apply for a restricted reporting order under Rule 50 of the Employment Tribunal Rules of Procedure, preventing any publication of Dr. Rao's name or the hospital's internal memos until the full hearing."

He closed the volume with both hands, the heavy paper thudding against the desk.

"That means total secrecy for eighteen months," Martin said. "No press coverage. No public awareness. You would sit in your council flat with no income, watching your mother's health collapse, while three corporate solicitors in London bill the NHS four hundred pounds an hour to file procedural postponement applications."

Maureen looked at the young man's face. He was twenty-five, earnest, poorly paid, spending his Tuesday mornings in a drafty Portakabin trying to protect vulnerable pensioners from the grinding machinery of administrative law.

"You think I should take the money, don't you, Martin?" she asked softly.

Martin took off his spectacles and looked down at his desk. When he looked back up, his eyes were troubled, heavy with the weight of the advice he had to give.

"I think the system was built by men who went to school with Mr. Sterling," Martin replied quietly. "And it was not built to give justice to women who clean floors.

The legal volunteer closes his folder with a heavy sigh and tells her to think of her family first.

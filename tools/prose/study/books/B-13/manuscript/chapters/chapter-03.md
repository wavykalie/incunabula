# Chapter 3: Under the Sink

The key to number 14 Cedar Grove turned stiffly in the brass barrel. Maureen lifted the front door slightly by its rusted handle to stop the warped bottom rail from scraping the linoleum in the hall.

The flat smelled of paraffin heater wick, damp wallpaper, and the carbolic soap she used on her aprons. It was five-forty on Wednesday morning. Through the thin plasterboard partition of number 16, she heard the muffled whine of Mr. Henderson's electric shaver, followed by the clinking of his water pipes. The estate was waking: milk floats clattering on the speed bumps, the low diesel roar of the council refuse lorry backing out of the depot on Marsh Lane, and the distant hum of the six o'clock commuter train cutting through the cutting toward the junction.

She did not turn on the hall light. She hung her heavy winter coat on the bent wire hook behind the door, took off her work shoes, and stepped into her felt slippers. Her feet throbbed with a dull, familiar ache that started in her arches and ran up into the tendons of her calves. Seven miles of walking on sealed terrazzo every night, five nights a week.

In the kitchen, the greaseproof wrappers from her lunch bag went into the pedal bin. The green paper sheet remained on the formica table beside the salt cellar, glowing pale under the low ceiling.

Maureen knelt before the small cupboard beneath the kitchen sink. The twin melamine doors were swollen at the base where the U-bend had leaked four winters ago before the council housing depot sent a plumber with three rolls of PTFE tape. Inside, the dark recess smelled of caustic soda, old newspapers, and damp floorboards.

She moved aside the two-litre plastic jug of thick bleach and the carton of washing powder. Behind the cold lead riser pipe lay a heavy black plastic carrier bag, wrapped three times around its contents and bound with red parcel tape.

Janice opened the tall grey stationery locker beside the medicine fridge. The shelves held three identical cardboard cartons of blue vinyl gloves, stamped with the logo of an offshore procurement consortium in Leeds. Janice pulled out a pair and stretched them over her damp fingers. The thin plastic split immediately across the right knuckle with a dry pop.

"Seventeen pence a box cheaper than the old latex stock," Janice said, dropping the torn glove into the clinical waste bin. "The regional procurement circular called it supply-chain rationalisation. That was Sterling's first policy directive when he took over the facilities portfolio three years ago. Save three thousand pounds on disposable protective equipment, then spend forty thousand on management consultants to explain why ward staff suffer dermatitis."

She pulled out a second pair, working them gingerly over her thumbs. "They think a hospital runs on spreadsheets, Maureen. If a patient sits in a chair for six hours without a bed, the administrative portal logs it as an ambulatory assessment period rather than a delayed admission. If an auxiliary nurse works twelve hours without a meal break, the rota software records it as voluntary shift extension. Everything gets renamed until the blood and the vomit disappear from the balance sheet. Kathleen remembers how it used to be. When Kathleen worked at the textile mill in Stockport, the overseer docked fifteen minutes of wages if a bobbin jammed. Sterling doesn't dock wages. He simply abolishes the post and calls it operational consolidation."

Maureen wiped the stainless-steel worktop around the sink with a damp sponge. The smell of diluted carbolic acid rose from the drain, sharp and clean against the staleness of the basement air.

She pulled the bundle into the pale light of the single fluorescent tube overhead.

Inside the outer carrier bag were three older bags from a supermarket chain that had gone into liquidation in 1998. And inside those lay the bound volume.

It was an A3 hardback ward register, bound in dark green buckram cloth with marbled paper covers. The spine was split at the top where water or damp had perished the glue, but the heavy white cartridge paper inside was clean. Across the front, written in white chinagraph pencil, were the words: WARD 4 — CASUALTY & ACUTE ADMISSIONS — OCTOBER 2003 TO MARCH 2005.

Maureen opened the cover. The pages were ruled into twelve columns: Date, Time, Patient Name, Age, Unit Number, Diagnosis, Admitting Officer, Medication Administered, Nurse in Charge, Discharge/Transfer, Remarks.

Page 142. The entry was dated 12 October 2004.

Finch, Liam. 19. Unit 04/8821. Acute status asthmaticus. Dr. Henderson. Nebulised salbutamol, IV hydrocortisone, 40mmol KCl in 500ml saline.

Beneath that line, entered in the same purple ballpoint ink as the loose green slip from the recycling sack, was a second entry in a different hand:

04:15. Cardiac arrest. Ventricular fibrillation. Resuscitation x 35 min unsuccessful. Dr. Rao attending. Chart removed by administration.

Maureen laid her flat hand over the page. Her thumb callus, hard and yellow as horn from thirty years of squeezing mop levers and scrubbing copper brassware, rested against the ink of her brother's name.

She remembered Liam in this kitchen in the summer of 2003, twelve months before that night on Ward 4. He had been seventeen then, six feet tall and thin as a lathe, standing on an upturned wooden fruit crate to scrape the greasy gloss paint off the pantry door with an old wallpaper scraper. He had whistled through his front teeth—the gap where a cricket ball had caught him on the school pitch at twelve—a tuneless, cheerful noise that drove their mother to distraction.

"Liam," Kathleen had shouted from the scullery, her hands white with flour from the soda bread, "if you whistle that one more time I'll take the rolling pin to your shins."

"It's the radio, Mam," he had called back, laughing down at her over his shoulder. "Top forty. You wouldn't know music if it bit your ankle."

He had had grease on his collar and white paint dust in his eyelashes. Three days later, he had brought home his first apprentice wage packet from the timber yard: forty-two pounds and fifty pence in a brown paper envelope with his national insurance number stamped in purple. He had laid the notes on this very table, pushed them across the oilcloth to Kathleen, and taken two pounds back for the pictures.

"That's for the coal, Mam," he had said.

When he died, Kathleen did not weep at first. She sat in the front parlour with her coat on, holding his timber yard timecard between both hands until the card softened and tore along the crease. The trust sent a formal condolence card signed by the patient liaison officer, enclosing a printed leaflet entitled Coping with Sudden Bereavement in Acute Settings.

Two weeks later, Kathleen had taken the bus down to the hospital administrative building. Maureen had walked with her, holding her mother's arm as they stood before the enquiry desk in the main foyer. A junior clerk in a nylon blouse had told them that the medical records were confidential under the Data Protection Act, and that requests for post-mortem transcripts required an application through the county coroner's office with a fee of forty-five pounds.

They had paid the forty-five pounds with postal orders saved from the coal money. Six months later, a letter arrived on stiff cream paper from the coroner's clerk: The coroner has reviewed the clinical summary provided by St. Jude's Royal Infirmary and is satisfied that death occurred from natural causes secondary to acute bronchospasm. No formal inquest will be held.

Kathleen had taken the letter to the church hall, to the citizens' advice bureau, to the constituency office of the Member of Parliament. Each official had looked at the cream paper, noted the signature of the coroner, and folded it back into its envelope. 'The hospital has followed standard clinical protocols, Mrs. Finch. Without evidence of procedural irregularity, there is no legal basis to reopen the verdict.'

Maureen looked at the page before her. There was the procedural irregularity: 40mmol KCl in 500ml saline. Forty millimoles of potassium chloride, an electrolyte that stopped a heart within minutes if infused without cardiac monitoring and slow titration. A death logged, stamped, and then excised from the public record by the removal of the paper chart.

She had taken the volume from the recycling skip in November 2006, when the hospital first began clearing out the Victorian records annex behind the old mortuary. It had lain in an open wooden crate beside thirty other discarded books, soaked with rain at the edges, waiting for the pulp lorry. She had wrapped it in a plastic bin liner, slipped it beneath her cleaning overall, and carried it home on the night bus.

For twenty years it had stayed beneath the copper pipe, wrapped in plastic, silent and untouched.

She turned the pages backward. The entries ran through the winter of 2003: coal miners with silicosis from the pits out at Thorne; dockers crushed between barges at the salt docks; women from the cotton mill with byssinosis, their lungs seized by cotton dust. Every entry was written in the neat copperplate cursive of the old ward sisters, the ink fading from jet black to sepia. The hospital had been an institution of stone and paper then, governed by matrons who inspected the corners of pillowcases with wooden rulers. Now it was governed by risk managers with laminated badges and outsourced private cleaning contracts.

Outside, the rain slackened against the kitchen window. The glass was clouded with condensation, small drops trickling down the pane and pooling in the rotted timber sill. Across the garden strip, Mrs. Gidley was hanging out a gray nylon sheet on the rotary washing line, her wooden clothes pegs gripped between her teeth.

Maureen smoothed the loose green sheet from the hospital sack and laid it between pages 142 and 143 of the bound register. It fit precisely, the column lines matching the printed margins of the book.

The room grew colder as the boiler clicked off. She rewrapped the volume in the three plastic bags, pressed the parcel tape tight around the edges, and slid the parcel back into the dark recess behind the copper pipe.

Maureen sat at the formica table, her tea mug growing cold beside the salt cellar. Through the thin walls of the council flat, she could hear the water hammer in number 12, followed by Mrs. Higgins coughing her dry smoker's cough into the courtyard.

Her mind traveled back to the winter after Liam died, when the flat had been so quiet that the ticking of the kitchen clock sounded like a hammer against stone. Kathleen had refused to light the coal fire in the parlour, saying that burning coal when Liam wasn't there to warm his boots was a waste of four shillings a hundredweight. She had sat in the kitchen wrapped in an old tartan traveling rug, her feet resting on an upturned wooden crate, writing letters in pencil on lined paper torn from school exercise books.

'Dear Mr. Coroner,' Kathleen had written, her hand shaking with the tremors that were the first silent sign of the sickness to come. 'My boy Liam was nineteen. He had no heart trouble. The hospital told me he had an attack of asthma, but the nurse who came to the door said there was a mistake with the drip. I want to know what was in the bottle. He was my youngest child, and he had his whole life before him.'

She had written to the Member of Parliament, Mr. Callaghan. She had written to the Bishop of Liverpool. She had written to the General Medical Council in London. Every three months, an envelope with an official crest would arrive in the morning post. The letters were always polite. They were always typed on heavy, cream bond paper that smelled of typewriter ribbon and cedar. They always said that the clinical audit had been conducted in full accordance with NHS governance frameworks, that the coroner had certified natural causes, and that the matter was formally closed.

'They're waiting for me to die, Maureen,' Kathleen had said one evening, looking out through the rain at the brick wall of the substation. 'They think if they wait long enough, the paper will turn yellow and the people who knew the truth will be under the grass. But God has a book too, Maureen. God has every name written down, and He doesn't lose the pages in the wash.'

Maureen had believed her then. She had believed that truth was an enduring stone that time could not wear away. But sitting in the cold kitchen twenty years later, she knew that institutions did not fear God; they feared audit liabilities and legal discovery. If the paper under her sink were burned, Liam's death would exist only in the fading memories of an eighty-two-year-old woman who could not remember what she had eaten for breakfast, and a fifty-eight-year-old cleaner who spent her nights scrubbing disinfectant into linoleum.

She pushed the bleach bottles back into place. The melamine doors shut with a dull snap of their magnetic catch.

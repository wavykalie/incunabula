The metal fire escape on the north wall of the east wing hung four storeys above the tarmac service road. The iron steps were made of perforated galvanized grating that let the cold autumn rain drip through to the concrete below, slick with grease and black city soot.

It was ten minutes past two on Wednesday morning. The hospital below was dark, except for the harsh blue fluorescent glare of the pathology laboratories across the courtyard and the amber sodium floodlights mounted on the perimeter wall.

Maureen stood on the third-floor landing, sheltered beneath the overhang of the flat asphalt roof. The cold wind whipped around the brick corner, smelling of diesel exhaust, damp chimney brick, and the faint, bitter scent of disinfectant venting from the laundry flues.

The heavy steel fire door behind her clicked open four inches.

A hand reached out through the opening, catching the sleeve of Maureen's blue nylon tabard.

"Get in out of the light," Janice Bradley whispered.

Janice pulled Maureen into the concrete stairwell behind the door, letting the heavy metal leaf swing shut until the latch engaged with a sharp click.

Janice was wearing her navy sister's coat over her uniform, her hair unpinned and hanging in damp, grey strands around her neck. In the dim yellow glow of the bulk-head emergency light, her eyes looked sunken, ringed with dark purple shadows.

"Did you see the skips?" Janice asked, her breathing fast and shallow.

"I was down there till one," Maureen said. "Pumping out the silt. Sterling had them throw eight carts of ward books straight into the compactor."

"He has put cameras on the loading bay," Janice said, clutching Maureen's forearm with cold, tight fingers. "Two digital cameras on steel poles above the waste bins. Infrared night lenses wired straight to the security lodge. He caught one of the night porters taking an old leather medical dictionary out of the skip at midnight. Security sacked the boy on the spot. Marched him off the site with two guards."

"Sterling is looking for Liam's file," Maureen said.

"He knows you have it," Janice whispered. Her teeth were chattering, whether from the freezing draft in the stairwell or sheer panic, Maureen could not tell. "He went through the electronic card access logs for the records office yesterday afternoon. Your domestic cleaning pass was swiped on the east annex door at 6:28 on Thursday evening. The clerk left at 6:15. You were the only person in that room for forty minutes."

Maureen leaned her head back against the cold concrete wall. "I was dusting the counters and emptying the wastebins. That is my job."

"Sterling is not a fool, Maureen!" Janice hissed. "He knows the file disappeared between Thursday evening and Friday morning. He called me into his office again tonight. He said the trust had initiated a formal forensic audit into missing clinical documents. He said if the file is returned before Friday noon, the matter will be treated as an internal misfiling error with no disciplinary action. But if it is not returned, he is referring the case to the West Yorkshire Police for criminal theft of trust property."

Janice looked down at Maureen's hands, her eyes wide with terror.

"You have to give it back, Maureen," Janice pleaded, her voice breaking into a dry, desperate whisper. "If the police come, they will search your flat. They will tear up your floorboards. They will find the file, and they will find those notebooks you keep. You will go to prison, Maureen. And what happens to Kathleen then? Who will pay Saint Anthony's? Who will visit her on Saturdays?"

Maureen looked at the dark green painted fire door. Outside on the iron landing, the rain drummed against the metal steps with a hollow, tinny chatter.

"Janice," Maureen said softly. "The care home sent me a fee notice this morning. They are raising Kathleen's co-payment to nine hundred and twenty pounds a month starting next week."

Janice froze. Her hand dropped away from Maureen's sleeve. "Nine hundred... that is more than your wage."

"It is my whole wage and eighty pounds more," Maureen said. Her voice was flat, steady as an iron plumb line. "If I cannot pay by Friday, they will discharge her to the county ward in Goole. You know what happens to dementia patients when they move them to Goole."

Janice covered her mouth with both hands. She leaned against the concrete wall, her shoulders shaking silently.

"What are you going to do?" Janice whispered through her fingers.

Down in the service courtyard four storeys below, the headlights of a security patrol van swept across the wet tarmac, illuminating the high wire fence and the yellow skips standing in the rain.

The metal fire escape shuddered as a heavy gust of wind slammed against the corrugated iron siding of the laundry flues. Below them, in the dark delivery courtyard, the yellow skip was half-filled with dripping cardboard boxes, their contents swollen and unreadable under the driving rain.

Janice Bradley pulled her woollen coat tighter around her chest. Her teeth were still clicking together in the cold air, her eyes wide and dark in the yellow light of the emergency bulkhead lamp.

"Sterling had the IT security team pull the server logs for the ward tablets yesterday afternoon," Janice whispered, leaning close to Maureen's ear. "They are auditing every single entry made under my username between two thousand and four and two thousand and eight. He is looking for discrepancies in the pharmacy reconciliation logs."

"There are discrepancies in every ward log from those years, Janice," Maureen said quietly. "You told me yourself that doctors borrowed drugs from Ward 3 whenever the dispensary was shut."

"That was standard clinical practice then!" Janice wept, her fingers clutching Maureen's sleeve with desperate strength. "Every sister in the hospital did it! If a patient in bay two was in agony at three in the morning, you didn't leave him screaming while you waited for a junior doctor to find the night key to the central pharmacy. You went to the emergency stock cupboard, you drew the ampoule, and you settled the paperwork on Monday morning."

She looked through the iron mesh of the fire door, down into the rain-swept courtyard where the security patrol van was turning slowly around the waste compactors.

"Sterling doesn't care about standard clinical practice," Janice said bitterly. "He is applying twenty-twenty-five regulatory standards to nineteen-ninety-four nursing routines. He told me that if the missing Ward 4 register isn't recovered, the trust will refer my pension to the NHS Counter Fraud Authority for forensic review. Thirty-five years of night shifts, Maureen. Thirty-five years of cleaning bedsores, lifting twelve-stone stroke patients, and holding the hands of dying lads whose families couldn't get here in time. And he wants to take my pension and leave me with seventy pounds a week on pension credit."

Maureen looked at the dark silhouette of the municipal cemetery on the hill across the valley. Liam lay up there in the rain, under the wet grass, with no pension and no future.

"Janice," Maureen said, her voice dropping into a register of profound, sorrowful calm. "Sterling is bluffing. He cannot refer you to Counter Fraud without disclosing why the file went missing. If he tells the fraud squad that a potassium overdose chart disappeared from the basement, the police will open a homicide inquiry into Liam's death. That is the one thing Vanguard will not tolerate."

Janice stared at her, her breath catching in her throat.

"You don't know these corporate people, Maureen," Janice whispered. "They don't think like nurses or cleaners. They have lawyers who can turn black into white before the tea gets cold."

From the stairwell above them, a metal latch rattled as someone began descending the fire stairs.

The metal fire door clicks shut behind them as the night wind whips through the stairwell.

The sluice room at the rear of Ward 4 measured six feet by nine. The walls were lined from floor to ceiling with white ceramic tiles that had craze-cracked with age, their glazed surfaces patterned with microscopic brown spiderwebs of old steam and bleach.

In the corner, the cast-iron macerator rumbled like a grinding mill, swallowing a cardboard bedpan with a wet, throbbing roar before flushing with twelve gallons of cold water.

Maureen stood over the deep stainless steel sink, rinsing her mop head under the hot water tap. The steam rose around her face, carrying the sharp, clean sting of carbolic soap and pine disinfectant. She took the mop handle in both hands, turned the head upside down, and pressed the heavy cotton yarns into the mechanical lever wringer of her cart.

The wooden handle of the wringer came down with a heavy creak. Grey water cascaded through the perforated zinc basket, splashing into the lower bucket.

The sluice room door pushed open.

Alistair Sterling stood in the doorway. The room was so narrow that his charcoal wool coat brushed against the wall tiles as he stepped inside. The door swung shut behind him on its brass pneumatic closer, cutting off the low murmur of the night ward.

"A very secluded workspace, Miss Finch," Sterling said. He looked around at the stainless steel draining racks, the rows of clean plastic washbowls, and the wall-mounted cupboard marked *POISONS & CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES — RESTRICTED*.

"It gets the job done," Maureen said. She did not release her grip on the wringer lever.

Sterling rested one gloved finger against the edge of the stainless steel sink. "I spent an hour with the trust legal team this afternoon. We were reviewing historical files from the 2004 reorganization. A very untidy period in the trust's governance history. Decentralized procurement, paper documentation, minimal oversight."

"That was before the computers came in," Maureen said.

"Indeed," Sterling agreed. "Before digital accountability. It appears that in October 2004, following a clinical incident on this very ward, a comprehensive internal inquiry was initiated by the regional health authority. A file was compiled: nursing observation sheets, doctor statements, internal incident memos. That file was assigned a reference code: STJ-W4-04-OCT-18."

He paused, watching her face with careful, clinical detachment.

"Curiously," Sterling continued, "when the shredder team reached that shelf bay in the basement records vault on Tuesday, the box was intact, but folder eighteen was missing. The green suspension rail was empty."

Maureen kept her weight leaned against the wringer lever. The cold metal of the handle pressed into the palm of her right hand, right against the yellow callus on her thumb.

"The basement flooded in 2007," she said, repeating the words she had spoken in the sub-basement corridor. "Water came up over the bottom racks. The porters cleared out three cartloads of rotten card."

"The water damage was confined to bays twelve through sixteen," Sterling countered smoothly. "The clinical incident archives were stored on bay four, six feet above the high-water line. Furthermore, the dust pattern on the suspension rail indicated that folder eighteen was removed within the last forty-eight hours."

He took half a step closer. The sharp scent of his citrus cologne clashed with the pungent carbolic of the mop bucket.

"This trust is entering a twenty-five-year private finance partnership with Vanguard," Sterling said, his voice dropping to a low, conversational tone. "The transaction value exceeds one hundred and forty million pounds. The contract terms include an indemnity clause regarding unresolved historical clinical liabilities. Any document that creates ambiguity regarding past clinical performance could delay financial close by three months."

"I am a domestic cleaner, Mr. Sterling," Maureen said. "I scrub the floors and empty the linen bins. I do not read the trust contracts."

Sterling smiled—a thin, deliberate flexing of his lips that did not warm his eyes.

"You have worked in this building for twenty-eight years, Miss Finch. Your brother died in bed fourteen in October 2004. You have a mother in Saint Anthony's whose nursing fees exceed your monthly net pay by four hundred and twenty pounds."

He looked down at the zinc mop bucket between them.

"I am an administrator, Miss Finch. I look at balance sheets, data flows, and risk matrices. But I also understand human motivation. If an employee were to find herself in possession of obsolete trust records, she might imagine they possess commercial or sentimental value. She might believe they could be used to extract historical accountability."

He leaned in slightly. His breath smelled of mint lozenges.

"Under the Theft Act 1968, the unauthorized removal of hospital records is a criminal offence carrying a custodial sentence. Under the Data Protection Act, breach of patient confidentiality carries unlimited civil penalties. The police are very unsympathetic to staff who compromise public healthcare reorganizations."

Maureen lifted the mop head from the wringer. The wet cotton strings hung heavy and straight, steaming in the cool air of the room.

"If you think I have stolen something from your filing cabinets, Mr. Sterling, you can call the security supervisor and search my locker."

The sluice room walls hummed with the vibration of the laundry extractors on the floor below. The stainless steel sink was scratched and clouded from thousands of gallons of caustic detergent and hot water, its brass drain ring worn down to pink copper where the chain had rubbed for thirty years.

Sterling did not look at the sink. He looked at Maureen's hands, noticing the thick yellow skin on the inner joint of her thumb and the dark purple veins that stood out along the back of her wrists.

"You have been a very reliable domestic worker, Miss Finch," Sterling said, his voice dropping into a quiet, conversational cadence that had the measured precision of a legal deposition. "Twenty-eight years on this ward. Zero unexcused absences. Exemplary appraisal scores for floor sanitation and infection control. When Vanguard takes over the facilities contract, their regional operations manager will retain a small core of experienced team leaders on supervisory pay scales. Thirty-two thousand pounds per annum. Full sick pay. A company pension plan with matching contributions up to six percent."

Maureen turned off the hot water tap. The sudden silence in the narrow tiled room was heavy, broken only by the drip-drip of the wringer lever into the zinc bucket.

"And what would a team leader have to do for thirty-two thousand pounds, Mr. Sterling?" she asked.

Sterling smiled—a small, clean flexing of his lips that did not touch his eyes. "A team leader ensures total operational alignment across the cleaning rosters. She identifies inefficiencies. She reports breaches of equipment protocol. And above all, she understands that the hospital trust cannot function if historical grievances are allowed to disrupt modern capital transactions."

He leaned one shoulder against the doorframe, his charcoal wool coat brushing the white tiles.

"The London investment syndicate that is funding the Vanguard acquisition has strict governance criteria," Sterling explained smoothly. "Before they release seventy-four million pounds of capital for the new surgical wing, their risk assessors require a clean legal profile. If an obsolete clinical record from twenty years ago is leaked to the regional media or used to mount an historical negligence claim, the financial syndicate will invoke the material adverse change clause in the lease agreement. The interest rate on our capital debt will increase by two hundred and fifty basis points. That represents two point four million pounds a year in debt service. That is the budget for thirty-four staff nurses and two geriatric wards."

Maureen picked up her mop. She held the wooden handle upright between her boots, her palms resting on the rounded pommel.

"Liam was not a balance sheet, Mr. Sterling," Maureen said, her voice hard as an iron chisel. "He was a nineteen-year-old boy who came in here with a broken collarbone and never walked out."

Sterling's expression did not soften. "In modern healthcare administration, Miss Finch, every clinical outcome is ultimately reflected on a balance sheet. The only question is whether that balance sheet serves the living or the dead."

He straightened his collar with two fingers.

Sterling smiles thinly and reminds her that taking trust property carries criminal penalties.

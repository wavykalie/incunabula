# Chapter 1: The Sluice and the Spiral

The yellow mop bucket held twelve litres of water from the low copper tap in the sluice room. Maureen Finch added three capfuls of pine concentrate from the dented plastic bottle beneath the deep porcelain sink. The vapour rose off the galvanised metal, catching the back of her throat with the sour, familiar bite of boiled resin and cold iron. She pressed the wringer lever down with both hands, throwing her shoulder over the joint until the perforated press met the rim and four heavy drops hit the drain tray below. Four drops. Her mother had taught her that count thirty-five years ago on the scullery stones of the municipal baths down by the canal: wring until four drops fall, no fewer, or you smear greasy gray film across clean terrazzo.

Ward 4 slept in five distinct keys. Down at the north end by the fire doors, the oxygen concentrator on bed twenty-two kept up an irregular hiss, like a bicycle pump worked into wet grass. Nearer the nurses' station, the two-second hum of the drug refrigerator clicked in every eleven minutes, rattling the glass vials of insulin and heparin against their wire racks. Between them lay the breathing of thirty elderly men in various stages of postoperative repair, cardiac observation, and stroke recovery: some rattling softly behind drawn cotton curtains, others drawing long, whistling draughts through slack lips, their false teeth soaking in pink plastic cups on the bedside lockers.

The ward smelled of steamed cabbage from yesterday's lunch, floor sealant, and the sharp chemical reek of chlorhexidine skin wash. Beneath those lay the older, deeper smell of the hospital itself: eighty years of damp river brickwork, boiler coal, and grease trapped behind cast-iron radiators that had not been dismantled since the coronation.

Maureen wheeled her rubber-tyred trolley over the brass dividing strip between the main corridor and Bay Two. The casters were greased weekly by Arthur, the night porter, but the left front wheel carried a flat nick from an encounter with an iron bedframe six months back. Clack-clog. Clack-clog. The corridor walls were painted that dull institutional two-tone common to every hospital built under the Attlee administration: dark olive gloss up to waist height, then a crumbly off-white emulsion reaching to the high acoustic tiles. At intervals of twenty feet, plastic hand sanitiser dispensers jutted from the plasterboard, their drip-trays stained blue with dried alcoholic gel.

"Who's that?" Mr. Garside asked from bed nine.

He had been admitted on Sunday evening following a transient ischaemic attack in the municipal library. His left eye was covered with an oval gauze pad taped down with brown surgical plaster. The left corner of his mouth drooped toward his stubbled jaw, but his right eye was wide and dark in the amber spill of the low baseboard nightlights. A plastic cannula was taped to the back of his right hand with clear dressing film, the polyurethane line taped in a neat loop up his wrist to prevent accidental snagging on the tubular steel bed rails.

St. Jude's had been constructed in 1894 as an infirmary for river dockers and their families. Beneath the suspended ceiling tiles, with their synthetic perforations and recessed fluorescent strip lights, the original Victorian brick arches remained solid and damp-chilled. In the service crawlways behind the linen closets, massive cast-iron steam pipes clanked and groaned like ship engines whenever the basement boilers cycled on. Maureen remembered the ward from thirty years ago, before the regional healthcare trust partitioned the long Nightingale hall into four-bed bays with pale laminate dividers. In those days, Sister Colquhoun had kept a bound admissions register with marbled board covers on the high walnut desk by the entrance, recording every patient's name and trade in purple fountain-pen script: boiler caulker, lighterman, cooper's apprentice, docker's widow.

Now the patients arrived through the computerised triage office downstairs, reduced to barcode wristbands and alphanumeric bed allocations on the whiteboard behind Janice's desk. Yet the dust remained identical. It gathered in the corners beneath the cast-iron radiators, a grey silt of shed skin cells, lint from institutional flannelette sheets, and dried road grit tracked in on visitor shoes. Maureen knew every seam of the linoleum by heart. She knew which brass screws had worked loose along the door saddles, and which window casements rattled when the east wind blew across the estuary.

She thought of Kathleen's first month at St. Jude's, before the illness stripped away her sister's sharp, sarcastic humour. Kathleen had sat upright in Bed 4, demanding loose-leaf tea instead of the instant catering granules Janice brewed in an electric urn. Kathleen had announced that a hospital incapable of boiling an honest kettle had no business calling itself an infirmary. Maureen had laughed then, standing beside the bed with a paper bag of warm Eccles cakes from the bakery on Duke Street. That was before the quiet arrived. That was before Kathleen ceased asking for tea, ceased asking for the evening paper, ceased asking why the curtains had to be drawn at eight o'clock.

"Just Finch, Mr. Garside," Maureen said softly. She parked the trolley against the wall, clear of the emergency call cords and the wall-mounted suction canisters. "Doing the skirting."

"The ferry's late," he said. He did not turn his head; his neck was stiff against the hard hospital pillow, his gray hospital gown bunched at the throat. "The slipway at Woodside's frozen over. We'll miss the seven-fifteen across."

"It's mild tonight, Mr. Garside," she said. She dropped her clean microfibre duster into the warm rinse basin. "No frost on the glass."

"You don't know the river like I do," he murmured. His fingers, thickened at the knuckles from fifty years of boilermaking in the lower dockyards, picked at the yellow woollen fringe of his cellular blanket. "Water's like black slate when the wind comes round from the bar. My father had his coat collar turned up to his cap. He had two ham sandwiches in greaseproof paper inside his pocket. He said to me, 'Arthur, keep your hands inside your mittens until we dock at the landing stage.'"

Maureen knelt on the cold linoleum by the skirting board beneath the heating radiator. The iron pipes clanked with trapped air, a slow, uneven percussion that ran through the entire north wing. She wiped the gray lip of the vinyl where dust gathered behind the electrical conduits and the medical gas outlets. When she reached the foot of bed nine, she sat back on her heels and drew the small blue spiral notebook from her inner apron pocket.

The notebook cost sixty-five pence at the newsagent beside the bus terminus. It was four inches wide, ruled in pale blue faint lines, with an elastic band looped through the wire coil. Her pen was a clear plastic ballpoint with a chewed cap.

She propped the pad on her knee and wrote:

14 Nov. Bay 2, bed 9. Arthur Garside, 79. Woodside slipway frozen. Father's coat collar turned up. Ham sandwiches in greaseproof paper. Keep your hands inside your mittens until we dock.

She had twenty-two filled notebooks in a shoebox beneath her bed at Cedar Grove. She did not know why she took down the names and the fragments. No doctor asked for them on the morning clinical rounds. The consultants walked past at nine o'clock with four junior doctors trailing behind them like goslings, their eyes fixed on the electronic tablets cradled in their forearms. The tablets recorded oxygen saturation, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine clearance, and fluid balance in neat blue bar charts. They recorded nothing about ham sandwiches on the Woodside ferry in 1954, or the girl in the red mackintosh who worked the ticket punch at the tram terminus before the tracks were torn up for the ring road. When patients died on Ward 4—and they died on average twice a week during the winter bronchial peaks—their paper charts were bundled into brown envelopes and sent to the archive racks in the sub-basement, while their words simply vanished into the extractor fans.

"Are you still there?" Mr. Garside whispered.

"Still here, Mr. Garside," Maureen said.

"Tell my father I have the return fare in my waistcoat pocket," he said. His voice was growing thicker, slipping into the heavy, rhythmic cadence of sleep. "Two pennies for the boat, and a halfpenny for the tram at the pier head."

"I'll tell him," she said.

She rinsed her cloth in the pine water, wringing it over the drain until the drops stopped falling. Across the bay, the plastic wall clock with the red sweep-second hand ticked past three-thirty. A tea trolley clattered on the service landing outside, and the smell of toasted bread drifted through the fire doors. Arthur the porter pushed through with two wire baskets of clean specimen bottles, his rubber shoes squeaking on the wet tiles.

"Quiet turn, Maureen?" Arthur asked, pausing by the water cooler.

"Mr. Garside's watching the ferry," Maureen said.

Arthur glanced at the bed, nodding with the tired familiarity of a man who had worked forty years of night turns in the same corridors. "They always go back to the water when the chest gives out. My old uncle was on the coal barges; spent his last four days shouting at the lock-keeper at Ellesmere Port." He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand. "Watch the basement stairs when you go down for the fresh mopheads. The radiator valve by laundry gave out at two; floor's like a skating rink."

"I'll take the service lift," she said.

Maureen picked up her bucket by its wire bail handle. The water inside sloshed against the yellow polypropylene wall, throwing a clean pine wave that broke over the wringer teeth. Beyond the bay windows, the lights of the chemical works across the river burned steady through the rain: orange flares on tall steel towers, silent against the dark clouds. The night shift had two hours left before the morning sisters clocked on at six. Two hours of wiping baseboards, mopping the sluice floor, and checking the paper towels in the staff lavatory.

She looked down at her hands. The nails were trimmed blunt, the skin around the cuticles reddened and cracked from constant immersion in warm soda solution. A blue bruise mottled the flesh between her thumb and index finger where she had pinched herself on the laundry chute door on Monday. Small marks, the everyday currency of a body worked hard in an old building.

Her mother's hands had looked identical at fifty-eight: the same thickened knuckles, the same yellow patch of hardened skin on the inner thumb joint from wringing coarse cleaning cloths. For forty years Kathleen had scrubbed the municipal wash-houses down by the canal basin, carrying home twenty-four shillings in a brown packet every Friday noon. Kathleen had known the names of every family on the dockside, who owed money to the coal merchant, who had a boy in the reformatory at Red Bank, and who had died of the croup in the cellars along Duke Street. When the corporation tore down the wash-houses to build the ring road in nineteen seventy-four, they had given Kathleen an eight-day chiming clock and thirty pounds in redundancy pay. The clock had broken three months later when Liam dropped his school satchel on the sideboard, but Kathleen had kept it on the mantelpiece anyway, its brass pendulum frozen at quarter past four.

Maureen stepped back into the central corridor. The floor was dark gray where the wet pine water lay, reflecting the long fluorescent tubes above in pale white rectangles. She drew her mop across the linoleum in broad figure-eights, pulling the dirty water backward into the center of the lane, leaving a clean border along the skirting boards.

Behind the closed door of Bay One, a bell rang once—a short, single chime that signaled a patient requesting a commode. Nurse Corcoran came out of the ward kitchen with a plastic bedpan in her hand, her blue paper tabard rustling against her thighs. She gave Maureen a quick, tight nod, the kind of nod shared by people who had spent eight hours on their feet without sitting down.

"Mr. Garside's settled," Maureen said as the nurse walked past.

"Good," Nurse Corcoran murmured. "His blood pressure was through the ceiling at midnight. The junior doctor wanted to give him labetalol, but Sister Bradley told him to wait until the morning registrar comes on."

Maureen wheeled her cart past the linen cupboards to Bay Three. The men in Bay Three were younger: two post-operative appendix cases from Monday, a scaffold worker with three fractured ribs from the new housing development out at Lowfield, and a long-distance lorry driver with a deep vein thrombosis in his left calf. They slept heavily, their breathing slow and untroubled by memories of frozen river ferries or dead fathers in woollen caps.

She knelt by the radiator beneath the window. Outside, a shunting engine blew its whistle in the goods yard behind the docks—two long, low blasts that died away into the rain.

She rose to her feet, her knees cracking in the quiet ward. Her apron pocket felt heavy with the little book, a cool square resting against her thigh. She pushed the trolley forward into Bay Three, watching the pale reflections of the streetlights swimming across the wet floor behind her.

Mr. Garside's breathing settled into a deep, rhythmic sigh. The gauze pad over his eye fluttered once, then lay still against his cheek.

Maureen closed the notebook with a sharp snap of the elastic band.

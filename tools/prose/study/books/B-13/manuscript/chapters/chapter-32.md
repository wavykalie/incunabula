The kitchen clock above the gas stove had a round white face with black Roman numerals that clicked forward every sixty seconds with a dry, mechanical shudder. It was ten minutes past two on Friday morning.

The council flat was cold. Maureen had turned off the paraffin heater an hour earlier to save fuel, leaving only the faint smell of burned oil lingering in the kitchen air.

She stood at the porcelain kitchen sink, washing her hands with a bar of yellow carbolic soap. The cold tap water stung her chapped knuckles, washing away the smell of ink and old paper. She dried her hands on the clean linen roller towel behind the door.

On the kitchen table sat a clean, heavy plastic carrier bag from the Co-op, printed with green shamrocks.

Maureen picked up the original 2004 green folder: *WARD 4: ADMISSIONS & CLINICAL INCIDENTS — WEEK ENDING 17 OCTOBER 2004*.

She placed the green folder flat into the bottom of the carrier bag. She folded the plastic handles neatly across the top.

She walked into the small hallway.

Her heavy winter coat—the dark grey woollen coat she had bought from the Saint Vincent de Paul charity shop four winters ago—hung from the brass hook behind the front door.

Beneath the photocopies, nestled against her hip, sat the twenty-two blue spiral notebooks, tied together in two compact bundles with green garden twine.

She reached for the coat on the hook.

Maureen slid her arms into the heavy wool sleeves of her coat.

The fabric was cold against her wrists, but as she pulled the heavy lapels across her chest and fastened the large horn buttons, the warmth of her own body began to fill the wool.

Maureen picked up the Co-op carrier bag from the table.

She reached for her woollen coat on the hall hook.

Her coat was heavy, lined with thick grey flannel that smelled of rain and lavender. Inside the deep interior poacher's pockets, the three brown manila envelopes lay flat against her ribs.

Beside the envelopes, resting against her hip, sat the two compact bundles containing the twenty-two blue spiral notebooks, tied securely with garden twine.

She put on the coat. The weight was familiar, comforting, like an iron cuirass worn into battle.

She touched her pocket to confirm that her brass house keys, her coin purse, and her bus pass were in place. Everything was ready.

She would save Kathleen.

She would protect her mother's room for the winter, ensure her peace, and let her live out her final days surrounded by kindness and gentle care.

And when she returned to this council flat on Sunday evening, the notebooks would be waiting on the table, ready to receive the memory of the living as well as the dead.

The grandfather clock in the church tower down on Lowfield Road struck quarter to three, its slow, heavy chimes floating across the roofs of the sleeping estate.

Maureen picked up the clean Co-op plastic bag from the kitchen table.

Inside lay the green 2004 folder. She looked at the brittle paper tabs through the plastic: *OCTOBER 2004: BED 13 TO 18.*

She felt no sadness for the paper.

The paper was merely the weapon the hospital had used to silence her mother and protect its surgical accreditation.

The real record—the living truth of Liam's nineteen years, his chisels, his laugh, his crooked smile, and his deep love for his mother—lay safely hidden in the inner pockets of her heavy grey coat.

She walked to the kitchen window and looked out into the night.

She had given that building thirty years of her life. She had given it her youth, her strength, and her brother's bones.

Now she was going back to take her mother's peace.

The kitchen in the third-floor flat was silent except for the low hiss of the gas ring beneath the kettle. Outside, the night was cold and clear, the stars bright above the dark ridge of the moors.

Maureen picked up the clean Co-op carrier bag containing Liam's original green folder.

She checked the handles, making sure the plastic was strong and untorn. Inside, the green card rested flat, ready for its final journey down Lowfield hill.

She thought of the twenty-two blue notebooks resting in the inner pockets of her heavy coat.

Those books were her true harvest.

In those pages, she had preserved what the hospital had tried so desperately to erase: the names, the voices, the smiles, and the final quiet words of eight hundred ordinary working people who had lived and died in this valley.

She was not leaving the hospital empty-handed.

She was taking away its true history—the history of human kindness, endurance, and love that no private consortium could ever buy or sell.

She walked to the hall mirror, straightened her woollen scarf, and checked the brass latch of the front door.

In two hours, the day would begin.

The kitchen clock ticks loudly in the dark as she pulls on her woollen coat.

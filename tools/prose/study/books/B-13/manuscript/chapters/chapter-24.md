The sister's office on Ward 4 was a glass-partitioned box situated between bays two and three. Inside, the walls were covered with cork noticeboards displaying laminated notices: *HAND HYGIENE AUDIT COMPLIANCE*, *FIRE EVACUATION PROCEDURES*, *DUTY ROSTER OCTOBER 2025*, and a small yellowed photograph of the ward staff at the Queen's Golden Jubilee street party in 2002.

It was half past five on Friday morning. The ward was beginning its slow transition from night to day. Down the hallway, the tea trolley was rattling toward bay one, accompanied by the clatter of plastic mugs and the smell of hot toast.

Maureen pushed open the office door with her hip, carrying a bundle of fresh hand towels for the staff washbasin.

The desk was empty. Sister Bradley had clocked off at five o'clock, leaving her handover notes in a red plastic folder beside the computer keyboard.

Above the desk hung a row of twenty-four wooden pigeonholes, constructed from old mahogany packing crates by a hospital joiner forty years ago. Each pigeonhole had a paper label taped beneath it with a staff member's name.

Maureen set the towels on the shelf beside the basin.

Her eyes drifted to the bottom row of pigeonholes. The third box from the left was marked: *M. FINCH (DOMESTIC).*

Inside the wooden slot lay a single object.

It was a heavy manila envelope, eight inches by eleven, made of stiff reinforced paper with a metal clasp. Across the front, typed in bold black letters from an office laser printer, was the inscription:

*ST. JUDE'S ROYAL INFIRMARY NHS TRUST*
*DIRECTORATE OF CORPORATE GOVERNANCE & HUMAN RESOURCES*
*PRIVATE & CONFIDENTIAL — STRICTLY ADDRESSEE ONLY*
*DELIVER BY HAND TO: MISS MAUREEN FINCH*

Maureen stood motionless before the wooden pigeonholes. The hum of the ward refrigerator seemed to grow louder, filling the small glass office with a heavy, vibrating drone.

She reached out her hand. Her fingers touched the rough card of the envelope. It was thick, heavy, containing multiple folded sheets of twenty-pound legal stationery.

She pulled the envelope from the slot.

Through the clear window of the envelope, she could read the first three lines of the typed header:

*FORMAL NOTICE OF INVESTIGATION MEETING*
*ALLEGED MISCONDUCT: UNAUTHORIZED REMOVAL & RETENTION OF CONFIDENTIAL TRUST RECORDS*
*DATE: MONDAY 27 OCTOBER, 10:00 HOURS*
*LOCATION: EXECUTIVE BOARDROOM, LEVEL 4*
*PANEL: MR. A. STERLING (DIRECTOR OF TRANSITION), MRS. V. CRANE (HEAD OF HR)*

Down the corridor, the heavy double doors of the ward entrance banged open.

The morning shift had arrived. A flurry of brisk footsteps, laughing voices, and the rustle of stiff cotton tunics echoed down the hallway as six day nurses walked in to take handover.

Maureen did not open the envelope. She did not need to read the remainder of the text.

A young staff nurse named Kelly, who had joined Ward 4 three months earlier from the university nursing school in Sheffield, walked into the office to hang up her coat.

"Morning, Maureen," Kelly said cheerfully, tucking her blonde ponytail into her collar. "Freezing out there today, isn't it? The heating on the estate bus was broken again. I had to sit on my hands the whole way from Highfield."

"The wind is coming off the moors, love," Maureen said, folding her cleaning cloths and placing them in the wire basket of her cart.

Kelly looked at the pigeonholes above the desk, noticing the heavy manila envelope resting in Maureen's slot.

"Looks like HR have been busy," Kelly remarked casually, reaching for her silver nursing fob watch on the shelf. "Sterling had three security guards in the records office yesterday afternoon. They were carrying out cardboard boxes with red hazard tape around them. My friend Sarah in outpatients said the trust is auditing everyone's contract before the Vanguard transfer."

"They have a lot of paperwork to finish," Maureen said.

"Sarah says if we sign the new contract by December, Vanguard is giving everyone a three-hundred-pound transition bonus," Kelly said, pinning the fob watch to her tunic pocket. "That'll pay for my Christmas shopping in Leeds. My mum says private management is better anyway. She says public hospitals are too slow and wasteful."

"Look after your patients, Kelly," Maureen said softly.

"Always do, Maureen," Kelly smiled, picking up her handover clipboard. "See you on Tuesday."

She pushed through the glass door into the bustling corridor, where the breakfast trolley was already clattering past the bays.

Maureen stood alone in the small office for thirty seconds. She felt the heavy envelope through the fabric of her coat pocket. The card was stiff, unyielding, carrying the full weight of the institution's legal machinery.

"Morning, Maureen," Dave grunted, wiping his brow with his blue cotton sleeve. "They've locked the loading bay gates again. Big Vanguard security guard with an orange high-vis jacket told me I had to scan every laundry bag on a handheld computer before I put it in the van."

"Did you scan them, Dave?" Maureen asked, pausing with her mop bucket.

"Scanned my foot," Dave laughed, a low, dry chuckle. "Told him if he wanted sixty dirty sheets scanned, he could pull them out of the bags himself and smell the disinfectant. He went back inside his little plastic booth and shut the window."

Maureen smiled, but her fingers remained pressed against the heavy manila envelope in her apron pocket.

Inside that envelope lay the summons to the fourth floor. The formal investigation meeting. The legal machinery of the trust board, assembled to crush a domestic cleaner who had looked too closely at the archive boxes.

She looked down the long corridor toward the entrance arch. Above the double swing doors, the plastic clock showed ten minutes to six.

In four hours, she would walk into the executive suite. She would sit in a leather chair opposite Alistair Sterling and Mrs. Crane. She would hear the allegations of theft, data breach, and gross misconduct.

Her heart beat with a steady, quiet rhythm, completely calm.

She had survived thirty years of hospital basements, flooded drains, and dying wards. She was not afraid of a man with an iPad and a gold tie clip.

She slides the stiff manila envelope into her pocket as the morning shift nurses clock in.

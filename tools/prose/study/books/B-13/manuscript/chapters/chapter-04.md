# Chapter 4: The Notice on the Pigeonhole

The bus to Saint Anthony's Residential Home took forty minutes from the estate, crawling through the morning traffic on the ring road while rain smeared the upper deck windows with a greasy gray haze.

Maureen sat on the lower deck, directly behind the driver's cab where the heater fan blew a lukewarm stream of air smelling of wet overcoats and diesel oil. Her handbag lay across her lap. Inside, beneath her purse and her spare handkerchief, was the white envelope she had received from the council benefits office on Monday.

Saint Anthony's occupied an Edwardian red-brick villa on the brow of Highfield Hill. It had once been the home of a wealthy worsted manufacturer, but the carriage drive was now covered in asphalt and bordered by plastic wheelie bins. Inside the heavy oak double doors, the air was warm, sweet, and close: lavender air freshener, overboiled carrots, and the medicinal tang of chlorhexidine skin wash.

The manager, Mrs. Appleby, was sorting the post at the reception counter. She was fifty, with pearl earrings and a cardigan the colour of tinned salmon.

"Morning, Maureen," she said. Her smile did not reach the lines around her mouth. She drew a long manila envelope from the row of wooden pigeonholes behind the desk. "I was going to put this in the post, but since you're here, it saves the stamp."

The letterhead was printed in dark blue ink: Saint Anthony's Care Group (Northern) Ltd. Fee Schedule Revision: Memory Care Unit.

Maureen did not open the flap. "The top-up?"

"Head office sent the circular yesterday," Mrs. Appleby said. She lowered her voice, leaning over the counter so the two residents dozing in the vinyl armchairs by the radiator would not hear. "Gas and electricity have gone up forty percent since October. The trust top-up from the local authority only covers the standard nursing rate. For the specialist dementia suite—the extra staffing ratio for the night turn—the family contribution is going to eight hundred and forty pounds a month from the first of January."

"Eight hundred and forty," Maureen repeated. The words felt flat and heavy on her tongue, like dry gravel. "It was seven hundred and ten in the spring."

"I know, love," Mrs. Appleby said. Her hand touched Maureen's forearm for a second, then retreated to the stack of invoices. "The council won't bridge the gap. If the account falls three months in arrears, the regional director flags the bed for transfer. They'd reassign Kathleen to the municipal annex out at Greystones."

Maureen looked through the wired-glass observation panel into the day room. Her mother sat in an armchair by the television, wearing a cardigan of faded pink wool. Her hands were moving over her lap, picking at the seam of her floral apron with steady, rhythmic patience.

Greystones had thirty-six beds to a room, partitioned with plastic curtains, forty miles south on the edge of the coalfield. It was an old workhouse infirmary converted in the nineteen-seventies, cold and drafty, where the staff turnover was fifty percent every six months and the residents sat in rows with their coats on.

"I have the overtime on the night rounds," Maureen said. "The extra bank shifts on Ward 4."

"Just make sure the bank standing order is amended by the fifteenth," Mrs. Appleby said. She picked up her pen. "She's had a quiet morning. Ate half a poached egg on toast. But she's asking after Liam again."

Maureen walked down the linoleum corridor to the day room. The television was playing an old quiz show, the sound turned low so only the laughter of the studio audience punctuated the hum of the strip lights.

She sat on the low footstool beside her mother's chair.

"Mam," she said.

Kathleen turned her head slowly. Her eyes, pale blue and watery with cataracts, drifted over Maureen's face, searching for a pattern among the lines.

"You're late from the market," Kathleen said. Her voice was thin, like paper drawn across a comb. "Did you get the haddock? Liam said he wanted fish cakes for his tea."

"I have the haddock in the larder, Mam," Maureen said. She took her mother's right hand. The skin was dry and cool, spotted with brown age marks like freckles on a hen's egg. "Everything's in hand."

Kathleen nodded. Her gaze returned to the television screen. She reached into the pocket of her pink cardigan and drew out a small satsuma, rolling it between her palms.

"He'll be cold coming off the timber wagons," Kathleen murmured. "The wind along the canal comes right through his denim jacket. I told him to wear his thick jersey, but you know how he is. Vain as a peacock about his hair."

She began to peel the satsuma. Her fingers, though bent with arthritis at the second joint, worked with astonishing, delicate patience. The orange rind came away in a single unbroken spiral, curling downward across her lap like a paper ribbon.

"Look at that, Maureen," Kathleen whispered, pointing with her thumbnail at the spiral peel. "One piece. Your father could never do that. He hacked them into chunks like firewood."

Maureen watched the spiral curl against the faded floral apron. She took a quarter of the fruit when her mother held it out, tasting the sharp, sweet juice on the back of her tongue.

The citrus scent hung in the dry, heated air of the room, cutting through the smell of cabbage and skin wash. Outside the window, a coal tit landed on the wooden bird table, pecked twice at a dry crust of toast, and flew off toward the laurel hedge.

Mrs. Appleby adjusted her pearl earrings, her gaze sliding past Maureen to the stack of direct debit forms on the counter. "I know it's hard, Maureen. But the cost of agency nursing has doubled since the winter virus hit the wards. If we don't have two qualified nurses on duty overnight, the Care Quality Commission serves us with an improvement notice. That means reduced admissions, and that means the fees have to rise across the board."

"The council pension doesn't rise," Maureen said.

"The council pays the local authority baseline rate," Mrs. Appleby replied, her voice dropping into that smooth, sympathetic tone that social care managers practiced for family reviews. "Three hundred and eighty pounds a week. But that only covers basic residential care—a shared room in the old wing, meals, and standard supervision. Kathleen is in the high-dependency dementia suite. She needs two carers to assist with bathing, her food has to be pureed, and she wanders between two and four in the morning. If the family top-up stops, the trust simply moves her bed."

"Greystones has no night staff in the rooms," Maureen said. "My cousin had her husband out there before he died. They lock the bedroom doors at nine o'clock and leave them with call bells they can't even reach."

"Greystones is the municipal provider," Mrs. Appleby said neutrally. "It meets the statutory minimum standard. Saint Anthony's is a private charitable trust. We can't carry deficits on individual residents; the trustees won't permit it."

Maureen looked at the glass case behind the reception desk where the care home displayed photographs of summer garden fetes and Christmas carol concerts. In every picture, the residents looked clean, well-dressed, and peaceful, sitting in wicker armchairs on the manicured lawn. That peace was bought at eight hundred and forty pounds a month. It was bought with every overtime hour she worked on Ward 4, wringing cotton mopheads until four drops fell onto the drain tray.

Brenda sat back against the painted brick wall of the basement boiler house corridor, her cigarette smoke curling up into the warm air around the overhead steam risers.

"Do you know what that young management trainee with the blue lanyard asked me yesterday, Maureen?" Brenda said, shaking her head with dry amusement. "He asked me if I had completed my digital module on chemical hazard communication."

"What did you tell him, Brenda?" Maureen asked, folding her yellow dusting cloth.

"I told him I've been mixing sodium hypochlorite and caustic floor stripper since nineteen seventy-six," Brenda replied, taking a slow drag of her roll-up. "I told him I can tell the concentration of an acid solution by the way it stings the hair on my arms. He looked at me like I was an escapee from the Victorian workhouse. Wrote three lines on his iPad about non-compliant operational culture."

Maureen took a sip of her tea from the enamel mug.

The air near the boiler house was always eighty degrees, smelling of dry lagging, lubricating oil, and the sweetish dampness of steam leaks. It was the only warm place in the entire sub-basement during the long winter nights.

"They want everything written down on a computer screen, Brenda," Maureen remarked quietly. "Because if it's on a screen, they can pretend it doesn't smell of carbolic and sick."

"Aye," Brenda agreed, tapping her ash into the tin lid. "They want a hospital without the mess. But sick folk are mess, Maureen. From the day they're born to the day they're laid out in the chapel, human beings are nothing but mess and trouble. And when the computers break down, it's still two lasses with a mop bucket who have to clean the floor."

Kathleen smoothed the hem of her cardigan, her thumb tracing the rim of a mismatched bone button.

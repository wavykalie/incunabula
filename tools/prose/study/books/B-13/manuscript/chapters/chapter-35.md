The domestic staff locker room in the basement of the west wing had not changed its layout since 1974. Two rows of tall, olive-green steel lockers stood back to back down the center of the room, their enamel doors scratched, dented, and pasted with peeling union stickers, pictures of pop stars from the eighties, and printed reminders about hand dermatitis.

It was ten o'clock on Friday morning. The room was empty, smelling of damp cotton uniforms, carbolic soap, and the steam rising from the laundry drainage pipes behind the partition.

Maureen stood before locker twenty-four. Her brass key turned in the small padlock with a dry, stiff click.

She swung the metal door open.

Inside, on the upper wire shelf, sat her personal items: a chipped white ceramic mug printed with the Leeds United football crest that Liam had given her for Christmas in 2003; a spare blue cleaning tabard with her name embroidered in white thread across the pocket; a pair of yellow rubber marigolds with holes worn through the thumbs; and a cardboard box of tea bags.

On the bottom of the locker lay a pair of black leather lace-up shoes with thick crepe rubber soles, worn down at the outer heels from thirty years of pushing floor polishers along hospital corridors.

Maureen pulled a clean canvas tote bag from her coat pocket.

She took the white ceramic mug. She wrapped it carefully in her spare tabard so the handle would not break, and placed it in the tote bag. She took the tea bags and the small plastic box of throat lozenges.

The yellow rubber gloves and the worn shoes she left behind. They belonged to the hospital. Some young agency girl from Leeds would throw them into the waste bin on Monday morning when the lockers were cleared for the Vanguard contractors.

She looked at the inside of the locker door.

Taped to the green painted steel was a small, faded color photograph, three inches square, showing Liam at nineteen. He was standing in Kathleen's council flat garden in his timber-stained work trousers, holding up a wooden plane, squinting into the summer sun with a wide, crooked grin.

Maureen reached out. Her thumb touched the smooth gloss of the photograph. With careful, gentle pressure, she worked her fingernail beneath the yellowed sellotape, peeling the picture away from the steel without tearing the corners.

She slipped the photograph into her coat pocket, right beside the photocopies of his chart.

The outer door swung open. Brenda Higgins walked into the locker room, her blue tabard unbuttoned, carrying a plastic bucket filled with damp floor cloths.

Brenda stopped when she saw Maureen's coat and the canvas bag on the bench.

"Maureen?" Brenda said, setting the bucket on the concrete floor. "What are you doing in your Sunday coat?"

Maureen took her yellow plastic identity pass from her lapel. The laminated card carried her black-and-white photograph from 1995, showing her with dark auburn hair and a round, unlined face beneath the heading: *DOMESTIC SERVICES — FINCH, M.*

She walked across the concrete floor and placed the plastic card in Brenda's hand.

"I have retired, Brenda," Maureen said softly.

Brenda stared at the identity card in her palm. The breath left her chest in a long, shuddering gasp. Her lined face seemed to collapse inward, the wrinkles deepening around her mouth.

"They forced you out," Brenda whispered, her eyes filling with sudden tears. "The suit. He did this, didn't he?"

"No one forced me, Brenda," Maureen said, laying her hand over Brenda's rough fingers. "It was time. Kathleen needs me. And I have done my thirty years."

Brenda closed her hand around the plastic card. She pulled Maureen into a fierce, clumsy embrace, her woollen tabard smelling of carbolic powder, cigarette smoke, and thirty years of shared morning tea.

"You were the best worker this hospital ever had, Maureen," Brenda wept, her forehead pressed against Maureen's shoulder. "Never took a sick day. Never complained about the dirty linen. What will Ward 4 do without you?"

"Ward 4 will be fine, Brenda," Maureen murmured, stroking Brenda's back. "Look after yourself. Don't let the new supervisors rush you on the waxing."

She pulled away gently. She picked up her canvas tote bag from the wooden bench.

She walked back to locker twenty-four. She pushed the steel door shut.

The concrete floor of the basement locker room was cold through the thin soles of Maureen's boots. Along the row of olive-green lockers, the air was still, carrying forty years of accumulated domestic history: the scent of carbolic washing powder, cheap lavender cologne bought from the market stalls, damp tea towels, and the faint, sweet smell of spearmint chewing gum.

Maureen sat down on the wooden bench that ran down the center of the aisle. The bench was made of three-inch pitch pine, scarred with the carved initials of cleaners and laundry lasses who had retired before the turn of the century: *M.B. 1978*, *J.T. 1982*, *C.H. 1989*.

She looked down at the Leeds United mug in her canvas tote bag.

Liam had bought it for her with his first Christmas bonus from Henderson's joinery in 2003. He had wrapped it in brown parcel paper tied with electrical tape, grinning from ear to ear as she tore the wrapping away by the coal fire in the council flat.

"Best team in Yorkshire for the best sister in the West Riding," Liam had laughed, kissing her cheek with his rough, sawdust-scented stubble. "Don't let those sisters on Ward 4 pinch it for their coffee."

She had kept that mug in locker twenty-four for twenty-two years. She had drunk thousands of cups of tea from it while sitting on this very bench, listening to Brenda complain about the supervisor, watching Janice Bradley cry over medication shortages, and waiting for the morning whistle to sound the end of the shift.

Brenda sat down beside her on the pine bench, her shoulders sagging under her blue nylon tabard. She reached into her pocket and brought out her tin of rolling tobacco, her fingers trembling as she pulled out a pinch of dark shag.

"I won't know who to drink my tea with on Mondays, Maureen," Brenda whispered, her voice rough with tears. "Thirty years. We started the same week after the miners went back. Remember the snow that winter? The buses couldn't get up Lowfield hill. We walked two miles through three-foot drifts in our wellingtons to scrub the casualty floors."

"I remember, Brenda," Maureen said softly, resting her hand on Brenda's knee. "We had dry socks in our pockets and two ham sandwiches in a greaseproof bag."

"Aye," Brenda smiled through her tears, wiping her nose on the hem of her tabard. "And we laughed. God help us, we laughed till our sides ached. Now there's no laughing on the wards. Just that supervisor with his digital stopwatch and his yellow clipboard, counting how many seconds it takes to wipe a skirting board."

She struck a match, the sulfur flare lighting up her lined, honest face.

"Don't worry about me, love," Brenda said, blowing the blue smoke toward the high hopper window. "I'll do my two years and take my six thousand pounds. But this place won't have a soul left in it once you're gone."

Maureen stood up, smoothing down the front of her coat.

The metal locker door clicks shut for the last time.

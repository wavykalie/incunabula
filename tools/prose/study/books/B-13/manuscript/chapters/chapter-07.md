# Chapter 7: The Box with the Red Label

At three-forty on Tuesday morning, Maureen took the service lift down to the sub-basement with her yellow cleaning bucket and two long-handled floor scrapers.

The corridor outside the archive room had been cleared of the dehumidifiers. The air was colder now, smelling of wet concrete, pulverized paper dust, and diesel exhaust from the idling collection truck outside the bay. Through the double doors, the steel shelving units stood stripped and naked, their gray uprights casting barred shadows across the floor under the raw work lights hung from the sprinkler pipes.

Two contract workers in high-visibility vests were lifting the last stack of boxes from the lower shelves.

Maureen moved along the wall with her mop, her strokes even, overlapping by four inches as she had done for thirty years. Her eyes moved across the cardboard cartons stacked on the central pallet.

Most of the boxes were stamped with the standard blue trust logo: Outpatient Clinics 1999–2003, Orthopaedic Theatre Logs 2001, General Pathology Daybooks.

Then she saw it.

WARD 4: ACUTE MEDICAL ADMISSIONS & CASUALTY LOGBOOKS
VOLS. 18–24: 2000–2005
INC. CRITICAL INCIDENT & MORTALITY TRANSCRIPTS

Above the typed label, someone had scrawled a note in red grease pencil: HELD PER CLINICAL GOVERNANCE / RETENTION REVIEW.

Alistair Sterling walked out of the archive office at the far end of the room. He carried a clipboard with a sheaf of carbon-copy destruction certificates.

"That's the final consignment for the mobile plant," Sterling said to the taller contractor. "The vehicle is outside Bay 3 now. Once these three consoles are locked, they go directly into the hopper. No loose sheets on the loading ramp."

"We've got five boxes left on the floor, mate," the contractor said, nodding toward the pallet with the brown carton.

"Empty them into console number two," Sterling said. He did not look at the label on the brown box. He was checking his wristwatch, his thumb tapping the bezel. "Everything pre-dating 2006 goes into the rotary cutter. I want the certificates signed and counter-sealed by five o'clock."

The contractor stepped toward the brown box. He slit the cloth tape with a utility knife, flipped the cardboard flaps back, and reached inside.

Maureen's heart hammered against her ribs like an iron spoon in an empty bucket. She saw the dark green buckram covers of the bound books inside—the twin volumes to the one resting beneath the copper U-bend under her kitchen sink.

The contractor lifted three books in one armful. He raised the steel lid of the green console and dropped them inside.

Thud. Thud. Thud.

The heavy paper struck the zinc bottom of the bin with a muffled, dead sound. The contractor reached for the remaining volumes, tipped them into the hopper, and slammed the steel lid down.

Sterling stepped forward. He took a heavy brass padlock from his coat pocket, slid the shackle through the steel hasp, and snapped it shut.

The click of the lock echoed off the bare concrete walls, high and clean.

Maureen watched from the shadow of the brick pillar as the contractor lifted the heavy cardboard box marked *Ward 4: 2000–2005*.

One of the files burst open.

A sheaf of pink and white clinical observation sheets spread across the damp floor, their edges fluttering in the draft from the delivery ramp. Maureen saw the familiar printed margins: *St. Jude's Hospital — Fluid Balance & Medication Chart*.

A contractor reached down with a heavy work glove, gathered the papers into a crumpled ball without looking at a single word, and jammed them into the hopper of console number two.

Maureen held her mop handle rigid against her ribs. Her fingers were numb with cold, her heart pounding with a slow, heavy rhythm that echoed in her ears.

And twenty paces away, Alistair Sterling stood with his iPad, certifying that the trust had completed its historical liabilities review.

On the second-floor balcony, she stopped to catch her breath.

This estate had been built in 1964 to rehouse eight hundred families from the terrace slums along the canal. Her father had helped dig the foundations with an excavator hired from the rail works. He used to point to the fifth-floor balconies from the bus stop and say, "We built those walls with solid Fletton brick, Maureen. They will stand when the colliery is gone and the council is bankrupt."

Her father had been right about the colliery and the council. The pit had closed in eighty-five, and the council had privatized the rent collection three years ago to a housing trust in Birmingham. But the brick stood. Cold, damp, unyielding to the Yorkshire winter.

Maureen took her brass latchkey from her apron pocket. She turned the lock, pushed open the timber door, and stepped into the dark hallway of the flat. The smell of cold linoleum and yesterday's tea dust met her at the threshold, quiet and still.

# Chapter 6: Tea in the Break Room

The staff break room behind the nurses' station on Ward 4 was narrow, lit by a fluorescent tube that flickered at the north end with a faint orange glow. The room contained two vinyl armchairs with ripped armrests mended with gray duct tape, a low coffee table littered with old copies of nursing journals, and a two-slice toaster that tripped the circuit breaker if both slots were pushed down together.

Sister Janice Bradley sat at the small formica table, her blue cardigan buttoned up to her chin against the draught from the fire exit. She was sixty-one, with short iron-gray hair and thick plastic spectacles that made her eyes look round and watery.

She held an enamel mug in both hands. She was pressing a spent tea bag against the inside of the rim with a plastic teaspoon, wringing out the dark tannin until the liquid pooled brown and oily in the bowl of the spoon.

"He was down in the basement, then?" Janice said without looking up.

Maureen set her clean bucket in the sink corner and sat on the wooden stool by the kettle. "Sterling. He had the men loading boxes by the laundry ramp."

Janice dropped the tea bag into a plastic butter tub that served as a waste bin. "He's been on the ward twice this week. Standing by the drug cupboard at three in the morning with that little glass machine in his hand, watching how long it takes Nurse Corcoran to draw up the morphine."

"He asked about the ward registers," Maureen said.

Janice's hands stopped moving. The plastic spoon remained between her thumb and forefinger, suspended over the mug.

Outside the door, the ward was quiet. An infusion pump gave off three short, high-pitched pips—air in the line—followed by the shuffle of Nurse Corcoran's rubber-soled clogs going down toward bed fourteen.

"He's not auditing paper to save space," Janice said, her voice dropping into a harsh whisper. "He's auditing liabilities. The trust board's negotiating a private management buyout with a consortium from London. Facilities, catering, domestics, and maintenance—all going into a private contract by the end of the financial year. If there are outstanding clinical negligence claims or unclosed coroner's referrals from the old days, the trust has to indemnify the buyers against damages."

"Liam's file wasn't closed," Maureen said.

Janice took off her spectacles. The bridge of her nose carried two deep, red indentations from the plastic pads. She pressed the heels of her hands into her eye sockets, holding them there until her knuckles went white.

"You think I don't remember?" Janice said. Her voice was flat, carrying the gravel of forty years of night air. "Two agency nurses on thirty-two beds. The medical registrar had been on his feet for thirty hours straight. He wrote the potassium chloride in the intravenous fluid column instead of the slow-infusion chart. When Liam's monitor alarmed, the bag was empty. Three hundred millilitres of saline with forty millimoles of potassium gone into his vein in twenty minutes."

"They told my mother his heart stopped," Maureen said.

"The clinical director came down from his bed at five in the morning," Janice whispered. She looked up, her eyes bloodshot in the harsh light. "He stood right where you're sitting, Maureen. He had the ward register on his knees. He said, 'Sister Bradley, if an inquest opens on this boy, the trust closes Ward 4 by Friday. We lose thirty-two beds, and you lose your registration. You sign the transfer slip to cardiac, or we both go before the disciplinary board.'"

She picked up her tea mug. Her hand shook slightly, the tea slopping against the white porcelain rim.

"I signed it," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. And I've looked at the floor every time you walked past with your mop. You think I don't see your face every night at three in the morning? You think I don't see your mother sitting up at Highfield, losing her wits, waiting for a lad who's buried in the churchyard?"

"She asked for haddock today," Maureen said quietly. "She thought he was coming home off the timber lorry."

Janice stared down into her tea, the gray surface reflecting the flickering neon strip overhead. "Sterling will find out what was in those boxes, Maureen. He has forensic accountants checking the pharmacy logs against the mortuary ledger. If he finds out you have that book under your kitchen sink, he won't just sack you. He'll call the police. Theft of NHS property, data breach, obstruction of governance."

"Let him call them," Maureen said.

"And what happens to Kathleen?" Janice snapped, her voice breaking into a harsh, wheezing rasp. "Who pays the eight hundred quid a month to Saint Anthony's when you're sitting in a police cell? The council will bundle your mother into the back of an ambulance and dump her at Greystones before your bail hearing's over. You think the trust cares about a dead boy from nineteen eighty-two? They care about the London buyers."

Janice Bradley pushed her spectacles back up her nose, the yellow skin under her eyes dark with exhaustion. She took a sip of her stewed tea, grimaced, and set the mug down.

"Do you remember Dr. Rao?" Janice asked, her voice dropping so low that Maureen had to lean forward to hear. "The medical director who came down that morning?"

"I remember his car," Maureen said. "A silver Rover with red leather seats. He parked it in the ambulance bay by the doors."

"He came onto Ward 4 wearing a cashmere coat over his pajamas," Janice whispered. "He smelled of expensive shaving cream and peppermint. He went into Room 6 where Liam was lying. The resuscitation trolley was still plugged into the wall, the defibrillator pads stuck to the boy's chest. Dr. Henderson—the young registrar who wrote the prescription—was sitting on the floor in the sluice room, his head between his knees, crying like a schoolboy."

"What did Rao do?"

"Rao pulled the sheet over Liam's face. Then he took the potassium bag off the drip stand, stuck it in his coat pocket, and took the clinical charts off the clipboard. He walked into my office, shut the door, and locked it. He had the ward daybook on the desk. He said to me, 'Sister Bradley, young Henderson made an error in the unit dosage. A decimal point in the wrong column. But Henderson is the nephew of the regional health authority chairman. If this error goes to an inquest, Henderson's career is finished, Ward 4 is closed for systemic safety failure, and you will be struck off the nursing register for failing to verify the infusion rate before hanging the bag.'"

Janice's hands gripped the edge of the formica table.

"He held the pen out to me," Janice said, tears finally welling in the corners of her watery eyes. "He had already written the transfer sheet: *Sudden cardiac arrest secondary to acute status asthmaticus. Transferred to cardiac care for post-mortem evaluation.* He told me that if I signed it, Liam's family would receive an immediate discretionary condolence payment from the trust welfare fund, and Henderson would be transferred to an administrative post in Manchester. If I refused, he would report me for gross clinical negligence."

"And you signed," Maureen said.

"I signed," Janice whispered. "I had three children at home, Maureen. My husband was six months out of work from the docks. If I lost my nursing registration, we'd have been in the parish breadline by Christmas. So I signed my name, and Rao took the charts and walked out to his Rover. I never saw those sheets again until you found that green slip in the sack."

The night wind rattled the high hopper windows along the corridor leading to the maternity block. Outside in the dark, the lights of the Lowfield colliery housing estate formed a grid of orange sodium points across the valley floor.

Maureen wheeled her cart past the clinical audit noticeboards.

Beneath the glass frames, the trust had posted its latest key performance indicators: *FOUR-HOUR EMERGENCY ADMISSION TARGET: 71.4%*, *CLOSTRIDIUM DIFFICILE INCIDENCE: ZERO CASES*, *BED TURNOVER EFFICIENCY: 94.2%*.

She looked at the numbers.

To the administrators in Leeds and London, these colored bar charts represented the pulse of the hospital. If a green bar rose two millimeters, executives received annual performance bonuses. If a red bar dropped, consultants were summoned to executive inquiry panels.

None of those charts recorded the things Maureen saw every night.

There was no bar chart for the seventy-year-old widow who sat in the corridor for five hours because the transport ambulance had broken down in Huddersfield. There was no metric for the young staff nurse who skipped her meal break to hold the hand of an eighteen-year-old lad whose leg had been amputated at the railway shunting yard.

The true life of this hospital did not live on the noticeboards. It lived in the gaps between the targets, in the quiet acts of endurance that kept human beings alive despite the metrics.

Maureen did not speak. She reached into her pocket, took out her clean microfibre duster, and laid it flat on the table between them, folding it into halves, then quarters, then eighths.

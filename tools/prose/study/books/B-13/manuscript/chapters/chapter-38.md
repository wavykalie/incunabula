The municipal cemetery on the hill above Lowfield was bathed in clear, pale Saturday morning sunlight. A light ground frost had silvered the grass between the headstones, melting into glistening dew drops where the sunshine touched the granite.

It was half past eleven. Down in the valley, the red-brick chimney stacks of Saint Jude's were half-hidden by a bank of blue autumn woodsmoke drifting from the allotments.

Maureen sat on the green cast-iron memorial bench beside the weeping birch tree. Her canvas shopping bag rested on the gravel path between her boots.

It was Janice Bradley.

She stopped at the end of the bench, her breath pluming white in the cold air. Her hair was brushed back into a soft, grey wave, no longer constrained by the rigid wire grips of her sister's uniform.

"Room for an old pensioner, Maureen?" Janice asked.

"Always room, Janice," Maureen replied, shifting along the iron slats.

Janice sat down with a quiet sigh of relief. She pulled her woollen scarf tighter around her neck, looking out across the cemetery toward the town.

From her leather handbag, Janice brought out a square bundle wrapped in white tissue paper. She unfolded the tissue with careful fingers, revealing twenty black-and-white and color photographs from the 1990s and 2000s.

"I cleared my desk on Thursday night," Janice said softly. "The new agency sister arrived at six on Friday morning with an iPad under her arm. Never asked me where the spare drip stands were. Just asked for the Wi-Fi password."

She smiled—a small, tired smile that warmed her eyes for the first time in months.

She passed the photographs across to Maureen.

The second picture was from October 2004. It showed Dr. Rao as a young man of twenty-six, thin, dark-eyed, looking exhausted in his white coat, standing beside the ward drug cupboard.

"He wasn't an evil boy, Maureen," Janice whispered, looking at the picture of the young doctor. "He was twenty-six years old. He had been awake for thirty-six hours. The system broke him, the way it breaks all of us."

"I know," Maureen said. "I never hated the boy."

Janice looked down at the gravel path. "Sterling showed me the shredder receipt yesterday. He said the original file was destroyed at 9:15 on Friday morning."

"The paper is gone, Janice," Maureen said gently. "And the past is at rest."

Janice turned her head. She looked at Maureen with a mixture of sorrow, gratitude, and a deep, unspoken understanding that bridged thirty years of ward shifts and silences.

From the steeple of Saint Jude's church down in the valley, the great bronze bells began to chime, their deep, resonant notes rolling across the frosted hills, marking the hour of noon.

Janice Bradley sat on the cast-iron bench, her hands resting in the pockets of her tweed overcoat, watching the yellow leaves tumble across the grass.

"Do you know what Dr. Rao said to me in the corridor, Maureen?" Janice asked, her voice dropping into a register of profound, lingering grief. "Twenty years ago, when the porters were wheeling Liam down the freight lift to the mortuary."

Maureen turned her head to look at the retired sister. "What did he say?"

"He stopped by the fire door," Janice whispered, her eyes shining with unshed tears. "He was shaking so badly he had to lean against the wall tiles to stand up. He looked at me, Maureen, and he said, 'Sister Bradley, I killed that boy because I was too proud to admit I couldn't keep my eyes open.' He started to cry, right there under the neon lights. He said, 'I went to medical school to heal people, and I have killed a nineteen-year-old carpenter on my first night shift.'"

She looked down at her gloved fingers.

"And Dr. Calthorpe took him by the elbow," Janice continued softly. "Calthorpe smiled his smooth consultant smile, handed Rao a handkerchief, and said, 'Pull yourself together, my boy. In acute clinical practice, we do not indulge in hysteria. We sign the clinical verification, we protect the institution, and we move on to the next patient.'"

Janice shook her head slowly.

"Rao moved on to Melbourne," Janice said. "Calthorpe moved on to the golf club. And I moved on to thirty more years of night shifts, carrying that boy's screams in my head every time I checked an intravenous drip."

Maureen reached out. She took Janice's gloved hand in her own bare, rough palm, closing her fingers over the sister's trembling knuckles.

"The guilt wasn't yours alone, Janice," Maureen said gently. "The system made you choose between a dead boy and your mother's shelter. No working woman should ever be asked to make that choice."

Janice looked at Maureen, her mouth trembling, a deep, cleansing peace finally softening the lines around her eyes.

The bells in the valley completed their twelve strokes, the final resonant chime vibrating through the frosty air until it was swallowed by the silence of the hills.

Janice Bradley reached into her overcoat pocket and brought out an old brass key attached to a round wooden fob stamped with the number *4*.

"The old sister's key to the Ward 4 narcotics cupboard," Janice said, turning the brass over in her palm. "I was supposed to hand it in to the pharmacy supervisor on my last shift. I forgot it was in my coat."

She looked at the worn metal notches, smoothed down by thirty years of daily turns in cast-iron locks.

"Keep it, Janice," Maureen said gently. "The narcotics cupboard was demolished three weeks ago. Sterling has installed electronic dispensing safes with fingerprint scanners."

Janice gave a short, quiet laugh—a sound that was half sorrow and half release. She pulled back her arm and threw the brass key with all her remaining strength.

The key flew in a high, glittering arc through the frosty air, spinning over the cemetery hedge before splashing down into the deep, dark water of the railway reservoir at the bottom of the cutting.

The water swallowed the key without a ripple.

"Thirty-five years," Janice murmured, watching the grey water. "Gone into the pond."

"Not gone, Janice," Maureen replied, resting her arm across the sister's shoulders. "We remember. That is what matters."

Janice turned to look at her, her eyes clear and untroubled in the autumn light. For the first time since October 2004, the shadow of fear had lifted from her face.

Janice lays a hand over Maureen's arm as the church bells ring for noon.

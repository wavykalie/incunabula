# Chapter 8: The Kitchen Table at Dawn

The winter dawn came up gray and thin behind the railway embankment.

Maureen sat at her kitchen table in her woollen dressing gown. Her hair, pinned up for the night shift, had slipped loose at the temples, gray strands falling across her forehead. The tea in her mug was cold, a pale brown skin forming across the surface where the condensed milk had settled.

Spread across the cracked oilcloth was the record of her life in pence and pounds.

On the left lay her blue bank passbook from the Trustee Savings Bank. The last entry, stamped in purple ink by the teller at the High Street branch on Friday, showed a balance of one hundred and forty-two pounds and eighteen pence.

In the center lay the white letter from Saint Anthony's Care Group: Monthly Resident Contribution: £840.00. Due on or before 15 January.

On the right lay her pay slip from Saint Jude's NHS Trust for the four weeks ending 30 November.

Gross basic pay (37.5 hours): £1,420.00.
Night-shift unsocial hours enhancement: £265.40.
Total deductions (Tax, NI, Superannuation): £312.15.
Net pay: £1,373.25.

She took her pencil from the chipped teacup by the salt cellar. She wrote the figures on the back of a gas bill envelope:

Net wages: £1,373.25
St. Anthony's top-up: -£840.00
Remaining: £533.25

Council rent (Cedar Grove): £310.00
Gas card and electric key: £110.00
Bus pass (four weeks): £58.00
Remaining for food and larder: £45.25

Forty-five pounds and twenty-five pence for four weeks. Eleven pounds and thirty-one pence a week. A loaf of sliced white bread was one pound twenty; a bag of porridge oats was ninety pence; a tin of corned beef was two pounds forty.

If she missed one shift—if the flu took her, or if her knee swelled as it had two winters ago when she slipped on the black ice outside the boilerhouse—the unsocial hours enhancement would vanish from the slip.

If the domestic staff were outsourced to the private management consortium in the spring, the new contract would strip the NHS terms: basic minimum wage, statutory sick pay only, zero unsocial enhancements for night rounds.

She would be short by two hundred and twenty pounds every four weeks.

Kathleen would be transferred to the municipal annex at Greystones before the daffodils came up in the park.

Maureen looked at the cupboard doors beneath the sink. The melamine was gray, marked with water stains around the handles.

Under the copper pipe lay the only copy in the country that proved Liam Finch had not died of childhood asthma. The only copy that proved a nineteen-year-old apprentice had been killed by forty millimoles of potassium chloride injected into his arm at four in the morning, while a tired registrar slept and a ward sister signed her name to a lie.

If she took that register to a solicitor in town, there would be an inquiry. There would be an investigation. And Alistair Sterling would know within twenty-four hours which night cleaner on Ward 4 had taken trust property from the paper recycling sacks.

She would be dismissed for gross misconduct. No reference. No severance pay. No unemployment benefit for six months.

And Kathleen's bed at Saint Anthony's would be cleared by the end of the week.

The kitchen at Cedar Grove was dead cold. Maureen lit the small gas ring on the cooker, filled the tin kettle from the tap, and set it on the iron trivet.

She did not turn on the paraffin heater; paraffin was eighty pence a litre at the hardware shop, and she had only three litres left in the red plastic jerrycan by the back door. She pulled her woollen shawl tight around her shoulders, tucking the frayed ends into the belt of her dressing gown.

She took the old biscuit tin from the top shelf of the pantry. Inside, beneath her birth certificate and her national insurance card, lay three black-and-white photographs wrapped in tissue paper.

The first was her father, taken in nineteen fifty-eight on the slipway at Cammell Laird, his boiler suit stiff with oil, his flat cap tilted back on his head as he held an iron rivet gun across his knee.

The second was Kathleen on her wedding day, standing outside Saint Mary's in a blue costume borrowed from her sister, her hair set in tight curls, her hands clasping a small bunch of carnations.

The third was Liam at sixteen, standing by the garden gate at Cedar Grove with his yellow bicycle. He was grinning into the sun, squinting against the glare, his white teeth showing that small chip in the front incisor where the cricket ball had caught him. He had looked so strong, his shoulders broadening out from the timber yard work, his forearms corded with muscle from hauling three-inch pine planks off the rail wagons.

'He'll be a foreman before he's twenty-five,' Kathleen had said that summer, watching him grease the bicycle chain on the gravel. 'He has the head for figures, that boy. He doesn't spend his money on beer and dogs like the rest of them.'

Maureen touched the surface of the photograph. The glossy paper had cracked along the corner where her mother's thumb had rested for twenty years.

The kettle began to sing, a thin, shrill whistle that cut through the silent flat.

Maureen rose, poured the boiling water into her chipped mug over an old tea bag, and stirred it with a teaspoon. She did not take milk; the milk had to last for Kathleen's tea when she visited Saint Anthony's on Saturday.

She sat back down at the oilcloth, looking at the pencil sums on the back of the gas bill envelope.

Forty-five pounds and twenty-five pence for food and larder.

If she spoke out, she would save Liam's name and destroy her mother's life. If she kept silent, her mother would sleep in clean sheets at Saint Anthony's while Liam's death was shredded into gray dust behind the hospital laundry.

The kitchen table was cold beneath Maureen's wrists. Outside the window, a coal train rumbled along the valley cutting, its diesel engines throbbing in low gear as it hauled thirty wagons of industrial coke toward the power station at Ferrybridge.

She looked at her two blue spiral notebooks resting on the oilcloth.

In those pages, written with blue ballpoint ink, were the names of forty-two men and women who had died on Ward 4 during the winter of 2003. She remembered every one of them: old Mr. Pollard who had lost three fingers at the timber docks; Mrs. Hinchcliffe who had sung Methodist hymns when the morphine wore off; and young Davie who had fallen from the brick kiln on Lowfield hill.

None of those people had executive obituaries in the regional press. None of them had memorial gardens named in their honor.

Their lives had ended on rubberized mattresses in side wards, accompanied only by the soft click of oxygen flowmeters and the steady sweep of Maureen's dust mop along the skirting boards.

And yet, in her notebooks, their stories lived.

She had recorded the names of their children, the jobs they had worked, the songs they had loved, and the final words they had spoken before their breathing slowed to a whisper.

"Memory is the poor person's estate," her mother had told her twenty years ago, sitting by the range with her mending basket. "The rich leave land and money in the bank. We leave our names in the minds of the folk who loved us."

Maureen picked up her tea mug and drained the dark, tannic dregs. The radiator beneath the window gave three sharp, metallic clanks, cooling down as the night deepened.

Maureen laid the pencil down on the envelope. She reached out, switched off the small lamp on the table, and watched the cold light spread across the rooftops of the estate.

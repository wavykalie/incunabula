The central municipal library stood on the corner of Commercial Street, an imposing Victorian red-brick building with arched stone windows and a slate roof topped by a copper weather vane that had seized facing east in the winter of 1993.

It was half past ten on Friday morning. Outside, a fine autumn drizzle fell over the municipal car park, coating the windshields of parked cars with a thin, glistening glaze.

Maureen pushed through the heavy mahogany revolving doors. The entrance hall was quiet, smelling of damp wool, beeswax floor polish, and the cold, slightly sweet odour of ancient buckram bindings.

She carried her black vinyl shopping bag over her right forearm. Inside the bag, beneath a loaf of sliced white bread and a plastic punnet of plums, lay Liam's file wrapped in a clean linen tea towel.

She walked past the reading tables where four retired men sat reading the daily broadsheets mounted on wooden split poles. She turned down the narrow corridor leading to the local history annex and the public photocopier.

The photocopier was a large, grey industrial machine with a heavy rubber lid and an illuminated coin box mounted on a metal stand beside the feeder. A printed sign taped to the glass read: *COPIES: 20p PER A4 SHEET. EXACT CHANGE ONLY. THE DESK CANNOT PROVIDE COINS.*

Maureen set her shopping bag on the wooden table beside the machine. She reached into her coat pocket and brought out a cloth change purse with a brass snap. She unclasped the purse and shook its contents into her palm: six fifty-pence coins and eight ten-pence pieces, saved from her overtime pay packets.

Her fingers were stiff with cold, but she worked with methodical precision.

She pulled Liam's file from the tea towel. The green card cover was cold to the touch. She opened the brass treasury tag and separated the ten individual sheets: the triage intake record, the nursing observation logs, the medication administration sheet, Dr. Rao's typed post-incident statement, and the purple-stamped legal governance memo.

She lifted the heavy rubber lid of the photocopier. The glass platen was cool and clear, reflecting the fluorescent strip light overhead.

She placed the triage sheet face down on the glass, aligning the top left corner against the engraved plastic alignment mark: *A4 — 100%*.

She inserted a fifty-pence coin into the metal slot. The coin dropped through the mechanism with a sharp, mechanical clink, followed by the whir of an electric solenoid. The small liquid crystal display on the control panel blinked: *CREDIT: £0.50. SELECT QUANTITY.*

Maureen pressed the button marked *3*.

She pressed the large green *START* button.

Inside the machine, an intense bar of brilliant green light illuminated beneath the platen. The scanner arm moved forward across the glass with a high-pitched, whining hum, casting a moving emerald glare against the front of Maureen's beige coat.

The paper feed rollers clattered. From the internal tray, three clean white sheets of eighty-gram bond paper were pulled through the electrostatic drum. The machine rumbled, its internal heater fan blowing a warm, dry draft of ozone-scented exhaust into the small alcove.

Three sheets dropped into the exit tray with a soft, sliding rustle.

Maureen picked them up. The print was crisp, stark black against pure white paper. On the first copy, the words stood out with startling clarity: *Finch, Liam Thomas. Age 19. Acute admission.*

She turned the sheet over. The paper was still hot from the fuser rollers, pleasant against her cold fingertips.

She lifted the rubber lid, removed the original triage sheet, and placed the second document onto the glass: Sister Bradley's handwritten nursing log from the night of October 16, 2004.

She fed another fifty-pence coin into the slot. Clink. Solenoid hum.

*QUANTITY: 3. START.*

The green light swept across the glass a second time.

Page by page, Maureen fed her coins and turned the historical record through the mechanical eye of the machine. The medication administration chart with Dr. Rao's fatal potassium overdose prescription. Janice Bradley's frantic handwritten notes describing the boy's burning arm and racing pulse. The purple rubber stamp reading *RESTRICTED — TRUST LEGAL GOVERNANCE*.

With each sweep of the green lamp, the truth was multiplied. Three sets of documents. Thirty crisp white pages.

When the final sheet dropped into the tray, Maureen gathered the papers into three neat stacks. She slipped each stack into a separate brown manila envelope she had bought at the post office on Thursday.

She reassembled the original green folder, re-threaded the green treasury tag through the corner holes, and wrapped it securely in the linen tea towel beneath the bread and the plums.

The municipal library's local history section occupied three quiet alcoves beneath the stained-glass memorial windows on the first-floor mezzanine. The leaded lights depicted the allegorical virtues of Industry, Temperance, and Fortitude, their amber and ruby panes throwing warm lozenges of color across the polished oak floorboards.

Maureen walked along the mahogany bookshelves while the copier cooling fan hummed in the corridor below. On the shelves stood the bound historical volumes of the West Riding Constabulary, the annual reports of the regional coal board, and the leather-bound transactions of the Yorkshire Medical Society from 1885 to 1974.

She stopped before a thick volume titled *St. Jude's Royal Infirmary: A Centenary Record of Public Healing, 1892–1992*.

She pulled the heavy book from the shelf. The paper was thick, creamy rag bond, smelling of old leather dressing and cedar wood. She turned the pages until she found the photograph of the opening of the surgical annex in October 1958.

In the center of the monochrome picture stood her grandfather, Thomas Finch, wearing his best Sunday serge suit and a flat cap, standing beside the town mayor and the hospital chairman. Her grandfather had been a master stonemason. His name was carved into the cornerstone of the maternity wing: *Built by the Hands of the People of this Borough for the Relief of Human Suffering.*

The men who built this hospital had not built it to generate twelve percent dividends for private equity partners in London. They had built it out of penny deductions from miners' pay packets, church collections, and the donated labor of railway joiners who worked on Sunday afternoons without wages.

Now, downstairs by the revolving doors, two security guards in fluorescent armbands were checking contractor badges, while Alistair Sterling and his corporate transition team prepared to lease the entire estate to Vanguard Healthcare for twenty-five years.

Maureen closed the centenary book and slid it back into its slot on the shelf.

She walked back down the cast-iron spiral staircase to the photocopier alcove. The machine stood waiting, its amber display light blinking softly in the shadows: *SYSTEM READY — INSERT COIN.*

Her fingers were steady as she reached into her coat for the next fifty-pence piece. Each coin was a week's savings from her tea money, but with every mechanical turn of the drum, Liam's true story was being fixed into permanent carbon ink that no shredder could ever destroy.

The municipal library's local history alcove was silent except for the soft rustle of newspaper pages from the main reading room below.

Maureen looked at the three manila envelopes on the table.

Each envelope contained a complete ten-page set of Liam's records: Dr. Rao's typed admission notes, Sister Bradley's handwritten nursing logs, the fatal potassium infusion order, and the purple-stamped legal governance memo.

She picked up one of the copied medication charts.

The black toner was fused deep into the white bond paper, sharp and permanent. Under the library's fluorescent tube, the numbers stood out like iron nails driven into a timber post: *Potassium Chloride 40mmol in 100ml saline IV over 1 hour.*

Any registered nurse in England who read that line would know instantly that a fatal dose had been administered. Any coroner who saw that document would recognize that the boy had died of medical poisoning, not congenital heart failure.

Maureen had three sets.

One set would stay in her coat pocket. One set would go into the tea tin behind the loose brick in the scullery wall. And one set would stay inside Liam's wooden tool chest in the bedroom wardrobe.

Even if Sterling's security guards searched her flat, they would never find all three.

The truth had been multiplied. It had escaped from the damp basement archive into the open air of the town, free from the threat of the Birmingham shredder lorries.

The warm, ozone-scented paper slides out of the copier tray into her waiting hands.

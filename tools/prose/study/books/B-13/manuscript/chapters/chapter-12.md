The visitor's log at Saint Anthony's Care Home was a heavy green hardback book that lay on the low counter beside the automatic double doors. Visitors were required to sign in with their full name, date, time of arrival, relationship to the resident, and whether they had experienced fever, cough, or diarrhoea within the previous forty-eight hours.

Maureen wrote her details in the third row of the Saturday morning page: *M. Finch. 10:15. Daughter. Room 14. No.*

The entrance hallway smelled of lavender air freshener, damp wool coats, and the persistent sour tang of steamed cabbage from the industrial kitchen. On the wall opposite the office, a laminated chart titled *RESIDENT CARE PROTOCOLS: DIGNITY AND CHOICE* displayed six color photographs of elderly people smiling in wheelchairs while young care assistants in purple tunics held their hands.

She walked down the long carpeted corridor toward the day room. The carpet was a mottled fawn pattern designed to disguise tea stains and wheelchair tyre tracks. Through the open doors of the bedrooms, Maureen could hear television sets playing game shows, the click-clack of walking frames on vinyl thresholds, and the occasional high, thin wail of a resident calling for tea.

In the day room, twelve high-backed vinyl armchairs were arranged in a semicircle facing a wall-mounted flat-screen television. The screen was showing a cooking programme with the volume muted.

Kathleen Finch sat in the armchair nearest the French windows that looked out onto the gravel garden. She was wearing her brown pleated skirt and a fawn cardigan with plastic horn buttons that Maureen had washed and ironed on Thursday. Her hair, once thick and auburn like Liam's, had thinned into wisps of white that curled against her pink scalp.

"Mother," Maureen said, pulling a wooden dining chair from the corner and placing it beside the armchair.

Kathleen did not look up. Her hands, mottled with brown liver spots and swollen at the knuckles, were gripping the arms of the vinyl chair with fierce, white-knuckled pressure. Her breathing was fast, shallow, whistling through her nose.

"Where is he, Maureen?" Kathleen whispered. Her eyes darted from the window to the television screen, then to the fire door at the back of the room. "The shift finished at twelve. He always comes through the gate by half twelve to wash his hands for dinner."

"Liam is not on shift today, Mother," Maureen said gently, keeping her voice low and even.

"He is on the shift," Kathleen said, her voice rising in pitch, turning sharp with panic. She twisted her body in the chair, trying to pull herself upright. "Mr. Henderson told him they had three window frames to finish for the new library. If he misses the afternoon, they will dock his wage. You know how strict Henderson is with the apprentices. He will sack him, Maureen. He will sack the boy!"

Two residents in the adjoining chairs turned their heads, their rheumy eyes blinking with slow confusion. In the corner by the tea trolley, an agency care assistant in a purple tunic looked over, her hand pausing on a plastic jug of orange squash.

Maureen reached into her black vinyl shopping bag. She brought out a large Jaffa orange, round and firm, its peel pitted and bright in the grey morning light. She also brought out a small kitchen paring knife with a wooden handle worn dark by decades of domestic use.

"Look here, Mother," Maureen said softly.

She held the orange up where Kathleen could see it. Kathleen's gaze caught on the bright sphere of orange. Her breathing hitched, a dry sob catching in her throat, but her white knuckles loosened slightly on the vinyl chair arm.

"Liam liked an orange after his dinner," Kathleen murmured, her voice dropping down an octave. "Always left the peel on the mantelpiece to dry for the fire."

"He did," Maureen said.

Maureen took the paring knife and scored the top of the citrus skin with a shallow, circular incision. She worked the blade beneath the orange rind, keeping the pressure steady, turning the fruit slowly in her left palm while her right thumb guided the cutting edge.

The peel came away in a continuous ribbon, half an inch wide, curling outward from the fruit like an apple peel. The sharp, clean mist of citrus oil sprayed into the air, instantly cutting through the stale odour of cabbage and carpet cleaner.

Kathleen watched the knife. Her eyes tracked the slow, circular movement of Maureen's thumb. The tremor in her chin began to subside.

"He was always good with his hands," Kathleen whispered. Her gaze had softened, drifting away from the fire door toward the green lawn outside the window. "Could plane a piece of oak until you could see your reflection in the grain. His father taught him that. Never force the timber, his father used to say. Let the blade do the work."

"His father was right," Maureen said.

The paring knife reached the bottom of the fruit. The single, unbroken spiral of orange peel fell away into Maureen's lap, leaving the pale, segmented fruit clean and intact.

Maureen broke the orange into six segments, laying them out on a clean paper napkin on Kathleen's lap. She took one segment and placed it between her mother's fingers.

Kathleen brought the fruit to her mouth, chewing slowly with her false teeth. A faint drop of orange juice appeared at the corner of her lower lip. Maureen wiped it away with the edge of the napkin.

In the day room at Saint Anthony's, the grandfather clock in the corner struck eleven with a tinny, wheezing chime. On the television screen, a cheerful presenter with bright yellow teeth was demonstrating how to glaze a fruit tart with apricot jam, but nobody among the twelve residents in the high-backed vinyl armchairs was watching.

Two chairs away from Kathleen, old Mrs. Crabtree was asleep with her chin resting on her floral bib, her toothless mouth slack, a thin thread of saliva glistening at the corner of her lips. Mrs. Crabtree had worked thirty-eight years as a ring spinner at the Lowfield Woolen Mill before the factory was demolished to build a retail park. Every morning at half past nine, she would wake up with a start, grip the arms of her chair, and shout: "Watch the belt, Mary! The roving is tangled in the spindle!"

Then the care assistant in the purple tunic would pat her shoulder and hand her a plastic beaker of blackcurrant cordial, and Mrs. Crabtree would drift back into her silent, nodding sleep.

Kathleen watched the orange peel curling across Maureen's lap. The sharp, oily mist of the citrus skin seemed to create a small island of freshness around the two of them, pushing back the sour institutional odor of old age and floor polish.

"Do you remember the winter of sixty-three, Maureen?" Kathleen murmured, her voice soft as falling snow. "The frost got into the scullery pipes before Christmas. Your father had to thaw the stopcock with an old paraffin rag tied to a poker. Liam was only a baby. We kept him in a wooden drawer from the chest of drawers by the hearth to keep him off the cold stone flags."

"I remember, Mother," Maureen said, guiding the paring knife through the pith.

"He had such bright eyes, that boy," Kathleen whispered, her gaze drifting toward the high casement window where two sparrows were fluttering along the brick sill. "Never cried when he cut his knee. Just came to the back door with his hands covered in sawdust, asking if there was a crust of bread with dripping."

"He was always hungry," Maureen said softly.

"Always hungry," Kathleen repeated. A faint, tender smile touched the corners of her dry lips. "And now he is working late at the joinery. Mr. Henderson must have a big order for the church pews."

Maureen did not correct her. She did not tell her that Henderson's joinery had been bulldozed fifteen years ago to make way for a bypass roundabout, or that Liam lay under three feet of damp clay on the municipal cemetery terrace. She simply broke the orange into segments, her fingers moving with quiet, infinite gentleness.

Kathleen's breathing slows as the sweet citrus scent fills the quiet corner of the room.

The medical records office in the east annex was empty at half past six in the evening. The clerk, a girl named Gillian who wore oversized tortoiseshell glasses and chewed her fingernails, always caught the 6:15 bus to Highfield. Her desk was clear except for an upright plastic tray holding blue ballpoints, a roll of sellotape, and a small ceramic hedgehog with a chipped snout.

Maureen wheeled her yellow cleaning cart through the double doors, letting the rubber bumper catch the wooden frame with a dull knock. The office smelled of warm laser toner, carpet adhesive, and the sweetish damp smell of cardboard files that had sat untouched in filing cabinets since the mid-nineties.

She pulled her cotton duster from her apron pocket. She sprayed the surface of the reception counter with surface cleaner, working the cloth in broad, steady circles. Her cloth moved over the laminated counter, lifting the day's grey grease from clerk elbows and visitor fingers.

The drawer was locked with a small Yale cylinder lock. A paper label had been taped over the keyhole with red security tape. On the tape, someone had written in black felt-tip pen: AUDIT HOLD — PENDING DESTRUCTION PROTOCOL B-14.

The tool was worn smooth from thirty years of scraping dried chewing gum and plaster residue from hospital skirting boards. Its edge was thin, rounded at the corners, and blackened with linseed oil.

The drawer slid forward three inches on greased steel ball bearings.

Inside, the manila folders were arranged in chronological order, hanging from green plastic suspension rails. Each folder had a handwritten tab along the top. Maureen flipped through the tabs with her gloved fingers.

OCTOBER 2004: BED 1 TO 6.
OCTOBER 2004: BED 7 TO 12.
OCTOBER 2004: BED 13 TO 18.

On the front cover, written in black fountain pen in Sister Bradley's familiar, compact handwriting, was the label: WARD 4: ADMISSIONS AND CLINICAL INCIDENTS — WEEK ENDING 17 OCTOBER 2004.

And then, forty-eight hours later, the second entry in red ink: Cardiac arrest, 03:40. Defibrillation unsuccessful. Declared deceased 04:15. Dr. Rao attending. Coroner notified.

She pushed the metal drawer back into the cabinet. It clicked shut, the latch resetting itself into the catch with a quiet snap. The red security tape across the keyhole remained unbroken; the drawer had simply slipped past the lock bar.

She picked up her spray bottle and wiped the steel front of the drawer, erasing the faint marks left by the putty knife.

Through the hallway window, the yellow headlights of an ambulance swept across the ceiling, followed by the dull, rhythmic wail of a reversing alarm down by the delivery ramp.

The medical records office had been built in the 1960s as a dispatch bay for the outpatient fracture clinic. Along the eastern wall, five rows of timber shelving held three thousand brown box files, their spines handwritten with white chalk numbers that had faded into grey smudges. The air near the ceiling was warm and dry from the cast-iron steam pipes that ran toward the maternity wing.

Maureen knew every corner of this room. In 1998, when the trust had attempted its first computerized patient index, she had spent four weeks moving sixty thousand paper records from the old hospital chapel into these metal cabinets. She remembered the dust: forty years of coal soot from the railway shunting yards, settled between the paper sheets like fine black sand. The clerks had worn white cotton gloves and paper masks, but Maureen had worked with a horsehair hand brush and two galvanized buckets of warm water mixed with carbolic soda.

The floor tiles beneath her cleaning cart were made of brown asphalt composition, pitted with indentations where heavy iron filing drawers had stood for thirty years. Whenever the delivery lorries rumbled down the ramp outside, the metal cabinets gave a faint, rhythmic rattle, their steel handles chiming against the painted drawers like small bells.

She paused beside the filing cabinet, listening to the silence of the east wing. Through the frosted glass of the entrance doors, the hallway was still. The only sound was the distant hum of the boiler house draft fans and the steady drip of the condensation pipe above the radiator. She looked down at the putty knife in her hand. Her father had bought the tool from an ironmonger on Kirkgate in 1952. The rosewood handle was dark with engine oil and palm sweat, its brass rivets worn down until they were flush with the timber.

"Never force a brass lock with a chisel, Maureen," her father used to say when he repaired the sash windows on the estate. "Steel cuts timber, but patience slips a latch."

She had remembered his words. She had slipped the latch without scratching the painted metal or tearing the paper seal. Inside her waistband, the cold card of folder eighteen was warming against her skin, molding to the curve of her hip beneath the nylon tunic.

Maureen memorized the reference code typed on the outer index card: STJ-W4-04-OCT-18. She wiped the desktop with her damp yellow cloth.

The administrative wing of Saint Anthony's Care Home was located in the converted coach house at the rear of the main Victorian building. The floor was laid with thin grey industrial carpet tiles that curled at the corners, smelling of stale coffee grounds, photocopier toner, and damp wall plaster.

It was ten o'clock on Monday morning. The autumn sunshine fell through the high casement window, illuminating millions of dust motes dancing in the cold air.

Maureen sat on a low wooden armchair opposite the manager's desk. Her hands were folded neatly in her lap, resting on her black vinyl handbag.

Mrs. Corcoran, the home administrator, was a woman of fifty with frosted blonde hair and large gold hoop earrings that swung against her neck whenever she turned a page. She wore a purple knitted suit that matched the corporate color of the residential care chain.

"We have reviewed the resident financial profiles across the entire regional estate, Miss Finch," Mrs. Corcoran said, her voice carrying the practiced, cheerful neutrality of an insurance claims assessor. She tapped a three-page printed document with the silver barrel of her pen. "As you are aware, utility tariffs have increased by forty-two percent over the last two quarters, and the introduction of the statutory living wage has added substantial cost pressure to our night nursing rosters."

"I pay four hundred and twenty pounds every month, Mrs. Corcoran," Maureen said. "On the first of the month. In cash at the counter. I have never been a day late."

"And we appreciate your regularity, Miss Finch," Mrs. Corcoran replied, smiling with her mouth while her eyes remained fixed on the printout. "However, the local authority block grant for high-dependency memory care has been frozen for the third consecutive year. The council contribution of five hundred and ten pounds per week no longer covers the baseline operational delivery for a resident requiring two-to-one assistance with personal hygiene and feeding."

She turned the document around and slid it across the wooden desk toward Maureen.

At the top of the page, beneath the embossed logo of *Oakhaven Senior Living Group*, was the heading: *NOTICE OF STATUTORY FEE REVISION & ACCOMMODATION CO-PAYMENT SCHEDULE*.

In section three, printed in bold black type, were the figures:

*Current Resident Co-Payment: £420.00 per calendar month.*
*Revised Resident Co-Payment: £920.00 per calendar month.*
*Effective Date: 1 November.*

Maureen stared at the black numbers on the white paper. Nine hundred and twenty pounds. Her monthly net pay as a Grade 2 domestic cleaner at Saint Jude's Royal Infirmary was eight hundred and forty-five pounds. Even with sixteen hours of weekend overtime cleaning the outpatients clinic, she took home nine hundred and ten pounds.

The revised care home fee was more than her entire monthly wage. It left ten pounds less than nothing for her rent, her gas prepayment card, her bus fares, and her food.

"I cannot pay this, Mrs. Corcoran," Maureen said. Her voice was flat, dry, without anger or pleading. "This is five hundred pounds more than my agreement."

Mrs. Corcoran gave a small, sympathetic sigh, tilting her head to one side so her gold earrings chimed against her collar.

"We understand this presents a transitional challenge for families, Miss Finch," Mrs. Corcoran said smoothly. "However, Saint Anthony's is an independent private healthcare provider. We are not subsidized by the National Health Service. If a resident's account falls into arrears exceeding twenty-eight days, our contractual governance requires us to initiate an expedited discharge protocol."

"Discharge to where?" Maureen asked.

"The local social services department would identify alternative local authority accommodation," Mrs. Corcoran said, tapping the desk with her fingernails. "Under current regional bed availability, that would most likely mean a placement in the municipal high-dependency annex at Highfield House in Goole, or the county psychiatric facility outside Hull."

Maureen looked through the window into the courtyard. Two elderly men in tweed caps were sitting on a green wooden bench, staring into the gravel driveway.

She knew Highfield House. Brenda's cousin had died there two years ago. A sixty-bed Victorian asylum with four residents to a room, bare linoleum floors, and agency staff who changed every forty-eight hours. If Kathleen were moved sixty miles across the county to a strange room with strange voices, she would stop eating within a week. Her mother would fade into the mattress and die before Christmas.

"Is there no reduction for long-standing residents?" Maureen asked. "My mother has been in room fourteen for six years."

"Our pricing structure is non-negotiable, Miss Finch," Mrs. Corcoran replied, her tone hardening slightly. "The revised agreement must be signed and returned to this office by Friday midday. The first revised instalment of nine hundred and twenty pounds is due on the first of November. If the signed mandate is not on file by Friday, we will be required to issue a thirty-day notice of bed vacancy to the council placement team."

She slid a black plastic ballpoint pen across the desktop, resting it beside the signature line at the bottom of the fee schedule.

Maureen looked at the pen. The plastic casing was printed with the white slogan: *Oakhaven: Caring for Tomorrow's Memories.*

She reached out her hand. Her fingers were steady, the nails cut short and clean. She took the pen, pressed the ballpoint against the rough paper, and signed her name in clear, upright letters: *Maureen Finch.*

She pushed the paper back across the desk.

Mrs. Corcoran opened a buff folder on her desk and drew out an itemized expenditure schedule titled *Oakhaven Senior Living: Unit Operational Overhead Allocation (Room 14)*.

"We believe in complete transparency with our resident sponsors, Miss Finch," Mrs. Corcoran said, turning the sheet so Maureen could see the columns of printed figures. "The baseline care grant from the local authority covers forty-two hours of shared personal care per week. However, your mother's latest clinical assessment indicates advanced cognitive drift and intermittent sundowning agitation. That requires level-four behavioral monitoring."

Maureen looked at the sheet. The charges were broken down with terrifying commercial specificity:

*Incontinence Management Consumables: £68.00 per month.*
*Nutritional Assistance & Hydration Support: £114.00 per month.*
*Wander-Prevention Sensor Mat Maintenance: £42.00 per month.*
*Laundry & Linen Disinfection Levy: £56.00 per month.*
*Energy Surcharge (Specialist Radiator Valve): £78.00 per month.*

Every aspect of her mother's declining life—every soiled sheet, every spoonful of custard, every nighttime hallucination about Liam's joinery shop—had been assigned a cash tariff by corporate accountants in London.

"Kathleen was a weaver at the textile mill for thirty years, Mrs. Corcoran," Maureen said softly. "She paid her national insurance contributions every week from nineteen forty-eight until she retired. She always believed the country would look after her when she grew old."

Mrs. Corcoran offered a small, professional smile that did not alter the cold appraisal in her eyes. "The welfare state was designed for an era when life expectancy was seventy-one, Miss Finch. Today, providing round-the-clock memory care for an eighty-four-year-old resident requires specialized plant, continuous staff training, and rigorous compliance infrastructure. Those facilities cannot be maintained on public sector block grants alone. Without private co-payments from families, Saint Anthony's would be forced into insolvency within six months."

Maureen took her Post Office passbook from her handbag. The blue cardboard cover was worn at the spine. She opened it to the final page. Her total savings—accumulated over twenty-eight years of twelve-hour cleaning shifts and weekend overtime—stood at two thousand four hundred and ten pounds.

If she paid the increased fee of nine hundred and twenty pounds out of her wages and savings, her money would be completely exhausted by February. By spring, she would be destitute, unable to pay her own rent, facing eviction from the council flat while Kathleen was expelled to the county wards in Goole.

"And if the council reassesses her dependency level?" Maureen asked. "Could the local authority increase their block grant?"

"The regional social care panel has a nine-month waiting list for funding reviews," Mrs. Corcoran replied, tapping the paper with her silver pen. "And under current austerity guidelines, eighty percent of appeals are rejected without oral hearings. If you wish to retain Room 14, Miss Finch, the revised co-payment must be remitted by the first of every month without exception."

She pointed to the signature line at the bottom of the fee agreement.

Maureen signs the payment agreement with a borrowed pen and walks out into the glare of midday.

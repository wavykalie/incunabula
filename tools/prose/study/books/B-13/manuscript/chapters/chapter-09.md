The copper tea urn in the basement staff room had a loose brass tap that dripped every nine seconds into a cracked ceramic saucer. Maureen watched the drops while she waited for the electric kettle to reach a boil. The copper of the urn was dull, clouded with grease from thirty years of fry-ups and steam, but someone in 1982 had scratched the initials E.R. into the base with the tip of a screwdriver.

Brenda Higgins sat opposite her on the green vinyl bench, peeling the brown crust from a paste sandwich. Her fingers were short and reddened by fifty years of caustic soda, carbolic soap, and industrial floor stripper. When she set her hands on the Formica tabletop, the joints of her middle fingers made a dry clicking sound like gravel shifting under a boot.

"They have changed the code on the sub-basement fire door again," Brenda said, her voice gravelly from forty years of untipped rolling tobacco. "Keypad with eight digits. Like Fort Knox down there. Porter said they are bringing in two shredder lorries from Birmingham on Thursday morning. The big ones with the diesel motors and conveyor belts that chew whole filing cabinets into grey confetti."

"Who told you that, Brenda?" Maureen asked.

"Young Gary from the boiler house," Brenda replied, taking a small bite of her paste sandwich. "He saw the delivery manifests on the transport desk by the weighbridge. Two twenty-ton wagons. Three days to pulp the entire back archive. Every admissions book from the old fever hospital up to the reorganization in 2005. Gone into the paper mill down in Scunthorpe."

Maureen kept her face still. The steam rose from the spout of the electric kettle, curling against the cold yellow brick of the basement wall. She took two thick ceramic mugs from the wooden drying rack, dropped a tea bag into each, and poured the boiling water. The dark brown liquor rose quickly, carrying the sharp, tannic scent of cheap Ceylon dust.

"Gary talks too much," Maureen said.

The disused service passage beneath Ward 4 ran two hundred yards east toward the old laundry boilerhouse. Decades ago, trolley porters had pushed heavy wicker hampers of boiled bedsheets along the subterranean track, guided by overhead gas pipes that had long since been disconnected. Maureen shone her yellow pocket torch along the whitewashed brick vaulting. Moisture seeped through the mortar joints, forming pale mineral crusts that resembled dried sea salt.

Stacks of obsolete filing boxes lined the northern wall, their cardboard warped and furred with grey mildew. Maureen knelt beside a stack marked *ESTATES AND CAPITAL ASSETS 1988-1994*. Inside, tied with rotting pink cotton tape, lay the original surveyors' blueprints for the hospital site. The drawings showed the boundary wall running sixty yards further south than the current chain-link fence, encompassing an orchard of damson trees and a two-storey brick dispensary that had been demolished in 1991 to make room for the executive car park.

Beneath the blueprints lay a sheaf of internal audit minutes from the regional health authority's land disposal committee. Maureen turned the brittle pages carefully with her thumb. The handwriting belonged to an older generation of hospital administrators, written with black ballpoint ink on carbon-copy paper. Every page contained calculations of square footage, soil contamination assessments, and projected commercial valuations for retail redevelopment. Even then, before Sterling had graduated from university, the institutional appetite for liquidation had been grinding forward. They had measured the value of the sickbeds against the acreage of commercial tarmac, waiting for the older generation of patients to die so the ground could be cleared for asphalt.

"He does," Brenda agreed readily. "Always sniffing round the student nurses down by the laundry bay. But he knows about the wagons. He has to sign the diesel chits for the loading bay space heaters. Sterling has got them working twelve-hour shifts down there. Private contractors in white paper suits and blue dust masks. You cannot even get down the ramp with a dirty linen cart without some lad with an orange lanyard asking for your photographic pass."

Maureen stirred both mugs with a bent teaspoon. She slid one across the table to Brenda, along with a chipped glass dish holding three lumps of white sugar.

"How is your Kathleen?" Brenda asked, lowering her voice until it barely carried above the rumble of the laundry machinery behind the partition wall. She stirred two lumps into her mug, the spoon ringing against the ceramic.

"The same," Maureen said. "Cold in her feet. Forgets what month it is. She told me yesterday that we needed to buy new curtains for the parlor before Easter."

"She was always a lovely woman, Kathleen," Brenda said softly. "Remember when she ran the tea bar at the cricket pavilion? Used to give the lads from the council estate two ginger biscuits for a penny instead of one. My Tommy thought the world of her. Whenever he came home with skinned knees from the slag heaps, she would wash him off at the outdoor tap and give him a barley sugar."

"She remembers the cricket," Maureen said. "She remembers the names of the fast bowlers from forty years ago. But she asked me this morning why our Liam had not walked in from his carpentry shift at the joinery."

Brenda paused, the mug halfway to her lips. She looked down at the dark circle of tea, her face softening into the creases around her mouth. "God love her. Twenty-one years, is it?"

"Twenty-one years come the middle of October," Maureen said. Her voice did not shake. She had practiced saying the phrase until it felt as flat and solid as a paving slab.

"He was a good lad, Liam," Brenda murmured. "Quiet. Not like the loud ones who hung around the bookmaker on Lowfield Road. Always had a smile when he walked past the washhouse with his timber rule sticking out of his pocket."

"He was nineteen," Maureen said.

They sat together in the low room beneath the laundry chute, listening to the muffled thud of hospital linen dropping down the zinc shaft from Ward 3 above. Every few minutes, a bundle of soiled sheets struck the canvas sorting bin with a heavy, wet slap. The basement air smelled of damp wool, carbolic powder, and the menthol tobacco in Brenda's tin.

Maureen held her mug between both palms. The heat penetrated the rough skin of her fingers, easing the ache in her thumbs where she gripped the mop handles each morning. She had two hours before her midday sweep of Ward 4.

"Sterling was in the records office this morning at seven," Maureen remarked, watching the steam rise.

"The suit?" Brenda made a sharp clicking sound with her tongue. "He does not miss a trick, that one. Walked right through the kitchen yesterday without looking at a living soul. Like we were part of the skirting board. One of the catering girls dropped a metal tray of custard pies and he just stepped over the yellow mess without breaking his stride. Never said pardon me, never said watch out. Just kept his nose in that little black telephone."

"He was going through the requisition books," Maureen said. "The ones from 2004."

Brenda set her mug down with a firm clatter. She leaned forward across the Formica, her grey eyes narrowing under heavy, lined lids. "Why would he be looking at twenty-year-old requisitions?"

"He wants the shelf space cleared," Maureen said. "For the private buyout."

"Vanguard," Brenda spat the name into the corner of the room. "I know their game. My sister's boy worked maintenance at Saint Margaret's over in Wakefield when Vanguard took the contract. First thing they did was cut the cleaning staff from thirty to eighteen. Then they brought in plastic mop buckets that split across the bottom in two weeks and told the lasses they had six minutes per four-bed bay. If you do not finish your floor in six minutes, they put a red mark against your payroll number. Three red marks and you are out on the street with no notice pay."

"Sterling said the hospital trust board signed the outline agreement on Friday," Maureen said.

"They will sign anything that lets them buy new leather chairs for the boardroom," Brenda said bitterly. She reached into her tabard pocket and pulled out a small tin of throat lozenges, offering one across the table. Maureen shook her head. Brenda popped one into her mouth, the sharp smell of menthol cutting through the basement fug. "They do not care about the patients on the wards. They care about their balance sheets and their management bonuses."

The heavy fire door swung open with a pneumatic hiss. Janice Bradley stepped into the room, wearing her dark navy sister's tunic with the silver epaulette studs. Her face looked drawn under the bare neon tube overhead, her hair pulled tight into a round bun pinned with three brown wire grips. She looked around the small room, her eyes resting first on Brenda, then on Maureen.

"Janice," Brenda said, shifting along the bench to make room. "Kettle is still hot."

"I have three minutes before the drug rounds," Janice said. Her voice was flat, drained of the brisk authority she carried on the ward corridors. She did not sit down. She stood by the enamel sink, running the cold water tap over her wrists to cool her skin. "Ward 4 has four admissions waiting in the emergency corridor. Two with chest infections, two with fractured hips from the council flats. We have eighteen beds and nineteen patients on the sheet."

"Where will you put the extra one?" Maureen asked.

"In the side room by the sluice," Janice said, drying her hands on a brown paper towel. "The one with the draft from the fire escape. It is either that or leave an eighty-year-old woman sitting on a plastic chair in the cold draft by the ambulance bay."

She threw the paper towel into the wire wastebasket. It landed with a soft rustle against the metal mesh.

"Did you finish the floor in bay two, Maureen?" Janice asked.

"Stripped and buffed," Maureen replied. "The linoleum was tacky from the urine spill this morning, but the polish took. The floor is clean."

"Good," Janice said. She turned toward the doorway, then paused, her fingers resting on the brass door latch. She glanced back at Maureen, her expression tight and guarded. "Sterling is coming down to the ward at two o'clock. He wants to inspect the domestic supply cupboards. Make sure the shelves are tidy."

"The shelves are always tidy, Sister," Maureen said.

"I know," Janice said quietly. "Make sure they are locked."

The door swung shut behind her. The room returned to the steady rhythm of the dripping brass tap.

Brenda finished her tea, poured the dregs into the ceramic sink, and wiped her lips with the back of her wrist. She stood up, smoothing down the front of her blue tabard. "Time to push the dust mop. If I am late for the corridor sweep, that supervisor with the red pencil will give me a warning."

"I will see you at four, Brenda," Maureen said.

"Look after yourself, love," Brenda said, patting Maureen's shoulder with a dry, worn hand. "And do not let that Sterling lad get under your skin. He is only paper."

Maureen remained seated at the table for two minutes after Brenda left. She took the metal key ring from her apron pocket. The small brass key to the Ward 4 records locker lay between the two large steel keys for the linen cupboards. She turned it over with her thumb, feeling the sharp notches along its edge.

Upstairs, the hospital hummed with the ceaseless friction of carts, call bells, and rubber shoes on polished floors. Somewhere three floors above, a ventilator clicked rhythmically in the intensive care bay. In the sub-basement below her feet, twenty tons of paper waited for the Birmingham shredders.

Maureen picked up her damp yellow cleaning duster, folded it neatly into four quarters, and pushed open the staff room door.

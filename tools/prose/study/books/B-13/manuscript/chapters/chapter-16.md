The bedroom in the third-floor council flat on the Lowfield Estate was cold, even with the small paraffin heater glowing on the hearth. The north wind came off the moors, rattling the thin wooden sash window that Maureen had wedged with two folded cardboard beer mats to stop the chatter.

It was half past eight on Wednesday morning. Maureen had clocked off her night shift at seven, caught the 7:20 estate bus through the grey morning drizzle, and walked the final four hundred yards past the boarded-up youth club.

She took off her wet wool coat and hung it on the back of the bedroom door.

In the corner of the room stood an old mahogany wardrobe that had belonged to her grandmother. Beside the wardrobe, where the wall met the chimney breast, the green linoleum flooring was loose along the skirting board.

Maureen knelt on the floorboards. She took a flat screwdriver from her sewing tin on the dressing table.

She inserted the screwdriver beneath the brass threshold strip that pinned the linoleum to the doorframe. With three careful turns, she backed out the small wood screws, laying them in an empty matchbox so they would not be lost in the floor cracks.

She peeled back the heavy linoleum. The backing was brittle with age, shedding grey jute dust as she rolled it back two feet toward the bed.

Beneath the floor covering lay the raw pine floorboards, eight inches wide, nailed down in 1964 with heavy square-headed floor brads. But the third board from the wall had been cut through with a tenon saw thirty years ago when the council had installed the central heating pipes.

Maureen lifted the loose board. It came up easily, pivoting on its tongue-and-groove joint.

The cavity between the floor joists was four inches deep, smelling of dry timber dust, mouse droppings, and old plaster. Inside the cavity, wrapped in three layers of heavy blue polythene tied with garden twine, lay her collection of notebooks.

There were twenty-one blue spiral-bound reporters' notebooks, filled with her neat, round handwriting. In those books, kept over twenty-five years of night shifts, Maureen had written down the things the hospital forgot: the names of old men who died alone before their daughters arrived from London, the final messages whispered by stroke patients who could no longer hold a pen, the true times of medication errors, and the quiet jokes told by cleaning women in the basement before the morning sweep.

She reached into her canvas shopping bag. She brought out the green manila folder: *WARD 4: ADMISSIONS & CLINICAL INCIDENTS — WEEK ENDING 17 OCTOBER 2004*.

She held the folder for a moment against her chest. The paper was cold against her worn wool jumper.

She placed the green folder flat inside a new, clean zip-lock freezer bag, pressing out the air before sealing the plastic track. Then she laid the sealed file into the cavity between the joists, placing it directly beneath the bundle of blue notebooks.

She smoothed the polythene wrap over the top, tucking the edges beneath the joist timber so nothing would snag if someone walked across the floor above.

She lowered the loose pine board back into its slot.

The wood settled into the rebated notch with a firm, solid thud. She rolled the heavy green linoleum back across the timber, pressing the edges down along the skirting board until the seams were completely flat.

She picked up the brass threshold strip, aligned the screw holes, and drove the three brass screws back into the timber with her screwdriver until their heads sat flush with the metal surface.

She stood up, brushing the white plaster dust from the knees of her trousers.

Outside, the estate was waking up. A milk float rattled down Lowfield Road with a clatter of glass bottles, its electric motor humming in the cold autumn air. From the kitchen downstairs, the pipes groaned as someone turned on a hot water tap.

Maureen put the screwdriver back into the sewing tin and closed the painted lid.

The bedroom in the council flat was cold, the air smelling of clean paraffin and the damp distemper on the plaster chimney breast. On the small bedside table, an old brass alarm clock with luminous green hands ticked away the minutes with a heavy, steady clatter: *Tock. Tock. Tock.*

Maureen sat on the edge of the iron bedstead, looking down at the opening in the floorboards.

The cavity between the joists was dark, lined with grey cobwebs that hung like lace from the rough pine beams. In that four-inch gap, sheltered beneath the linoleum and the wardrobe, her life's secret work lay hidden from the world.

She reached down and touched the cold polythene wrapping of the twenty-one blue notebooks.

She remembered the first notebook, begun on a freezing November night in the year 2000. An old man named Harold Wilkinson had died in Bed 6 on Ward 4. His daughter lived in Canada and could not afford the airfare. Maureen had sat with the old man during her meal break, holding his cold, trembling hand while he talked about his racing pigeons and his wife who had died of the scarlet fever in 1948. When Harold died, the night doctor had signed the verification slip in thirty seconds, the porter had wheeled the body to the mortuary fridge, and by seven in the morning a new admission with a broken hip was lying between the clean sheets.

Harold Wilkinson had vanished from the hospital without leaving a trace, except for a three-word line in the ward daybook: *Wilkinson, H. Deceased.*

Maureen had gone home to her council flat, sat down at the kitchen table, and written down everything Harold had told her: the names of his pigeons, the blue ribbon he had won at the Yorkshire show, the soft words he had spoken as his breath slowed, and the way the morning light had touched the brass cross on the ward wall.

She had realized then that if the working people of this town did not record their own dead, the hospital would reduce them to bed occupancy statistics and clinical disposal manifests.

Over twenty-five years, she had filled twenty-one books.

She had recorded the stroke patients who could only communicate by blinking; the young mothers from the council estate who wept silently into their pillows after a stillbirth; the retired miners whose lungs rattled like gravel under the cellular blankets; and the tired sisters who sat in the sluice room at four in the morning, crying with their aprons pressed against their faces.

And now Liam's file lay beside them. The green folder was safe in the dark, nestled between the joists, wrapped in heavy plastic where no corporate auditor and no police search dog would ever smell it.

The cavity between the floor joists smelled of old lime plaster and thirty years of dry floor dust. Maureen rested her hand on the pine joist, feeling the deep axe marks left by the council carpenters who had framed this flat in 1964.

Those men had worked with heavy tenon saws and broad-headed adzes, fitting timber together with the rough pride of West Riding tradesmen.

Her brother Liam had inherited that same pride.

When he was fourteen, Liam had built a wooden tool chest out of scrap mahogany floorboards he had rescued from the demolition of the old Methodist chapel. He had spent three weeks planing the timber by hand in the scullery, filling the council flat with the sweet, spicy scent of ancient cedar and pitch pine.

He had carved his initials into the inner lid: *L.T.F. 1999.*

She still had that tool chest in the bottom of her wardrobe, filled with his steel chisels, his wooden mallets, and his brass-bound spirit level.

Whenever she touched those tools, she could feel the heat of his young hands. She could hear his whistle as he planed an oak board flat, testing the edge with his thumb to see if the grain was true.

Sterling could shred his hospital files. He could destroy the clinical incident reports, pay off the solicitors, and lease the hospital to a private equity firm in London.

He could never erase the boy who had built that tool chest.

Maureen smoothed the blue plastic wrapping over the twenty-one notebooks, making sure the edges were tucked tight beneath the timber.

The floorboard settles back into place with a dry, muffled creak.

The executive boardroom on the fourth floor of the administration wing smelled of new wool carpet, lavender furniture polish, and the dry, chemical ozone of a laser printer that had just finished a sixty-page print run. A long mahogany conference table, polished to a dark mirror finish, occupied the center of the room. Twelve black leather swivel chairs were arranged around it, each positioned with mathematical precision opposite a blotter pad of heavy cream paper.

It was ten o'clock on Monday morning. The room was chilled to exactly eighteen degrees by an overhead air conditioning cassette that hummed with a low, continuous vibration. Through the double-glazed picture windows, the red-brick chimney stacks of the old boiler house stood stark against a pale, washed-out autumn sky.

Maureen sat in one of the leather swivel chairs at the far end of the table. She had not removed her beige wool coat. Her fingers rested on the worn handle of her black vinyl handbag, which she kept balanced squarely on her knees.

Alistair Sterling sat six feet away at the head of the table. He was flanked by Mrs. Valerie Crane, the trust head of human resources, a woman in her late forties with sharp, angular features, tortoiseshell spectacles, and a charcoal trouser suit that looked as though it had never been creased by human posture.

Between Sterling and Mrs. Crane lay three manila folders, an iPad in a leather folio case, and a white porcelain cup of black coffee that sent up a thin, straight ribbon of steam.

"Thank you for attending promptly, Miss Finch," Sterling began. His voice was calm, conversational, entirely devoid of rancor. He adjusted the knot of his silk tie with his thumb and forefinger. "We noted that you declined to be accompanied by a trade union representative or a workplace colleague. I must confirm for the formal record that you were notified of your statutory right to representation under Section 10 of the Employment Relations Act 1999."

"I don't need a union lad to tell me why I am here, Mr. Sterling," Maureen said. Her voice sounded thin in the vast carpeted room, but each syllable was distinct and flat.

Mrs. Crane made a small, precise checkmark in the margin of her legal pad with a silver ballpoint pen.

"As you wish," Sterling said. He turned the iPad toward Maureen and tapped the screen twice.

The screen displayed four high-resolution monochrome still images, captured by the infrared security camera mounted above the loading dock. Each image carried an orange timestamp in the lower right corner: *22/10/2025 00:18:42*.

The first image showed a woman in a blue domestic tabard standing beside the open doors of the sub-basement archive cage during the flood cleanup. The second showed the same figure bending down over a flooded shelving rack. The third captured her lifting a heavy bound folder from the water and sliding it into the waste skip. The fourth image, taken twenty minutes later at the staff exit, showed the same woman walking past the security turnstile, carrying a flat object wrapped in a plastic shopping bag beneath her coat.

"These images were captured during the emergency basement clearance on Tuesday night," Sterling said, resting his forearms on the mahogany table. "Following an audit of the archive bay four holdings, the facilities management team confirmed that folder STJ-W4-04-OCT-18—the clinical incident investigation file relating to the death of Liam Finch—is missing from its designated retention box."

Mrs. Crane cleared her throat, a soft, dry rasp. "The unauthorized removal, concealment, or destruction of hospital records constitutes gross misconduct under Section 14 of the Trust Disciplinary Policy, Miss Finch. It is also an actionable breach of the Data Protection Act 2018 and the Computer Misuse and Common Law duty of confidentiality. Such actions carry immediate termination of employment, forfeiture of accrued severance entitlements, and potential referral to the Crown Prosecution Service."

Maureen did not look at Mrs. Crane. She kept her gaze fixed on the steam rising from Sterling's coffee cup.

"My brother died in bed fourteen on Ward 4," Maureen said. "He was nineteen years old. You called it natural heart failure on his death certificate. You had the coroner sign a paper saying his heart had a birth flaw in the valves."

Sterling's expression did not change. Not a muscle in his face moved. He picked up his coffee cup, took a measured sip, and set it back on the saucer without a click.

"The clinical findings of 2004 were certified by registered medical practitioners and reviewed by Her Majesty's Coroner," Sterling said smoothly. "Whatever personal recollections you may retain regarding the circumstances of your brother's care, the hospital trust cannot permit historical files to be appropriated by domestic staff for private grievance. Saint Jude's is in the final phase of a commercial refinancing transaction with Vanguard Healthcare. The due diligence protocols require complete data integrity across all operational records."

He picked up the first manila folder, opened the metal prong, and drew out a four-page typed document printed on heavy official trust letterhead.

The air in the boardroom was dry, filtered through ceiling grilles that removed the smell of Yorkshire rain and coal smoke, leaving only the neutral odor of temperature-controlled air. On the wall behind Sterling hung an oil portrait of Sir Arthur Waterhouse, the Victorian textile magnate who had funded the construction of the west wing in 1895. Sir Arthur had mutton-chop whiskers and an iron chain of office across his black waistcoat, his painted eyes staring down with stern industrial piety.

Maureen looked at the carpet beneath her worn boots. The carpet was eighty-percent wool, dyed in a shade of deep petrol blue that she had vacuumed every Sunday morning for fifteen years. She knew how much it had cost: twelve thousand pounds, approved by the executive committee during the same winter that the domestic team had their tea allowance cut to save four hundred pounds on the ancillary budget.

Mrs. Crane adjusted her tortoiseshell spectacles, sliding a second printed sheet across the mahogany.

"We have also reviewed your electronic door access logs for the previous three weeks, Miss Finch," Mrs. Crane stated, her voice flat and formal. "Your proximity badge was recorded at the sub-basement fire door on seven separate occasions outside your scheduled shift hours. On two of those occasions, the records vault doors were accessed within four minutes of your badge swipe."

"The latch on the sub-basement fire door sticks whenever the heating pipes expand," Maureen said, keeping her eyes fixed on the silver coffee pot. "I carry a tin of graphite lubricant in my cleaning bucket. If I don't grease the bolt, the night porters kick the timber until the lock plate loosens."

"Maintenance of fire door hardware is the contractual responsibility of the estates engineering division, Miss Finch," Sterling replied smoothly. "Under Vanguard's service level specification, ancillary cleaning staff are strictly prohibited from performing mechanical interventions on security assets. Every unauthorized presence in a high-security document repository creates a severe data vulnerability under ISO 27001 certification."

Maureen took a slow breath. Her fingers tightened around the handle of her vinyl bag.

"Liam was not a data vulnerability, Mr. Sterling," she said, her voice dropping down an octave. "He was a nineteen-year-old apprentice who came into this hospital with a cracked bone. His mother washed his Sunday shirt for his funeral with water from an outdoor tap because the council had turned the mains off for repairs."

Sterling did not blink. He tapped his pen against the blotter with a rhythmic, measured cadence: one, two, three.

Sterling places a printed disciplinary charge sheet flat on the mahogany table.

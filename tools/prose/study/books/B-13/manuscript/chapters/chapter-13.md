The radiator in the corridor outside Ward 4 clanked three times every minute, like an iron boot kicking the skirting board. Maureen pushed her dust-control sweeper down the centre of the corridor, its wide red fringe gliding silently across the polished green vinyl.

It was ten minutes past one in the morning. The main ward lights had been dimmed at ten o'clock, leaving only the low amber night-lights glowing two inches above the skirting boards. In the bays, eighteen elderly men slept under grey cellular blankets, their breathing rising and falling in an irregular chorus of wheezes, dry coughs, and the soft whistle of oxygen tubing.

A pair of polished black leather brogues clicked against the linoleum behind her.

Maureen did not turn around. She maintained her steady, rhythmic stride, guiding the broom with both hands, letting the red cotton yarns gather the fine grit tracked in from the ambulance bays.

"You have a very methodical sweep pattern, Miss Finch," Alistair Sterling said.

He stepped alongside her, matching his pace to hers. He was wearing a dark charcoal wool coat over his grey suit, his collar unbuttoned at the neck, but his silver tie clip was still perfectly horizontal. In his left hand he carried a thin black electronic tablet in a rubberized shockproof case.

"The grit settles along the edges if you don't bring it to the centre first, sir," Maureen said, keeping her eyes fixed on the line of the wall.

"Quite right," Sterling said. His voice was quiet, hushed to an intimate murmur that barely carried beyond the two of them. "Efficiency in secondary processes is what separates an accredited trust from a special measures trust. Vanguard has benchmarked dust-capture rates across forty-two acute hospitals. Did you know that hospital-acquired respiratory infections fall by eleven percent when corridor floor grit is reduced to three grams per square metre?"

"We sweep twice a night," Maureen said. "Once at ten, once at four before the day shift clocks on."

Sterling stopped outside the door to bay four. He tapped the black screen of his tablet with a clean, manicured fingernail. The glass lit up with a pale blue glow, casting cold shadows across his high cheekbones and neat grey hair.

"Ward 4 has thirty-eight percent more storage footprint than the modern standard for an eighteen-bed acute geriatric ward," Sterling observed, reading from the display. "This floor was built in 1958 as a convalescent unit for dockworkers. Deep linen presses, built-in mahogany timber cupboards, double-depth sluice alcoves. Under our digital facilities remodelling, seventy percent of that joinery will be demolished. We need that square footage for battery charging docks and modular dispensing stations."

"The mahogany cupboards were put in by the shipbuilders' guild," Maureen remarked, leaning the broom against the doorframe while she pulled a yellow cloth from her cart.

Sterling smiled, a brief tightening of the corners of his mouth. "Sentimentality is a very expensive indulgence in modern healthcare, Miss Finch. When Vanguard takes over the estate, every square foot will be monetized according to clinical yield. A cupboard full of unwashed bedpans and yellowed paper records yields zero pounds per annum. A robotic dispensing carousel yields a twelve percent reduction in pharmacy turnaround time."

He walked slowly into bay four, his black brogues making almost no sound on the vinyl. He stopped at the foot of bed eighteen.

In the bed, an old man named Arthur slept with his mouth open, his thin ribs visible beneath the thin cotton nightshirt. An intravenous drip line ran from a plastic bag of dextrose on a stainless steel stand down to a cannula taped to the back of his withered hand.

"Arthur Phelan," Sterling read from the screen. "Eighty-two years of age. Admitted with chronic bronchitis and mild confusion. Four days in an acute bed. Cost to the trust: one thousand eight hundred and forty pounds. Discharge delayed due to lack of a social care package."

He turned his head to look directly at Maureen. His eyes were cold, grey as river ice in January.

"In twenty-four hours, Mr. Phelan will be transferred to a step-down nursing facility in Goole," Sterling said. "Private beds, outsourced social care liaison. The trust saves four hundred pounds a day by transferring him out before the weekend. That is the discipline this hospital requires."

"Arthur's daughter lives two streets away on Lowfield Road," Maureen said. Her voice remained completely level. "She walks up every evening at six to feed him his custard. If you send him to Goole, she cannot afford the two buses."

"The hospital trust is not a social club, Miss Finch," Sterling replied softly. "We deliver clinical interventions within contracted budget parameters. Personal convenience cannot dictate acute bed allocations."

He looked past her toward the heavy timber door of the sluice room at the far end of the bay. The brass plate on the door was tarnished brown around the screw holes.

"The archive contractors reported another anomaly yesterday," Sterling said, his voice dropping another fraction of an octave. "During the basement reconciliation, they found three requisition slips from Ward 4 signed in October 2004 that do not match the central pharmacy ledger. Potassium chloride ampoules. Four boxes of twenty, signed out to this ward, but never entered into the clinical administration logs."

Maureen picked up her broom. Her fingers found the smooth, grease-blackened wood of the handle.

"The sisters ordered their own drugs in those days," Maureen said. "The pharmacy was closed at nights. If a doctor needed something urgent, the night sister went down with the brass master key."

"And did the night sister also take the registers home when an incident occurred?" Sterling asked.

The silence between them stretched out, measured by the rhythmic clank of the corridor radiator. Three clanks. Four clanks.

Maureen looked at the brass plate on the sluice room door. "I clean the floors, Mr. Sterling. The sisters kept the keys to the drug cupboards."

The corridor outside bay three was silent except for the low hum of the floor buffing machine parked beside the linen store. The walls were painted in two shades of green: dark olive below the dado rail to disguise scuff marks from portering trolleys, and pale eau-de-nil above to reflect the neon strip lights.

Maureen leaned her broom against the wooden skirting board. She looked through the glass observation panel into bay three.

An old miner named Jack was sitting upright in bed twelve, his oxygen mask resting on his chest, staring at his gnarled hands in the dim amber night-light. Jack's fingers were blue at the nails, swollen with sixty years of arthritis and coal dust. Whenever he breathed, his chest made a dry, whistling rattle like wind blowing through an abandoned chimney flue.

Maureen had known Jack since she was a girl. He had worked the coal face with her father at Prince of Wales colliery. When her father died in sixty-eight, Jack had walked up the hill in his Sunday suit with two stone of potatoes from his allotment and laid them on Kathleen's kitchen table without saying a word.

Now Jack was ninety-two, waiting for a social care bed that the county council had frozen for the third quarter.

Sterling walked past the observation window without glancing through the glass. His eyes were fixed on the illuminated screen of his iPad, his fingers scrolling through the bed turnaround metrics for the acute geriatric quadrant.

To Sterling, Jack was an operational bottleneck: an eighty-day bed occupancy variance costing the hospital four hundred pounds a day in lost elective throughput.

To Maureen, Jack was the man who had brought potatoes to her mother when the house was cold and her father was in his grave.

Sterling taps the screen of his iPad with a manicured fingernail and moves to the exit.

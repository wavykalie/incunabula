The autumn drizzle fell over the forecourt of Saint Jude's Royal Infirmary with a quiet, persistent hiss. It coated the black asphalt in a grey sheen that mirrored the flashing yellow beacons of arriving ambulances and the red brake lights of taxis queuing at the drop-off bay.

It was half past eight on Friday morning. The air smelled of wet wool, diesel exhaust, crushed sycamore leaves, and the cold carbolic draft drifting from the basement ventilation grates.

Maureen walked up the sloping pavement from the Lowfield bus stop. She moved with her usual slow, measured stride, her boots gripping the wet flagstones. Her grey woollen coat was buttoned tight to the neck, its heavy fabric sheltering the three sets of photocopies and the twenty-two blue notebooks pressed flat against her ribs.

In her right hand, she carried the green plastic Co-op shopping bag. Inside lay the original 2004 green folder: *WARD 4: ADMISSIONS & CLINICAL INCIDENTS — WEEK ENDING 17 OCTOBER 2004*.

Two porters in blue overalls stood beneath the concrete overhang of the main entrance, sharing a roll-up cigarette before their shift. One of them was Dave, who had helped pump out the flooded basement on Tuesday night.

"Morning, Maureen," Dave called out, touching the peak of his woollen cap. "You're dressed smart today. Not on the mop?"

"Day off, Dave," Maureen replied, nodding to him as she passed. "Business with the management."

"Better you than me, love," Dave said, blowing a stream of blue smoke into the drizzle. "They've got security guards on the fourth floor today. Vanguard's solicitors arrived in three black Mercedes from Leeds. Big leather briefcases. Looks like the deal is getting inked before dinner."

"Looks like it," Maureen said.

She stopped at the foot of the entrance ramp.

Beneath the carving, the stone had turned black from a century of coal smoke, stained with streaks of green pigeon guano and diesel soot.

Maureen shifted the Co-op bag to her left hand. Her fingers were cold, but her pulse was steady, beating with the calm certainty of an iron pendulum.

She stepped onto the rubber mat of the entrance vestibule.

The main entrance doors revolved with a heavy rubber sigh, throwing a blast of warm, dry air into the vestibule. Maureen stepped out of the drizzle, shaking the water droplets from the edges of her plastic carrier bag.

Maureen stopped beside the main reception desk.

"Now then, Maureen," Arthur said, glancing at her beige coat and canvas shoulder bag. "You're walking light on your feet this morning. Taking the day off from the wringer?"

"Taking more than the day off, Arthur," Maureen replied, resting her free hand on the warm wooden counter rail. "I've handed my notice in to the personnel office."

Arthur's hand paused above his tea mug. He looked at her through his half-moon reading glasses, his bushy grey eyebrows drawing together in quiet astonishment.

"You're finishing?" Arthur murmured, lowering his voice so the queue of outpatients at the pharmacy hatch could not hear. "Thirty years, isn't it?"

"Twenty-eight come November," Maureen said. "Long enough to scrub every corridor in this building three times over."

"Long enough," Arthur agreed with a heavy nod. He looked across the foyer toward the glass elevators where two Vanguard managers in tailored suits were standing with leather folio cases under their arms. "It's not the same place it was, Maureen. When my lad had his appendix out in eighty-four, you knew every doctor on the ward. Now you have to swipe a barcode just to take a wheelchair down to the car park. The suits are taking over the shop."

"They're taking the shop, Arthur," Maureen said softly. "But they don't own what went before."

She smiled at the old security guard—a brief, warm parting that needed no further explanation. She adjusted her grip on the plastic carrier bag.

Among the names on the lower column was her great-uncle, Corporal James Finch, killed at the Somme in July 1916.

For a century, the Finch family had served this hospital and this town. They had laid its bricks, stoked its boilers, fought its wars, and scrubbed its floors.

And now a private facilities syndicate from London was taking the keys, replacing thirty years of local service with short-term agency contracts and computer efficiency matrices.

Maureen looked at the green plastic bag in her hand.

She was not walking into Sterling's office as a defeated cleaner begging for mercy. She was walking in as an equal, making a business transaction with an administrator who needed her silence as desperately as she needed his funds.

She walked past the RVS tea counter, where an elderly volunteer in a pink apron was pouring tea from a large stainless steel pot.

"Morning, Maureen," the volunteer smiled. "Bit wet out there today."

"Just a Yorkshire drizzle, Mary," Maureen replied, nodding with quiet warmth.

She turned toward the elevator bank, her boots carrying her forward with calm, unshakable resolve.

She pushes through the glass doors into the warm, clinical air of the main foyer.

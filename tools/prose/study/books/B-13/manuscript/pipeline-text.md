# Chapter 1: The Sluice and the Spiral

The yellow mop bucket held twelve litres of water from the low copper tap in the sluice room. Maureen Finch added three capfuls of pine concentrate from the dented plastic bottle beneath the deep porcelain sink. The vapour rose off the galvanised metal, catching the back of her throat with the sour, familiar bite of boiled resin and cold iron. She pressed the wringer lever down with both hands, throwing her shoulder over the joint until the perforated press met the rim and four heavy drops hit the drain tray below. Four drops. Her mother had taught her that count thirty-five years ago on the scullery stones of the municipal baths down by the canal: wring until four drops fall, no fewer, or you smear greasy gray film across clean terrazzo.

Ward 4 slept in five distinct keys. Down at the north end by the fire doors, the oxygen concentrator on bed twenty-two kept up an irregular hiss, like a bicycle pump worked into wet grass. Nearer the nurses' station, the two-second hum of the drug refrigerator clicked in every eleven minutes, rattling the glass vials of insulin and heparin against their wire racks. Between them lay the breathing of thirty elderly men in various stages of postoperative repair, cardiac observation, and stroke recovery: some rattling softly behind drawn cotton curtains, others drawing long, whistling draughts through slack lips, their false teeth soaking in pink plastic cups on the bedside lockers.

The ward smelled of steamed cabbage from yesterday's lunch, floor sealant, and the sharp chemical reek of chlorhexidine skin wash. Beneath those lay the older, deeper smell of the hospital itself: eighty years of damp river brickwork, boiler coal, and grease trapped behind cast-iron radiators that had not been dismantled since the coronation.

Maureen wheeled her rubber-tyred trolley over the brass dividing strip between the main corridor and Bay Two. The casters were greased weekly by Arthur, the night porter, but the left front wheel carried a flat nick from an encounter with an iron bedframe six months back. Clack-clog. Clack-clog. The corridor walls were painted that dull institutional two-tone common to every hospital built under the Attlee administration: dark olive gloss up to waist height, then a crumbly off-white emulsion reaching to the high acoustic tiles. At intervals of twenty feet, plastic hand sanitiser dispensers jutted from the plasterboard, their drip-trays stained blue with dried alcoholic gel.

"Who's that?" Mr. Garside asked from bed nine.

He had been admitted on Sunday evening following a transient ischaemic attack in the municipal library. His left eye was covered with an oval gauze pad taped down with brown surgical plaster. The left corner of his mouth drooped toward his stubbled jaw, but his right eye was wide and dark in the amber spill of the low baseboard nightlights. A plastic cannula was taped to the back of his right hand with clear dressing film, the polyurethane line taped in a neat loop up his wrist to prevent accidental snagging on the tubular steel bed rails.

St. Jude's had been constructed in 1894 as an infirmary for river dockers and their families. Beneath the suspended ceiling tiles, with their synthetic perforations and recessed fluorescent strip lights, the original Victorian brick arches remained solid and damp-chilled. In the service crawlways behind the linen closets, massive cast-iron steam pipes clanked and groaned like ship engines whenever the basement boilers cycled on. Maureen remembered the ward from thirty years ago, before the regional healthcare trust partitioned the long Nightingale hall into four-bed bays with pale laminate dividers. In those days, Sister Colquhoun had kept a bound admissions register with marbled board covers on the high walnut desk by the entrance, recording every patient's name and trade in purple fountain-pen script: boiler caulker, lighterman, cooper's apprentice, docker's widow.

Now the patients arrived through the computerised triage office downstairs, reduced to barcode wristbands and alphanumeric bed allocations on the whiteboard behind Janice's desk. Yet the dust remained identical. It gathered in the corners beneath the cast-iron radiators, a grey silt of shed skin cells, lint from institutional flannelette sheets, and dried road grit tracked in on visitor shoes. Maureen knew every seam of the linoleum by heart. She knew which brass screws had worked loose along the door saddles, and which window casements rattled when the east wind blew across the estuary.

She thought of Kathleen's first month at St. Jude's, before the illness stripped away her sister's sharp, sarcastic humour. Kathleen had sat upright in Bed 4, demanding loose-leaf tea instead of the instant catering granules Janice brewed in an electric urn. Kathleen had announced that a hospital incapable of boiling an honest kettle had no business calling itself an infirmary. Maureen had laughed then, standing beside the bed with a paper bag of warm Eccles cakes from the bakery on Duke Street. That was before the quiet arrived. That was before Kathleen ceased asking for tea, ceased asking for the evening paper, ceased asking why the curtains had to be drawn at eight o'clock.

"Just Finch, Mr. Garside," Maureen said softly. She parked the trolley against the wall, clear of the emergency call cords and the wall-mounted suction canisters. "Doing the skirting."

"The ferry's late," he said. He did not turn his head; his neck was stiff against the hard hospital pillow, his gray hospital gown bunched at the throat. "The slipway at Woodside's frozen over. We'll miss the seven-fifteen across."

"It's mild tonight, Mr. Garside," she said. She dropped her clean microfibre duster into the warm rinse basin. "No frost on the glass."

"You don't know the river like I do," he murmured. His fingers, thickened at the knuckles from fifty years of boilermaking in the lower dockyards, picked at the yellow woollen fringe of his cellular blanket. "Water's like black slate when the wind comes round from the bar. My father had his coat collar turned up to his cap. He had two ham sandwiches in greaseproof paper inside his pocket. He said to me, 'Arthur, keep your hands inside your mittens until we dock at the landing stage.'"

Maureen knelt on the cold linoleum by the skirting board beneath the heating radiator. The iron pipes clanked with trapped air, a slow, uneven percussion that ran through the entire north wing. She wiped the gray lip of the vinyl where dust gathered behind the electrical conduits and the medical gas outlets. When she reached the foot of bed nine, she sat back on her heels and drew the small blue spiral notebook from her inner apron pocket.

The notebook cost sixty-five pence at the newsagent beside the bus terminus. It was four inches wide, ruled in pale blue faint lines, with an elastic band looped through the wire coil. Her pen was a clear plastic ballpoint with a chewed cap.

She propped the pad on her knee and wrote:

14 Nov. Bay 2, bed 9. Arthur Garside, 79. Woodside slipway frozen. Father's coat collar turned up. Ham sandwiches in greaseproof paper. Keep your hands inside your mittens until we dock.

She had twenty-two filled notebooks in a shoebox beneath her bed at Cedar Grove. She did not know why she took down the names and the fragments. No doctor asked for them on the morning clinical rounds. The consultants walked past at nine o'clock with four junior doctors trailing behind them like goslings, their eyes fixed on the electronic tablets cradled in their forearms. The tablets recorded oxygen saturation, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine clearance, and fluid balance in neat blue bar charts. They recorded nothing about ham sandwiches on the Woodside ferry in 1954, or the girl in the red mackintosh who worked the ticket punch at the tram terminus before the tracks were torn up for the ring road. When patients died on Ward 4—and they died on average twice a week during the winter bronchial peaks—their paper charts were bundled into brown envelopes and sent to the archive racks in the sub-basement, while their words simply vanished into the extractor fans.

"Are you still there?" Mr. Garside whispered.

"Still here, Mr. Garside," Maureen said.

"Tell my father I have the return fare in my waistcoat pocket," he said. His voice was growing thicker, slipping into the heavy, rhythmic cadence of sleep. "Two pennies for the boat, and a halfpenny for the tram at the pier head."

"I'll tell him," she said.

She rinsed her cloth in the pine water, wringing it over the drain until the drops stopped falling. Across the bay, the plastic wall clock with the red sweep-second hand ticked past three-thirty. A tea trolley clattered on the service landing outside, and the smell of toasted bread drifted through the fire doors. Arthur the porter pushed through with two wire baskets of clean specimen bottles, his rubber shoes squeaking on the wet tiles.

"Quiet turn, Maureen?" Arthur asked, pausing by the water cooler.

"Mr. Garside's watching the ferry," Maureen said.

Arthur glanced at the bed, nodding with the tired familiarity of a man who had worked forty years of night turns in the same corridors. "They always go back to the water when the chest gives out. My old uncle was on the coal barges; spent his last four days shouting at the lock-keeper at Ellesmere Port." He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand. "Watch the basement stairs when you go down for the fresh mopheads. The radiator valve by laundry gave out at two; floor's like a skating rink."

"I'll take the service lift," she said.

Maureen picked up her bucket by its wire bail handle. The water inside sloshed against the yellow polypropylene wall, throwing a clean pine wave that broke over the wringer teeth. Beyond the bay windows, the lights of the chemical works across the river burned steady through the rain: orange flares on tall steel towers, silent against the dark clouds. The night shift had two hours left before the morning sisters clocked on at six. Two hours of wiping baseboards, mopping the sluice floor, and checking the paper towels in the staff lavatory.

She looked down at her hands. The nails were trimmed blunt, the skin around the cuticles reddened and cracked from constant immersion in warm soda solution. A blue bruise mottled the flesh between her thumb and index finger where she had pinched herself on the laundry chute door on Monday. Small marks, the everyday currency of a body worked hard in an old building.

Her mother's hands had looked identical at fifty-eight: the same thickened knuckles, the same yellow patch of hardened skin on the inner thumb joint from wringing coarse cleaning cloths. For forty years Kathleen had scrubbed the municipal wash-houses down by the canal basin, carrying home twenty-four shillings in a brown packet every Friday noon. Kathleen had known the names of every family on the dockside, who owed money to the coal merchant, who had a boy in the reformatory at Red Bank, and who had died of the croup in the cellars along Duke Street. When the corporation tore down the wash-houses to build the ring road in nineteen seventy-four, they had given Kathleen an eight-day chiming clock and thirty pounds in redundancy pay. The clock had broken three months later when Liam dropped his school satchel on the sideboard, but Kathleen had kept it on the mantelpiece anyway, its brass pendulum frozen at quarter past four.

Maureen stepped back into the central corridor. The floor was dark gray where the wet pine water lay, reflecting the long fluorescent tubes above in pale white rectangles. She drew her mop across the linoleum in broad figure-eights, pulling the dirty water backward into the center of the lane, leaving a clean border along the skirting boards.

Behind the closed door of Bay One, a bell rang once—a short, single chime that signaled a patient requesting a commode. Nurse Corcoran came out of the ward kitchen with a plastic bedpan in her hand, her blue paper tabard rustling against her thighs. She gave Maureen a quick, tight nod, the kind of nod shared by people who had spent eight hours on their feet without sitting down.

"Mr. Garside's settled," Maureen said as the nurse walked past.

"Good," Nurse Corcoran murmured. "His blood pressure was through the ceiling at midnight. The junior doctor wanted to give him labetalol, but Sister Bradley told him to wait until the morning registrar comes on."

Maureen wheeled her cart past the linen cupboards to Bay Three. The men in Bay Three were younger: two post-operative appendix cases from Monday, a scaffold worker with three fractured ribs from the new housing development out at Lowfield, and a long-distance lorry driver with a deep vein thrombosis in his left calf. They slept heavily, their breathing slow and untroubled by memories of frozen river ferries or dead fathers in woollen caps.

She knelt by the radiator beneath the window. Outside, a shunting engine blew its whistle in the goods yard behind the docks—two long, low blasts that died away into the rain.

She rose to her feet, her knees cracking in the quiet ward. Her apron pocket felt heavy with the little book, a cool square resting against her thigh. She pushed the trolley forward into Bay Three, watching the pale reflections of the streetlights swimming across the wet floor behind her.

Mr. Garside's breathing settled into a deep, rhythmic sigh. The gauze pad over his eye fluttered once, then lay still against his cheek.

Maureen closed the notebook with a sharp snap of the elastic band.

# Chapter 2: The Sacks in the Bay

At four-fifteen in the morning, the hospital went hollow. The night sister sat in her glass cubicle behind three plastic clipboards, her reading glasses pushed up into her gray curls. Down in the delivery bay, the yellow sodium lamps cast long amber cones across the wet asphalt where the clinical waste skips stood chained to iron stanchions. A wet southwesterly wind blew off the marsh, carrying the smell of tidal mud, diesel soot from the shunting yards, and the stale vinegar tang of the laundry exhaust vent.

Maureen pulled on her heavy green rubber gloves. The cuffs came halfway to her elbows, stiff with dried disinfectant and cold.

Beside the emergency fire exit of Ward 4 sat three heavy-gauge plastic sacks. The yellow sacks held contaminated dressings, catheter tubing, and used incontinence pads; they were sealed with red plastic zip-ties and destined for the high-temperature incinerator behind the boilerhouse. The clear sacks held clean administrative paper: drug packaging slips, discharge summary carbons, duplicate diet lists, and the printed nursing handover sheets from the previous evening's shift change.

Under trust policy introduced the previous autumn, all administrative paper containing patient names was classified as Level 2 Confidential Data. In theory, it went directly into the gray locked consoles supplied by an outsourced shredding contractor from Leeds. In practice, the consoles on Ward 4 had been full since Thursday, their narrow posting slots choked with folded card stock. Sister Bradley had left the surplus in clear sacks by the fire door with a handwritten note taped to the brickwork: Domestic staff: please bag for collection.

Maureen stepped into the recess behind the sluice door. The air here smelled of damp laundry, copper pipework, and the carbolic block in the urinal trough. She untied the green wire twist from the mouth of the first clear sack.

Her hands moved with the quick, unthinking precision of someone who had gutted fish on the municipal market stalls as a girl. She did not touch the tea-stained tissues or the plastic sandwich wrappers. Her fingers sought the thick, rough texture of the three-part carbonless paper used for manual bed transfers.

Most of the sheets were routine: admissions from the acute assessment unit, transfers down to geriatrics at Saint Jude's West Wing, ward diet tallies marked with black felt pen. But at the bottom of the sack, folded into four beneath a bundle of pharmacy returns receipts, lay an older form.

It was an A4 duplicate sheet printed on pale green paper. The trust had stopped using green paper in 2012 when the first computerised terminals were installed in the admissions office. The heading across the top read: St. Jude's NHS Trust — Ward 4 Acute Medical — Bed Turnover & Critical Incident Register.

Someone had pulled it from a storage box during the clear-out of the sister's back cupboard. The handwriting in the remarks column was written in faded purple ballpoint ink:

Bed 14. Finch, Liam. 19 yrs. Admitted 02:40 acute asthma / bronchial spasm. 04:15 sudden cardiac arrest following potassium chloride infusion. Resuscitation abandoned 04:52. Medical Director notified. Paper chart retained in Clinical Governance Office per Dr. M. R.

Maureen's fingers remained steady, but her breath caught in the wool of her collar. Her brother's name. Written in purple ink, twenty-two years after the night they told her mother that Liam's heart had simply given out from childhood asthma.

The paper felt cold against her skin, the carbon copy beneath the top sheet slightly smeared with moisture. She remembered the morning of 13 October 2004 with the clarity of a photograph left in vinegar: the policeman at the front door of Cedar Grove, his helmet held between both hands, the rain running off his cape onto the mat. He had said, 'Are you Mrs. Kathleen Finch? I have a message from the hospital almoner regarding your son.'

That morning had smelled of frying fat from next door and the cold drizzle on the cabbage patch. Kathleen had dropped her teacup on the hearth tiles; it had broken into three clean pieces, the milky brown tea pooling in the cracks of the cement.

Maureen looked at the form in her hands. The top margin bore a circular purple rubber stamp: CONFIDENTIAL — CLINICAL AUDIT ONLY. Beneath the stamp was the signature of the night sister on duty that evening: J. Bradley, SRN.

Janice Bradley had signed it. Janice, who sat twenty yards away in the ward office, drinking weak tea from a cracked enamel mug, her ankles swollen inside her white support stockings.

Maureen folded the green sheet twice, pressing the creases flat against the stainless steel draining board. She took off her left glove with her teeth, pulled her plastic lunch bag from her tote bag behind the mop buckets, and slid the green paper between two greaseproof wrappers that had once held cheese scones.

Arthur the night porter wheeled his empty laundry cage past the sluice recess, his rubber soles squeaking on the damp terrazzo. He stopped, leaned against the door jamb, and took a tin of tobacco from his coat pocket.

"You're behind on Bay Two, Maureen," Arthur said, rolling a cigarette with one thumb against the crease of his paper. "Sister Bradley's already asking if the floor's dry before the morning drug round."

"Tell her it's drying," Maureen said, keeping her back to him as her hands remained inside the sack. "Mr. Garside took ten minutes settling. He was watching for his father on the ferry."

Arthur nodded, striking a match against his thumbnail. The sulfur flare illuminated his gray stubble and the purple veins across his nose. "Old Garside was on the boilermakers' committee back in seventy-two. Led the strike against the yard closures; spent six weeks on the picket line with no strike pay and five kids at home. When the yard went under, he took thirty pounds and bought a second-hand lathe to turn brass fittings in his garden shed. Now he's lying in Bay Two, and the young registrars don't even know what a rivet looks like."

"Everything changes, Arthur," Maureen murmured.

"Not the work," Arthur said, blowing a thin stream of blue smoke into the laundry vent. "They change the names of the committees, they bring in gentlemen from London with silk ties and computer screens, but the linen still comes down with blood on it, and the floor still needs twelve litres of pine water every three hours. Don't let that fellow Sterling catch you sorting paper; he's had security checking the bins behind pharmacy all week."

"I'm just tying the sacks," she said.

"See that you do," Arthur said, pushing his trolley away down the corridor, its iron casters rattling over the expansion joints.

The wet mop swung in a steady semi-circle across the green vinyl of bay one. Maureen worked with the rhythmic economy of thirty years of night labor, her shoulders moving in harmony with the swing of the ash handle.

She looked at the brass radiator valves beneath the high sash windows.

In 1995, when the trust had replaced the old coal-fired boilers with gas-fired steam generators, the fitters had left the heavy cast-iron radiators in place. The pipes were three inches thick, covered in twenty coats of cream lead paint that had blistered into alligator skin over the hot water bends. Whenever the thermostat opened in the basement, the steam roared through the iron with a sound like a distant goods train crossing a viaduct.

She remembered cleaning this bay on the night the Berlin Wall came down. Brenda had brought a portable transistor radio into the sluice room, and four student nurses had stood around the macerator in their paper aprons, listening to the German crowds singing in the cold air.

The world outside had changed its borders four times since then. Empires had fallen, factories had vanished, and the coal seams beneath this hospital had flooded with groundwater.

Yet here on Ward 4, the human body remained what it had always been: fragile, leaking, prone to sudden collapse, and dependent on the quiet patience of women who knew how to wash a soiled sheet and soothe a dying breath.

Outside in the corridor, an iron laundry trolley rattled past toward the lift. Maureen knotted the sack mouth with a fresh green wire twist and set it back against the wall.

# Chapter 3: Under the Sink

The key to number 14 Cedar Grove turned stiffly in the brass barrel. Maureen lifted the front door slightly by its rusted handle to stop the warped bottom rail from scraping the linoleum in the hall.

The flat smelled of paraffin heater wick, damp wallpaper, and the carbolic soap she used on her aprons. It was five-forty on Wednesday morning. Through the thin plasterboard partition of number 16, she heard the muffled whine of Mr. Henderson's electric shaver, followed by the clinking of his water pipes. The estate was waking: milk floats clattering on the speed bumps, the low diesel roar of the council refuse lorry backing out of the depot on Marsh Lane, and the distant hum of the six o'clock commuter train cutting through the cutting toward the junction.

She did not turn on the hall light. She hung her heavy winter coat on the bent wire hook behind the door, took off her work shoes, and stepped into her felt slippers. Her feet throbbed with a dull, familiar ache that started in her arches and ran up into the tendons of her calves. Seven miles of walking on sealed terrazzo every night, five nights a week.

In the kitchen, the greaseproof wrappers from her lunch bag went into the pedal bin. The green paper sheet remained on the formica table beside the salt cellar, glowing pale under the low ceiling.

Maureen knelt before the small cupboard beneath the kitchen sink. The twin melamine doors were swollen at the base where the U-bend had leaked four winters ago before the council housing depot sent a plumber with three rolls of PTFE tape. Inside, the dark recess smelled of caustic soda, old newspapers, and damp floorboards.

She moved aside the two-litre plastic jug of thick bleach and the carton of washing powder. Behind the cold lead riser pipe lay a heavy black plastic carrier bag, wrapped three times around its contents and bound with red parcel tape.

Janice opened the tall grey stationery locker beside the medicine fridge. The shelves held three identical cardboard cartons of blue vinyl gloves, stamped with the logo of an offshore procurement consortium in Leeds. Janice pulled out a pair and stretched them over her damp fingers. The thin plastic split immediately across the right knuckle with a dry pop.

"Seventeen pence a box cheaper than the old latex stock," Janice said, dropping the torn glove into the clinical waste bin. "The regional procurement circular called it supply-chain rationalisation. That was Sterling's first policy directive when he took over the facilities portfolio three years ago. Save three thousand pounds on disposable protective equipment, then spend forty thousand on management consultants to explain why ward staff suffer dermatitis."

She pulled out a second pair, working them gingerly over her thumbs. "They think a hospital runs on spreadsheets, Maureen. If a patient sits in a chair for six hours without a bed, the administrative portal logs it as an ambulatory assessment period rather than a delayed admission. If an auxiliary nurse works twelve hours without a meal break, the rota software records it as voluntary shift extension. Everything gets renamed until the blood and the vomit disappear from the balance sheet. Kathleen remembers how it used to be. When Kathleen worked at the textile mill in Stockport, the overseer docked fifteen minutes of wages if a bobbin jammed. Sterling doesn't dock wages. He simply abolishes the post and calls it operational consolidation."

Maureen wiped the stainless-steel worktop around the sink with a damp sponge. The smell of diluted carbolic acid rose from the drain, sharp and clean against the staleness of the basement air.

She pulled the bundle into the pale light of the single fluorescent tube overhead.

Inside the outer carrier bag were three older bags from a supermarket chain that had gone into liquidation in 1998. And inside those lay the bound volume.

It was an A3 hardback ward register, bound in dark green buckram cloth with marbled paper covers. The spine was split at the top where water or damp had perished the glue, but the heavy white cartridge paper inside was clean. Across the front, written in white chinagraph pencil, were the words: WARD 4 — CASUALTY & ACUTE ADMISSIONS — OCTOBER 2003 TO MARCH 2005.

Maureen opened the cover. The pages were ruled into twelve columns: Date, Time, Patient Name, Age, Unit Number, Diagnosis, Admitting Officer, Medication Administered, Nurse in Charge, Discharge/Transfer, Remarks.

Page 142. The entry was dated 12 October 2004.

Finch, Liam. 19. Unit 04/8821. Acute status asthmaticus. Dr. Henderson. Nebulised salbutamol, IV hydrocortisone, 40mmol KCl in 500ml saline.

Beneath that line, entered in the same purple ballpoint ink as the loose green slip from the recycling sack, was a second entry in a different hand:

04:15. Cardiac arrest. Ventricular fibrillation. Resuscitation x 35 min unsuccessful. Dr. Rao attending. Chart removed by administration.

Maureen laid her flat hand over the page. Her thumb callus, hard and yellow as horn from thirty years of squeezing mop levers and scrubbing copper brassware, rested against the ink of her brother's name.

She remembered Liam in this kitchen in the summer of 2003, twelve months before that night on Ward 4. He had been seventeen then, six feet tall and thin as a lathe, standing on an upturned wooden fruit crate to scrape the greasy gloss paint off the pantry door with an old wallpaper scraper. He had whistled through his front teeth—the gap where a cricket ball had caught him on the school pitch at twelve—a tuneless, cheerful noise that drove their mother to distraction.

"Liam," Kathleen had shouted from the scullery, her hands white with flour from the soda bread, "if you whistle that one more time I'll take the rolling pin to your shins."

"It's the radio, Mam," he had called back, laughing down at her over his shoulder. "Top forty. You wouldn't know music if it bit your ankle."

He had had grease on his collar and white paint dust in his eyelashes. Three days later, he had brought home his first apprentice wage packet from the timber yard: forty-two pounds and fifty pence in a brown paper envelope with his national insurance number stamped in purple. He had laid the notes on this very table, pushed them across the oilcloth to Kathleen, and taken two pounds back for the pictures.

"That's for the coal, Mam," he had said.

When he died, Kathleen did not weep at first. She sat in the front parlour with her coat on, holding his timber yard timecard between both hands until the card softened and tore along the crease. The trust sent a formal condolence card signed by the patient liaison officer, enclosing a printed leaflet entitled Coping with Sudden Bereavement in Acute Settings.

Two weeks later, Kathleen had taken the bus down to the hospital administrative building. Maureen had walked with her, holding her mother's arm as they stood before the enquiry desk in the main foyer. A junior clerk in a nylon blouse had told them that the medical records were confidential under the Data Protection Act, and that requests for post-mortem transcripts required an application through the county coroner's office with a fee of forty-five pounds.

They had paid the forty-five pounds with postal orders saved from the coal money. Six months later, a letter arrived on stiff cream paper from the coroner's clerk: The coroner has reviewed the clinical summary provided by St. Jude's Royal Infirmary and is satisfied that death occurred from natural causes secondary to acute bronchospasm. No formal inquest will be held.

Kathleen had taken the letter to the church hall, to the citizens' advice bureau, to the constituency office of the Member of Parliament. Each official had looked at the cream paper, noted the signature of the coroner, and folded it back into its envelope. 'The hospital has followed standard clinical protocols, Mrs. Finch. Without evidence of procedural irregularity, there is no legal basis to reopen the verdict.'

Maureen looked at the page before her. There was the procedural irregularity: 40mmol KCl in 500ml saline. Forty millimoles of potassium chloride, an electrolyte that stopped a heart within minutes if infused without cardiac monitoring and slow titration. A death logged, stamped, and then excised from the public record by the removal of the paper chart.

She had taken the volume from the recycling skip in November 2006, when the hospital first began clearing out the Victorian records annex behind the old mortuary. It had lain in an open wooden crate beside thirty other discarded books, soaked with rain at the edges, waiting for the pulp lorry. She had wrapped it in a plastic bin liner, slipped it beneath her cleaning overall, and carried it home on the night bus.

For twenty years it had stayed beneath the copper pipe, wrapped in plastic, silent and untouched.

She turned the pages backward. The entries ran through the winter of 2003: coal miners with silicosis from the pits out at Thorne; dockers crushed between barges at the salt docks; women from the cotton mill with byssinosis, their lungs seized by cotton dust. Every entry was written in the neat copperplate cursive of the old ward sisters, the ink fading from jet black to sepia. The hospital had been an institution of stone and paper then, governed by matrons who inspected the corners of pillowcases with wooden rulers. Now it was governed by risk managers with laminated badges and outsourced private cleaning contracts.

Outside, the rain slackened against the kitchen window. The glass was clouded with condensation, small drops trickling down the pane and pooling in the rotted timber sill. Across the garden strip, Mrs. Gidley was hanging out a gray nylon sheet on the rotary washing line, her wooden clothes pegs gripped between her teeth.

Maureen smoothed the loose green sheet from the hospital sack and laid it between pages 142 and 143 of the bound register. It fit precisely, the column lines matching the printed margins of the book.

The room grew colder as the boiler clicked off. She rewrapped the volume in the three plastic bags, pressed the parcel tape tight around the edges, and slid the parcel back into the dark recess behind the copper pipe.

Maureen sat at the formica table, her tea mug growing cold beside the salt cellar. Through the thin walls of the council flat, she could hear the water hammer in number 12, followed by Mrs. Higgins coughing her dry smoker's cough into the courtyard.

Her mind traveled back to the winter after Liam died, when the flat had been so quiet that the ticking of the kitchen clock sounded like a hammer against stone. Kathleen had refused to light the coal fire in the parlour, saying that burning coal when Liam wasn't there to warm his boots was a waste of four shillings a hundredweight. She had sat in the kitchen wrapped in an old tartan traveling rug, her feet resting on an upturned wooden crate, writing letters in pencil on lined paper torn from school exercise books.

'Dear Mr. Coroner,' Kathleen had written, her hand shaking with the tremors that were the first silent sign of the sickness to come. 'My boy Liam was nineteen. He had no heart trouble. The hospital told me he had an attack of asthma, but the nurse who came to the door said there was a mistake with the drip. I want to know what was in the bottle. He was my youngest child, and he had his whole life before him.'

She had written to the Member of Parliament, Mr. Callaghan. She had written to the Bishop of Liverpool. She had written to the General Medical Council in London. Every three months, an envelope with an official crest would arrive in the morning post. The letters were always polite. They were always typed on heavy, cream bond paper that smelled of typewriter ribbon and cedar. They always said that the clinical audit had been conducted in full accordance with NHS governance frameworks, that the coroner had certified natural causes, and that the matter was formally closed.

'They're waiting for me to die, Maureen,' Kathleen had said one evening, looking out through the rain at the brick wall of the substation. 'They think if they wait long enough, the paper will turn yellow and the people who knew the truth will be under the grass. But God has a book too, Maureen. God has every name written down, and He doesn't lose the pages in the wash.'

Maureen had believed her then. She had believed that truth was an enduring stone that time could not wear away. But sitting in the cold kitchen twenty years later, she knew that institutions did not fear God; they feared audit liabilities and legal discovery. If the paper under her sink were burned, Liam's death would exist only in the fading memories of an eighty-two-year-old woman who could not remember what she had eaten for breakfast, and a fifty-eight-year-old cleaner who spent her nights scrubbing disinfectant into linoleum.

She pushed the bleach bottles back into place. The melamine doors shut with a dull snap of their magnetic catch.

# Chapter 4: The Notice on the Pigeonhole

The bus to Saint Anthony's Residential Home took forty minutes from the estate, crawling through the morning traffic on the ring road while rain smeared the upper deck windows with a greasy gray haze.

Maureen sat on the lower deck, directly behind the driver's cab where the heater fan blew a lukewarm stream of air smelling of wet overcoats and diesel oil. Her handbag lay across her lap. Inside, beneath her purse and her spare handkerchief, was the white envelope she had received from the council benefits office on Monday.

Saint Anthony's occupied an Edwardian red-brick villa on the brow of Highfield Hill. It had once been the home of a wealthy worsted manufacturer, but the carriage drive was now covered in asphalt and bordered by plastic wheelie bins. Inside the heavy oak double doors, the air was warm, sweet, and close: lavender air freshener, overboiled carrots, and the medicinal tang of chlorhexidine skin wash.

The manager, Mrs. Appleby, was sorting the post at the reception counter. She was fifty, with pearl earrings and a cardigan the colour of tinned salmon.

"Morning, Maureen," she said. Her smile did not reach the lines around her mouth. She drew a long manila envelope from the row of wooden pigeonholes behind the desk. "I was going to put this in the post, but since you're here, it saves the stamp."

The letterhead was printed in dark blue ink: Saint Anthony's Care Group (Northern) Ltd. Fee Schedule Revision: Memory Care Unit.

Maureen did not open the flap. "The top-up?"

"Head office sent the circular yesterday," Mrs. Appleby said. She lowered her voice, leaning over the counter so the two residents dozing in the vinyl armchairs by the radiator would not hear. "Gas and electricity have gone up forty percent since October. The trust top-up from the local authority only covers the standard nursing rate. For the specialist dementia suite—the extra staffing ratio for the night turn—the family contribution is going to eight hundred and forty pounds a month from the first of January."

"Eight hundred and forty," Maureen repeated. The words felt flat and heavy on her tongue, like dry gravel. "It was seven hundred and ten in the spring."

"I know, love," Mrs. Appleby said. Her hand touched Maureen's forearm for a second, then retreated to the stack of invoices. "The council won't bridge the gap. If the account falls three months in arrears, the regional director flags the bed for transfer. They'd reassign Kathleen to the municipal annex out at Greystones."

Maureen looked through the wired-glass observation panel into the day room. Her mother sat in an armchair by the television, wearing a cardigan of faded pink wool. Her hands were moving over her lap, picking at the seam of her floral apron with steady, rhythmic patience.

Greystones had thirty-six beds to a room, partitioned with plastic curtains, forty miles south on the edge of the coalfield. It was an old workhouse infirmary converted in the nineteen-seventies, cold and drafty, where the staff turnover was fifty percent every six months and the residents sat in rows with their coats on.

"I have the overtime on the night rounds," Maureen said. "The extra bank shifts on Ward 4."

"Just make sure the bank standing order is amended by the fifteenth," Mrs. Appleby said. She picked up her pen. "She's had a quiet morning. Ate half a poached egg on toast. But she's asking after Liam again."

Maureen walked down the linoleum corridor to the day room. The television was playing an old quiz show, the sound turned low so only the laughter of the studio audience punctuated the hum of the strip lights.

She sat on the low footstool beside her mother's chair.

"Mam," she said.

Kathleen turned her head slowly. Her eyes, pale blue and watery with cataracts, drifted over Maureen's face, searching for a pattern among the lines.

"You're late from the market," Kathleen said. Her voice was thin, like paper drawn across a comb. "Did you get the haddock? Liam said he wanted fish cakes for his tea."

"I have the haddock in the larder, Mam," Maureen said. She took her mother's right hand. The skin was dry and cool, spotted with brown age marks like freckles on a hen's egg. "Everything's in hand."

Kathleen nodded. Her gaze returned to the television screen. She reached into the pocket of her pink cardigan and drew out a small satsuma, rolling it between her palms.

"He'll be cold coming off the timber wagons," Kathleen murmured. "The wind along the canal comes right through his denim jacket. I told him to wear his thick jersey, but you know how he is. Vain as a peacock about his hair."

She began to peel the satsuma. Her fingers, though bent with arthritis at the second joint, worked with astonishing, delicate patience. The orange rind came away in a single unbroken spiral, curling downward across her lap like a paper ribbon.

"Look at that, Maureen," Kathleen whispered, pointing with her thumbnail at the spiral peel. "One piece. Your father could never do that. He hacked them into chunks like firewood."

Maureen watched the spiral curl against the faded floral apron. She took a quarter of the fruit when her mother held it out, tasting the sharp, sweet juice on the back of her tongue.

The citrus scent hung in the dry, heated air of the room, cutting through the smell of cabbage and skin wash. Outside the window, a coal tit landed on the wooden bird table, pecked twice at a dry crust of toast, and flew off toward the laurel hedge.

Mrs. Appleby adjusted her pearl earrings, her gaze sliding past Maureen to the stack of direct debit forms on the counter. "I know it's hard, Maureen. But the cost of agency nursing has doubled since the winter virus hit the wards. If we don't have two qualified nurses on duty overnight, the Care Quality Commission serves us with an improvement notice. That means reduced admissions, and that means the fees have to rise across the board."

"The council pension doesn't rise," Maureen said.

"The council pays the local authority baseline rate," Mrs. Appleby replied, her voice dropping into that smooth, sympathetic tone that social care managers practiced for family reviews. "Three hundred and eighty pounds a week. But that only covers basic residential care—a shared room in the old wing, meals, and standard supervision. Kathleen is in the high-dependency dementia suite. She needs two carers to assist with bathing, her food has to be pureed, and she wanders between two and four in the morning. If the family top-up stops, the trust simply moves her bed."

"Greystones has no night staff in the rooms," Maureen said. "My cousin had her husband out there before he died. They lock the bedroom doors at nine o'clock and leave them with call bells they can't even reach."

"Greystones is the municipal provider," Mrs. Appleby said neutrally. "It meets the statutory minimum standard. Saint Anthony's is a private charitable trust. We can't carry deficits on individual residents; the trustees won't permit it."

Maureen looked at the glass case behind the reception desk where the care home displayed photographs of summer garden fetes and Christmas carol concerts. In every picture, the residents looked clean, well-dressed, and peaceful, sitting in wicker armchairs on the manicured lawn. That peace was bought at eight hundred and forty pounds a month. It was bought with every overtime hour she worked on Ward 4, wringing cotton mopheads until four drops fell onto the drain tray.

Brenda sat back against the painted brick wall of the basement boiler house corridor, her cigarette smoke curling up into the warm air around the overhead steam risers.

"Do you know what that young management trainee with the blue lanyard asked me yesterday, Maureen?" Brenda said, shaking her head with dry amusement. "He asked me if I had completed my digital module on chemical hazard communication."

"What did you tell him, Brenda?" Maureen asked, folding her yellow dusting cloth.

"I told him I've been mixing sodium hypochlorite and caustic floor stripper since nineteen seventy-six," Brenda replied, taking a slow drag of her roll-up. "I told him I can tell the concentration of an acid solution by the way it stings the hair on my arms. He looked at me like I was an escapee from the Victorian workhouse. Wrote three lines on his iPad about non-compliant operational culture."

Maureen took a sip of her tea from the enamel mug.

The air near the boiler house was always eighty degrees, smelling of dry lagging, lubricating oil, and the sweetish dampness of steam leaks. It was the only warm place in the entire sub-basement during the long winter nights.

"They want everything written down on a computer screen, Brenda," Maureen remarked quietly. "Because if it's on a screen, they can pretend it doesn't smell of carbolic and sick."

"Aye," Brenda agreed, tapping her ash into the tin lid. "They want a hospital without the mess. But sick folk are mess, Maureen. From the day they're born to the day they're laid out in the chapel, human beings are nothing but mess and trouble. And when the computers break down, it's still two lasses with a mop bucket who have to clean the floor."

Kathleen smoothed the hem of her cardigan, her thumb tracing the rim of a mismatched bone button.

# Chapter 5: The Red Signs in the Basement

On Thursday night, the basement corridors of Saint Jude's smelled of hot cardboard, damp masonry, and the oily reek of old machinery.

A radiator valve had failed near the laundry chute on Monday, and two industrial dehumidifiers hummed against the brick walls, their ribbed plastic drainage hoses snaking across the concrete floor into a floor grate. Above the double doors leading into the Medical Records Archive, two large laminated posters had been tacked to the wooden lintel with industrial staples.

The posters were printed in bright scarlet ink:

PROJECT CLEAN SWEEP — PHASE ONE
Operational Transition & Digital Records Rationalisation
All historical paper holdings pre-dating 2010 are scheduled for consolidation, scanning, and off-site destruction per Trust Directive 24/8.
Authorised Personnel Only.

Maureen stood thirty paces back, her hands resting on the rubber handle of her cleaning trolley.

The double doors stood wide open. Inside, where thirty rows of gray steel shelving had held cardboard box files since the hospital was rebuilt after the war, four men in navy blue overalls were stacking heavy lidded cartons onto wooden pallets. A battery-powered pallet truck beeped with a shrill, intermittent alarm as it reversed into the main aisle. The overhead strip lights, unshielded and buzzing with sixty-cycle hum, illuminated mountains of yellowing paper: card files bound with pink legal tape, pathology biopsy registers, patient index drawers, and casualty daybooks from decades when the city was still choked with iron foundries and coal smoke.

Standing beside the warehouse desk was a man she had not seen on the night shift before.

He was thirty-seven or thirty-eight, wearing a tailored charcoal suit without a waistcoat, a pale blue shirt, and a dark silk tie fastened with a plain silver bar clip. He carried an iPad in a slim black leather sleeve. As Maureen watched, he raised his right hand, took a microfibre cloth from his breast pocket, and wiped the glass screen with two swift, diagonal strokes.

"Excuse me," he said.

He had turned without Maureen hearing his shoes on the concrete. His dress shoes were black Oxford calfskin, polished to a mirror shine that showed the yellow reflection of the overhead strip lights.

"Good evening, sir," Maureen said. Her voice dropped into the flat, neutral register she used for consultants and senior nursing officers.

"Domestic services?" he asked. He looked at her blue nylon tunic, the yellow plastic identity badge pinned above her pocket, and the heavy zinc bucket on her cart.

"Finch," she said. "Night domestic, Ward 4."

He tapped the screen of the tablet with the knuckle of his middle finger. A digital roster appeared in green and white columns. "Alistair Sterling. Director of Operational Transition. We're clearing the corridor approaches for the shredding contractors. The industrial trucks arrive at four in the morning. I don't want cleaning equipment obstructing the turning circle by the laundry ramp."

"The trolley stays in the alcove, Mr. Sterling," she said. "Behind the fire doors."

"Ensure it does," he said. He looked past her shoulder toward the archive interior, where two men were lifting a box marked Medical Records: Casualty 2002–2005 onto the pallet. "Every hour that truck idles outside the loading bay costs the trust four hundred and twenty pounds in plant hire. The board has signed off on thirty-two thousand pounds of facilities modernization for this quadrant alone. We cannot have operational drag from legacy housekeeping routines."

"No, sir," she said.

He glanced at his wristwatch—a thin black digital face that glowed pale green for three seconds before going dark. "Have you worked Ward 4 long, Miss Finch?"

"Twenty-eight years," she said.

Sterling's eyes narrowed slightly. He flicked the silver tie clip with his thumb, a small, metallic snap that echoed in the vaulted corridor. "Then you'll remember the old paper filing system. The ward registers."

"The ward registers went to the records office every six months," she said. She did not blink. Her right thumb found the hardened yellow callus on its inner joint, pressing it against the seam of her trousers. "We kept the sluice and the floor clean. The paperwork was the sisters' business."

Sterling nodded slowly. His expression remained smooth, pleasant, completely without heat. "Quite right. Historical records are a compliance liability, Miss Finch. They occupy cubic metres that could house acute diagnostic equipment, and they cost eighty thousand pounds a year in climate control and pest prevention. In six weeks, every clinical record in this hospital will sit on a secure server in Dublin. Clean, searchable, and incapable of being misplaced."

He walked two paces closer to her trolley, his eyes dropping to the bundle of cleaning cloths in the wire basket.

"The audit team reported three legacy admissions volumes unaccounted for during the pre-clearance inventory," he said, his tone casual, almost conversational. "Two from the old thoracic unit, and one from Ward 4. Acute admissions from the early two thousands. Missing from the lower shelving bays."

"The basements were flooded twice in the winter of 2007," Maureen said, her voice steady as an iron bar. "The water came up eight inches over the floorboards. The porters took four skips of rotted paper to the incinerator."

Sterling smiled—a thin, practiced movement of the lips that left his gray eyes completely untouched. "Indeed. Water damage is the standard explanation in provincial facilities. Fortunately, our digital migration forensic protocols allow us to cross-reference pharmacy dispensing logs against death registrations. Any discrepancies in mortality reporting will be resolved before the facilities management tender closes in March."

He adjusted his lapel with two fingers.

"The trust board cannot enter a private facilities partnership with unquantified legacy liabilities hanging over the balance sheet," Sterling said. "Private investors do not tolerate unresolved historical inquiries. We either present an auditable, sanitised operational profile, or the entire capital allocation for Saint Jude's is diverted to the regional teaching hospital in Leeds. That means ward closures, Miss Finch. It means four hundred redundancies across domestic, portering, and maintenance staff."

Maureen met his eyes. His gaze was clear, pale, and utterly devoid of hostility. He was not an angry man; he was an accountant executing a spreadsheet formula.

"We do our work, Mr. Sterling," she said.

"See that you do," he replied. "A modern trust requires total alignment. When private facilities management takes over domestic services in June, staffing levels will be benchmarked against national efficiency metrics. Staff who cooperate with transition protocols will find their pension entitlements preserved under TUPE regulations. Those who generate friction will simply be phased out during the restructuring."

He pointed toward the service lift.

"Keep the corridors clear, Miss Finch. We have eighty tonnes of paper leaving this facility by Friday."

He turned back toward the warehouse aisle, his leather soles squeaking sharply on the damp floorboards as he called out an instruction to the men with the pallets.

Sterling tapped his tablet screen again, bringing up a complex multi-layered architectural drawing of the hospital estate. Red and blue boundary lines divided the Victorian brick wards from the concrete additions of the seventies.

"The facilities management concession model requires absolute transparency," Sterling continued, walking slowly along the wall of the basement corridor. "The London bidding consortium—Vanguard Healthcare Services—will assume responsibility for all non-clinical operations on a twenty-five-year private finance lease. That includes estate maintenance, catering, sterilization, security, and domestic cleaning. Their financial models assume an immediate eight percent reduction in operational overheads."

"They'll cut the cleaners," Maureen said.

Sterling turned his cool, pale eyes toward her. "They will introduce mechanized automation, Miss Finch. Modern industrial floor scrubbers operated by centralized teams, rather than individual ward domestics assigned to specific areas. The current model—where night staff spend twenty minutes cleaning baseboards while chatting with patients—is an anachronism. It generates unquantified wage inflation without measurable improvements in microbiological clearance."

"The patients need someone to talk to at night," she said.

"The patients are under the clinical care of registered nursing staff," Sterling countered smoothly. "Domestic services exist to achieve microbiological sanitation per square meter within established cost parameters. A cleaner who spends her shift listening to elderly stroke patients reminisce about the dockyards is not performing a clinical function; she is simply creating operational inefficiency."

He gestured toward the open archive doors, where the pallet truck was lifting another load of boxes.

"Every legacy box in that vault creates a legal liability under the Freedom of Information Act. Historical patient files, handwritten nursing daybooks, incident reports from twenty years ago—they are magnets for speculative litigation from claims management companies. By consolidating these holdings and certifying their destruction under statutory records management guidelines, we insulate the trust against historical claims. That allows the buyout to proceed without price discounting."

Maureen looked at the gray dust settling on his polished shoes. "And if a family wants to know what happened to someone who died?"

"The digital summary index will record the date, the certified cause of death, and the attending physician," Sterling said. "That fulfills every statutory obligation under the Public Records Act. The secondary paper records—the subjective notes, the handovers, the ward daybooks—are non-statutory ephemera. They have zero legal standing, and keeping them is a failure of modern governance."

Maureen turned her trolley around. She pushed it back toward the service lift, her footsteps muffled by the hum of the dehumidifiers.

# Chapter 6: Tea in the Break Room

The staff break room behind the nurses' station on Ward 4 was narrow, lit by a fluorescent tube that flickered at the north end with a faint orange glow. The room contained two vinyl armchairs with ripped armrests mended with gray duct tape, a low coffee table littered with old copies of nursing journals, and a two-slice toaster that tripped the circuit breaker if both slots were pushed down together.

Sister Janice Bradley sat at the small formica table, her blue cardigan buttoned up to her chin against the draught from the fire exit. She was sixty-one, with short iron-gray hair and thick plastic spectacles that made her eyes look round and watery.

"He was down in the basement, then?" Janice said without looking up.

Maureen set her clean bucket in the sink corner and sat on the wooden stool by the kettle. "Sterling. He had the men loading boxes by the laundry ramp."

Janice's hands stopped moving. The plastic spoon remained between her thumb and forefinger, suspended over the mug.

"The clinical director came down from his bed at five in the morning," Janice whispered. She looked up, her eyes bloodshot in the harsh light. "He stood right where you're sitting, Maureen. "I signed it," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. And I've looked at the floor every time you walked past with your mop. You think I don't see your face every night at three in the morning? Theft of NHS property, data breach, obstruction of governance."

"Let him call them," Maureen said.

"And what happens to Kathleen?" Janice snapped, her voice breaking into a harsh, wheezing rasp. "Who pays the eight hundred quid a month to Saint Anthony's when you're sitting in a police cell? The council will bundle your mother into the back of an ambulance and dump her at Greystones before your bail hearing's over. You think the trust cares about a dead boy from nineteen eighty-two? Janice Bradley pushed her spectacles back up her nose, the yellow skin under her eyes dark with exhaustion. She took a sip of her stewed tea, grimaced, and set the mug down.

"I remember his car," Maureen said. "A silver Rover with red leather seats. He parked it in the ambulance bay by the doors."

The resuscitation trolley was still plugged into the wall, the defibrillator pads stuck to the boy's chest. Janice's hands gripped the edge of the formica table.

"He held the pen out to me," Janice said, tears finally welling in the corners of her watery eyes. "He had already written the transfer sheet: *Sudden cardiac arrest secondary to acute status asthmaticus. If I refused, he would report me for gross clinical negligence."

"And you signed," Maureen said.

"I signed," Janice whispered. "I had three children at home, Maureen. My husband was six months out of work from the docks. If I lost my nursing registration, we'd have been in the parish breadline by Christmas. So I signed my name, and Rao took the charts and walked out to his Rover. I never saw those sheets again until you found that green slip in the sack."

The night wind rattled the high hopper windows along the corridor leading to the maternity block. Outside in the dark, the lights of the Lowfield colliery housing estate formed a grid of orange sodium points across the valley floor.

Maureen wheeled her cart past the clinical audit noticeboards.

Beneath the glass frames, the trust had posted its latest key performance indicators: *FOUR-HOUR EMERGENCY ADMISSION TARGET: 71.4%*, *CLOSTRIDIUM DIFFICILE INCIDENCE: ZERO CASES*, *BED TURNOVER EFFICIENCY: 94.2%*.

She looked at the numbers.

To the administrators in Leeds and London, these colored bar charts represented the pulse of the hospital. If a green bar rose two millimeters, executives received annual performance bonuses. If a red bar dropped, consultants were summoned to executive inquiry panels.

None of those charts recorded the things Maureen saw every night.

There was no bar chart for the seventy-year-old widow who sat in the corridor for five hours because the transport ambulance had broken down in Huddersfield. There was no metric for the young staff nurse who skipped her meal break to hold the hand of an eighteen-year-old lad whose leg had been amputated at the railway shunting yard.

The true life of this hospital did not live on the noticeboards. It lived in the gaps between the targets, in the quiet acts of endurance that kept human beings alive despite the metrics.

Maureen did not speak. She reached into her pocket, took out her clean microfibre duster, and laid it flat on the table between them, folding it into halves, then quarters, then eighths.

# Chapter 8: The Kitchen Table at Dawn

The winter dawn came up gray and thin behind the railway embankment.

Maureen sat at her kitchen table in her woollen dressing gown. Her hair, pinned up for the night shift, had slipped loose at the temples, gray strands falling across her forehead. The tea in her mug was cold, a pale brown skin forming across the surface where the condensed milk had settled.

On the left lay her blue bank passbook from the Trustee Savings Bank. The last entry, stamped in purple ink by the teller at the High Street branch on Friday, showed a balance of one hundred and forty-two pounds and eighteen pence.

*We have completed our preliminary review of the clinical records and staff statements arising from the incident on Ward 4 on the morning of 16 October 2004.

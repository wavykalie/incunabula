# The Recorded House

### Chapter 1

The Number 38 smelled of wet dog, damp cardboard, and the sweet, synthetic reek of disposable grape vapes that the school kids from the comprehensive dropped between the seat cushions.

Nell Mercer sat on the upper deck, three rows from the front, where the hot-air blower under the seat rattled like a tin of loose bolts and blew lukewarm, greasy air against her calves. Outside the scratched perspex window, the town of Blackthorn Green slid by in shades of sodden ash: pebble-dash semis with concrete drives where company saloons sat parked half on the pavement; the bypass roundabout with its grey fibreglass planter shaped like a Victorian tub; the abandoned Esso garage that had spent four years becoming a hand car wash, its blue plastic tarpaulins snapping like whip-cracks in the November gale.

The bus ran once an hour. If you missed the 16:18 from the market square, you stood under the rusted shelter outside the Iceland until 17:18, your shins spattered with dirty ditch-water every time a supermarket articulated lorry took the corner too tight. If you missed the 17:18, you walked the three miles down the B4012 in the dark, dodging into the nettles every time headlights came cresting the railway bridge.

Nell had not missed the 16:18, but she had stayed on it two stops past her own road because her grandfather’s coat was too heavy for her lap and she had not wanted to drag it down the stairs while the bus was braking.

It was an Austrian loden overcoat, dark moss-green, made of boiled wool so thick it felt like roofing felt. Leonard had bought it at the St Jude’s Hospice shop beside the post office on Tuesday afternoon for eleven pounds fifty. He had come home with it folded over his forearm like a prize bull’s rosette, his pale, liver-spotted cheeks flushed with the triumph of an eighty-year-old widower who had outwitted the retail economy.

*Look at that, Nelly,* he had said, spreading it across the dining table among the junk mail and the pharmacy bags. *Real horn buttons. Leather shanks. You couldn't buy the wool for fifty quid. An estate clearance, the girl said. An old gentleman over at the siding bungalows.*

Nell’s mother, Karen, had looked up from the sink where she was scrubbing potatoes for the stew, her forearms pink and chapped from the late shift at the distribution centre in Didcot. *It smells of mothballs, Dad. And someone else’s damp. You’ll hang it in the porch until the frost takes the smell out, or it doesn't come past the kitchen door.*

Leonard had hung it in the porch, on the brass hook beside the cracked Wellington boots, but he had taken it down every morning to inspect the lapels with the thumb of his right hand—the hand that was missing the tip of the ring finger from his thirty years on the track gang for British Rail.

Nell had borrowed it that afternoon because the zip on her puffer jacket had separated at the teeth while she was waiting outside the chemist for Leonard’s glyceryl trinitrate spray, and the temperature had dropped four degrees the moment the sun slipped behind the cooling towers of the disused cement works.

The coat reached past her knees. When she pulled the collar up, the coarse wool rasped against her jaw, smelling faintly of cedarwood, dry dust, and the vinegar tang of stale pipe tobacco. It weighed at least six pounds.

She stood up before the bus reached the stop at Blackthorn Close, swaying against the yellow stanchions as the driver took the speed hump by the recreation ground with a dull, hydraulic thump. She clutched the heavy wool around her chest with both fists. Her fingers sank into the deep side pockets—the wide, welted bellows pockets designed for shotgun cartridges or winter gloves.

Her left hand found nothing but a twist of blue tissue paper and two dry beech nuts.

Her right hand went through a tear in the pocket lining.

The slit was three inches long, neatly frayed along the chain-stitch where the cotton pocket bag joined the heavy wool facing. Beneath the tear, nestled in the dark hollow between the boiled wool shell and the satin hem lining, her fingertips hit something hard, square-cornered, and cold.

Nell frowned, pausing on the bottom step of the bus as the doors wheezed open.

"Come on, love, let's have you," the driver said without turning his head. He had a thermos of tea wedged into the ticket cradle and a laminated sign taped to the change tray that read: *EXACT FARE ONLY PLEASE*.

"Sorry," Nell muttered, stepping down onto the wet tarmac.

The bus pulled away with a wet roar of diesel exhaust, its red tail-lights smearing across the oily film that covered the surface of the puddle at the curb.

Nell walked up the rise of Blackthorn Close with her right hand buried deep inside the coat’s seam. The object was about four inches long, two and a half inches wide, and half an inch thick. The edges were ribbed. In the center, she could feel two circular holes, smooth on the inside, with tiny plastic teeth protruding around the rims.

It was a cassette tape.

She pulled it out under the orange sodium glare of the streetlamp outside number 8.

It was an unbranded, smoke-grey C-60, the kind sold in five-packs at corner newsagents in the nineteen-nineties. The paper label on side A had been peeled off, leaving a scarred rectangle of white adhesive furred with grey wool lint. Across the top edge, someone had written four digits in red permanent marker, long faded to the color of dried liver:

`14-B`

Nell held it to the light. Through the translucent plastic shell, she could see the dark brown ribbon of magnetic tape wound unevenly onto the left spool. About two-thirds of the tape was on the supply hub; the rest had been pulled through the playback head and wound onto the take-up side.

A horn sounded at the bend of the cul-de-sac: an Amazon delivery van, its hazard lights blinking frantic yellow against the brick garden walls, reversing at speed out of a shared driveway. Nell slipped the cassette back into the pocket, stepped over the low garden wall of number 14, and pushed through the wooden gate.

The latch dropped behind her with a dry, familiar *clack*. It was an iron Suffolk latch, stiff with thirty layers of gloss paint, that required you to lift the thumb-piece while you pulled the gate inward, otherwise the keeper caught on the rusted screw head.

Nell had known that sound since she was five years old. It was the sound of being home, on a street where nothing ever changed, where the only thing louder than the wind off the bypass was the train every fifty minutes that didn't stop.

### Chapter 2

The hall smelled of boiled cabbage, white toast, and the paraffin heater that Leonard insisted on keeping in the corner of the passage despite the three battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms Karen had screwed to the doorframes.

"That you, Nelly?" Leonard’s voice came from the back room. It had the dry, whistling edge of a man whose lungs had swallowed forty years of limestone ballast dust and machine oil.

"It’s me, Grandad," she called, kicking her trainers off onto the rubber mat by the door. She slipped the heavy coat off her shoulders. It collapsed onto the hall chair like a dead sheep, its hem trailing on the worn linoleum.

She kept the cassette in her hand.

Leonard was sitting in his high-backed wing chair in the corner of the small sitting room, a tartan blanket tucked neatly across his knees despite the suffocating heat of the room. On the small laminate drop-leaf table beside him sat his tea—milky, half-drunk in a chipped mug with a faded transfer of the 1977 Silver Jubilee—and a plate containing the crusts of a processed cheese sandwich.

On the floor beside his left slipper sat the machine.

It was a Ferguson 3208 portable cassette recorder, cased in silver-sprayed polystyrene that had yellowed around the volume slider to the shade of an old tooth. Leonard had owned it since 1982. He had used it to record the Sunday evening hymn programmes off the BBC Home Service, and later, after his wife, Martha, died in the hospice in Abingdon, to play the four talking books on railway history that the county library service mailed him in plastic pouches twice a year.

The machine had five chunky, black plastic piano-keys on the front deck: `RECORD`, `REWIND`, `FORWARD`, `PLAY`, and `STOP/EJECT`.

"You had the big coat, then," Leonard said, his milky blue eyes peering at her over the tops of his gold-rimmed reading glasses. "Warm, isn't it? Like wearing a blanket. Don't let your mother see you had it out in the wet. She’ll have the Dettol on it."

"The zip on mine died," Nell said. She stood by the tiled fireplace, her fingers turning the small plastic cassette over and over in her jeans pocket. "Grandad, did you check the pockets when you bought it?"

"Check the pockets?" Leonard picked up his tea, his thumb trembling slightly as he brought the rim to his lip. "Course I checked them. Found a plastic comb and a coupon for fifty pence off a tin of floor wax. Expired in ninety-four. Why? What’s in it?"

"Nothing," she said automatically, the reflex of a seventeen-year-old who lived with her family in eight hundred square feet of brick and plasterboard. "Just an old tape. Down behind the lining."

Leonard set the mug down on the saucer with a rattle. "A tape? What sort of tape?"

"Just a cassette. Blank one."

"Let's have a look, then."

Nell pulled it out and handed it to him.

Leonard took it between his thick, calloused thumbs. The yellowed nails were grooved like bark. He held it close to his glasses, turning it toward the forty-watt lamp on the dresser. He squinted at the red marker on the edge.

"`14-B`," he read aloud. His forehead furrowed, the deep vertical creases between his white eyebrows drawing together until they looked like scars. "Not mine. Never saw it. Must’ve belonged to the old chap over at the siding. Arthur Cross, his name was. Used to be a clerk at the timber yard before they flattened it for the warehousing. Died in September, they said. Sister put the whole house into the hospice van."

"Does the Ferguson still play?" Nell asked.

"Course it plays," Leonard said, slightly offended. "Put a new belt in it three years back. Elastic band out of the postman’s bunch, worked better than the Japanese one. Press the `STOP/EJECT` button."

Nell knelt on the carpet beside his chair. The nylon pile was worn down to the grey webbing in front of the heater. She pressed the wide, rectangular key labeled `STOP/EJECT`.

With a stiff, plastic *clonk*, the cassette door swung up on its greasy brass spring.

Inside the well, the two white plastic drive hubs stood erect, their splines slightly worn. The rubber pinch-roller was shiny and brown, stained with forty years of oxide shed by thousands of hours of choral singing.

She set the cassette into the carrier clips, side A facing up, the fat spool on the left. She pushed the door down until the metal latch clicked home.

"Volume’s on three," Leonard said, leaning forward, his dry palms resting on his tartan knees. "Don't turn it up too high. The speaker cone’s got a split in the paper from when your cousin dropped it behind the television."

Nell pressed `PLAY`.

The key went down with a heavy, mechanical resistance, locking into place with a hollow thud.

The small electric motor inside the Ferguson gave a high, thin whine as it took up the slack. Through the clear perspex window of the door, the left-hand spool began to turn—slowly, jerkily, the white plastic teeth ticking against the tape hub.

For six seconds, there was only the loud, bacon-fat hiss of unrecorded tape: the dry, rhythmic whisper of an old oxide layer dragging across the permalloy head.

*Shhh-shhh-shhh-shhh.*

Then the hiss cleared, dropping an octave as the recording bias kicked in, and a voice spoke into the small, hot room.

### Chapter 3

It was a man’s voice.

It was neither young nor old, neither loud nor quiet. It had the flat, dry, slightly nasal cadence of someone from the north Midlands who had lived twenty years in the home counties without ever quite losing his short *a*’s. It was the voice of a man who worked behind a counter in an auto-parts warehouse, or sat in a small wooden cabin at the entrance to a council tip checking commercial waste permits.

"Fourteen Blackthorn Close," the voice said.

The words were not shouted. They were spoken at the distance of about six inches from an unshielded dynamic microphone, so that every breath against the capsule produced a small, hollow thump in the Ferguson’s four-inch speaker.

Nell froze.

Beside her, Leonard’s tea mug gave a sharp, porcelain *clink* against the saucer.

"The job took seven minutes," the voice said. "The rain started at twenty past six, just like the regional forecast said on the radio. We left the Vauxhall parked on the verge by the telephone exchange on the bypass, two wheels up on the grass so the milk tanker didn't have to steer round it. Walked in through the alley beside number two."

A pause. In the background of the recording, through the hiss, there was a faint, metallic clatter: the sound of a teaspoon dropping into a ceramic mug, or a pair of pliers being laid down on an unpainted workbench.

"The gate latch on fourteen is an old Suffolk type," the voice continued, methodical, devoid of hurry. "It needs lifting from the outside with a pocketknife or a bit of wire because the keeper’s gummed up with brown gloss. We didn't need the wire. The latch had been left on the sneck with a piece of clothes-peg spring. We stepped over the low wall anyway to save the noise."

Nell turned her head slowly to look at her grandfather.

Leonard was not looking at her. His chin was lowered toward his chest, his mouth slightly open, his gold-rimmed glasses tilted forward so that his eyes were hidden in the shadow of his grey brows. His knuckles, gripped around the fringe of the tartan rug, were white.

"The dog was already dead," the voice said.

The voice paused again. A breath was drawn in through the nose, long and wet.

"The dog was an Alsatian cross called Prince. He had been put down three years back on account of his hips, so there was no call on the utility room door. The back kitchen door is timber, three-lever mortice, but the frame is wet-rot at the bottom two inches where the downpipe leaks. One crowbar under the sill, three inches to the left of the brass handle, and the whole rebate split clean off without breaking a pane."

Nell’s heart took a long, sickening lurch downward into the pit of her stomach.

The downpipe outside the scullery door had been leaking since the winter before last. Her uncle Martin had jammed a plastic ice-cream tub under the joint in August to stop the water soaking into the brickwork, but every time the gutters overflowed, the splash hit the lower wooden rail of the back door. The timber there was soft; you could push your thumbnail into the grain like butter.

"The old man was in the wing chair," the tape said.

The voice was describing it as though it had already happened. Not as a plan. Not as an instruction. As a report.

"He had the Jubilee mug on the table. The television wasn't on because he likes the wireless at that hour, listening to the six o'clock news on four. He didn't stand up. He couldn't stand up quick enough on account of his knee. We told him to keep his hands on the rug and not to look round at the doorway."

"Turn it off," Leonard whispered.

His voice was so thin Nell could barely hear it over the hiss of the motor.

"Grandad—"

"Turn it off, Nelly. Turn it off now."

Nell didn't move. She couldn't. Her hand was pressed flat against the floral carpet, the nylon fibres biting into the skin of her palm.

The voice on the tape went on:

"We took the strongbox from the floorboards under the wardrobe in the back bedroom. The third board from the skirting, the one with the two brass dome-head screws that don't match the steel ones. It had the five hundred in twenty-pound notes from the railway pension commutation, the deeds to the allotment plot at Lower Weir, and the silver pocket watch with the cracked enamel dial that belonged to his brother George who died at El Alamein."

A long silence.

On the tape, a floorboard creaked—a double creak, high then low, exactly like the third tread of the stairs at number 14.

"The girl wasn't back," the voice said. "Her bus from the college was the seventeen-eighteen, but it was running twenty minutes late on account of the roadworks at the railway bridge. She came in through the back gate at ten to six. We were already back at the Vauxhall by then, turning round in the entrance to the sewage works."

The sound of a lighter clicking twice. A deep exhalation of smoke against the microphone.

"Everything went off smooth. No blood. No glass. Left the door shut behind us so the heat didn't get out. The old man was still sitting there when we pulled onto the bypass. It was seven minutes past six."

A sharp, hollow *click*.

The voice stopped.

The machine continued to run. The brown tape slid past the playback head, whispering its endless, flat hiss into the quiet room.

*Shhh-shhh-shhh-shhh.*

Nell reached out with a trembling finger and hit `STOP`.

The key popped up with a loud, violent *clack* that sounded like a pistol shot in the small room. The motor died.

The sitting room was utterly silent, except for the rhythmic, bubbling sigh of the paraffin heater in the corner and the distant hum of the traffic on the bypass—the heavy six-axle lorries shifting down through their gears as they approached the roundabout at the edge of the town.

### Chapter 4

Nell remained on her knees. Her legs had gone completely numb from the cold floor, but she did not shift her weight.

"Grandad?"

Leonard did not move. His hands were still locked into the fringe of the tartan rug. His breathing had become rapid and shallow, a dry, rattling sound at the back of his throat that sounded like dry leaves being crushed in a paper sack.

"Grandad, look at me."

She reached out and laid her hand over his knuckles. His skin was ice-cold. Beneath the thin, parchment-like flesh, she could feel the hard, rigid tendons vibrating with a steady, high-frequency tremor.

"Where did you get that coat?" she asked.

"I told you," he muttered. He didn't look at her; his eyes remained fixed on the lower shelf of the sideboard, where two commemorative railway plates from the 1980 Stockton and Darlington anniversary sat in plastic display stands. "The hospice shop. Tuesday."

"Did you know Arthur Cross?"

"Everyone knew Arthur," Leonard said, his voice flat, exhausted. "He was thirty years at the timber yard. He was on the parish council when they tried to stop them building the bypass through the water meadows. He had the bungalow down by the level crossing."

"Did he have a voice like that?"

"No," Leonard said immediately. "Arthur was from Norfolk. He talked broad. You could barely understand him when he had his teeth out. That man... that man wasn't Arthur."

Nell looked down at the Ferguson. The smoke-grey plastic of the cassette sat quietly behind the scratched window, innocent and dull.

`14-B.`

"Grandad," she said, her voice dropping to a whisper, though there was no one in the house to hear them. "What day did he say?"

Leonard didn't answer.

"He said *the rain started at twenty past six*," Nell said, her mind racing backward through the words on the tape, examining them with the terrifying, microscopic clarity that comes with shock. "He said *the roadworks at the railway bridge*. Grandad, they only put the temporary lights on the railway bridge yesterday morning. The council water gang had the lane dug up to fix the main."

Leonard slowly turned his head.

His eyes were wide, the pupils dilated until the watery blue iris was only a thin rim around the black centers.

"Yesterday," he whispered.

"They put the traffic lights up on Tuesday at ten," Nell said. "The sign says they’re there until Saturday evening. And the college bus... the college bus doesn't run the seventeen-eighteen on weekends. That’s the weekday timetable."

She stood up. Her knees cracked with a dry pop that made her wince. She walked across the small room to the window.

The curtains were not drawn yet. Outside, the sodium streetlamp cast long, orange shadows of the hawthorn hedge across the wet tarmac of the Close. Beyond the garden wall, the grey brick houses of numbers 12 and 16 were dark, their occupants still sitting in commuter trains between London and Oxford or inching along the A34 in the freezing drizzle.

At the end of the road, three hundred yards away, the yellow lights of the temporary roadworks at the railway bridge blinked from amber to red.

"What is in the floor under your wardrobe, Grandad?"

Leonard closed his eyes.

He leaned his head back against the grease-stained antimacassar on the wing chair. For a second, Nell thought he was going to faint; his skin had taken on the grey, waxy translucence of lard, and his upper lip was beaded with tiny droplets of sweat despite the chill in the room.

"It’s just papers," he whispered.

"Is the watch there?"

"The watch is Martha’s brother’s," he said, his lips barely moving. "George. He was twenty-one when he caught it in the desert. Never had a grave. Just a name on the wall at Alamein. They sent the watch back in a little cardboard box with three cigarettes and his paybook."

"And the five hundred pounds?"

Leonard swallowed with difficulty. "From the pension commutation. When they privatised the freight division in ninety-five. They offered three thousand lump sum or seventy quid a month. I took the cash and gave your grandmother two thousand for the roof, and I kept five hundred back. For... for when the oil ran out. Or the roof went again."

"Did you tell anyone?" Nell’s voice was shaking now. "Did you tell Uncle Martin? Did you tell Mum?"

"No," he said fiercely. "Your mother would have put it into the building society to pay the interest on the credit card she had for the boiler. And Martin would have bought a trailer for his lawnmower business that went bust six months later. It was mine. It’s been under that board since nineteen ninety-six."

"And the two brass dome-head screws?"

Leonard looked down at his slippers.

"The old floor screws were rusted into the joist," he said, so quietly she had to lean in to hear him. "I had to drill them out with the breast-drill. I didn't have any three-inch steel woodscrews left in the shed, so I used the brass ones from the hinge packet off the coal bunker."

Nell stood frozen in the middle of the carpet.

The voice on the tape had known about the brass dome-head screws. It had known about the railway pension commutation. It had known about George’s cracked enamel watch. It had known that Prince had been put down three years ago, that the scullery door had dry-rot behind the downpipe, and that the lock on the front gate had been jammed with brown gloss paint.

And it had spoken of the burglary in the past tense.

*The job took seven minutes.*

*We left the Vauxhall parked on the verge by the telephone exchange.*

*Everything went off smooth.*

Nell reached into her pocket, pulled out her phone, and tapped the screen.

The digital clock read:

`16:54`
`Thursday, 14 November`

Tomorrow was Friday. The fifteenth.

Or was it today?

She looked out the window. It wasn't raining yet. The clouds were low, a bruised, violet-black ceiling moving fast across the sky from the west, but the tarmac was only damp from the morning’s mist.

*The rain started at twenty past six.*

Nell felt a sudden, violent knot form in her chest.

"Grandad," she said, her voice dropping into a register that was no longer that of a seventeen-year-old granddaughter asking for bus money. "When did Arthur Cross die?"

"September," Leonard repeated numbly. "September the twelfth. I went to the crematorium in Banbury with your uncle Martin. There were four people there. The minister, Arthur’s sister from Kettering, me, and the chap who bought the timber yard land to store caravans."

"Who was the sister?"

"I don't know," Leonard said, shaking his head. "Old girl. Seventy-odd. Wore a purple coat. She didn't stay for the tea at the pub; she took the four-o'clock train straight back to the Midlands."

"And who cleared the bungalow?"

"The hospice," Leonard said. "St Jude’s. They have the van with the two lads on the community service scheme. They come in and take the lot—furniture, clothes, the pots and pans. Anything they can't sell in the shop, they take down the tip in Didcot."

Nell turned back to the Ferguson.

She looked at the coat lying on the hall chair. The moss-green loden wool, with its horn buttons and its leather shanks.

The coat had belonged to Arthur Cross. Or it had been in Arthur Cross’s bungalow when he died. Or someone had put the coat into the hospice collection bag.

And inside the lining, nestled against the hem where no one would feel it unless they slipped their whole hand through a torn stitch, was a tape describing the violent robbery of 14 Blackthorn Close, spoken by a man who sounded like he was reading an entry in a logbook.

### Chapter 5

At 17:10, Nell left the house.

"Don't you go out," she told Leonard before she closed the front door. She had pulled the bolt on the top of the timber door and the latch on the bottom, checking both twice with the heel of her hand. "Don't open the door to anyone. Not Martin, not the neighbours. If someone knocks, you let them knock. Mum will be home on the six-forty from Didcot."

"Where are you going, Nelly?" Leonard had asked from his chair, looking smaller and older than he ever had, his hands still clutching the tartan blanket.

"I’m going to the police," she said.

The police station in Blackthorn Green had been closed seven years ago under the county constabulary consolidation scheme. The red-brick building on the high street, with its frosted-glass blue lamp over the door and its Victorian holding cells in the basement, was now a private dental practice offering laser teeth whitening and cosmetic botox.

The nearest operational police station with a public enquiry desk was in Stannington, four miles away, on the other side of the bypass.

To get to Stannington without a car, you took the Number 38.

Nell stood under the cracked polycarbonate shelter at the end of Blackthorn Close, her hands shoved deep into the pockets of her unzipped puffer jacket, her teeth chattering with the cold.

The timetable on the rusted metal post was covered with graffiti and yellowed behind scratched acrylic:

`SERVICE 38: MONDAY TO FRIDAY`
`BLACKTHORN CLOSE (NORTHBOUND)`
`... 15:23 | 16:23 | 17:23 | 18:23 | 19:23 ...`

It was 17:16.

The road was quiet. Every two or three minutes, a commuter car would turn into the Close—an Audi estate, a Nissan Qashqai—its headlights sweeping across the front gardens, illuminating the gravel drives, the plastic recycling bins lined up like sentries, the tidy privet hedges. People coming home from the science park in Milton or the railway station in Didcot. People with company fuel cards and heated leather seats.

Nell took the cassette out of her pocket.

She turned it over in her bare fingers. The plastic was ice-cold.

Why a cassette?

In 2026, nobody used cassettes except pensioners and people who bought vintage tape decks on eBay to play twenty-five-pound reissues of indie albums. You couldn't buy a blank cassette at the supermarket; you couldn't buy one at the Argos in Stannington. The only places that had them were charity shops, car boot sales, and the bottom drawers of retired railway clerks who still had an old Ferguson in the corner of the sitting room.

Had the voice been recorded thirty years ago?

*No.*

She remembered the words:

*The roadworks at the railway bridge.*

*Prince had been put down three years back.*

Prince had died in November 2023. Nell had helped Leonard lift the dog’s stiff, heavy body into the back of Uncle Martin’s estate car to take him to the vet in Wantage. The dog had been twelve years old, his back legs trailing behind him on the carpet like dead branches.

The tape had been recorded *after* November 2023.

And the roadworks at the railway bridge had only appeared forty-eight hours ago.

That meant the tape had been recorded within the last two days. Or within the last twelve hours.

Nell felt a cold drop of water hit the bridge of her nose.

She looked up at the sky.

A second drop hit her cheek. Then a third, heavy and flat, smacking against the plastic roof of the bus shelter with a sharp *tick*.

She looked at her phone.

`17:20`

*The rain started at twenty past six.*

Not twenty past five. Twenty past six.

Her breath came out in a quick, ragged puff of white vapor. She looked down the empty stretch of the B4012. Two miles away, across the flat water meadows, she could see the twin headlights of the Number 38 cresting the canal bridge, its amber destination board glowing through the gathering dusk:

`38 STANNINGTON VIA BYPASS`

### Chapter 6

The Stannington Police Station sat on an industrial estate between a screw-fix warehouse and a drive-thru Costa, a low-slung, flat-roofed building of corrugated steel and grey cladding that looked less like a police station than a regional distribution depot for office stationery.

The public enquiry office was a ten-by-twelve-foot vestibule with three plastic chairs bolted to the floor, an empty metal leaflet rack labeled *COMMUNITY SAFETY PARTNERSHIP*, and a security window made of three-inch-thick laminated glass with a circular steel intercom grill in the middle.

Behind the glass sat a woman of about fifty in an unbuttoned blue uniform tunic with no epaulettes, her grey hair pulled back into a tight bun with a blue elastic band. She was typing on a computer keyboard with two fingers, her reading glasses hanging from a beaded cord around her neck.

Nell stood in front of the counter, the cassette clutched in her right hand inside her pocket.

"Excuse me," Nell said through the grill.

The woman didn't look up immediately. She finished typing a line, hit `Enter` with the knuckle of her middle finger, and then peered up through the smudged glass.

"Yes, love?"

"I need to report something," Nell said. Her voice sounded thin, strange, swallowed by the acoustic deadness of the security glass. "A burglary."

The woman reached for a blue ballpoint pen from a plastic mug that read *WORLD'S BEST NAN*.

"Has it happened?" she asked, pulling a pad of printed incident logs toward her.

"No," Nell said.

The woman’s hand stopped on the pen.

She looked at Nell again, taking in her wet puffer jacket with the broken zip, her muddy trainers, her sixteen-year-old face with the rain-flattened hair sticking to her forehead. Her expression settled into that heavy, tired, infinitely patient blankness that public servants reserve for people who have come in out of the rain to complain about satellites or neighbours’ conifers.

"You want to report a threat, then?" the woman asked. "Someone threatened to break in?"

"No," Nell said, leaning closer to the cold steel of the intercom. "It’s not a threat. It’s... I found a tape."

"A tape."

"A cassette tape. In a coat. In a charity shop." Nell could hear how it sounded even as the words came out of her mouth: disjointed, frantic, absurd. "My grandad bought the coat on Tuesday. I found the tape in the lining. And when we played it, there’s a man’s voice describing a break-in at our house. Exactly. Every room. What they took. How they got in."

The woman looked at the empty notepad on her counter. She didn't write anything down.

"And when did this break-in happen?"

"It hasn't," Nell said, her voice rising despite her effort to keep it steady. "That’s what I’m telling you! It hasn't happened yet! But he describes it like it *already* happened. He talks about the roadworks at the bridge that only started yesterday. He talks about our dog that died three years ago. He talks about the third floorboard under my grandad’s wardrobe—"

"Love," the woman said softly, cutting her off with the practiced ease of someone who had heard every permutation of human grievance in the Thames Valley constabulary area. "What’s your name?"

"Nell. Nell Mercer. I live at fourteen Blackthorn Close."

"And how old are you, Nell?"

"Seventeen."

The woman leaned forward, her elbows on the formica desk, resting her chin on her hands. "Nell, is this for college?"

Nell stared at her. "What?"

"Are you doing Media Studies over at the tertiary college? Or Drama?"

"No! I do Biology and Geography! I don't do Drama!"

"Because we had three lads in here last month from the technical school," the woman said, her voice dropping into a tone of gentle, unyielding condescension. "They had a video camera on a tripod and an old mobile phone, doing some sort of prank for the internet. Claimed they’d found a bomb threat in a library book. We had to have the duty sergeant out, call their head of year, whole afternoon wasted."

"This isn't a prank!" Nell pulled the cassette from her pocket and pressed it flat against the three-inch glass. "Look at it! It’s a real tape! It’s got `14-B` written on it in marker! My grandad’s seventy-nine years old, he has a heart condition, he’s sitting in his chair right now terrified out of his life because the voice on this tape says someone’s going to break in through our back door at twenty past six and steal his pension money!"

The woman didn't look at the tape. She looked at Nell’s eyes.

"Nell," she said calmly, "if someone has threatened your grandfather, or if you believe someone is planning a crime, you can leave your details and we can have a community support officer come out to Blackthorn Close when they’re next on the patch. But there isn't a car on that side of the bypass tonight. There’s an RTC on the A34 with two overturned artics, and the whole shift is down at the interchange directing traffic."

"An officer when they’re next on the patch?" Nell’s hand was shaking so hard the cassette clicked against the glass. "He said *the rain started at twenty past six*! It’s raining now! It’s twenty past five! In one hour—"

"If there is an intruder on your premises," the woman said, her voice hardening slightly, the patience thinning into bureaucratic finality, "you dial nine-nine-nine. You do not come to the station with an old tape you found in a second-hand coat. You go home, you make sure your doors are locked, and if you see anyone suspicious loitering in the road, you call the emergency line. Do you understand?"

"You won't even listen to it?"

"We don't have a cassette player, Nell," the woman said. She pointed a ballpoint pen toward the computer monitor beside her. "We don't have equipment for analog media at the front desk. If an officer needs to examine audio evidence, it has to be booked into property, logged with an incident number, and sent to the technical services unit in Kidlington for digitisation. That takes three weeks."

Nell stared at her through the smudged glass.

The woman sat there, solid, bored, smelling faintly of tea and instant soup, protected by three inches of bullet-resistant acrylic from the cold, the dark, and the rain outside. Behind her, through the open door of the back office, Nell could see a noticeboard covered in health-and-safety posters, a kettle on a filing cabinet, and a photocopier flashing a blue error light: *PAPER JAM IN TRAY 2*.

Nobody was coming.

There was no car. There was no officer on the patch. There was only a woman with a novelty mug and a form that would never be filled out.

Nell pulled the cassette off the glass.

She dropped it back into her pocket, turned around without saying another word, and pushed through the heavy acoustic doors into the cold night.

### Chapter 7

The rain was coming down in earnest now.

It wasn't a downpour; it was that thin, needle-like English drizzle that drifts sideways on the wind, soaking through coats at the seams and turning the gravel on the paths into a greasy paste.

Nell stood on the pavement outside the police station. The digital clock on the Costa drive-thru sign across the access road read:

`17:42`
`TEMP: 4°C`

Thirty-eight minutes until twenty past six.

The next Number 38 back to Blackthorn Green was not until 18:23.

If she waited for the bus, she would arrive at Blackthorn Close at 18:40. Twenty minutes *after* the time on the tape.

She couldn't wait.

She began to walk.

She took the pedestrian path that ran behind the industrial estate, following the line of the old railway embankment toward the bypass. Her trainers were soaked through within two hundred yards, the cold water squelching between her toes with every step. Her hair was plastered to her forehead, dripping into her eyes, but she didn't stop to wipe it away.

She walked fast, her arms clamped across her ribs, her breath coming in short, ragged gasps that burned in her throat.

*Why the past tense?*

The question pounded in her head with the rhythm of her feet against the wet gravel.

*The job took seven minutes.*

*We left the Vauxhall parked on the verge by the telephone exchange.*

If someone was planning a robbery, you wrote a list. Or you talked to your partner in a pub. Or you sent a message on an encrypted app. You didn't sit in a room with a microphone and a cassette deck and talk about it like it had already happened.

Unless...

Unless it *had* already happened?

Could it have happened?

Nell stopped on the dark cinder path beneath the concrete bridge of the bypass.

Above her, the heavy six-axle artics roared across the expansion joints with a sound like artillery fire: *ba-dum, ba-dum, ba-dum*. The concrete pillars were covered with wet green moss and faded tags sprayed in silver paint: *STAN BOYS*, *K-2*, *NO HOPE*.

Could the burglary have happened years ago?

Could the voice be describing a real break-in from 1995?

*No.*

The roadworks at the railway bridge.

Prince had been dead three years.

The pension commutation.

And the coat.

She pulled her phone from her pocket with numb fingers. The screen was wet, the touchscreen unresponsive until she wiped it violently against the inside of her sleeve.

She opened the search engine.

She typed:

`Arthur Cross Blackthorn Green obituary`

The screen loaded slowly, the cellular signal dropping to one bar beneath the concrete mass of the bridge.

A single result appeared from the *Oxford Mail*, dated 18 September:

> **CROSS, Arthur William.** Peacefully at the John Radcliffe Hospital on 12 September, aged 81 years. Formerly of Blackthorn Green and the Great Western Timber Company. A private cremation has taken place. Donations in memory to St Jude’s Hospice, Wantage.

Nell tapped the screen, scrolling down.

There were no other results. No tributes. No family notices. Just a three-line classified ad placed by the funeral directors.

She typed another search:

`14-B Blackthorn Close`

Nothing. Blackthorn Close had numbers 1 to 24, all built in 1974 by the local rural district council before being sold off under Right to Buy in the nineteen-eighties. There was no number 14-B. There was only number 14: a semi-detached brick house with three bedrooms, a concrete-tiled roof, and an iron Suffolk latch on the front gate.

Nell stared at the phone screen.

Then what did the `B` stand for?

`14-B.`

Fourteen... Box?

Fourteen... Burglary?

Fourteen... *Break-in*?

A cold shiver went down her spine that had nothing to do with the freezing rain dripping from the concrete span above her head.

What if fourteen wasn't an address?

What if fourteen was a number in a sequence?

*Job Fourteen. Target B.*

Or *Batch Fourteen. Tape B.*

She shoved the phone back into her pocket, ducked out from under the bridge, and broke into a run.

### Chapter 8

The road from the bypass to Blackthorn Green had no pavement for the first half-mile.

It was an old country lane, flanked on both sides by deep, waterlogged ditches choked with brambles and dead elder trees. Every time a car approached from behind, its headlights blinding in the rearview mirrors of Nell’s wet glasses, she had to scramble up onto the grass verge, her feet sinking ankle-deep into the rotting leaves, waiting for the red tail-lights to disappear around the bend before dropping back down onto the tarmac.

By the time she reached the telephone exchange, it was 18:04.

The telephone exchange was a windowless, single-storey bunker of dark engineering brick built in the late nineteen-sixties, surrounded by an eight-foot chain-link fence topped with three strands of rusted barbed wire. It sat on a triangular patch of wasteland between the old canal arm and the bypass slip road, half-hidden behind an overgrown hedge of leylandii that had not been trimmed in twenty years.

The verge outside the exchange was wide, flat, and churned into deep ruts by the maintenance vans that occasionally visited the site.

Nell slowed to a walk, her chest heaving, the blood roaring in her ears like sea water.

She looked at the grass.

Under the faint, orange glow of the distant bypass lights, the wet turf was scarred by two parallel trenches of dark, exposed mud: fresh tire tracks.

They were wide, deep ruts, cut by radial tires with a blocky, commercial tread. The water in the bottom of the ruts was still cloudy, the silt suspended in the puddle, not yet settled to the clay bottom.

Someone had parked there within the last hour.

Nell stood on the edge of the road, the rain running off the brim of her hood in a steady stream.

*We left the Vauxhall parked on the verge by the telephone exchange... two wheels up on the grass so the milk tanker didn't have to steer round it.*

She looked down into the ditch beside the chain-link fence.

Among the dead nettles and the crushed beer cans, something white caught the light.

She slid down the muddy bank, her hands catching on the rusted wire to steady herself. She reached down into the weeds.

It was an empty cigarette packet: Richmond Blue, twenty kingsize.

She turned it over. The cardboard was damp, but the cellophane wrapper was still stiff, unclouded by moisture. Inside the lid, written in blue ballpoint pen in small, cramped capitals, was a string of letters and numbers:

`18:05 - BYP`
`18:12 - JUNC`
`18:20 - 14 IN`
`18:27 - 14 OUT`
`18:35 - A34 S`

Nell’s fingers closed around the cardboard until it crumpled in her fist.

A timetable.

`18:20 - 14 IN.`

In through the door at twenty past six.

`18:27 - 14 OUT.`

Seven minutes.

*The job took seven minutes.*

She looked at her phone.

`18:08`

Twelve minutes.

She scrambled up the muddy bank of the ditch, her fingernails tearing against the stones, and ran toward the lights of Blackthorn Close.

### Chapter 9

The alley beside number 2 was dark.

It was a narrow, paved passage between two high creosoted lap-panel fences, designed in the nineteen-seventies to allow the council dustcarts to collect the bins from the back gardens without driving into the cul-de-sac. Over fifty years, the fences had tilted inward until the passage felt like a trench, the sky reduced to a narrow strip of violet clouds between the rotting gravel boards.

Nell ran through the darkness, her feet splashing through puddles of standing water that smelled of dog mess and decomposing moss.

Her house was at the far end, where the alley opened out onto the back access path behind numbers 10, 12, and 14.

As she reached the corner of number 12’s garage, she stopped.

She pressed her back against the wet timber of the fence, drawing herself into the narrow shadow between an overgrown bay tree and a stack of plastic garden chairs.

Through the branches, she could see the back of number 14.

The scullery light was on: a single, bare sixty-watt bulb behind the frosted glass of the small window above the sink. Through the steam on the glass, she could see the silhouette of her grandfather’s kettle sitting on the gas ring.

The kitchen door was shut.

The downpipe was dripping into the plastic ice-cream tub: *drip... drip... drip*.

And beside the low brick wall that separated number 14 from the recreation ground, two men were standing.

They were not moving.

They were dressed in dark, hooded waterproof jackets, their faces obscured by the shadows of their peaks. One of them was tall, lean, with long arms that hung loose at his sides. The other was shorter, heavier, his shoulders hunched against the rain, carrying a narrow canvas tool roll under his left arm.

Neither of them was speaking.

They stood in the weeds beside the compost heap, their backs to the alley, watching the scullery window with the patient, motionless stillness of cattle in an open field.

Nell’s breath seized in her throat.

Her heart was hammering so violently against her ribs she was certain the sound would carry across the six yards of wet grass between them.

She looked at her phone. She shielded the screen with her palm, turning the brightness down to its lowest setting.

`18:17`

Three minutes.

She looked at the tall man.

In his right hand, held low against his thigh, was an eighteen-inch steel wrecking bar: the blue-painted kind sold at builders’ merchants, its hexagonal shaft worn down to bare, grey metal at the curved pry-end.

The shorter man raised his left wrist. The greenish glow of a digital watch face lit up his cheek for a split second: a pale, round face with a thin, wet moustache and dark, deep-set eyes that looked sunken into his skull.

He tapped the tall man on the shoulder.

Two taps.

The tall man stepped forward.

He didn't walk across the grass. He stepped onto the concrete edging stones that bordered the vegetable patch, moving with a silent, sliding gait that made no sound against the wet earth.

He reached the back gate.

He didn't touch the iron latch.

He reached his left hand over the top rail, slid his fingers down the inside of the post, and pressed his thumb against the keeper.

Nell remembered the voice on the tape:

*The latch had been left on the sneck with a piece of clothes-peg spring.*

The gate swung open without a creak.

### Chapter 10

Nell did not think.

If she had thought—if she had paused for half a second to calculate the distance, the weight of the steel bar, her own five-foot-four frame against two grown men with crowbars—she would have stayed in the shadow of the bay tree. She would have dialed 999, listened to the four rings, spoken to an operator thirty miles away in Kidlington, and waited eight minutes for a car that was currently redirecting artics on the A34.

Instead, she screamed.

It was not a word. It was a high, tearing, animal shriek that tore out of the back of her throat with all the air in her lungs, a sound so loud and discordant that it cut through the rain and the hum of the bypass like an electric saw.

She grabbed the nearest object her hands could find: a heavy, cracked red-clay ridge tile that had been leaning against the base of the fence since Uncle Martin had repaired the shed roof in June.

She hurled it across the garden.

The tile didn't hit the men. It hit the rusted corrugated-iron roof of Leonard’s garden shed with a deafening, metallic crash that sounded like a car hitting a garage door: *CLANG-RATTLE-CRASH*.

The two men froze.

The tall man whipped around, the steel bar coming up in his right hand, his body dropping into a low, defensive crouch.

In the yellow light from the scullery window, his face was visible for half a second: young, no more than twenty-five, with a sharp, broken nose, pale skin, and a thin, white scar that ran from the corner of his left nostril down into his upper lip.

His eyes met Nell’s through the branches of the bay tree.

There was no confusion in his face. No panic.

There was only a cold, venomous, calculating shock: the expression of a tradesman who had arrived to fit a carpet and found the floorboards missing.

"Who’s that?" the shorter man hissed from the gate. His voice was the voice from the tape.

There was no doubt. It was the same flat, nasal, mid-Midlands drawl, stripped of its recording-studio calm, tight with sudden, savage adrenaline.

"Get the door," the tall one muttered, his voice lower, rougher. "Just take the frame off. He’s inside."

"No!" the shorter man snapped, his hand grabbing the tall one’s sleeve. "The timetable’s blown. Look at the road!"

At the front of the house, beyond the side gate, the headlights of a car swept across the brickwork of number 14.

It was not a police car. It was a battered blue Ford Fiesta: Karen’s car, coming home from the late shift ten minutes early because the packing line in Didcot had run out of corrugated shipping cartons.

The little three-cylinder engine whined as Karen shifted into first gear to climb the dropped curb of the driveway.

The tall man hesitated for two seconds. His knuckles were white around the blue shaft of the wrecking bar. His eyes flicked from the scullery door to the side passage, then back to the darkness where Nell was standing.

"You," he whispered.

He didn't shout it. He said it straight at her, across the six yards of wet air.

"We know your name, Nell Mercer."

Then he turned, vaulted the low wall into the recreation ground with a single, fluid heave of his long arms, and vanished into the darkness behind the tennis courts.

The shorter man was already gone.

Nell heard the wet *squelch* of his boots hitting the cinder path by the bowling green, the sound fading into the steady, drumming roar of the rain against the shed roof.

### Chapter 11

Karen was carrying two plastic bags from the Iceland when she came through the front gate.

"Nell?" she said, stopping on the path as Nell came around the corner of the house, covered in grey mud from the ditch, her hair dripping into her collar, her hands scraped raw from the ridge tile. "What on earth are you doing in the garden in this weather? Where’s your coat?"

Nell didn't answer. She grabbed her mother’s wrist—the wrist with the plastic hospital-style ID bracelet from the logistics depot—and dragged her through the front door.

"Lock it," Nell whispered, slamming the door shut. She turned the Yale latch, threw the heavy brass mortice bolt, and shoved the iron chain into its slot. "Lock the bottom bolt, Mum. Do it now."

"Nell, what is the matter with you? Have you lost your mind?"

Leonard came out of the sitting room, leaning heavily on his wooden walking stick, his glasses crooked on his nose.

"Nelly?" he said, his voice trembling. "Was that... was that a noise at the back?"

"They were there," Nell said. She was shaking so violently her teeth clicked together like dice in a cup. She leaned her back against the solid timber of the front door, sliding down until she was sitting on the rubber mat among the boots. "They were at the gate. With the crowbar. Two of them."

Karen dropped the shopping bags onto the hall carpet. A frozen bag of oven chips burst, spilling white, frosty batons across the linoleum.

"Who was there? Did you see someone?"

"The men from the tape," Nell said. She pulled the smoke-grey cassette from her pocket and held it up. "The tape from Grandad’s coat. They were here, Mum. They had the timetable written on an empty fag packet. They were going to break the scullery door at twenty past six."

Karen looked from Nell to the cassette, then to her father.

"Dad," Karen said, her voice rising into that sharp, exhausted register that meant she was three minutes from tears. "What is she talking about? What tape?"

Leonard looked down at his granddaughter sitting on the floor.

The color had drained from his face entirely. He looked eighty-five years old, his shoulders rounded, his neck thin and stringy inside the collar of his checked flannel shirt.

"Show her, Nelly," he whispered.

### Chapter 12

They sat in the kitchen with the curtains drawn and the overhead light off, illuminated only by the small, amber neon indicator on the electric kettle.

The Ferguson sat on the formica table between a jar of instant coffee and a loaf of sliced white bread.

Nell pressed `PLAY`.

The room filled again with the flat, mechanical hiss of the tape:

*Shhh-shhh-shhh-shhh.*

Then the voice:

"Fourteen Blackthorn Close. The job took seven minutes. The rain started at twenty past six, just like the regional forecast said on the radio..."

Karen sat with both hands pressed over her mouth, her eyes wide, staring at the little silver machine as if it were a venomous spider that had crawled out of the fruit bowl.

When the voice reached the description of the floorboards—*the third board from the skirting, the one with the two brass dome-head screws*—she dropped her hands to the table, her fingernails digging into the floral pattern of the oilcloth.

"Dad," she said. Her voice was barely a croak. "How does he know that?"

Leonard didn't answer. He was staring at the salt cellar.

"Dad! That’s your room! That’s the board where you keep Martha’s jewellery!"

"Not Martha’s jewellery," Leonard said softly. "The watch. And the cash."

"What cash?"

"The five hundred," he said. "From the railway."

Karen stood up so fast her chair screeched against the floorboards. "You’ve had five hundred pounds in cash under the floor for thirty years? While I’ve been paying eighteen percent interest on the council tax arrears?"

"It wasn't for that!" the old man shouted, his voice cracking with sudden, unexpected fury. "It was for the burial! I didn't want the parish burying me like they buried George! In a trench with twelve others and a wooden peg!"

"Stop it!" Nell yelled, slamming her palm down onto the table. "Both of you! Listen to the tape!"

The machine had reached the end of the recording:

"...The girl wasn't back. Her bus from the college was the seventeen-eighteen, but it was running twenty minutes late on account of the roadworks at the railway bridge. She came in through the back gate at ten to six. We were already back at the Vauxhall by then, turning round in the entrance to the sewage works."

The lighter clicked. The exhalation of smoke.

"Everything went off smooth. No blood. No glass. Left the door shut behind us so the heat didn't get out. The old man was still sitting there when we pulled onto the bypass. It was seven minutes past six."

*Click.*

The machine hummed.

Nell reached over and hit `STOP`.

"He knew the bus was going to be twenty minutes late," Nell said into the silence. "The roadworks only have one lane open. Every bus since Tuesday has been twenty minutes late. He didn't guess that. He *timed* it."

Karen sank back into her chair. Her face was grey. "Nell... who is he?"

"I don't know," Nell said. "The police said it was a student prank. They said they didn't have a cassette player."

"The police?" Karen’s voice cracked. "You went to the police?"

"To Stannington. The woman at the desk didn't even take my name. She told me to dial nine-nine-nine if someone was in the garden."

"We’re dialling it now," Karen said, reaching for her handbag on the worktop. "We’re calling them right now. There were two men in the garden with crowbars! That’s an attempted break-in!"

"And what will they find?" Nell asked.

Karen froze, her hand inside her bag.

"What will they find, Mum? A broken ridge tile on the shed roof. Footprints in the mud that could belong to anyone. And an old tape in a second-hand coat that Grandad bought three days ago."

"It’s evidence!"

"Evidence of what?" Nell leaned across the table. "Evidence that someone recorded a story on a tape? The woman at the station looked at me like I was mad. If they come out here tonight, they’ll spend ten minutes shining a torch at the compost heap, write down Grandad’s date of birth, tell us to leave the hall light on, and drive back to the bypass. And then what?"

"They’ll patrol," Karen said weakly.

"They won't patrol," Nell said, her voice hard, stripped of any teenage deference. "You know they won't. There’s one car for thirty square miles. They don't come down this Close unless someone’s car is on fire."

She turned to Leonard.

"Grandad," she said. "The coat."

Leonard looked up.

"Where did the coat come from?"

"I told you," he whispered. "Arthur Cross."

"Did you take it off the rail yourself?"

Leonard’s eyes shifted.

For the first time since she had walked into the house with the heavy wool over her arm, Nell saw a crack in her grandfather’s story.

It was tiny—just a twitch of the left eyelid, a slight tightening of the lips around his yellowed lower teeth—but it was there.

"Grandad," she said, her voice dropping into a dangerous, quiet calm. "Did you buy that coat off the rail at St Jude’s?"

Leonard looked down at his hands.

The silence in the kitchen stretched out until the only sound was the refrigerator compressor kicking in with a low, hydraulic hum.

"No," Leonard said.

### Chapter 13

Karen stared at her father. "What do you mean, *no*? You told me you gave eleven-fifty for it on Tuesday!"

"I didn't buy it off the rail," Leonard said. His voice was steady now, resigned, like a man who had reached the end of a long, narrow tunnel and found the exit bricked up. "It was behind the counter. In a cardboard box with my name on it."

Nell felt the air leave her lungs.

"Your name?"

"A cardboard box," Leonard repeated. "An old crisp box. Golden Wonder. Written on the flap in blue marker: *Mr L. Mercer. Hold behind counter.*"

"Who gave it to you?" Nell asked.

"Mrs Clout," he said. "Brenda Clout. She’s the volunteer on Tuesday mornings. Her husband was the signalman at Didcot North when I was on the ballast gang. She said a chap brought it in on Monday afternoon. Said it belonged to Arthur Cross, and that Arthur had always wanted me to have it because of the wool. Said Arthur had left instructions before he went into the hospital."

"Did she know the chap?"

"No," Leonard said. "Said he was a young chap. Wore a high-vis jacket like the delivery drivers wear. Didn't leave a name. Just dropped the box on the counter, said *That’s for Len Mercer, he’ll know what it is*, and walked out."

"And you paid eleven-fifty for it?" Karen asked, bewildered.

"Brenda said she had to put something through the till for the hospice books," Leonard muttered, a faint flush of shame returning to his cheeks. "Said she couldn't just hand over an Austrian loden coat for nothing, even if Arthur wanted me to have it. So I gave her the tenner I had in my purse and two pound coins from the tea tin, and she gave me fifty pence change."

Nell stood up.

She walked out into the dark hall, picked up the heavy moss-green coat from the chair, and carried it back into the kitchen.

She dumped it onto the formica table.

It hit the surface with a heavy, dead *thud*.

"Turn the light on, Mum," Nell said.

Karen reached out and flicked the switch.

The fluorescent tube above the sink flickered three times, buzzed like an angry hornet, and then settled into a cold, blue-white glare that showed every grease spot on the walls and every tea stain on the oilcloth.

Nell spread the coat out flat.

The boiled wool was stiff, dark, impervious to the rain. The horn buttons were large, carved with an alternating cross-hatch pattern, secured with thick leather loops stitched through the facing.

Nell ran her hands down the lining.

It was brown acetate satin, cold and slippery under her fingers.

She turned the coat over.

She examined the collar. Behind the neck, where a tailor’s label would normally be sewn, there was only a rectangle of frayed white buckram where a label had been cut away with a razor blade. The tiny stumps of black thread were still visible in the wool.

She reached for the right-hand pocket where she had found the tape.

She pulled the torn cotton pocket bag inside out.

The tear wasn't accidental.

The chain-stitch hadn't rotted or pulled loose from the strain of heavy keys. It had been cut.

Nell held the fabric close to her eyes.

The threads were severed clean, cut at a forty-five-degree angle with a pair of sharp embroidery scissors or a craft knife. The cut was exactly three inches wide—just large enough for an adult hand to slide a C-60 cassette through the opening and wedge it into the space between the wool and the lining hem.

And then she saw the second thing.

In the small ticket pocket inside the left breast—a pocket so narrow you could barely slide two fingers into it—there was a tiny, stiff piece of card.

Nell used her fingernails to work it out.

It was a railway luggage tag: old-fashioned manila card with reinforced brass eyelets at both ends, the kind British Rail used for registered parcel freight in the nineteen-eighties.

Across the front, written in faded black Indian ink with a steel-nibbed drafting pen, were three lines of text:

`CONSIGNMENT: 409-S`
`ORIGIN: DIDCOT MARSHALLING YARD / SIDING 4`
`DESTINATION: CROSS / MERCER`
`DATE: 14-11-1994`

Underneath the date, in the same red permanent marker that had been used on the cassette, someone had written a single word:

`RECORDED.`

### Chapter 14

The kitchen was so quiet they could hear the electric clock on the microwave ticking its digital seconds.

Leonard stared at the manila tag.

His mouth moved, but no sound came out. His hands, resting on the edge of the formica table, were shaking so hard that his teacup rattled against the sugar bowl.

"Grandad," Nell said.

"November ninety-four," the old man whispered.

"What happened in November ninety-four?"

Karen looked at her father, her eyes narrowing with a sudden, sharp recognition that seemed to cut through her panic. "That was the winter you had off work, Dad. The winter you said you fell off the platform at Appleford and broke your collarbone."

Leonard didn't look at her.

"You weren't at work for four months," Karen said, her voice dropping into a flat, accusing register. "Mum had to take the cleaning job at the secondary school to pay the gas bill. You stayed in the shed all day. Every time the phone rang, you made me answer it."

Leonard reached out and took the tag between his trembling fingers.

"It wasn't Appleford," he said.

"Where was it, Dad?"

"Siding Four," Leonard whispered. "At the Didcot yard."

He looked up at Nell. His eyes were watering, the red veins in the whites standing out like tiny cracked maps.

"There were three of us," he said. "Me, Arthur Cross, and a chap called Dennis Vane. Dennis was the yard foreman. He had the keys to the transit sheds where they kept the bonded goods coming off the Southampton freight trains."

Nell leaned forward, her elbows on the heavy green wool of the coat.

"What was in Siding Four, Grandad?"

"A wagon," Leonard said. "A four-wheel ventilated van. Sealed with lead customs seals. It had been shunted into the siding on the tenth of November because it had a hot axle box. Dennis had the paperwork. It was supposed to be carrying machine parts from Germany for the car plant in Cowley."

He paused, his chest heaving with a long, dry breath.

"It wasn't machine parts," he said. "It was cigarettes. Two hundred cases of Benson and Hedges. Four hundred thousand fags. And thirty crates of Dutch gin."

Karen let out a short, sharp gasp. "You stole a train wagon?"

"We didn't steal the wagon!" Leonard shouted, his face turning dark red. "The wagon stayed on the rails! We unloaded it! Over three nights, into Arthur’s timber lorry. We hid the stuff in the sawdust pit at the back of the mill."

"And the consignment number?" Nell asked, pointing at the tag. "`409-S`?"

"That was the waybill," Leonard said. "Dennis kept the waybill in his locker so the railway police wouldn't see it was missing from the manifest. If anyone asked, the wagon had been sent back to Southampton for wheel-turning."

"And what happened to Dennis Vane?"

Leonard looked down at the table.

"Dennis was greedy," he said quietly. "He didn't want to wait for the fence in London to clear the stuff. He tried to sell forty cases out of the back of his brother’s van at the market in Bicester. The transport police were waiting for him by the cattle pens."

"Did he go to prison?" Nell asked.

"Five years," Leonard said. "He did three and a half at the Verne on Portland. He never gave our names. Arthur and me... we took our share of the money from the first lot, and the rest of the stuff stayed in the sawdust pit until the timber yard caught fire in ninety-six."

"And Dennis Vane?" Nell asked again. "Where is he now?"

"Dead," Leonard said. "Died ten years back in a nursing home in Northampton. He had a stroke. Couldn't speak his own name."

Nell picked up the cassette from the table.

She held it up to the light of the fluorescent tube.

`14-B.`

"Dennis Vane might be dead," Nell said. "Arthur Cross might be dead. But someone knew about Siding Four. Someone knew that you and Arthur were the ones who took that consignment."

"Why?" Karen cried, throwing her hands up. "Why thirty years later? For five hundred pounds and a cracked pocket watch?"

"It’s not for the watch," Nell said.

She turned the cassette over in her hands, looking at the little smoke-grey rectangle with its scarred label.

"Grandad... did Dennis Vane have a son?"

Leonard went very still.

His eyes widened, fixed on the corner of the dresser where his railway plates sat in their plastic stands.

"Dennis had two boys," he whispered. "Twins."

"What were their names?"

"Julian," Leonard said, his voice barely audible over the hum of the fridge. "And... and Gary."

Nell looked at the back door.

Through the curtains, she could hear the rain drumming against the scullery glass: steady, indifferent, cold.

*We left the Vauxhall parked on the verge by the telephone exchange...*

*The job took seven minutes...*

"They didn't record a tape describing what happened," Nell whispered, the full, terrifying mechanics of the thing finally clicking together in her brain like the teeth of a brass gear. "They recorded a tape describing what they were *going* to do."

"Why?" Karen asked. "Why record it at all?"

"Because of the past tense," Nell said.

She looked at her mother.

"Think about it, Mum. If the police find a tape after a burglary—a tape found in an old man’s coat, describing the break-in in the past tense, down to the second—what do they think?"

Karen stared at her. "They think... they think it’s an insurance job. Or a confession."

"No," Nell said, her skin prickling with ice. "They think the person who owns the coat was the one who planned it. Or they think it’s a recording made *after* the fact to prove the job was done."

She pointed at the date on the luggage tag:

`14-11-1994.`

And then she looked at her phone:

`14 November 2026.`

"It was thirty years ago today," Nell said. "To the exact day."

### Chapter 15

The front gate didn't click.

Nell heard it because she was listening—not with her ears, but with the skin of her neck, with the soles of her feet resting flat on the cold linoleum.

It was not the gate latch. It was the sound of a shoe heel slipping half an inch on the wet moss of the garden path.

Nell held up her hand.

"Hush," she breathed.

Karen froze, her tea mug halfway to her mouth. Leonard sat rigid in his chair, his eyes fixed on the hallway door.

Through the silence of the house, from the front porch, came a sound so faint it was almost swallowed by the ticking of the microwave:

The metal flap of the letterbox lifting.

A fraction of an inch.

Just enough for the cold, outside air to hiss through the rubber draft-excluder.

Nell rose from her chair without making a sound. She slid her feet across the floor in her woollen socks, stopping at the kitchen doorway.

The hall was dark.

Through the frosted glass panel above the front door, the orange light from the streetlamp threw a dim, rectangular grid across the wallpaper.

In the middle of the front door, two inches above the brass letterbox, the flap stayed open.

Something thin, dark, and flexible was being slid through the brushes.

It wasn't a crowbar.

It was an endoscope: a flexible plastic tube with a tiny, glass camera lens at the tip, its black cable uncoiling silently through the draft-excluder, turning slowly in the air as the person on the porch manipulated the handle from the outside.

A tiny, blue LED at the tip of the lens illuminated the hall carpet.

The lens swung left, pointing toward the coat rack where the Wellington boots sat in their tray.

Then it swung right, aiming straight through the open kitchen door toward the table where Leonard and Karen were sitting.

Nell didn't move.

She stood flattened against the kitchen doorframe, her cheek pressed against the cold gloss paint, her breath held inside her chest until her lungs burned.

On the porch, through the letterbox, a voice whispered.

It was the voice from the tape.

"The chain’s on," the voice muttered. "And the bottom bolt. They’re inside."

A second voice, rougher, younger—the tall man with the scar:

"The car’s in the drive. The woman’s back."

"Leave it," the older voice said. "The window’s blown. The girl saw my face."

"He won't come to the yard."

"He will," the older voice said, cold, deliberate, terrifyingly certain. "He’ll come when he sees what’s in the tin."

The blue light on the endoscope went out.

The flexible tube was pulled backward through the brushes of the letterbox with a soft, dry *rustle*.

The metal flap dropped shut.

*Clack.*

Then the sound of two sets of feet—one heavy, one light—stepping off the concrete porch onto the grass verge, moving away toward the cul-de-sac.

Nell waited forty seconds.

She didn't move until she heard the distant, muffled cough of a four-cylinder diesel engine starting up on the other side of the hawthorn hedge: an old van, its starter motor grinding three times before the cylinders caught with a heavy, clattering rattle.

The sound moved down the Close, turned onto the B4012, and faded toward the bypass.

### Chapter 16

Karen was on her knees by the kitchen sink, vomiting into the plughole.

### Chapter 17

The back bedroom smelled of lavender sachets, old wool blankets, and the liniment Leonard rubbed into his arthritic knees every night before bed.

The furniture was dark mahogany veneer from the nineteen-fifties: a dressing table with an oval swinging mirror that had lost its silvering around the bevelled edges, a single bed with a candlewick bedspread, and a massive, two-door wardrobe that stood against the internal partition wall.

Nell knelt on the carpet beside the wardrobe.

The floorboards were narrow, six-inch Baltic pine, stained dark brown with bitumen around the margins where the carpet didn't reach.

She counted from the skirting board under the window:

One.

Two.

Three.

The third board had a cross-cut six inches from the wardrobe foot, where an electrician had notched it to run a cable in the nineteen-seventies.

*The third board from the skirting, the one with the two brass dome-head screws that don't match the steel ones.*

"They didn't find it, Grandad," Nell said quietly. "You heard them. They didn't get in."

Nell took it.

She knelt over the board. She set the blade into the slot of the first brass screw.

She removed the second screw.

She slid the tip of the screwdriver into the cross-cut notch and levered the board upward.

The pine board lifted with a long, complaining groan of old nails.

Beneath the floor, in the dark, dusty hollow between two eight-inch joists, sat a layer of grey rockwool insulation covered in decades of soot and dead spiders.

And nestled in the middle of the rockwool was a box.

It was an old cash tin, about ten inches long, six inches wide, and four inches deep, painted in dark blue enamel that had chipped away at the corners to reveal the dull grey tinplate beneath. It had a brass drop-handle on the lid and a simple wafer lock in the front.

Nell reached down into the joist space.

Her hands came away black with soot. She lifted the tin out.

It was surprisingly heavy—at least eight or nine pounds.

"The key’s on my watch chain," Leonard said from the bed, his voice so thin it seemed to come from another room.

Nell looked at him.

He pulled a small, brass split-ring from his waistcoat pocket. On the ring was a Yale front-door key, a brass church key for opening beer bottles, and a tiny, flat, nickel-plated key with a barrel-shaped shank.

Nell took the key.

She set the blue tin on the floorboards between her knees.

She inserted the key into the wafer lock.

It turned with a soft, oily *click*.

She lifted the lid.

### Chapter 18

Inside the tin, lying on a bed of yellowed cotton wool, was a bundle of twenty-pound notes: thirty notes, folded twice and secured with a dried, perished rubber band that cracked into brown crumbs as soon as Nell touched it.

Beside the money lay a silver pocket watch, its case tarnished almost to black, the glass crystal cracked across the center like a spider’s web.

`OCTOBER - DECEMBER 1994`

### Chapter 19

Nell looked up from the ledger.

Leonard was watching her.

He was not shaking anymore. The tremor in his hands had stopped, replaced by a strange, heavy stillness that made him look like a statue carved out of chalk.

"Grandad," Nell said. "Who was the third man?"

"There were only three of us," Leonard said.

"You said you, Arthur Cross, and Dennis Vane."

"Aye."

"Then who is this?" She pointed at the envelope. "Why does it say *The Third Man*?"

"Dennis didn't find that wagon by accident, Nelly," he said, the words coming out slow, heavy, dragged up from thirty years of silence. "You don't just happen across a hot axle box on a train coming off the docks. A hot box is arranged. You slacken the wedge on the brass bearing with a ten-inch spanner before the train leaves the yard at Basingstoke, and by the time it hits Didcot, the axle box is smoking like a chimney."

"The guard," Leonard said.

"His sister’s boy," Leonard whispered. "From Kettering. The one who had the radio in his brake van. He was mad on recording things. "What was his name?"

"Brian," Leonard said. "Brian Gidley."

Nell stopped breathing.

The air in the bedroom seemed to freeze into solid glass.

*Gidley.*

She remembered the police station in Stannington.

The modern building of grey cladding and corrugated steel.

The woman behind the three-inch security glass with her novelty mug and her reading glasses on a beaded cord.

`INSPECTOR M. HOLLIS`
`SERGEANT B. GIDLEY`
`PC K. TRENT`

A man of about fifty-four, thick-set, with thinning grey hair cut close to the scalp, a round face, and a thin, grey moustache above a wide, flat mouth.

Beneath the photograph was the name:

`PS 4412 BRIAN GIDLEY`
`STANNINGTON & DISTRICT NEIGHBOURHOOD TEAM`

The man who had stood at the back gate with the canvas tool roll under his arm.

*The job took seven minutes...*

*The rain started at twenty past six, just like the regional forecast said on the radio...*

### Chapter 20

"It was an alibi," Nell said.

She was pacing the narrow strip of carpet between the bed and the window, her phone clutched in both hands, her mind moving with the terrifying speed of an engine that had dropped its governor.

"It wasn't an instruction tape," she said, turning on her mother and grandfather. "It wasn't a rehearsal. It was an *alibi*."

"What are you talking about?" Karen cried. "An alibi for who?"

"For him!" Nell pointed at the phone screen. "For Sergeant Gidley! Don't you see how it works?"

She held up the cassette.

"He records the tape on Thursday morning. He describes the burglary in the past tense, down to the second, including the weather, the bus delay, the roadworks, the broken frame on the back door. He puts the tape into the pocket of Arthur Cross’s old coat and leaves it at the charity shop for Grandad."

"Why?"

"Because if the job goes wrong—if Grandad wakes up, if someone screams, if the neighbours look out the window—he doesn't break in! But if it goes right, what happens tomorrow?"

She looked at her grandfather.

"Tomorrow morning, Grandad wakes up. The back door is broken. The blue tin is gone. Grandad calls the police. Who investigates burglaries in Blackthorn Green?"

"The neighborhood team," Karen whispered, her hand going to her mouth.

"Sergeant Gidley," Nell said. "He arrives in his patrol car with a notebook. He looks at the scullery door. He looks at the floorboards. And then he sees Grandad’s new coat hanging in the hall. He searches the coat. He finds the tape."

The cold, brilliant, filthy logic of it hung in the air of the bedroom like smoke.

"He plays the tape," Nell continued. "And the tape has a voice describing the burglary that just happened, in detail, with information only the homeowner could know: the railway pension, George’s watch, the brass dome-head screws from the coal bunker. And the coat belongs to Len Mercer. What does Gidley tell his inspector?"

"He tells them... he tells them Dad staged it," Karen breathed, the horror dawning in her eyes. "He tells them Dad arranged a fake burglary to claim the insurance on the house. Or that Dad was involved in something thirty years ago and staged a break-in to hide it."

"And the blue tin?" Nell asked. "Does the blue tin go on the crime report?"

"No," Leonard said, his head sinking into his chest. "The blue tin never existed. If Dennis’s nephew takes the tin, there’s no record of it anywhere. Because the freight log inside proves that Gidley was the one who pulled the wedge on the axle box thirty years ago."

"And if Grandad tells them about Siding Four?" Nell asked. "Who believes an eighty-year-old retired trackman who admits he stole four hundred thousand cigarettes in nineteen ninety-four? Against a police sergeant with twenty-five years on the force and a commendation from the Chief Constable?"

Nobody.

The case would be closed within forty-eight hours: *Elderly male, early-stage dementia, suspected fraudulent report / historical confabulation. No further action.*

And Brian Gidley would walk away with the ledger that proved his family had stolen a government-bonded freight consignment, five hundred pounds in cash, and a thirty-year-old grudge settled in full.

Except for one thing.

Nell looked down at the cassette in her hand.

"The bus wasn't twenty minutes late," she said.

Karen blinked. "What?"

"The bus," Nell said, a wild, sharp smile cutting across her pale face. "On the tape, he said the seventeen-eighteen bus was twenty minutes late because of the roadworks at the bridge. But I didn't take the seventeen-eighteen. I took the sixteen-eighteen. I stayed on it two stops past the Close. I was already home when he dropped the coat off."

She looked out the window.

The rain was still falling, washing the mud from the hawthorn hedge, turning the road at the end of the Close into a dark, mirror-like canal under the sodium lamps.

"He recorded the alibi before the event," Nell said. "And he put the coat in the box on Tuesday. But the roadworks at the bridge didn't start on Tuesday. The roadworks started on *Wednesday*."

She turned the cassette over in her palm.

"He made a mistake with the timetable," she said. "And now he’s coming back for the tin."

### Chapter 21

At 19:40, the phone in the hallway began to ring.

The sound was jarring: the high, mechanical jangle of an old GPO rotary-style bell that Karen had bought at a car boot sale because she liked the chrome dial.

*Brrr-ring.*

*Brrr-ring.*

The three of them stood at the top of the stairs, looking down into the dark well of the hallway.

*Brrr-ring.*

*Brrr-ring.*

She picked up the heavy, black plastic receiver.

"Fourteen Blackthorn Close," she said, her voice completely level.

"Nell," the voice said.

It was Gidley.

He was not using his radio voice. He was not using his police voice. It was the voice from the Ferguson: flat, dry, slightly nasal, smelling of tobacco and cold tea.

"You’ve got the tin on the table, haven't you?" he said.

"My name isn't on that waybill," Gidley said, his voice dropping half an octave, the patience disappearing completely.

"You’re a clever girl, Nell," he said. "Cleverer than your grandad ever was. Len was always soft. He took his five hundred and he bought a secondhand washing machine and two commemorative plates. He could have had forty thousand if he’d had the stomach to clear the rest of the siding."

"What do you want?" Nell asked.

"I want the tin," Gidley said. "And I want the tape."

"And then I forget that number fourteen has an unsecured back door with dry-rot in the frame," Gidley said. "I forget that your uncle Martin has three unregistered agricultural trailers behind his unit at the industrial estate that don't have chassis plates. And I forget that your mother hasn't declared the twelve hundred quid she made cash-in-hand doing the night inventory for the Turkish lads at the car wash."

Karen, standing at the top of the stairs, let out a tiny, stifled sob.

"You’re parked at the sewage works," Nell said.

"I’m parked where I can see the junction," Gidley said. "The Number 38 passes the end of the road at twenty past eight. It’s the last bus from Stannington tonight. When that bus goes past, the road’s empty until six tomorrow morning."

"I’ll give you twenty minutes, Nell. Put the tin and the cassette into Arthur Cross’s coat. Hang the coat over the front garden wall. Then go inside, turn the lights off, and take your mother and the old man up to the front bedroom."

"And if we don't?"

"If you don't," Gidley said, his voice dropping into a register of cold, official menace that made her blood run backward, "I come in with the crowbar. And when the response car from Kidlington turns up at two in the morning, they find an eighty-year-old man who had a heart attack during a break-in, a girl with a fractured skull who fell down the stairs in the dark, and a kitchen fire caused by an old paraffin heater that had a leak in the fuel valve."

He clicked his tongue against his teeth.

"You’ve got nineteen minutes, love. Make sure the latch on the gate is on the sneck."

The line went dead.

### Chapter 22

The kitchen was dark again.

Nell stood by the table with the blue cash tin under her arm.

Karen was sitting on the floor by the larder cupboard, her arms wrapped around her knees, rocking back and forth in the gloom.

"We have to give it to him," Karen whispered, her teeth chattering. "Nell, give it to him! He’s a policeman! He knows the magistrates, he knows the coroners, he knows everyone in the county! If he burns the house down, who’s going to stop him?"

"Mum," Nell said, turning to her mother on the floor. "Get up."

"Because we have twelve minutes," Nell said. "And we’re going to make a new tape."

### Chapter 23

The garden shed at number 14 was eight feet by six feet, built of creosoted shiplap timber on a single course of engineering bricks, smelling of dry peat, lawnmower petrol, and the sharp, sulfuric tang of decomposing lead-acid batteries.

Nell knelt on the oil-stained timber floorboards beside the potting bench.

Her grandfather stood at the door, his walking stick hooked over his left forearm, holding an unlit paraffin lantern to shield the match he was striking.

"Don't light it," Nell whispered. "He’s at the sewage works. He’s got binoculars."

Leonard dropped the match into a tin of grease. "What are you doing with the wire, Nelly?"

"The Ferguson has a remote socket," she said, pointing to the two small holes on the side of the tape deck labeled `REMOTE` and `MIC`. "A two-point-five-millimetre jack. It’s for a foot-pedal. When the circuit closes, the motor runs."

She stripped the grey PVC insulation off the twin-and-earth cable with her teeth, spitting the plastic fragments onto the sawdust.

"Dennis had the dies," Leonard muttered. "He made them in the machine shop at the locomotive works."

`ITEM 01-14: FABRIQUE NATIONALE HERSTAL P90 SUBMACHINE GUNS (9MM CALIBRE) — 40 UNITS.`
`ITEM 15-28: HECKLER & KOCH MP5-A3 (PARABELLUM) — 60 UNITS.`
`ITEM 29-44: SEMTEX-H MILITARY PLASTIC EXPLOSIVE — 180 KILOGRAMS.`
`ITEM 45-60: DETONATING ASSEMBLIES / ELECTRIC DELAY CAPSULES — 400 UNITS.`

Nell’s breath stopped.

The small kitchen seemed to shrink until the walls were pressing against her temples.

*Semtex.*

*Submachine guns.*

She looked at her grandfather.

Leonard’s face was not grey anymore. It was white—a dead, bloodless, marble white that made the veins in his temples look like blue ink drawn on greaseproof paper.

His mouth fell open. His bottom lip trembled, his false teeth clicking against the roof of his mouth with a dry, hollow sound.

"No," he whispered.

"Yes, Mr Mercer," Miller said softly.

"It was crates," Leonard cried, his hands coming off his knees, clawing at the edge of the formica table. "Wooden crates! Marked with the red crown! "Where did the crates go, Mr Mercer?"

"To the mill," Leonard gasped, his chest heaving, his fingers clutching at the breast of his flannel shirt where George’s watch had been. "The timber mill at Lower Weir. "The fire was a diversion, Mr Mercer," she said.

"And who owned the quarry, Nell?"

"The third man," Nell said.

Miller nodded once.

"The third man," she said. "The man whose name was in the envelope your grandfather kept under the wardrobe for thirty years. "His name is Sir Ronald Finch," Miller said.

### Chapter 31

The name did not belong to the world of Blackthorn Close.

`NOTICE OF REDEVELOPMENT: THE SIDING BUNGALOWS`
`VALE OF WHITE HORSE DISTRICT COUNCIL`
`APPLICATION REF: P26/V0881/FUL`

"Finch Logistics bought the old timber yard and the siding bungalows from Arthur Cross’s estate in October," Miller said. "The bungalows," she said.

### Chapter 32

At 11:00, the police left.

### Chapter 33

Julian Vane had not been in Alicante.

He had been in Blackthorn Green since Monday morning.

He had stood at the corner of the cul-de-sac with a clipboard and an orange vest, watching the houses, watching the bus stop, watching the old men with their shopping bags and the girls coming home from the tertiary college with their backpacks.

*Why?*

### Chapter 34

The sitting room was so quiet they could hear the electric hum of the digital television on standby in the corner.

### Chapter 35

The realization hung over the house like a poison cloud.

Julian Vane was not running for the ferry.

He had not taken the A34 south toward Southampton.

He was still here.

He was in Blackthorn Green, trapped by his own thirty-year-old delusion, believing that Leonard Mercer had outsmarted his father, that the old railway trackman had switched the documents, and that somewhere in this grey commuter town, hidden in a tea tin, a coal bunker, or a garden shed, lay the paper that was worth five million pounds.

"Aye, Nelly?"

"What’s inside the engine shed at Siding Four?"

### Chapter 36

The walk to Siding Four followed the line of the old railway embankment.

In the nineteen-eighties, before the freight sidings were closed and the marshalling yard was sold off for warehousing, this had been a four-track mainline: two lines for the passenger expresses between Paddington and South Wales, two lines for the coal trains coming down from the Forest of Dean to the power station at Didcot.

Now, it was a wasteland of willow-herb, rusting wire fences, and shattered concrete sleepers overgrown with blackberry brambles.

Nell walked along the ballast path, her boots crunching in the grey granite gravel.

To her left, the bypass hummed with its eternal, mindless roar of sixty-mile-an-hour logistics traffic: Eddie Stobart artics, DPD delivery vans, Amazon parcel trucks, moving goods from the ports to the commuter belts, from the distribution centers to the doorsteps, keeping thirty million people fed and clothed without anyone ever having to look at the mud or the rails.

To her right lay the siding.

It sat in a shallow cutting between the canal arm and the back fences of the industrial estate: two hundred yards of weed-choked tracks, three abandoned platelayers’ huts with their roofs fallen in, and at the far end, half-hidden behind a screen of self-seeded sycamores, Arthur Cross’s bungalow.

It was not a house.

It was an old Great Western railway coach—a wooden-bodied clerestory brake carriage built at Swindon in 1912—that had been lifted off its bogies and set down on brick piers in 1948 to serve as a crossing-keeper’s cabin. Over sixty years, Arthur Cross had built a shiplap extension on the north side, a corrugated iron scullery on the south side, and a porch made of old railway sleepers across the front, until the whole structure looked like an overgrown garden shed that had swallowed a train.

A low wire fence, hung with rusted tin signs that read *BRITISH WATERWAYS — PRIVATE ACCESS*, separated the garden from the trackbed.

The gate was secured with an iron chain and a padlock.

Nell reached the gate.

She stopped.

The chain was not rusted.

The padlock—a heavy, brass Squire padlock with a hardened steel shackle—was brand new. The brass was shiny, unscratched, the keyhole greased with clear silicone oil.

And on the top rail of the wooden gate, pressed into the grey, weathered grain of the timber, was a piece of white chalk.

Someone had drawn a symbol:

A circle, three inches across, with a cross through the center.

Underneath the circle, written in the same red permanent marker that had been used on the cassette, was a single letter:

`V`

Nell stood on the ballast.

The wind off the water meadows blew through the sycamore trees, rattling the dry seed-pods with a sound like dry bones.

She didn't touch the chain.

She followed the wire fence along the ditch until she reached the corner where the canal overflow culvert ran beneath the embankment.

Here, the fence had been pulled away from the concrete post, creating an eighteen-inch gap behind an elder bush.

Nell got down on her hands and knees.

She crawled through the wet nettles, dragging the heavy green coat beneath her, her hands sinking into the black silt of the ditch.

She came up inside the garden.

The garden was a jungle of rotting runner-bean canes, rusted iron drums, and heaps of coal slack covered in grey tarpaulins.

In the middle of the grass stood the bungalow.

The windows were boarded up with half-inch marine plywood, each sheet secured with six coach screws.

Except for the scullery window.

The plywood on the scullery window had been pried open at the bottom corner, the wood splintered where a crowbar had been driven under the frame.

Nell approached the window.

She kept low, her back pressed against the corrugated iron siding of the scullery, her breath coming in short, silent puffs of mist.

She reached the opening.

She looked inside.

### Chapter 37

The scullery of Arthur Cross’s bungalow smelled of mice, cold ashes, and ninety years of dead grease.

Through the gap in the plywood, the grey light fell across a stone sink with a brass hand-pump, a rusty gas ring sitting on an upturned wooden crate, and three shelves of old glass jam jars containing rusted wood-screws, lead washers, and bicycle valves.

*Side B.*

A sharp, metallic click.

"Thirty years," he whispered into the empty scullery.

*Tink... tink... tink.*

### Chapter 38

### Chapter 39

### Chapter 40

The engine shed at Siding Four was eighty feet long and thirty feet wide, built of dark Staffordshire blue brick with a double-pitched roof of Welsh slate that had lost half its tiles to sixty years of winter gales.

On the front of each crate, stenciled in faded black military lettering, was a four-line marking:

`EXPLOSIVE: HIGH / CLASS 1.1D`
`FILLER: SEMTEX-H (PLASTICIZED)`
`NET EXPLOSIVE CONTENT: 10.0 KG`
`LOT NO: VCH-1991-884`
`ORIGIN: VCHZ SYNTHESIA / PARDUBICE (CZ)`

Nell stared down into the pit.

Sixteen crates.

One hundred and sixty kilograms of Czech military high explosive.

Enough to level half of Blackthorn Green.

Enough to turn the entire Finch Logistics Park into a smoking, sixty-foot crater of shattered concrete and twisted structural steel.

Julian dropped down into the pit.

His combat boots crunched in the coal slack. He knelt beside the nearest box, his fingers trembling as he touched the rusted steel strapping.

"Nineteen ninety-one," he whispered. "Synthesia. From the Czech army depots after the Wall came down."

He looked up at Nell.

His face was streaked with black coal dust, his eyes shining in the white glare of the Tilley lamp.

"How did they get it into Didcot?" he asked.

"Through the docks," a voice said from the darkness of the shed door.

Nell’s neck went cold.

She didn't scream. She didn't turn around fast.

She slowly stood up straight on the edge of the pit, her hands buried deep in the pockets of the moss-green loden coat.

In the open doorway of the engine shed, silhouetted against the grey afternoon light of the cutting, three men were standing.

The man in the center was tall, slender, eighty years old, wearing a beautifully tailored charcoal cashmere overcoat, an ivory silk scarf tucked neatly into the lapels, and a pair of handmade brown leather brogues that were completely clean of mud.

He leaned lightly on a gold-headed malacca cane.

Beside him stood two younger men in dark suits, their hands clasped in front of their waists, their faces wide, flat, blank, wearing earpieces with coiled transparent acoustic tubes that vanished into the collars of their shirts.

He looked at the Tilley lamp.

He looked at the open pit.

He looked at Nell Mercer standing on the brink of the hole in Arthur Cross’s boiled wool coat.

"The docks were very easy in nineteen ninety-four, my dear," Sir Ronald said, his voice the exact, effortless, aristocratic voice from the Uher tape recorder. "One simply had to know which customs surveyor had a second family in Gosport."

### Chapter 41

The silence in the engine shed was so dense that the hissing of the Tilley lamp sounded like a steam safety valve lifting on a boiler.

### Chapter 44

The Army arrived in three olive-green four-wheel-drive trucks from 11 EOD Regiment at Northolt.

### Chapter 59

The crystal was still cracked, but the balance wheel was moving with that steady, unbreakable rhythm:

*Tick... tick... tick... tick.*

"Brian Gidley was a fool, Nelly," Leonard said.

"I know, Grandad."

"He thought you could write the world down on a piece of tape before it happened," the old man said, his voice slow, deep, carrying eighty years of memory and iron. "He thought that if you had the words right, the trains would have to run the way you said they’d run."

He shook his head.

"The world doesn't run on a script, Nelly," he whispered. "The world runs on the permanent way. It runs on the ballast, and the fishplates, and the grease in the axle boxes. And it runs on the men who stand in the freezing dark with an eight-pound hammer to make sure the rails don't spread when the night mail comes through."

Nell laid her hand over his knuckles.

"We have side B, Grandad," she said.

Leonard looked down at the Ferguson.

He reached out his thumb—the thumb with the missing joint beside it.

He pressed the wide, black key marked `PLAY`.

The key went down with that familiar, heavy mechanical resistance:

*Clonk.*

The small motor hummed.

The left-hand spool began to turn, slowly, smoothly, feeding the brown oxide tape past the permalloy head.

From the yellowed four-inch speaker came the hiss of the room:

*Shhh-shhh-shhh.*

Then the sound of George’s watch ticking.

Then the sound of the tea kettle singing on the gas ring in the kitchen.

Then Leonard’s own voice, recorded four months ago on the twentieth of November:

*The house is brick... Three-lever Yale on the front, two four-inch coach screws on the back... The dog is dead. Prince. Put down three years back on account of his hips. But the girl is here. She takes the sixteen-eighteen from the college. She comes in through the gate at five past five.*

Nell listened to it.

She didn't look at the clock. She didn't look at her phone.

She sat on the floor beside her grandfather’s chair in the warm, bright room, listening to the recording of her own life, spoken by the man who had laid the rails and kept the line.

Outside the double-glazed window, across the dark water meadows where the Siding Meadow lay sleeping under the winter stars, the train went through:

The 17:18 express from Paddington to Cheltenham, its six coaches glowing like a ribbon of amber lanterns against the black hills, its whistle sounding once—clear, high, musical, and true—before it rolled away down the valley into the quiet English night.

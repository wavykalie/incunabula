# Chapter 7: The Wintergreen Balm

Arthur did not turn the electric lamp on in the bedroom.

He sat on the edge of the mattress, his overcoat thrown across the foot of the bed for extra warmth, his woolen socks pulled high over his calves. The room was freezing, smelling of the dry lavender bags Mary used to tuck into the linen press and the damp wallpaper that was peeling in long, curved curls behind the wardrobe.

He unscrewed the blue plastic jar of wintergreen balm.

The scent filled the dark room instantly, biting and medicinal, catching the back of his throat. He dug his fingers into the white grease and began working it into his right knee, pressing his thumbs into the hollow beneath the patella where the fluid gathered after a day on his feet.

*Crackle. Grind.*

The joint sounded like two dry stones being turned together in an iron bucket.

He closed his eyes.

He remembered nineteen ninety-seven. The winter the harbor froze from the timber wharves to the lighthouse. Mary had been fifty-six then, still working three mornings a week at the baker’s near the tram shed. She had slipped on the second-floor landing where the roof had leaked onto the stone stair, cracking her left wrist against the cast-iron balustrade. Arthur had carried her up the twenty-four steps to their flat, his own knees young enough then to bear her weight without buckling, feeling her small, bird-like ribs shivering against his chest.

*I’m getting clumsy, Artie,* she had laughed through her tears, holding her swollen wrist against her cardigan. *The stairs are getting steeper every winter.*

They hadn't been clumsy. The building had simply been doing what stone tenements do when the owners stop spending money on the lead gutters and the roof slates: it was slowly rotting from the sky downward.

By two thousand and fourteen, the last year she was alive, she couldn't manage the stairs at all.

For eleven months, Mary had lived in this bedroom and the kitchen, her entire universe shrunk to fifty square yards of worn linoleum and four windows looking out over Albert Basin. When she needed the doctor, Arthur had to help her down the forty-eight steps, one riser every two minutes, her breathing coming in harsh, whistling gasps that frightened the neighbours.

On step sixteen, she had sat down on the cold stone and refused to move.

*Leave me here a minute, Artie,* she had whispered, her face gray as ditch water, her fingers clutching his coat sleeve. *Just let me sit on the stone. It’s cool on my back.*

Arthur had knelt beside her in the drafty close, holding her cold hands, watching Danuta come out with a woollen shawl to wrap around her shoulders. It had taken forty-five minutes to get her down to the street where the ambulance was waiting with its diesel engine rumbling in the rain.

*If we had a lift, Artie,* she had murmured that night in the ward at the Western Infirmary. *Just a little box with a gate. So an old body could see the street without dying on the stairs.*

She had died four days later of congestive heart failure.

Arthur stopped rubbing. His thumbs were slick with grease, his skin burning from the methyl salicylate.

He looked down at his legs in the dim amber spill of the streetlamps. His knees were thick, distorted by osteophytes, the bone expanding where the cartilage had worn away to bare bone. The specialist at the infirmary had told him two years ago: *Grade four osteoarthritis, Mr. Cross. Bone on bone. We can list you for bilateral knee replacements, but with your hypertension and your age, the recovery will be rough. You need level living. Ground floor. No steps.*

*I have forty-eight steps to my front door, doctor,* Arthur had told him.

*Then you need a new flat,* the doctor had said, already clicking through to the next patient on his computer screen.

A new flat.

In this city, the council housing waiting list for single pensioners had six thousand names on it. If you applied for sheltered housing, they offered you a concrete bedsit in a high-rise in Drumchapel, seven miles from the river, five miles from Mary’s grave at the cemetery, with broken security buzzers and urine in the elevators.

Arthur wiped his hands on his flannel rag.

He pulled his feet up onto the bed and drew the heavy woolen blankets over his shoulders. His breath rose in thin white plumes against the dark ceiling.

Through the floorboards, the building was finally settling into silence. Callum had stopped pacing. Maya had stopped crying. Down on the ground floor, Danuta’s sewing machine was dark and quiet.

Arthur closed his eyes and listened to the harbor wind rattling the sash weights in their wooden boxes, feeling a slow, strange heat rising into his chest.

Arthur lay flat on his back, staring at the ceiling plaster where the sodium light from the dock lamp-post drew a diagonal stripe of yellow across the cornices.

The cold was creeping through the two woollen blankets and his tweed overcoat, sinking into his hip bones and the small of his back. The mattress was thirty years old, stuffed with horsehair that had settled into two deep hollows: one where he slept, and the other, slightly shallower, where Mary had slept until the winter of two thousand and fourteen.

He reached out his right hand into the cold space beside him.

His knuckles touched the rough weave of the empty blanket. Even after eight years, his body still kept the boundary, never rolling into the center of the bed, leaving Mary's side undisturbed as if she might walk through the bedroom door with a cup of hot water and a slice of toast.

*You're an old fool, Arthur Cross,* he thought.

He remembered her hands in those final months. They had been so thin the skin seemed translucent, like tracing paper laid over blue ink veins. When the spasms took her chest, she would grip his fingers with a sudden, desperate strength that bruised his knuckles.

*Don't let them take me to the infirmary, Artie,* she had whispered in the middle of a freezing December night. *I want to die in my own bed. I want to hear the gulls on the roof.*

He had kept her at home as long as he could. He had carried her from the bed to the armchair by the window every morning, supporting her weight under her armpits, her slippers dragging along the linoleum. When she needed the toilet, he had carried her down the narrow corridor, his own knees screaming with pain, praying with every step that his legs would not buckle beneath them both.

One afternoon in January, the district nurse had come up the stairs, breathless and red-faced from the climb.

*Mr. Cross, this is impossible,* the nurse had said, setting her medical bag on the kitchen table. *You are sixty-one years old with severe degenerative arthritis. If you drop your wife on these stairs, you will break her neck and your own. She needs nursing care with elevator access and oxygen on the wall.*

*She wants to stay in her home,* Arthur had said, his voice flat and stubborn.

*This isn't a home anymore, Mr. Cross,* the nurse had replied, looking at the water stains on the ceiling and the gas fire sputtering in the grate. *It’s an obstacle course.*

Two weeks later, the ambulance had come.

Arthur rolled onto his side, pulling the blankets tighter over his ears.

Through the floorboards, the tenement gave a long, settling groan as the harbor tide reached the slack water mark. In the quiet between wind gusts, he could hear the faint, continuous hum of the climate-control air chiller on the roof of the Vanguard Marketing Pavilion across the basin.

A machine that kept the air warm and dry without a sound.

He closed his eyes and tried to sleep, but beneath his eyelids, the blue digital lines of Flat G-02 continued to glow on the dark glass of Marcus Ward's computer screen: level thresholds, walk-in shower, zero steps.

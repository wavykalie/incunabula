# Chapter 3: The Pickled Cucumbers

At ten o'clock, Danuta Kowalski tapped on Arthur’s door.

It was three light raps of her knuckles, spaced with deliberate precision, followed by a soft clearing of her throat on the landing. Arthur set his morning paper on the armchair, pushed himself up by the wooden armrests, and walked down the hall.

He pulled the Yale latch back.

Danuta stood on the stone floor, wearing a quilted navy housecoat over a thick woollen cardigan, her gray hair pinned into a tight bun at the nape of her neck. Her thick, wire-rimmed spectacles were fogged around the edges from the stair draft. In her hands she carried a two-pound glass pickle jar sealed with a rubber ring and a spring wire clasp, the brine cloudy with fresh dill seeds and sliced garlic cloves.

"Pan Cross," she said, nodding her head with that formal continental courtesy she had never abandoned. "I bring the dill cucumbers from the market. The salt is good this week. Not too sharp."

"You shouldn't have climbed the stairs with that weight, Danuta," Arthur said, taking the jar from her. The glass was cool and heavy in his palms. "Your hip was bothering you on Tuesday."

"The hip is old, Arthur. Like the building." She smiled, her eyes crinkling behind the thick lenses. "If I do not climb the stairs, who brings you the vegetables? You live on tinned soup and bread like a bachelor boy."

"Come in by the fire," he said, stepping aside.

"No, no. I have cabbage on the ring downstairs." She rested one hand on the red sandstone architrave of his doorframe, her fingers running over the rough, chisel-dressed surface. "The stone is sweating today. You see this? Water on the landing."

Arthur looked at the corner where the stair masonry met the external wall. A dark, damp stain ran from the ceiling cornice down to the floorboards, glistening with moisture.

"Sea fog," Arthur said. "Wind’s off the basin."

"In Gdynia, the brick does not sweat like this," Danuta murmured. She wiped her spectacles on the hem of her housecoat, her eyes drifting toward the stairwell window. "In Gdynia, when the winter came, the frost went deep into the mortar and stayed dry until April. Here, it is always water. In the walls, in the curtains, in the shoes. But it is strong. Forty years you are here, Arthur?"

"Forty-two in October," he said.

His mind slipped backward, smooth as a bus slipping into third gear on the quay road. October nineteen eighty-four. The dock strike was in its eighth month, and the whole port smelled of wet coke and defeat. He had taken Mary by the elbow at the corner of Albert Street, both of them drenched to the skin, carrying two cardboard suitcases and a zinc bucket full of kitchen cutlery. The rent had been seven pounds a week to the Harbour Trust. The flat had smelled of stale gas and damp horsehair plaster, but Mary had walked straight to the bay window, wiped the grime with her sleeve, and looked down at the tugboats turning in the basin.

*We can see the water, Artie,* she had said, her face wet with sea rain and excitement. *Look at the lights on the pier head. It's like having a palace over the river.*

They had slept that first night on two woollen coats laid over the bare pine floorboards, their backs aching, listening to the steam engines shunting coal wagons on the high level tracks.

"Mary planted the geraniums on the sill that first summer," Arthur said softly, his voice dropping into the quiet well.

Danuta nodded, her face softening. "She had the red ones. In the green ceramic pots. When I came in ninety-four, she was the first to speak to me on the stair. She brought me a bowl of hot broth with barley. I had no English then, only three words: *please*, *thank you*, and *railway station*. Mary sat on my stool and peeled two potatoes to show me how the gas cooker worked."

"She was good at that," Arthur said.

"She was a saint," Danuta said firmly, crossing herself with a quick, practiced movement of her right thumb. "A quiet saint. Not like these young ones today with their telephones and their shouting on the landing."

"Callum is worried about the rent," Arthur said.

Danuta’s face tightened, the lines around her mouth deepening into sharp creases. "Callum is young. He thinks every man in a motorcar wants to steal his bread. But this building belongs to the port. Queen Victoria was on the throne when they laid these foundations. Three feet of red stone. You cannot pull down three feet of stone with an Edinburgh fountain pen."

Arthur looked at the jar in his hands. The dill sprigs floated in the cloudy vinegar like submerged river weeds.

"There was a van down by the cellar gratings this morning," Arthur said.

"Boys with torches," she dismissed with a wave of her hand. "Looking for scrap lead. The scrap men are always stealing the flashings off the wash-house roof. If they come back, I tell Father Stanislaw at St. Mary’s. He speaks to the councillor."

She turned toward the stairs, taking hold of the cast-iron banister.

"Eat the cucumbers with boiled bacon, Arthur," she said over her shoulder. "It is good for the blood. The salt keeps the knees warm."

"Thank you, Danuta," he said.

He stood in the open doorway, watching her descent. She took the stairs slowly, one foot after the other onto each riser, her housecoat rustling against the stone wall. Step forty-eight, forty-seven, forty-six. Down on the second floor, little Maya gave a short, muffled laugh behind the Reids' door, followed by the soft hum of Callum’s radio playing the morning news.

Arthur stepped back into Flat 2B. He carried the heavy glass jar to the kitchen table, setting it down beside Mary’s empty copper kettle. He reached out with one finger and touched the worn red sandstone of the inner bay window pier, tracing the rough tool marks left by a stonemason's chisel a hundred and twenty years ago. The stone was cold as river ice.

Danuta set her mug down on the wooden dresser with a small, deliberate click. She looked around the small kitchen, her eyes moving from the framed black-and-white photograph of Mary on the wall to the brass clock ticking on the shelf above the gas cooker.

"Mary had the recipe for the plum cake," Danuta said, her voice dropping into a quiet, rhythmic hum. "In ninety-eight, when my Tadeusz was in the infirmary with the pleurisy, she made the plum cake with the cinnamon crumble on top. She brought three slices down on a china plate with blue flowers. She said to me: *Danuta, a man with water in his lungs needs sugar and cinnamon. If he does not eat, the spirit leaves the bones.*"

"She always believed in cinnamon," Arthur said, a faint smile touching his mouth. "Used to put it in the porridge on winter mornings. Said it kept the blood moving when the frost was on the inside of the sash panes."

"It is true," Danuta said. "In the Baltic, in winter, we drank hot tea with cherry vodka and cloves. When the shipyard gates opened at six, three thousand men walked across the ice on the basin. You could hear their boots five miles away—scrunch, scrunch, scrunch on the hard snow. If you did not have hot tea inside your belly, your fingers would stick to the steel plates. You would leave your skin on the crane hook."

She leaned forward, resting both forearms on the oilcloth.

"This developer, Arthur. This Mr. Ward. Callum showed me the brochure on the landing. It has pictures of people in fine clothes sitting in the sun. But where are the coal cellars? Where does an old woman hang her washing in November? You cannot hang damp wool sheets inside a room with carpet and glass. The wool will rot."

"They have electric drying machines, Danuta," Arthur said mildly. "Big tumble dryers in the scullery."

"Electric machines ruin the linen," she snapped, her chin lifting with stubborn pride. "A sheet needs the salt wind from the river. The wind blows the soot out of the cotton and makes it smell of the tide. Forty years I have hung my washing on the drying green behind the wash-house. Every Tuesday morning, rain or frost. If you take away the drying green, you take away the week. You lose the day."

Arthur looked at her hands. The knuckles were thickened and crooked, shaped by half a century of cold water, carbolic soap, and the heavy iron wheel of her sewing machine.

"Callum says the court will protect us," Arthur said.

"The court belongs to the people who own the paper, Arthur," she murmured, her voice softening into a weary whisper. "In Poland, the court had big wooden doors and policemen with gray coats. When they closed the parish school in our village, the priest took the church books to the magistrate. The magistrate sat behind a desk of green leather and said: *The state has determined that the building is required for the tractor collective.* That was all. Two sentences on stamped paper, and the tractor collective had our garden."

She stood up slowly, her knees making a dry, cracking sound that matched Arthur’s own.

"We must pray to Our Lady of Czestochowa, Arthur. She protected the monastery against the Swedes when the walls were burning. She can protect three feet of Scottish sandstone against an Edinburgh company."

Arthur did not answer. He watched her walk to the hallway door, her feet moving with that slow, rolling gait that came from twenty-four years of carrying heavy loads up and down the worn red stairs.

# Chapter 11: The Private Terms

Marcus Ward reached into the breast pocket of his charcoal overcoat and produced a slim black leather folder. He unzipped it and drew out three sheets of heavy bond paper, neatly stapled at the top left corner with a stainless steel clip.

"This is the preliminary relocation protocol, Arthur," Ward said, sliding the document across the table. "Everything we discussed is set down here in plain language. No legal obfuscation. Paragraph four states the guaranteed sub-market lifetime tenancy for Flat G-02. Paragraph six states the relocation disturbance grant: five thousand pounds paid into your account upon signing, to cover the cost of professional packers and new soft furnishings."

Arthur did not pick up the paper. He let his thumb rest on the horn handle of his walking stick.

His father’s face rose in his memory, sharp as a line drawing on white paper. Nineteen seventy-four. The national dock strike. Arthur had been twenty-five, driving his first municipal route on the single-decker cross-town service. His father, Donald Cross, had been a crane driver on the Albert Quay coal hoists for thirty years, his lungs whistling with anthracite dust every time he took a deep breath.

The harbor company had called the senior crane drivers into the dockmaster’s office on a wet Tuesday in November.

*Take early severance, Donald,* the manager had told him, pushing an envelope across the mahogany desk. *Two thousand pounds cash in a building society draft. You sign the voluntary retirement slip, and you let the younger lads take the redundancy terms.*

Donald Cross had looked at the envelope, then looked the manager in the eye.

*If I take that money, Gordon,* his father had said, his voice quiet enough to be heard over the coal shunts outside, *what do I say to young Miller on the next hoist when the gates are chained shut on Monday morning? Do I tell him I had forty years on the dock, so it doesn't matter if his bairns starve?*

Donald Cross had walked out of the office, left the draft on the desk, and stood on the picket line until his boots rotted through the soles. When the harbor trust automated the coal hoists two years later, they gave him statutory redundancy: four hundred and ten pounds and a bronze lapel badge that turned green in the scullery drawer.

"Arthur?" Ward's voice was soft, inquiring.

Arthur looked up. Ward was watching him with that patient, therapeutic gaze that seemed trained to anticipate every objection before it was spoken.

"What happens to the rest of the close, Mr. Ward?" Arthur asked.

"The other tenants will be treated in strict accordance with the Housing Scotland Act," Ward replied evenly. "They have been served statutory Section 33 notices. We are offering three thousand pounds per household for voluntary surrender before June first. For those who require council rehousing, our community liaison officer is working directly with the city housing department to prioritize their applications on the choice-based letting system."

"You know what the council waiting list is like in this city," Arthur said. "Callum Reid has a three-year-old girl. Danuta Kowalski has been in Flat 1A for twenty-four years. She has no family in Britain."

"Mrs. Kowalski is seventy-one," Ward said, nodding sympathetically. "We are already in discussions with a specialist housing association in Clydebank that operates sheltered accommodation with on-site Polish-speaking care staff. It would be a significant upgrade in her standard of living. She wouldn't have to carry her washing down forty-eight stone steps in freezing weather."

"Danuta doesn't want to go to Clydebank," Arthur said. "She wants to stay in Albert Dock."

"Albert Dock is becoming a commercial deep-water port and a residential marina, Arthur," Ward said. He stood up, walked to the tall window, and looked out across the gray water toward the shipping channel. "The coal sidings are gone. The flour mill closed five years ago. The maritime trust cannot maintain Victorian tenements with leaking lead valleys and crumbling sandstone chimneys. Within ten years, every square foot of this waterfront will be renewed. That isn't cruelty; it is the natural life cycle of a port city."

Ward turned back toward the room, his silhouette sharp against the gray harbor light.

"You have lived sixty-seven years, Arthur. You drove the city buses through thirty-five winters. You looked after your wife when she was dying on those stairs. You have paid your debt to this city three times over. Why should you spend the last ten years of your life trapped in a cold attic because a twenty-eight-year-old container worker wants to fight a political battle he cannot win?"

The words struck Arthur like a physical blow, heavy and precise.

He looked down at the paper on the table. *Paragraph four: Lifetime Protected Occupancy. Paragraph six: Disturbance Grant £5,000.*

The silver wall clock behind the reception desk clicked, its thin second hand sweeping smoothly through the quiet air of the pavilion. Ten o'clock. Outside, the rain was beating against the glass with a steady, rhythmic hiss, but inside the room, not a single drop of cold air stirred.

Arthur stared at the black fountain-pen ink on paragraph four: *The Landlord grants to the Tenant an irrevocable, lifetime protected tenancy of the residential subjects known as Flat G-02, The Meridian, at a fixed monthly rental of £380.00...*

"What if Vanguard sells the building?" Arthur asked, his voice low.

"The covenant is registered in the Land Register of Scotland against the superior title of the land," Ward replied immediately. "It runs with the property in perpetuity. If Vanguard sells the freehold to a pension fund or a residential trust, any successor in title is bound by the identical terms. You cannot be evicted under Section 33. You cannot be subject to discretionary rent reviews. You have absolute security of tenure until the day you die."

Arthur looked through the glass window of the pavilion.

Across the water, the red sandstone walls of Baltic Buildings looked dark and tired under the heavy rain. He thought of his kitchen: the brown linoleum with the worn trail between the sink and cooker; Mary's copper kettle with the dent in the belly where he had dropped it on the scullery stones in nineteen ninety-one; the narrow pantry where Mary kept her jars of homemade marrow ginger jam on newspaper-lined shelves.

Forty years of life did not fit into a four-page legal covenant.

"And if I refuse?" Arthur asked.

Ward’s expression did not change. His gray-blue eyes remained calm, polite, and completely steady.

"If you refuse, Arthur, Vanguard will proceed with the statutory eviction process under Section 33. It will take ten to twelve months. The sheriff court will grant the warrant because Vanguard holds approved outline planning permission and full demolition consent from the Scottish Ministers. When the decree of ejection is executed by the sheriff officers, you will be evicted from Baltic Buildings along with every other resident."

Ward paused, letting the silence hang in the warm room.

"You will receive the statutory disturbance grant of three thousand pounds. Nothing more. You will be entered onto the municipal homelessness register. The council will offer you emergency temporary accommodation in a hostel or an outlying housing estate. At sixty-nine years old, with your medical condition, you will be competing for a sheltered ground-floor unit against four thousand other applicants."

Ward leaned forward, his voice dropping into a gentle, urgent whisper.

"Do not let Callum Reid make you a martyr for a cause that was lost five years ago, Arthur. When the grain elevator came down, the fate of Albert Dock was sealed. Capital will rebuild this waterfront. You cannot stop the tide; you can only choose whether you drown on the rocks or step into a warm boat."

Arthur looked down at his driving gloves resting on his knee. The worn black leather was cracked at the thumb seams, stained with diesel oil from forty years of turning heavy steering wheels.

"I need to think," Arthur said.

"Of course," Ward said, standing up. "Take the weekend. Consider your health. Consider the winters ahead. A car will be at your door at nine on Monday morning. If you choose to sign, Flat G-02 is yours."

Arthur rose from his chair, took his stick, and walked toward the revolving door without looking back.

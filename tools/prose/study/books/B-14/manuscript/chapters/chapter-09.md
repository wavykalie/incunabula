# Chapter 9: The Marketing Pavilion

The black saloon car glided to a halt at the head of Albert Quay pier. The chauffeur, a silent young man in a dark charcoal suit, stepped out into the raw sea drizzle and opened the passenger door. Arthur Cross pushed his walking stick onto the wet asphalt, gripped the chrome handle of the door, and levered himself out into the wind.

His knees burning from the cold, he looked up at the Vanguard Marketing Pavilion.

The pavilion was built of dark steel beams and plate glass, resting on the concrete apron where the old grain warehouse had stood until nineteen ninety-two. Warm amber light spilled through thirty feet of floor-to-ceiling double-glazed windows, washing over a polished gray concrete terrace. Above the brushed steel entrance door, discreet stainless lettering read: *THE MERIDIAN · SALES & EXHIBITION SUITE*.

Arthur pushed through the revolving glass door.

The transition was instantaneous. The freezing salt wind and the roar of the river diesel tugs were cut off with the sound of a soft pneumatic seal. The air inside was warm—seventy degrees by the digital thermometer on the wall—smelling of freshly roasted ground espresso, wool pile carpet, and the dry, sweet ozone of climate-controlled electronics.

"Mr. Cross," a voice said smoothly from across the hall.

Marcus Ward stepped from behind a curved desk of white composite stone. He was thirty-nine, tall, slender, wearing a tailored charcoal cashmere overcoat over an open-collared slate-blue shirt. His hair was trimmed close, his skin clean-shaven, his left pinky finger carrying a heavy gold signet ring that caught the recessed ceiling spotlights. He did not extend his hand immediately; he stepped forward with an unhurried, measured stride and offered a welcoming smile.

"I’m Marcus Ward. Thank you for making the journey across the quay. Please, let me take your coat."

"It's damp from the pier," Arthur said, his voice feeling rough and heavy in the warm room.

"Wool dries quickly in here," Ward said. He took the heavy tweed overcoat by the shoulders with practiced ease and hung it on a concealed walnut rail behind the reception desk. "We keep the humidity at forty-five percent. Perfect for the architectural timbers. May I offer you some coffee? We have an Ethiopian roast from the roastery on Commerce Street, or fresh tea if you prefer."

"Tea, if you have it," Arthur said. "Black. No sugar."

"Of course." Ward touched an intercom button on the desk, murmured two words, then gestured toward the center of the exhibition hall. "Come through, Mr. Cross. Take your time. No hurry at all."

In the center of the hall stood the architectural scale model of The Meridian.

It was eight feet long, mounted on a plinth of dark volcanic slate and encased in a frameless acrylic cube. The tower rose from the slate base: twenty-eight storeys of bronze-tinted glass, horizontal zinc brise-soleil panels, and pale composite stone columns that caught the internal miniature fiber-optic lighting. Tiny scale trees made of twisted wire and green foam stood in a paved courtyard at the base. At the rear, where the muddy cobblestones of Albert Lane now ran, the model showed a level granite esplanade with outdoor seating and water fountains.

Arthur leaned his hands on his walking stick, staring through the acrylic cover.

"Twenty-eight storeys," Arthur murmured.

"Eighty-four meters to the roof parapet," Ward said, standing beside him with his hands folded behind his back. He rotated the gold signet ring on his left pinky three slow turns. "Seventy percent lower carbon footprint than traditional Victorian masonry. Triple-glazed acoustic envelopes throughout. When the port shunting engines run at three in the morning, the ambient sound level in these apartments is under twenty-four decibels. You could hear a pin drop on the rug."

"Baltic Buildings sits right there," Arthur said, pointing his finger at the paved courtyard.

"It does," Ward said gently. His voice was soft, reasonable, carrying the calm cadence of an architect explaining a roof elevation. "It has served its purpose for a hundred and twenty years, Mr. Cross. But red sandstone in a marine environment has an engineering shelf-life. The salt penetrates the lime mortar, the iron wall ties corrode, and the damp works its way into the timber flues. You know better than anyone how cold those stairs are in January."

Arthur looked at his knuckles on the handle of his stick.

A young woman in a black skirt brought a bone-china cup on a silver saucer and set it on a glass side table. The tea was pale amber, steaming with the clean scent of Ceylon leaves.

"Sit down, Arthur," Ward said, gesturing to a low armchair upholstered in gray woven wool. "Let me show you where we see your future."

Arthur lowered himself into the chair. The cushions were firm, supporting his lower back with an ease he had not felt since the orthopaedic clinics at the Western Infirmary. Ward pulled a second chair opposite him, picked up an iPad with a brushed titanium case, and tapped the glass screen.

Arthur walked around the plinth of the acrylic model, his hand resting on the polished volcanic slate. Through the clear casing, the miniature world of The Meridian seemed completely detached from the wet, salty air outside. Tiny streetlamps lit the landscaped walkways with cool white LED beams; tiny bronze glass balconies hung over an esplanade paved with gray granite setts.

"Notice the landscaping, Arthur," Ward said, following him with slow, measured steps. "We’re planting mature silver birch and Scots pine in the central courtyard. Tree stock brought down from a specialist nursery in Perthshire. The root balls are suspended over the underground parking structure in engineered aerated soil."

"No coal dust on those leaves," Arthur murmured.

"No coal dust anywhere," Ward agreed, smiling faintly. "The municipal coal hoists were dismantled twenty years ago. The port authority’s deep-water strategy is container freight and renewable marine engineering. The old heavy cargo berths are being converted into commercial leisure moorings. Within five years, this basin will look like the redeveloped docks in Copenhagen or Hamburg."

Arthur looked at the corner of the model where Baltic Buildings should have stood. In its place was a two-storey glass pavilion with an outdoor timber deck: *The Meridian Residents’ Health Club & Hydrotherapy Suite*.

"Where does the No. 16 bus turn around?" Arthur asked.

Ward blinked, caught off guard for an instant, before his polished corporate calm reasserted itself. "The municipal transit loop is being relocated three hundred yards east, to the new transport interchange beside the retail park. Electric hybrid shuttles every ten minutes into the city center. Low-floor boarding, zero emissions."

Arthur tapped the acrylic case with his index finger. "On a wet Tuesday in November, three hundred yards in an easterly gale is half a mile to an old woman with a shopping trolley."

"That is why Flat G-02 has an internal direct-dial concierge service," Ward replied smoothly. "If our residents need groceries brought from the retail park, the building duty team handles the transfer directly to their door. You wouldn't have to carry a pint of milk into the wind ever again."

Ward walked to the reception counter, picked up two brochures bound in dark slate-gray linen, and handed one to Arthur. The brochure was embossed with silver foil lettering: *THE MERIDIAN · TECHNICAL SPECIFICATIONS & RESIDENTIAL COVENANTS*.

"Take your time, Arthur," Ward said, his voice dropping into that calm, reassuring register. "Have a look at the floor plate for the ground level."

Arthur opened the linen cover. Inside, printed on thick cream paper, were high-resolution elevations showing the structural cross-section of the building: two feet of solid reinforced concrete foundations, acoustic rubber isolation mats separating the floor slabs, triple-pane argon-filled glazing units, and geothermal heat exchangers drilled two hundred meters into the bedrock beneath the harbor basin.

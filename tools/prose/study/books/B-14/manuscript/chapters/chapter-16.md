# Chapter 16: The Signed Memorandum

At half past nine on Monday morning, Arthur Cross walked into the Vanguard Marketing Pavilion on Albert Quay.

The revolving glass door swept him into the warm, climate-controlled silence of the exhibition hall. The chauffeur who had collected him from Baltic Buildings took his walking stick and set it against the reception desk.

Marcus Ward was standing by the large architectural model, reviewing a stack of bound planning documents with a junior surveyor. When he saw Arthur, he dismissed the young man with a brief nod and walked across the gray wool carpet with a welcoming smile.

"Mr. Cross. Punctual to the minute. A bus driver's habit, I imagine."

"A lifetime habit, Mr. Ward," Arthur said.

"Come into the private office," Ward said, gesturing toward a walnut-paneled door at the back of the pavilion. "It's quieter."

The office was furnished with a dark oak meeting table, two leather cantilever chairs, and a floor-to-ceiling picture window looking out across the harbor basin toward Baltic Buildings. On the table lay three copies of the relocation agreement, neatly bound in blue presentation card, accompanied by a heavy brass desk pen and a small velvet box.

Ward pulled out a chair for Arthur, then sat opposite him.

"You've had the weekend to consider our discussion, Arthur," Ward said, his tone gentle, businesslike, devoid of pressure. "Have you reached a decision?"

Arthur looked through the plate glass window. Across the water, he could see the red sandstone chimney pots of Baltic Buildings standing against the gray winter clouds. Somewhere on the second floor, Callum Reid was probably pacing his kitchen, waiting for the clock to strike twelve so he could take his signed tenant charter to the sheriff clerk’s office.

Arthur reached inside his tweed overcoat, drew out his nickel reading spectacles, and hooked the wire temples behind his ears.

"Show me paragraph four again," Arthur said.

Ward smiled, turned the blue folder toward Arthur, and pointed to the text with the tip of his gold fountain pen.

*Paragraph 4: The Landlord grants to the Tenant an irrevocable, lifetime protected tenancy of the residential subjects known as Flat G-02, The Meridian, at a fixed monthly rental of £380.00, inclusive of all building insurance, communal service charges, and hydronic heating loop maintenance.*

"And paragraph six," Arthur murmured.

*Paragraph 6: The Landlord shall pay to the Tenant upon execution of this Agreement the non-refundable sum of £5,000.00 (Five Thousand Pounds Sterling) as a comprehensive relocation disturbance allowance.*

Arthur read both paragraphs twice. His hands were steady. His breathing was slow, measured, even.

"If I sign this," Arthur said, looking Ward directly in the eyes, "when do I get access to Flat G-02?"

"The ground floor of The Meridian will be completed and fitted out by September fifteenth," Ward replied. "Two weeks before the statutory decant deadline for Baltic Buildings. Our professional removals team will pack your entire home, transport your furniture across the quay, and unpack every item in your new apartment under your direct supervision. You will not have to lift a single cardboard box."

Ward opened the small velvet box on the desk.

Inside lay a heavy, credit-card-sized rectangle of frosted white plastic, embedded with a microchip and stamped with the Vanguard corporate emblem. Beside it sat a heavy brass cylinder key with three precision dimples cut into the shaft.

"This is the electronic access keycard for Flat G-02," Ward said softly. "It is registered in your name on the central security server from this morning."

Arthur looked at the smooth, weightless piece of plastic. It looked nothing like the worn brass Yale key in his waistcoat pocket—the key that had opened Flat 2B ten thousand times, the key Mary had held in her hands when they moved in on that wet October morning in nineteen eighty-four.

He took the heavy brass pen from Ward’s desk.

The ink was black, smooth, flowing across the heavy bond paper without a single blot. On the line marked *Tenant / Authorized Signatory*, Arthur Cross wrote his name: *Arthur Cross*, his hand firm, the signature identical to the one on thirty-five years of municipal bus depot duty sheets.

He signed all three copies.

Ward took the pen, counter-signed with a swift, elegant flourish, and stamped each document with the corporate seal of Vanguard Urban Regeneration Ltd.

"Welcome to The Meridian, Mr. Cross," Ward said, extending his hand across the desk.

Arthur took Ward’s hand. The developer’s grip was warm, dry, and firm.

Arthur picked up the white plastic keycard from the velvet box. It felt cold against his palm, light as a dry leaf. He slipped it into his inner overcoat pocket, took his walking stick, and stood up from the leather chair.

Arthur walked through the central exhibition hall of the pavilion toward the exit.

Before he reached the door, Marcus Ward led him through a set of double walnut doors into the full-scale mock-up of Flat G-02. The showroom was laid out exactly as the brochure promised: wide-plank pale oak flooring underfoot, pristine white walls, recessed ceiling LED tracks casting warm amber light across contemporary Scandinavian furniture.

Arthur stood in the center of the living room.

He looked down at his boots. His worn leather work boots, stained with dock grease and salt water, looked grotesque against the pale oak floorboards. Yet as he stood there, he felt a strange, steady warmth rising through the soles of his boots. The underfloor heating was running. The warmth seeped through his damp woolen socks, through the leather soles, directly into the stiff joints of his ankles.

He took three steps forward. No creak. No groaning floorboards. No vibration.

He walked into the bathroom. The walk-in shower was six feet wide, paved with textured gray porcelain tiles running level into a stainless steel channel drain. On the wall hung a brushed chrome mixer valve with large, smooth lever handles. No steps. No four-inch lip to hurdle with stiff knees.

"Everything will be ready on September fifteenth, Arthur," Ward said from the doorway, his hands folded in his overcoat pockets. "You will hand over the key to Flat 2B in the morning, and by dinner time you will be sitting in your armchair here, watching the harbor through triple-glazed silence."

Arthur touched the cold chrome of the shower rail.

"September fifteenth," Arthur said.

"September fifteenth," Ward confirmed with a warm nod.

Arthur turned and walked back into the reception foyer. The driver held the heavy glass door open for him. Arthur stepped out onto the wet concrete terrace of Albert Quay, the cold harbor wind hitting his face with the bitter smell of coal smoke, diesel, and river mud.

He put his hand into his waistcoat pocket. His fingers closed around the smooth, weightless rectangle of the plastic keycard, cold and hard against the nickel casing of his pocket watch.

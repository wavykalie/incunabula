# Chapter 38: The Maritime Wing

The journey took one hour and forty-four minutes.

Arthur Cross took the thirty-six bus from the quay into Greenock central, standing at the bus stop in his heavy wool overcoat with his hands in his pockets. The second bus was the nine, an Alexander Dennis Enviro200 that rattled along the coastal trunk road past the container terminals, the sugar silos, and the vast empty expanses where the fitting-out berths had been cleared for warehousing. He sat in the disabled seating area directly behind the driver, watching his reflection in the perspex partition.

The Waverley Court Care Annex sat three miles inland from the water, behind a retail park with a drive-through coffee outlet and a DIY superstore.

It was a single-story building constructed in nineteen-ninety-two, finished in roughcast pebble-dash painted pale peach, with brown concrete interlocking roof tiles and white UPVC double-glazed windows protected by external trickle vents. A low wire fence separated the tarmac parking bays from a square lawn of manicured rye grass bordered by bark mulch and miniature conifer shrubs.

Arthur walked through the automatic sliding glass doors into the reception area.

The air smelled intensely of industrial citrus floor cleaner, stewed tea, and laundry steam. A young woman in a purple tunic with an identification badge pinned to her pocket sat behind a curved laminated counter.

"Visiting Mrs. Kowalski," Arthur said, taking off his tweed cap. "Arthur Cross. From Albert Quay."

The girl checked a touchscreen monitor with her index finger.

"Danuta," she said. "Room twelve. Maritime Wing. Down the blue corridor, Arthur. Second lounge on your left. She’s watching the afternoon concert."

"Thank you," Arthur said.

The blue corridor was wide, fitted with continuous tubular plastic handrails in contrasting turquoise. The floor was covered in pale blue linoleum with heat-welded seams, buffed to a high gloss that squeaked beneath the wet rubber soles of Arthur’s shoes. Along the walls were framed prints of historical Scottish lighthouses—the Bell Rock, the Mull of Kintyre, Pladda—each labeled in twenty-four-point type for residents with cognitive deterioration.

Arthur found the second lounge.

It was a large rectangular room with sixteen vinyl-upholstered armchairs arranged in a wide horseshoe facing an eighty-inch flat-screen television mounted high on the wall. The television was broadcasting a recorded performance of a brass band playing marches from the nineteen-forties, the sound turned up loud enough to rattle the plastic ceiling vents.

Seven elderly people sat in the armchairs. Three were asleep, their chins resting on their chests. A woman in a pink cardigan was tearing a paper napkin into long, uniform strips with methodical precision.

In the fourth armchair from the window sat Danuta Kowalski.

She was wearing a grey jersey skirt, a clean white blouse with a Peter Pan collar, and a navy-blue cardigan with plastic pearl buttons that Arthur had never seen before. Her white hair was washed, dried, and curled into soft, tight waves that sat close to her scalp—the work of an institutional hairdresser who did sixteen heads every Friday morning. On her feet were light blue slippers with velcro fastening straps.

Arthur walked across the linoleum.

His knees carried him smoothly, without heat, but his breath was shallow in his chest.

"Danuta," he said.

Danuta turned her head. Her pale blue eyes moved across his overcoat, his woolen scarf, and settled on his cap. Her expression did not change. There was no start of recognition, no widening of the eyes, no sudden smile.

"Mr. Cross," she said.

Her voice was clean, precise, carrying a high, formal cadence that sounded as though she were addressing an official from the council housing department or an inspector from the sanitary board.

"Afternoon, Danuta," Arthur said. He indicated the empty green vinyl armchair beside her. "May I?"

"Please," she said. "The chair is not reserved. Mrs. MacPherson had her lunch in bed today. Her chest is weak."

Arthur sat down. The vinyl of the armchair was cold through his overcoat, slick and unyielding, smelling of chlorinated disinfectant.

He looked at her hands. They lay folded in her lap on top of a folded linen handkerchief. Her fingernails were trimmed short, without the rough, dry cuticles she had carried for forty years from scrubbing the sandstone stairs with soda crystals.

"I brought you some tablet," Arthur said. He reached into his coat pocket and brought out a small white paper bag from the baker's shop in Cathcart Street. "Butter tablet. The girl in the shop said it was made yesterday morning."

Danuta looked at the bag. She did not reach out to take it.

"That is very kind of you, Mr. Cross," she said. "You should leave it on the side table. The nurses like all food items to be labeled with the room number before we take them to the room. In case of dietary restrictions."

Arthur set the bag on the low melamine table between their chairs. The paper bag sat beside a laminated plastic water jug with a blue measurement scale printed up the side: *100ml, 200ml, 300ml*.

"How are they treating you?" Arthur asked.

"The facility is very modern," Danuta said. She looked back toward the television screen where the brass band was playing *The Floral Dance*. "The heating is central. The radiators are warm day and night. We have three hot meals a day served in the dining room. At twelve o'clock we had braised beef with boiled potatoes and diced carrots. Very soft. You do not need strong teeth."

"Is the bed comfortable?"

"The bed has an electric motor," she said, her voice entirely flat. "You press a button on a cord and the head rises up so you can read. There is an emergency cord beside the pillow with a red plastic handle. If you pull it, a buzzer sounds in the nurses' office and a girl comes within three minutes. They are very punctual."

Arthur looked at her profile. Her skin was paler than it had been on Albert Quay, almost translucent under the fluorescent tubes of the ceiling. The small brown liver spots on her temples stood out like freckles on a child's forehead.

"They took the chimneys down on Tuesday, Danuta," Arthur said.

Danuta did not turn her head. Her eyes remained fixed on the television, where a man with a trombone was bowing to a silent auditorium.

"Did they?" she said.

"The Komatsu excavator with the long boom," Arthur continued. His words felt clumsy, heavy, like trying to pass large coal through a narrow riddle. "The operator took the south stack first. Flat 2B. My kitchen was exposed to the street for an hour before the floors collapsed. The yellow wallpaper was still on the laths. The paper Mary and I put up in eighty-four."

Danuta blinked once. A slow, deliberate lowering of her thin, wrinkled lids.

"The wallpaper was very nice," she said. "Mary had good taste in patterns. She liked flowers."

"Danuta," Arthur said, leaning his body toward her armchair. "It's Arthur."

Danuta turned her face toward him then.

Her pale blue eyes met his. She regarded him without hatred or anger, her gaze holding an immense distance, the cold neutrality of someone who had survived the burning of one world in nineteen-forty-four, and the quiet demolition of a second world in twenty-twenty-one, and had now withdrawn into the final, unassailable fortress of absolute politeness.

"I know who you are, Mr. Cross," she said softly. "You are my neighbor from Flat 2B. We lived on the close for forty years."

"We were more than neighbors, Danuta. Stefan died in my arms on your kitchen floor."

Danuta looked down at her folded hands. Her thumbs remained still, locked over the white linen handkerchief.

"Stefan is dead thirty-eight years, Arthur," she said. Her voice dropped to a whisper that barely carried under the brass band music. "The kitchen floor is not there anymore. It is a heap of red rubble in a contractor’s lorry going to the landfill at Bishopton. The building is gone. The close is gone. You live in your new building with your warm floors, and I live here with my electric bed and my diced carrots."

She looked up at him again. Her expression was calm, devastatingly serene, the dignity of an empress sitting in an asylum day room.

"We are not neighbors now, Arthur," she said. "There is no close to connect our doors. You have your life, and I have room twelve."

Arthur looked at her lap.

He wanted to reach out. He wanted to take her small, cold hand, to feel the swollen knuckles that had scrubbed the four flights of stairs every Monday morning, to tell her that he was lonely in Flat G-02, that the silence in The Meridian was suffocating him, that he would give up the warm screed and the wet room if he could have one hour of the freezing wind blowing through the lath-and-plaster of Flat 2B with her radio playing in the background.

The words died in his throat.

To speak them would be an obscenity. It would be an old man asking a dying woman to comfort him for the crime of having chosen his own comfort over her presence.

Danuta looked back toward the television.

"They have tea at three-thirty," she said politely. "With digestive biscuits. If you wait another ten minutes, the nurse will bring an extra cup for the visitor."

"I have to catch the nine bus back to Greenock," Arthur said.

He stood up from the green vinyl armchair.

The plastic upholstery made a dry, sucking release as his weight lifted. His right knee did not pain him; he rose with effortless balance, his feet firm on the polished blue linoleum.

Danuta looked at him one last time.

"Travel safe, Mr. Cross," she said. "Mind the step on the bus platform. The rain makes the ribbed rubber slippery."

"Goodbye, Danuta."

Arthur stood beside the armchair for ten seconds. The brass band on the television finished *The Floral Dance* with a loud, triumphant clash of cymbals. The three sleeping residents did not stir. The woman in the pink cardigan began tearing her fourth paper napkin into strips.

Arthur placed his hand on the plastic armrest, finding nothing left to say.

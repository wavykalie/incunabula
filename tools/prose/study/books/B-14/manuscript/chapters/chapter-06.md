# Chapter 6: The Meeting on the Landing

At eight o’clock that evening, Callum banged on Arthur’s door.

He did not tap like Danuta. He hammered three heavy blows with his closed fist that set the brass Yale latch vibrating in its strike plate. Arthur was already standing in the hallway, his overcoat buttoned to his chin, his woollen scarf around his neck.

He pulled the latch back.

Callum stood on the stone landing, his breathing rapid, his eyes bloodshot from the cold and tobacco smoke. Beside him stood Danuta, clutching her quilted housecoat around her throat, her spectacles pushed back onto her forehead. Behind them on the second-floor stair stood young Mrs. MacIntyre from Flat 3A, holding her nursing cape wrapped tightly around her shoulders.

"We’re having a meeting," Callum said, his voice flat, challenging. "Right here. The close."

"It’s freezing out here, Callum," Arthur said mildly.

"Good. Keep our minds sharp." Callum turned to face the stairs, resting one foot on the stone riser, looking like a dockland shop steward addressing a shift on the quayside. "Every single flat got one. Twelve notices. Hand-delivered by registered post. October first we have to be out."

"Mr. Ward from Vanguard," Danuta said, her accent thick with distress. "The name on the paper is Marcus Ward. Director."

"I don't care if his name is the Prince of Wales," Callum cut in. "The law says they can’t just evict a whole close on a Section 33 without alternative accommodation. My union branch has a solicitor. I spoke to him at six o’clock. He says if we form a registered tenant committee—all twelve flats, undivided—we can hold them up in the sheriff court for eighteen months."

Mrs. MacIntyre shivered, pulling her nursing cape tighter. "My sister works at the council housing office, Callum. She says Vanguard has planning permission for the whole basin. It was signed off by the Scottish Ministers last month. If the ministers approve it, the sheriff court can’t stop the demolition warrant."

"They can’t demolish it with us inside it, can they?" Callum snapped. "We refuse to sign the voluntary decant papers. Every single one of us. Nobody takes a cash settlement. Nobody talks to Vanguard on their own."

He turned his fierce, dark gaze straight onto Arthur.

"You’re the oldest tenant in the building, Arthur. Forty years. The council knows you. The local paper knows you from when you retired from the buses. If you lead the committee, the press will take notice."

Arthur looked at the worn stone between his boots. He felt the cold rising through his leather soles, settling straight into his ankles.

"What did the union lawyer say about the legal costs, Callum?" Arthur asked quietly.

"We get legal aid," Callum said, his voice rising defensively. "Or the union hardship fund helps out."

"And if we lose?" Arthur asked. "In twelve months, when the sheriff officers arrive with the eviction warrants? What does Vanguard offer then?"

"They offer nothing then!" Callum shouted, his face reddening. "That’s the point of a fight, Arthur! You fight for your home! Or do you just hand them your keys and let them bulldoze Mary’s kitchen?"

Danuta gasped, reaching out to touch Arthur's sleeve. "Callum, no. Do not speak of Mary."

Arthur did not look up. His voice remained low, measured, unhurried, the voice of a man who had sat through forty years of union branch meetings and knew how they ended. "Mary’s kitchen is four walls and a leaky sash window, Callum. We have damp in the flues that hasn’t dried out since nineteen ninety-two. The gas pipes are iron and half-choked with scale."

"It's a home!" Callum slammed his fist against the cast-iron banister, the iron rail ringing through the spiral well. "It’s Sophie’s home! It’s Maya’s home! Where am I supposed to put my daughter? In a shared hostel with junkies on the Paisley Road?"

"Nobody is saying that," Arthur said.

"Then you sign the tenant committee charter," Callum said, thrusting a crumpled notebook page and a blue biro into Arthur's face. "Right now. On the landing. You sign as chairman of the Baltic Buildings Action Committee."

Arthur looked at the paper. In clumsy, angry capital letters, Callum had written: *WE THE UNDERSIGNED RESIDENTS OF BALTIC BUILDINGS REFUSE ALL VOLUNTARY RELOCATION AND DEMAND PERMANENT TENANCY RIGHTS.*

Danuta was watching Arthur, her dark eyes filled with a desperate, pleading hope. Mrs. MacIntyre was watching him with tired resignation.

Arthur kept his hands in his overcoat pockets.

"Leave the paper with me, Callum," Arthur said softly. "Let me read the Vanguard notice properly first."

Callum’s jaw clenched. His hand dropped, the biro clicking against his knuckles. "You're going to fold, aren't you? You're an old man with no family left, and you're just going to let them buy you off."

"Goodnight, Callum," Arthur said.

He stepped back into Flat 2B. Before Callum could speak again, Arthur pulled the heavy timber door shut, letting the brass latch drop into place with a sharp, definitive click.

Mrs. MacIntyre pulled her blue nurse's cardigan tight around her throat, looking down the spiral stairs toward the street door.

"My union rep at the Western Infirmary told us last week that the health board is selling off three nurses' homes in Partick," she said, her voice shaking with quiet anger. "They tell us we can rent in the private sector with a housing voucher. But no private landlord takes council vouchers when they can rent to overseas students from the university for a thousand pounds a room. If we lose Baltic Buildings, Callum, I'll have to move back to my mother's council house in Motherwell and commute two hours every morning on the train."

"We won't lose it," Callum said, his voice rising, echoing through the hollow core of the stairwell. "Not if we refuse to sign. The law says sitting tenants have the right of first refusal if the building is sold for residential conversion. The housing collective has a barrister in Edinburgh who specializes in landlord-tenant disputes. She’s won three appeals against private equity funds in Leith."

"Leith is not Albert Dock," Danuta murmured from the bottom step, adjusting her thick spectacles. "In Leith, they had artists and students and politicians with television cameras. Who comes to Albert Dock? Only the coal lorries and the scrap vans."

"Then we make them come!" Callum shouted, striking his palm against the cast-iron banister until the metal rang like an alarm bell. "We bring the television cameras here! We put a banner across the second-floor windows: *SAVE BALTIC BUILDINGS · FORTY YEARS OF WORKING FAMILIES*. Let Marcus Ward explain to the evening news why he’s putting an NHS nurse and a three-year-old child on the street so he can build twenty-eight storeys of luxury glass!"

Sophie opened the door of Flat 2A, carrying Maya on her hip. The child was rubbing her eyes, her pink pajamas smudged with biscuit crumbs.

"Callum, please," Sophie whispered from the doorway. "You're frightening the bairn. Come inside."

Callum looked at his wife and daughter. His shoulders slumped, the fiery energy draining from his face, leaving him looking hollow-cheeked, pale, and desperate under the dim landing light.

"I’m just trying to keep a roof over her head, Sophie," he muttered.

"I know," she said gently. "Come inside. The tea is cold."

Callum turned back to Arthur, his dark eyes burning with unresolved defiance. "Monday at noon, Arthur. Fiona will be at the community law office on Commercial Road with the registered charter. If you're with us, you'll be there with your pen."

Arthur did not answer. He stepped back across his threshold and pulled his heavy timber door shut, letting the brass latch click firmly into place.

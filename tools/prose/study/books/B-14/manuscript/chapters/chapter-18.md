# Chapter 18: The Open Door of 3A

The first flat surrendered on the third Thursday of March.

Arthur was descending the spiral staircase at ten in the morning to fetch his newspaper when he found the entrance corridor blocked by two removal men in brown overalls carrying a pale blue sofa. Behind them walked young Mrs. MacIntyre, the auxiliary nurse from Flat 3A, wearing her blue raincoat, a cardboard box containing her toaster and kettle held against her ribs.

Arthur stepped back onto the half-landing to give them room.

"You're leaving, Fiona?" Arthur asked quietly.

Mrs. MacIntyre stopped on the stone tread. Her eyes were red, the skin beneath her cheekbones pale and hollowed by twelve-hour night shifts at the infirmary. She looked at her shoes, then at the open doorway of Flat 3A above them.

"They offered two thousand five hundred cash, Arthur," she whispered, her voice barely carrying over the sound of the rain outside. "Vanguard's liaison officer came to the hospital on Tuesday. He said if I took the voluntary settlement before the end of the month, they’d pay my deposit on a one-bedroom flat in Partick and write off my final month's rent."

"You said you were going to hold out with Callum," Arthur said, feeling a cold ache settle behind his breastbone.

"Callum isn't working seventy hours a week on the geriatric wards, Arthur," she said, her voice tightening with a mixture of shame and anger. "Callum has Sophie to cook his tea. I’m thirty-one years old, my back is ruined from lifting stroke patients, and I can't come home at seven in the morning to an unheated stairwell with steel fences outside the door. I took the cheque. The keys go into the liaison office at twelve."

The removal men carried down an iron bed frame, the tubular steel legs clanking against the sandstone banister.

"Good luck, Fiona," Arthur said.

"I’m sorry, Arthur," she muttered, not meeting his gaze as she walked down the remaining stairs into the damp corridor.

Arthur climbed to the third-floor landing.

The door of Flat 3A stood wide open. The brass Yale rim latch had been propped back with a piece of folded cardboard wedged into the strike hole. Arthur stepped across the threshold onto the bare floorboards.

The apartment was hollow, stripped down to its bare bones within three hours. In the living room, rectangular patches of pale yellow wallpaper marked where framed prints of the Clyde steamers had hung for fifteen years. Across the floor lay discarded rolls of brown parcel tape, crumpled greaseproof paper, and two plastic curtain hooks snapped in the rush of packing. The bay window looked out over the steel hoarding below, framing the yellow arm of the Vanguard excavator clawing at the mud.

He walked into the small scullery.

The linoleum had been lifted, leaving sticky black bitumen adhesive clinging to the pine floorboards. On the scullery windowsill sat an abandoned plastic soap dish, cracked at the corner, holding a sliver of dried white soap that smelled faintly of almond blossom.

Through the empty rooms, the draft from the open close door blew unimpeded, a cold, whistling current that made the dangling electric ceiling cords sway in the silence.

Arthur reached out and touched the doorframe of Flat 3A. The red pine was splintered near the top hinge where the removalists had squeezed the wardrobe through the narrow frame. In Baltic Buildings, twelve homes had shared this stone spiral for forty years. Now the first tooth had been knocked from the jaw, leaving only an empty socket through which the harbor wind rushed.

Arthur walked back down the empty hallway of Flat 3A to the front room.

In the center of the bare pine floorboards lay an abandoned calendar from the Western Infirmary Nurses' League, its pages showing full-color photographs of the hospital gardens in summer. A red circle had been drawn in felt pen around the date of March twenty-eighth, with two words scrawled in black ink: *Deposit paid*.

He walked to the bay window.

From the third floor, the view across Albert Basin had always been the best in the close. Through the rain-streaked glass, you could see right down the dredged channel to the navigation lighthouse on the spit, where the river broadened into the gray expanse of the Firth. Arthur remembered coming up here in nineteen ninety-six with old Jock MacIntyre, Fiona's father, to watch the tall ships race come into the harbor on midsummer eve. They had leaned on the sill with two bottles of heavy export beer, watching sixty sailing ships drop anchor under a pink evening sky.

*They'll never build anything that beats this view, Artie,* Jock had said, clapping Arthur's shoulder with a hand missing two fingers from the dock hoists. *A king couldn't buy this water.*

Now, directly opposite the window, rising out of the mud where the timber wharves had been, stood the first three storeys of The Meridian's concrete core. Steel rebar bristled from the poured concrete slabs like iron grass; yellow climbing shuttering rose into the mist; floodlights illuminated the wet gray stone where twenty-eight storeys of luxury apartments would soon block the river view completely.

Arthur looked down at the courtyard below.

Fiona MacIntyre was loading her cardboard boxes into the back of a rented white van. The driver, an older man in a navy woollen cap, slammed the rear doors shut, locked the paddle latch, and climbed behind the wheel. The van pulled through the narrow gap in the galvanised hoarding, its diesel exhaust drifting across the wet mud.

Arthur looked at his reflection in the cold glass of the window.

His face was lined, gray, the skin around his jaw sagging with exhaustion. He thought of Flat 2B above him. He thought of his own kitchen, where Mary's copper kettle sat on the gas ring, waiting for a day that would never arrive.

He stepped back out onto the third-floor landing, pulled the door of Flat 3A shut until the brass latch clicked against its strike, and began the slow climb back to the top floor.

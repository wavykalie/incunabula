# Chapter 24: The Summary Warrants

The sheriff officers arrived at ten o’clock on Tuesday morning.

They came in two unmarked dark blue saloon cars, accompanied by two uniformed police officers and a locksmith carrying an electric drill in a metal case. The officers were men in gray trench coats and black leather gloves, holding red cardboard folders tied with black ribbon: formal decrees of ejection issued by the Sheriff Principal of Glasgow and Strathkelvin.

Arthur watched from the bottom landing as they climbed the stone stairs.

Callum Reid met them on the second floor, standing in the doorway of Flat 2A with his arms spread across the frame. Sophie stood behind him, clutching Maya against her chest, her face white as bone.

"You can't come in here!" Callum shouted, his voice cracking with exhaustion. "We filed an appeal with the Court of Session on Friday!"

The senior sheriff officer, a man with a mustache and a flat Edinburgh accent, did not raise his voice. He opened his red folder and presented the legal decree.

"The appeal was dismissed at nine-fifteen this morning for lack of collective standing, Mr. Reid," the officer stated, reading from the typed document. "Summary warrant of ejection granted under Section 36 of the Sheriff Courts Scotland Act. You have two hours to remove your personal effects, after which the premises will be secured by the landlord's agents."

"Two hours?" Callum screamed, lunging forward. "I have a three-year-old child! Where am I supposed to put my furniture in two hours?"

The two police officers stepped onto the landing, their hands resting on their utility belts, positioning their bodies between Callum and the court official.

"Don't make this difficult, son," the older police officer said in a low, tired voice. "Get your wife and the bairn packed. The council emergency accommodation team has two rooms reserved for you at the Queens Park commercial hostel."

"A hostel?" Callum’s voice dropped into a high, broken whimper. "A homeless hostel with shared bathrooms?"

Sophie stepped forward, her hand pulling Callum's sleeve. "Callum, don't. Please. Just let them have it. Let's get Maya's clothes."

Callum’s arms dropped from the doorframe. He looked down at the red sandstone landing, his shoulders shaking with silent, dry sobs. The defiance that had sustained him for three months collapsed completely, leaving only a broken, defeated boy in a stained port jacket.

By noon, the second floor was cleared.

Four council workers carried down two mattresses, three cardboard boxes of baby clothes, and a battered zinc pram. The locksmith drilled out the brass cylinder of Callum’s Yale lock, replaced it with a heavy steel padlock through an iron hasp, and screwed a perforated steel security screen over the wooden door panels.

On the four remaining occupied doors—Flat 1A, Flat 1B, Flat 2B, and Flat 3B—the sheriff officers pasted white statutory eviction summonses, the gummed labels fluttering in the draft from the broken landing window.

Arthur stood on the bottom stair, watching Callum carry Maya through the chain-link corridor into the street drizzle, the child crying into her father’s collar.

The close was silent now, save for the electric whine of the locksmith's drill securing the steel plate over the Reids' doorframe with four ringing hammer blows.

The sheriff officers worked with brisk, administrative precision.

While the two uniformed police officers stood on the second-floor landing, the senior officer checked each name off his clipboard: *Flat 2A: Reid, Callum & Sophie. Eviction executed under warrant dated 18 March.*

Arthur stood on the bottom riser of the stone stair, watching the removalists carry down the remnants of the Reid household.

A wooden cot with white painted bars, dismantled into four flat pieces and tied together with rough parcel string. A plastic box of children’s picture books. A battered microwave oven. A blue nylon holdall stuffed with Callum’s port work gear: his safety boots, his heavy waterproof trousers, his yellow hard hat with the union sticker peeling off the crown.

Callum came down the stairs carrying three-year-old Maya on his left hip.

The child was not crying now; she was staring with wide, terrified eyes at the dark stairwell, her small thumb jammed into her mouth, her other hand clutching the wool of her father's collar. Sophie walked behind them, carrying two carrier bags of tinned food and an electric kettle, her head down, her shoulders shaking with silent, dry sobs.

As Callum reached the bottom landing, he stopped two feet from Arthur.

Arthur did not look away. He stood with both hands resting on his walking stick, his face gray, his jaw set in that hard, immovable mask he had worn for thirty-five years behind the driver’s wheel.

Callum looked at Arthur.

He did not shout. He did not curse. The rage had burned itself out, leaving only a cold, hollow contempt that was far more terrible than any fury. His eyes dropped to the pocket of Arthur’s tweed overcoat, where the outline of the Vanguard keycard was visible against the cloth.

"I hope you stay warm, Arthur," Callum whispered, his voice dead, flat, cold as wet sandstone. "I hope the floor is nice and warm for your knees."

He turned and walked through the close entrance, his work boots splashing through the brown puddles in the chain-link corridor.

Arthur stood motionless on the bottom step.

Behind him, the locksmith was finishing the job on Flat 2A. The electric impact driver gave four high, screaming bursts—*whine-clack, whine-clack, whine-clack, whine-clack*—as four six-inch security screws drove the perforated steel screen into the historic pine architrave, sealing the door into darkness forever.

The senior sheriff officer came down the stairs, clicked his ballpoint pen, and checked his pocket watch.

"Twelve-fifteen," the officer muttered to his colleague. "Time for a pie before the court session."

They pushed through the storm door into the street drizzle, their blue saloon cars pulling away into the dock road, leaving Arthur Cross standing alone in the silent, frozen close.

# Chapter 4: The Registered Letters

At half past one, the royal mail van pulled up against the curb outside Baltic Buildings. It was a red diesel van with a dent in the sliding side door, its engine idling with a loud, uneven clatter that sent diesel fumes through the broken glass of the lower entrance transom.

Arthur was coming down the stairs to check his gas card balance when the postman pushed through the heavy street door.

The postman was not young Miller, who usually carried the dock round. It was an older man with gray sideburns and a black waterproof clipboard strapped to his left wrist by an elastic band. He carried a heavy canvas satchel over his shoulder, stuffed with thick, legal-sized envelopes bearing red fluorescent registered delivery labels.

"Cross?" the postman asked, glancing down at a computer-printed manifest on his clipboard. "Arthur Cross?"

"That's me," Arthur said, stopping on the third riser from the bottom.

"Need a signature, mate. Red label." The postman pulled a stiff white envelope from his bag. It was heavy, printed on expensive woven paper, with a clear plastic address window showing Arthur's name and *Flat 2B, Baltic Buildings, Albert Dock*. In the upper left corner sat a embossed blue logo: *Vanguard Urban Regeneration Ltd, 14 Charlotte Square, Edinburgh*.

"What's this then?" Arthur asked.

"Tenement notice, looks like," the postman said indifferently. He clicked a black ballpoint pen and held out the clipboard. "Got twelve of 'em. Every flat in the close. Sign on line four. Inside the box."

Arthur took the pen. His fingers felt thick and clumsy inside his gloves. He pulled off his right glove with his teeth and signed his name in the narrow box: *A. Cross*, the signature small and cramped, the same signature he had put on the municipal bus depot duty sheets for thirty-five years.

"Cheers," the postman said. He ripped the red barcode receipt from the face of the envelope and slapped it onto his clipboard. Then he walked to the communal letterboxes mounted on the wall beneath the stairs.

The letterboxes were old wooden pigeonholes, varnished yellow pine that had darkened to the color of treacle. The postman began slotting identical white envelopes into each compartment: *Flat 1A: Kowalski. Flat 1B: Vacant. Flat 2A: Reid. Flat 2B: Cross. Flat 3A: MacIntyre. Flat 3B: Henderson (Deceased).*

Danuta’s door opened two inches, restrained by her brass security chain. Her spectacles caught the light from the street transom.

"Letter for me?" she asked in a thin voice.

"Registered delivery, love," the postman said without looking up. "Need a signature for Flat 1A."

"I do not buy anything from the post," she said, her fingers gripping the edge of the timber door.

"It’s not an order, missus. It’s formal legal notification. You have to sign or I take it back to the sorting office and they serve it by recorded sheriff officer."

Arthur stepped down off the bottom stair. "Sign it, Danuta. It’s just the delivery receipt."

Danuta looked at Arthur, her eyes wide behind her glasses. She unhooked the brass chain with a trembling hand, took the postman's pen, and wrote her name in slow, jagged letters: *D. Kowalski*.

The postman tore off her receipt, handed her the envelope, and hoisted his heavy canvas bag back onto his shoulder. "Have a good afternoon, folks," he muttered, pushing through the storm door into the cold drizzle of the street.

The close fell silent, save for the hum of the damp wind through the broken windowpane above.

Arthur looked down at the envelope in his hand. The paper was dry, crisp, and cold. He slid his thumb under the gummed flap and tore it open with a clean, sharp rip.

Inside was a four-page document on heavy eighty-gram stock, accompanied by a glossy, full-color brochure printed on coated art paper. Across the top of the first page ran the heading:

*NOTICE UNDER SECTION 33 OF THE HOUSING (SCOTLAND) ACT / REVISED REDEVELOPMENT SCHEME: THE MERIDIAN TOWER.*

Arthur read the dense legal clauses in the dim light of the entrance hall:

*Take notice that Vanguard Urban Regeneration Ltd, having acquired the superior freehold interest in the lands and subjects known as 1–12 Baltic Buildings, Albert Dock, hereby gives formal notification of its intention to initiate site consolidation, demolition, and comprehensive residential regeneration works commencing October 1st...*

*Tenants holding standard short assured tenancies or historical statutory tenancies are hereby invited to enter into voluntary decant negotiations...*

He unfolded the glossy brochure.

Across the front spread was an architectural computer rendering of a soaring tower of bronze glass, brushed zinc, and white composite stone, rising twenty-eight storeys into a clean, cloudless blue sky that bore no resemblance to the gray skies of Albert Dock. At the foot of the tower, where the damp stone walls of Baltic Buildings now stood, the artist had drawn a sunlit pedestrian plaza with outdoor café tables, polished granite benches, and smiling young couples holding paper coffee cups.

Arthur turned the page.

*THE MERIDIAN: CLASS A LUXURY WATERFRONT LIVING.*
*Elegance. Acoustic serenity. Energy efficiency.*

Behind him, Danuta gave a small, sharp gasp. She was holding her letter close to her spectacles, her lips moving as she struggled through the legal English.

"Arthur," she whispered, her voice cracking. "What does it say? Demolition? What is *demolition*?"

Arthur folded the letter slowly, creasing the thick paper along its original folds. He looked down at the red registered delivery receipt that had slipped from his fingers onto the cold black-and-white tiles of the entrance floor.

Callum Reid stood in the entrance vestibule, his thumb hooked into the pocket of his port fleece, his eyes scanning the four pages of legal text with furious intensity.

"Look at clause eight, Arthur," Callum said, his voice dropping into a harsh, conspiratorial whisper. "Clause eight: *The Tenant agrees that upon receipt of the statutory decant allowance, all claims arising from statutory protected occupancy under the Rent Scotland Act or the Housing Scotland Act shall be extinguished unconditionally.* Extinguished! They’re not just asking us to move; they’re asking us to sign away our existence!"

Arthur stood on the bottom stair, holding his registered envelope. The paper felt heavy, cool, and stiff in his hand.

"Vanguard is a subsidiary of a real estate fund registered in the Cayman Islands," Callum continued, tapping the back of the envelope with his ink-stained thumbnail. "I looked them up on the company registry at the union office during lunch. Their holding company owns three million square feet of dockland between here and Aberdeen. They buy the municipal freehold from the harbor trust for peanuts, promise the council five hundred construction jobs and twenty percent affordable housing, and then they revise the planning application two years later to scrap the affordable units because of *abnormal remediation costs*."

"They're developers, Callum," Arthur said. "That’s what developers do."

"And the council lets them do it!" Callum kicked the bottom riser of the stone stairs, his steel toe-cap making a dull, heavy thud against the red masonry. "The housing convenor was in the paper last week, smiling next to this Marcus Ward bloke, holding a golden shovel at the ground-breaking on the pier. They call it the *Albert Dock Waterfront Gateway*. A gateway for who? For London hedge fund managers with yachts! Not for people who drove the buses or worked the grain elevators!"

Danuta stood by her door, clutching her white envelope against her apron like an iron shield.

"Callum," she whispered. "Do not shout. The postman will hear you in the street."

"Let him hear me! Let the whole street hear me!" Callum turned to face the stairs, looking up into the dark spiral well. "Every flat in this close has paid its rent on the nail for forty years. My father lived in Flat 2A before me. He worked the coal hoists until his back broke in ninety-two. Sophie and I brought Maya home from the maternity hospital to this close. We painted her room yellow with our own hands. We bought the carpet from the discount warehouse on Duke Street. You think I’m letting some Edinburgh accountant in a cashmere coat throw my daughter’s cot into a skip?"

Arthur looked at the young man. He saw the white knuckles, the vein throbbing in the side of his neck, the raw, frantic terror hidden just beneath the fury. He had seen the same terror in his own father’s face in nineteen seventy-four, when the coal hoists were closed and the men were called into the dockmaster’s office.

"What time is the meeting tonight, Callum?" Arthur asked quietly.

"Eight o'clock," Callum said, his breathing ragged. "On the second-floor landing. Bring your letter and your pension book. We’re drawing up the action committee charter."

Callum turned and took the stairs two at a time, his boots pounding against the stone risers until his front door slammed above.

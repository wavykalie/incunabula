# Chapter 14: The Morning Milk

On Saturday morning, Arthur went down to collect his milk from the entrance hall.

The close was freezing, the air smelling of carbolic soap and raw sea wind. As he reached the bottom landing, Danuta Kowalski opened her door. She was fully dressed in her black woollen skirt and knitted cardigan, her spectacles freshly polished, holding a small plate covered with greaseproof paper.

"Pan Cross," she said, her face lighting up with genuine warmth. "I see you coming down. I make warm potato scones on the griddle. Two for your breakfast."

"You're too good to me, Danuta," Arthur said, taking the warm plate. The smell of fried butter and flour rose into the drafty vestibule, rich and comforting. "You shouldn't waste your butter on an old man."

"A neighbour is not a waste, Arthur," she said firmly. She stepped out onto the landing tiles, wiping her hands on her linen apron. "I saw Callum’s lawyer yesterday. She has good eyes. A serious woman. She told us if we stay together, the council must listen."

Arthur held the warm plate in his left hand, his walking stick in his right. He looked at the bank of letterboxes on the wall.

"Callum’s fighting hard," Arthur said.

"He fights for his child," Danuta said, her voice softening. "A father must fight. When my Tadeusz was young, in the shipyard in Gdynia, he stood at the gate for fourteen days when the company cut the grain rations. He said: *If we do not stand today, tomorrow our sons will have no bread.* It is the same for Callum."

She leaned closer, touching Arthur's tweed sleeve with her callused fingers.

"You will sign the lawyer's paper on Monday, Arthur? Callum said you were thinking."

Arthur looked down at the black-and-white tiles beneath their boots. Several of the tiles were cracked, gray cement showing through where the decorative glaze had chipped away forty years ago.

"The lawyer said it could take eighteen months in the court, Danuta," Arthur said slowly. "Eighteen months of legal fees. And at the end of it, Vanguard owns the land. The sheriff cannot overturn an act of parliament."

"Eighteen months is eighteen months of life, Arthur," she replied, her dark eyes looking straight into his. "In eighteen months, who knows what happens? The government changes. The economy falls down. The developer loses his money in the bank. But if we run away today, we lose today."

She reached out and adjusted the lapel of his overcoat, a motherly gesture that Mary used to make before he left for the morning shift.

"We are blessed to have you in the building, Pan Cross. You are an honest man. The young ones look to you because you do not tell lies."

Arthur felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach, hard and heavy as an iron nut.

"Go inside and eat your scones before they go cold, Danuta," he said quietly.

"Yes, yes. I have cabbage to shred." She smiled, gave him a small nod, and stepped back into Flat 1A. Her brass Yale latch clicked shut with that familiar, hollow scrape that Arthur had heard ten thousand times.

He stood alone in the entrance hall for a long minute, looking at the heavy pitch-pine storm door that led to the street.

Through the frosted transom, he could see the gray silhouette of the dock cranes swinging through the morning mist, silent, relentless, moving iron from one ship to another without caring about the people who walked beneath them.

Arthur took his milk bottle from the communal crate beside the storm door and turned to go back up the stairs.

Danuta Kowalski was standing in the doorway of Flat 1A, holding a warm enamel dish covered with a clean white linen tea towel. She had her thick woollen cardigan buttoned up to her chin, her spectacles resting on her nose, her gray hair pinned back into its tight, neat bun.

"Pan Cross," she called out softly. "You are early this morning."

"Could not sleep, Danuta," Arthur said, stopping on the second riser. "The wind was rattling the sashes all night."

"The wind comes from the Baltic," she said, nodding with deep understanding. "When the wind is from the east, my mother used to say the sea is looking for salt. Here, take the potato cakes. They are fresh from the griddle. Good and hot."

Arthur set his milk bottle on the stone step and took the warm dish from her hands. The smell of fried potato, butter, and black pepper was rich and comforting in the drafty, carbolic-scented hall.

"You're very kind, Danuta," he said.

"A neighbour must be kind, Arthur. If we are not kind to each other, the stones will eat us." She rested her hand on the red sandstone architrave, her rough fingers tracing the chisel marks in the stone. "Callum told me he went to the union hall yesterday. He says forty men from the container terminal have signed a pledge to support our close."

Arthur looked down at the dish in his hands.

"A pledge doesn't pay a barrister, Danuta," Arthur said quietly.

"A pledge shows the heart, Arthur," she replied, her eyes steady behind her thick lenses. "When the shipyard in Gdynia went on strike in nineteen eighty, we had no money. We had no coal. But the railwaymen in Torun sent three wagons of flour, and the farmers in Kaszuby sent twenty sacks of potatoes. We lived on potato bread for three months, but the government had to talk to Lech Walesa. They had to sign the Gdansk Agreement."

"Vanguard isn't the Polish government, Danuta," Arthur said, feeling the cold weight in his chest. "Marcus Ward is an investment manager. If the court holds him up, he just pays the lawyers out of his interest fund. But Callum doesn't have an interest fund. If Callum misses three agency shifts, he can't buy nappies for Maya."

Danuta looked at him, her face growing serious, her head tilting slightly as if hearing an unfamiliar note in his voice.

"You have spoken to this Mr. Ward, Arthur?" she asked softly.

The question was so quiet, so devoid of suspicion, that it cut Arthur deeper than Callum’s shouting had.

Arthur gripped the handle of his walking stick. His knuckles whitened against the dark horn.

"He sent me a letter, Danuta," Arthur said, keeping his eyes on the warm cloth over the potato cakes. "Like he sent everyone."

Danuta smiled, her face relaxing back into its gentle, maternal wrinkles. "Yes. The white letters. Father Stanislaw said we should burn them in the scullery stove. We stay here, Arthur. We stay until the spring comes."

She stepped back into Flat 1A, her slippers whispering against the hall tiles. The brass latch gave its familiar scrape, then clicked home.

Arthur stood alone on the second riser, the warm plate in his left hand, his right knee throbbing with every beat of his heart.

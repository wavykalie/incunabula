# Chapter 2: The Copper Kettle

Arthur did not take off his overcoat immediately. He hung his wet woolen scarf over the brass hall hook beside the door, letting the wool drip onto the saucer he kept on the floor for that purpose. The hall was freezing, narrow as a tram gangway, walled in dark brown tongue-and-groove wainscoting that Mary had stained with walnut oil thirty summers ago.

He walked into the kitchen, his boots squeaking on the linoleum runner.

The tall sash bay window looked southeast toward the Albert Basin. The glass was clouded with thick moisture, greasy condensation running down the panes in gray rivulets that gathered in the hollows of the wooden sill. He took a dry flannel rag from the scullery door and began wiping the glass from left to right.

Across the water, the container terminal was operating under orange floodlights. The great blue gantry cranes moved back and forth along their rails with a distant, grinding roar that sounded like iron grinding against rock. Arthur watched a red container drop smoothly into the hold of a feeder ship, its diesel exhaust rising straight up into the still, freezing air.

When the window was clear, he dropped the wet cloth into the Belfast sink.

The sink was deep, white fireclay, yellowed around the brass waste by forty years of iron-heavy city water. Above it jutted two cold-water taps: one modern brass bib-cock fitted five years ago by the housing trust, and the original lead gooseneck pipe with an iron wheel-valve that had supplied the flat since eighteen ninety-five.

He turned the brass tap.

The water coughed in the pipes—a double shudder that ran through the joists under his feet—before settling into a cold, steady stream. He held Mary's hammered copper kettle under the spout. The kettle filled with a hollow rushing sound, the copper sides sweating cold instantly. He judged the weight by the pull in his wrist: three pints, enough for a mug now and another when the post arrived.

He carried the kettle to the gas cooker beside the dresser.

The cooker was a two-burner cast-iron plate on stubby legs, black enamel chipped away around the burner rings to expose the gray metal underneath. He struck a wooden safety match against the rough strip of the box. The flame flared bright sulfur, then settled blue. He turned the ribbed brass petcock ninety degrees, holding the match to the jet until the flame jumped across with a sharp pop.

He set the kettle down. The blue flames flattened against the copper bottom, spreading heat into the room with the dry, chemical smell of burning town gas.

While the water heated, he took his small toolbox from beneath the sink.

The box was seasoned pine, fitted with an iron hasp. Inside lay his tools: two flathead screwdrivers with boxwood handles, a pair of slip-joint pliers, a roll of black friction tape, and an oilcan with a six-inch brass snout. He picked up the oilcan and a smaller screwdriver, walked back to the entrance hall, and knelt by the front door.

His right knee gave a sharp, wet crunch as he bent. He braced his forearm against the timber stile, waiting for the stitch of pain to ease.

The Yale rim latch was cast bronze, held to the pitch-pine door with four steel slotted screws. Arthur inspected the strike plate on the doorframe. The wood around the top screw was showing a fine hairline split where the door had slammed during an autumn gale. He backed out the two central screws half a turn, freed the brass cover, and pointed the snout of the oilcan into the tumbler assembly.

Two drops of three-in-one oil fell onto the phosphor-bronze spring.

He worked the thumb-turn back and forth. The mechanism responded instantly, the dry rasp softening into a smooth, liquid snap that barely made a sound in the quiet hallway. He wiped the surplus oil from the casing with his cotton rag, tightened the screws until the heads seated perfectly flush, and checked the strike clearance with the edge of his thumb.

A sixteenth of an inch. Tight enough to stop the draft, loose enough not to bind when the sea air swelled the timber.

In the kitchen, the kettle gave its first thin whistle.

Arthur levered himself up from the floor, leaning heavily on the doorframe. His joints felt stiff, the bone surfaces grating together like ungreased axle bearings. He walked slowly back down the passage, his fingers trailing along the wainscoting for support.

He lifted the kettle from the flame and set it on the cast-iron trivet.

He took down the brown earthenware teapot from the cupboard, warmed the belly with two ounces of boiling water, swirled it, and tipped it into the sink. Two heaped teaspoons of loose Assam tea from the red tin caddy on the dresser. He poured the boiling water in a steady, circular stream, watching the dried leaves rise to the surface in a dark, fragrant froth.

He covered the pot with the quilted tea cosy Mary had knitted in nineteen ninety-eight: green wool with a yellow pompom on top, now faded to the color of dry moss.

He sat down in his wooden armchair by the window, pulled his pocket watch from his waistcoat, and clicked the lid open.

Six-forty-eight.

The No. 16 municipal bus would be leaving the Albert Quay terminus right now, taking the corner by the grain elevator in second gear. Arthur closed his eyes and listened, waiting for the familiar distant roar of the diesel engine coming along the dock wall. The engine rumble grew louder as the bus passed the foot of Baltic Buildings, the vibration humming through the sash counterweights in their hollow wooden boxes.

Arthur took a slow sip of the scalding black tea. It burned his tongue, bitter and clean, settling the chill in his chest. Outside, the gray light was strengthening over the dock basin, turning the wet slates of the grain warehouse from black to pewter.

He stood by the dresser, looking at the brass tap above the Belfast sink. A slow drip had begun to form at the gland nut, dropping every eighteen seconds with a sharp, hollow plink against the white fireclay. Plink. Plink. In an old tenement, water was either a servant or an enemy. If the gland nut loosened, the leak would find the gap between the lead waste pipe and the floorboards, soaking into the lime plaster of Danuta's scullery ceiling within three days.

Arthur took his slip-joint pliers from the pine toolbox. He wrapped a strip of friction tape around the brass hexagon to protect the soft metal from the steel jaws, set the teeth, and gave the nut a quarter turn clockwise.

The brass groaned, then tightened home. The drip stopped.

He wiped the neck of the pipe with his flannel rag, checking with his dry index finger for any trace of moisture. The metal was cold as river ice, sweating a thin film of condensation from the sea fog outside. In nineteen eighty-five, during the big frost that froze the canal locks, the municipal main along Albert Street had burst, leaving twelve tenements without running water for nine days. Arthur remembered carrying zinc buckets of water from the corporation standpipe three streets away, four buckets every evening after his bus shift, his shoulders aching as he climbed the spiral stair in the dark. Mary had boiled every drop on the gas ring, filling stone hot-water bottles to wrap in woollen blankets so their bed would not feel like a grave.

He returned the pliers to the wooden box and set the box under the sink.

The kettle on the trivet was steaming gently now, the sweet, bitter scent of black tea rising through the tea cosy. Arthur poured a full mug. The dark liquid was so hot that the steam fogged his eyelashes as he lifted the mug to his mouth.

He took his seat by the bay window once more.

Down in the lane, the refuse lorry had arrived, its hydraulic compactor groaning with a high, mechanical whine as it crushed timber crates from the ship chandler’s yard. Two gulls fought over a fish head on the granite setts beside the cellar grating. Arthur watched them through the glass, his fingers absorbing the heat from the thick porcelain mug, feeling the warmth spread slowly up his wrists into the stiff muscles of his arms.

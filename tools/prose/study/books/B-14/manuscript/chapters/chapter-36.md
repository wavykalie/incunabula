# Chapter 36: The Tenancy Register

Marcus Ward arrived at two o'clock on Wednesday afternoon carrying a dark green wicker hamper tied with a gold satin ribbon and a slim black leather binder stamped in gold foil with the Vanguard corporate device. He was dressed in a tailored charcoal two-piece suit with a white poplin shirt, unbuttoned at the collar, and black oxfords polished to a glassy reflection.

"Arthur," Ward said, stepping over the flush threshold into the vestibule. "May I?"

"Come in," Arthur said.

Arthur had his slippers on, his grey tweed trousers, and the green cardigan with the horn buttons. He stepped back into the living room, leaving the solid oak apartment door open behind them.

Ward walked directly to the kitchen island. He set the wicker hamper on the quartz counter with a gentle, calculated precision that produced no sound. Through the open weave of the wicker, Arthur could see two bottles of sparkling elderflower cordial, an eight-ounce jar of heather honey from Strathspey, a box of Highland shortbread rounds in a tartan tin, and a ceramic ramekin containing smoked trout pate vacuum-sealed beneath clear plastic film.

"A small token from the board," Ward said, unbuttoning his suit jacket. "The Chairman asked specifically that our foundation tenants receive the executive hamper. It’s sourced from the estate farms at Balloch. No additives, no industrial processing. Pure regional provenance."

"Thank you," Arthur said.

Ward drew out a sleek chrome fountain pen from his breast pocket and laid it beside the leather binder. He flipped the binder open to page twelve.

The document inside was printed on ninety-gram watermarked parchment paper, headed: *VANGUARD URBAN REGENERATION PLC: RESIDENTIAL LIFE TENANCY COVENANT (PHASE ONE RESIDENTS)*.

"We’re completing the formal registry today, Arthur," Ward explained. His tone was brisk, pleasant, the voice of a professional who enjoyed the clean resolution of a complex municipal file. "The voluntary relocation schedule we executed in July was an interim occupancy license. Today we formalize your permanent statutory life tenancy under the private residential housing act."

He tapped the middle of the page with the chrome barrel of the pen.

"Under Clause Four, Sub-section B, your rent remains indexed to the 2018 local authority social benchmark in perpetuity, adjusted annually by no more than the consumer price index or two percent, whichever is lower. The service charge—including district heating, communal maintenance, acoustic filter replacement, and twenty-four-hour concierge monitoring—is permanently waived by Vanguard as part of the section seventy-five planning gain agreement with Inverclyde Council."

Arthur looked at the paper. The text was arranged in two justified columns of nine-point Garamond type, dense with statutory citations: *Sections 44 through 48 of the Housing (Scotland) Act 2016; Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (Scotland) Amendment Order 2020*.

"What about the other tenants?" Arthur asked.

Ward’s pen paused against the parchment. He did not look up immediately; he adjusted the alignment of the binder against the quartz edge by a fraction of an inch.

"All relocation claims have been resolved within the statutory framework, Arthur," Ward said smoothly. "The Reid family’s appeal before the housing tribunal was formally withdrawn yesterday following an agreement on their Erskine tenancy transfer grant. Vanguard has underwritten their first twelve months of utility supplements as an ex-gratia goodwill gesture. Mr. Reid signed the settlement at his solicitor's office in Paisley at eleven this morning."

"He has twelve months of heating," Arthur said.

"He has a secure tenancy in a modern council property with an energy performance rating of C," Ward replied. "Far superior to the damp, hazardous environment of Baltic Buildings. The environmental health report on Flat two-A identified four distinct species of toxic black mold behind the scullery laths, along with thirty-eight percent moisture saturation in the floor joists. In another eighteen months, that entire floor would have suffered structural deflection. We haven't displaced anyone, Arthur. We have liberated them from a nineteenth-century health trap."

Ward lifted the fountain pen and held it toward Arthur, nib first, with the polished steel clip facing upward.

"This is your register, Arthur. Once your signature is inscribed in the permanent corporate record, your right of occupation is absolute. No landlord, no developer, no municipal council can terminate your tenancy for the remainder of your natural life."

Arthur took the pen. The metal was heavy, cold from the October air outside, balanced comfortably in the crook of his thumb.

He looked down at the signature line.

The line was marked with a tiny printed arrow and the words: *Arthur Cross, Tenant for Life*. Beside the line sat a red wax wafer embossed with the Vanguard compass seal, waiting for the legal attestation.

Arthur thought of his grandfather’s rent book from nineteen-twenty-two. It had been a small booklet with a cover of dark blue stiff card, three inches by four, stitched with waxed linen thread. Every week, old Mr. MacFarlane’s father had sat in the tenement scullery with a portable wooden inkwell and a steel-nibbed dip pen, scratching a receipt for three shillings and four pence: *Rec’d from J. Cross, one week’s rent for two rooms and kitchen*. There had been no covenants. No section seventy-five planning gains. No ex-gratia goodwill gestures. There had only been the stone, the lime, and the weekly stamp.

Arthur did not look at Ward's face.

He lowered the nib to the watermarked parchment.

He wrote his name: *Arthur Cross*.

The black ink flowed from the gold-plated nib with a silent, fluid glide, soaking into the cotton fibers of the paper. It was smooth, effortless, requiring none of the hard pressure he had needed to press a ballpoint through three carbon copies in the bus depot.

He laid the pen down on the quartz island.

Ward picked up the pen, screwed the threaded chrome cap back onto the barrel with three sharp twists, and closed the black leather binder. The magnetic clasp inside the leather caught with a quiet, expensive *click*.

"Splendid," Ward said. He unclipped a second document from the folder, sliding it across the counter. It was a laminated card titled *Resident Portal Access Credentials*. It contained a ten-digit alphanumeric password and a monochrome square QR code for logging maintenance requests through a smartphone application.

"If you ever need anything," Ward added, tapping the card with a manicured nail, "a dripping valve, an acoustic gasket adjustment, replacement filters for the mechanical heat recovery unit, you don't chase a factor down the stairs. You log it on the portal. Our facility engineering team is on site within two hours."

"I don't have a mobile phone," Arthur said.

"The concierge desk handles manual logging from eight to six," Ward said without missing a beat. "Just tell young Liam at the reception terminal. He'll log the ticket directly into the building management system. We have automated tracking on every residential pod. Total peace of mind."

Ward took up his leather binder, slipped his chrome pen into his jacket pocket, and buttoned his jacket. He turned toward the entry door, then paused on the engineered oak threshold of the vestibule. He extended his right hand. The fingers were long, clean, the fingernails trimmed square and buffed. The cuffs of his white poplin shirt were secured with small silver knots that matched the silver crest on his briefcase.

Arthur reached out his hand. His skin was rough, scarred across the knuckles from fifty years of cold brass latches, bus platform handrails, and winter coal scuttles.

Ward shook his hand with a firm, dry grip and stepped out into the carpeted corridor.

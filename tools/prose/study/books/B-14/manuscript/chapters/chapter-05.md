# Chapter 5: The Shouting Upstairs

Arthur sat in his armchair in the dark.

The street lamps along the dock wall had flickered on at half past four, casting long, sodium-yellow rectangles across the ceiling of Flat 2B. Outside, the tide was coming back into the basin, the heavy river water slapping against the wooden fenders of the pier with a wet, hollow rhythm that sounded like someone beating a carpet with a wicker paddle.

He had not put the gas fire on. The room was cold enough that he could see the thin vapor of his breath whenever he leaned forward to rub his knees.

Above him, the floorboards of Flat 2A were groaning.

Callum had come home from the container terminal at five. Arthur had heard the street door slam downstairs, then the heavy, pounding cadence of work boots taking the stairs two at a time—thump, thump, thump, nineteen, twenty, twenty-one—followed by the vicious kick against the bottom riser of the second-floor landing.

Then the shouting had begun.

It was not the muffled, ordinary bickering of a young couple over electric bills or dirty laundry. It was the raw, breathless shouting of people who had been hit in the face with an iron wrench.

"They can't do it, Sophie!" Callum’s voice came down through the horsehair plaster of Arthur’s ceiling, clear as a bell. "I went to the union convenor at the gate. He looked at the paperwork. He says Vanguard bought the ground out from under the council in December. It was in the commercial papers three months ago!"

"Keep your voice down," Sophie’s voice was high, strained to breaking. "You'll wake the bairn."

"The bairn won't have a roof over her head by Halloween!" A heavy thump shook the ceiling rose above Arthur’s head, making the glass crystals of Mary's old light pendant tinkle softly in the dark. A boot thrown against the skirting board. "Look at the date on that letter, Sophie! October first! That’s eight months! Where are we going to go on my wages? You show me a two-bedroom flat in this city for four hundred quid a month!"

"Callum, please—"

"There aren't any! They’re knocking down the whole terrace from the grain elevator to the lock gates! It's all going into that bloody glass tower! Two grand a month for a one-bed flat with a view of the mud!"

Arthur sat motionless, his hands resting flat on his knees.

Through the floorboards, he felt the building vibrating. Tenement buildings were not silent boxes; they were acoustic instruments, built of dry red pine joists, lime plaster, and hollow sandstone flues that ran from the basement wash-houses to the chimney pots eighty feet in the air. When someone dropped a spoon on the ground floor, the vibration ran up the masonry like a pulse. When Callum paced the floor of Flat 2A, the sound travelled down into Arthur's skull.

*Pace. Turn. Pace. Turn.*

Arthur reached to the small side table and picked up his tin of wintergreen balm. The lid was stiff, sticking on its threads until he forced it with his thumb. The sharp, medicinal scent of methyl salicylate flared in the freezing air, cutting through the smell of damp wallpaper and stale tea.

He scooped a generous lump of the white ointment onto his fingers. He pulled up his trouser legs, exposing his swollen, knobbly kneecaps. The skin was pale, shiny with tension, crossed by tiny purple capillaries around the joint capsules.

He rubbed the balm into the bone with deep, circular strokes of his thumbs.

The friction brought a sudden, stinging heat to the skin, but beneath the heat, deep in the cartilage where the femur met the tibia, the cold ache remained untouched. It was an ache forty years in the making. Forty years of stepping off the platform of a double-decker bus into the freezing slush of the dock road. Forty years of holding twenty tons of steel against the curb with a mechanical clutch.

Above him, Maya began to wail.

It was a thin, frightened cry, the cry of a three-year-old who understood the terror in her father’s voice without understanding a word he spoke. Sophie’s footsteps hurried across the ceiling, the light, rapid tapping of slippers moving toward the back bedroom.

"Shh, my lamb. Shh, Maya. Daddy’s just tired. Shh."

Callum’s voice dropped, turning into a low, jagged rumble that vibrated through the plaster like an idling lorry engine. Then came the scrape of a kitchen chair being dragged across vinyl.

Arthur looked out the bay window.

Across the black water of Albert Basin, the dockland was changing. Two years ago, the grain silos had been pulled down by demolition contractors from Manchester, their concrete towers dropped into dust within thirty seconds. Now, where the grain dust used to blow across the pier, stood three giant steel crane towers, their red aircraft warning lights blinking against the low clouds. The marketing suite for the new dockland quarter sat on the pier head: a single-storey pavilion of plate glass and polished steel, lit from within by warm, amber spotlights that shone through the rain.

Through the glass of the pavilion, even from two hundred yards away, Arthur could see the illuminated white model of the new development glowing like an altar.

He wiped his oily fingers on his flannel rag.

Down in the close, a door opened. Danuta. Her heavy steps came out onto the first landing, then paused. She was listening, just as he was listening. In Baltic Buildings, twelve households were sitting behind their pitch-pine doors, holding four pages of Edinburgh legal text in their hands, listening to the rain and each other’s breathing.

A sharp thump from upstairs shook the ceiling plaster again. Callum had stood up, his chair legs screeching against the floorboards as he pushed it back.

Arthur sat in his armchair, listening to the building breathe.

Tenement buildings had their own internal clocks, regular as a harbor tide table. At half past six, the gas pressure always dropped slightly as twelve cookers were lit across the four floors, the blue flame on his gas plate shrinking half an inch with a soft, hollow sigh. At seven, the cold-water risers began their evening percussion: a long, shuddering groan as Mrs. MacIntyre on the third floor ran her bath after her hospital shift, followed by the metallic click-clack of the copper ball-cock refilling the attic header tank above Arthur’s bedroom ceiling.

Tonight, the sounds were different.

There was no sound of frying onions from Flat 1B. There was no radio playing the Scottish dance music from old Mrs. Henderson’s flat across the landing. Instead, through the dry timber floorboards and the lime plaster walls, came the low, persistent vibration of human anxiety.

In Flat 1A, Danuta was praying.

Arthur knew the sound. She had a wooden rosary with glass beads that her mother had given her in Gdynia in nineteen fifty-five. When she was troubled, she knelt on the rag rug beside her bed and let the glass beads click against the polished brass crucifix—a dry, rhythmic tap-tap-tap that conducted through the hearthstone and up the brick chimney flue directly into Arthur’s hearth.

*Zdrowas Mario, laski pelna, Pan z Toba...*

The words were indistinct, blurred by eighty feet of soot-lined brick, but the cadence was unmistakable: a woman whispering in the dark to a God who had not saved the shipyards and would not save the tenement.

Above him in Flat 2A, Callum had stopped pacing.

Arthur heard the soft creak of the Reids' kitchen door, then the sound of water running in the sink. Sophie was washing Maya’s plastic feeding bowls. She washed them with careful, deliberate slowness, the tin sink ringing each time a spoon touched the bottom. Then came Callum’s voice, low and exhausted, stripped of all the bravado he had shown on the stairs:

"The shop steward says they’re cutting twenty men from the straddle-carrier shift on Friday, Sophie."

"Callum, don't."

"I have to tell you. If I lose the agency shifts at the terminal, we won't have the rent for April, let alone a deposit for a private flat. Private landlords want three months' rent upfront and a credit check from a permanent employer. What am I going to give them? A zero-hour contract from a logistics agency?"

"We'll find something," Sophie whispered, her voice catching on a sob. "My sister says there are council flats in Castlemilk—"

"Castlemilk is twelve miles out, Sophie! There’s no buses to the docks at four in the morning! How am I supposed to get to the five o'clock container turn? Walk twelve miles in the sleet?"

The water tap squeaked shut. A chair legs scraped across the floor, then the sound of a woman weeping quietly into a tea towel.

Arthur closed his eyes in the dark room.

He reached down to his knees, pressing his thumbs into the swollen joint capsules. The pain was sharp, throbbing in time with his pulse, an iron ring tightening around the bone. He thought of Flat G-02: the underfloor heating, the walk-in shower, the warm oak planks that never chilled the feet.

The contrast was like a knife in his ribs.

Between the joists beneath his feet, the sound of the close below took on the peculiar clarity of night in a tenement. At half-past seven, the heavy street door swung open on its iron hinges with a dry, ungreased groan that vibrated up forty feet of stone ashlar. Heavy footsteps sounded on the Caithness flags of the ground floor—slow, deliberate, dragging slightly at the left heel.

It was Henderson, coming back from the working men's club on West Blackhall Street. Henderson walked with a rhythm Arthur could recognize in his sleep: *scrape-step, scrape-step*, the gait of a man who had lost his left metatarsal to a falling steel winch plate at the Victoria Harbour in seventy-four. Henderson stopped on the first landing outside Danuta’s door, coughed into his woolen sleeve, and spat into his handkerchief. Then his key rattled against the brass escutcheon of Flat 1B, two clicks, and the door closed with a muffled *thud*.

Arthur sat motionless until the power station horn blew eight o'clock, marking the start of the night shift down at the harbor gates.

The horn was an electric siren mounted on the turbine house roof, its long, mournful drone rising three octaves before dropping into a flat, vibrating howl that traveled four miles across the surface of the Firth of Clyde. On nights when the wind came off the water from the south-west, the siren sounded as though it were mounted on the roof of Baltic Buildings itself, shaking the soot from the chimney throat and setting the brass fire irons jingling on the glazed hearth tiles.

Forty years ago, Mary would hear that eight o'clock horn and say, *That's the shift turned, Arthur. Time to draw the damper.*

Arthur would take the curved iron poker from the hearth fender, hook the eyelet of the flue register, and shove the heavy cast-iron plate three inches back into the chimney throat to choke the draft. Then he would shovel two scoops of damp anthracite dross over the burning coals, banking the fire for the night so there would be red embers to catch the morning kindling when he rose at five for the Gourock bus run.

Now the hearth was cold, filled with grey, powdery ash from the previous week's peat briquettes.

Arthur stood up from his chair, his hands braced against the wooden arms to ease the sudden, sharp tear in his right patella. He walked across the room to the dresser, his slippers shuffling softly over the worn linoleum. The linoleum was brown, patterned with a faded geometric design of interlocking squares that Mary had bought from a shop on Duke Street during the summer of nineteen eighty-eight. Over forty years of walking between the gas cooker and the sink, his boots had worn through the glaze, exposing the dark, fibrous jute backing in a smooth oval trail.

He reached for the matches on the shelf above the gas ring.

He struck one, the sulfur flaring bright in the darkened room, and touched it to the brass jet of the gas mantle on the wall. The fragile white gauze puffed with a soft, hollow sigh, then began to glow—a pale, greenish-amber luminescence that washed across the yellowed wallpaper and Mary's willow-pattern plates on the plate rack. The light did not warm the room, but it pushed back the cold shadows that gathered in the corners of the high ceiling.

He took his tea caddy down from the shelf.

The tin was square, painted with pictures of Scottish lochs and red stags, the paint scratched and dented around the rim where Mary had opened it every morning with the handle of a teaspoon. He lifted the lid and breathed in the scent: dried black tea leaves, old tinplate, and the faint, sweet trace of dried orange peel Mary used to keep inside the caddy to keep the leaves fresh.

Through the floorboards, the building was settling into its late-night rhythm.

The water pipes in the wall cavity shuddered as someone ran a tap on the first floor. A radio was playing the late shipping forecast from the pier head station: *Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire, gale force eight rising nine, severe gale imminent.* Arthur closed his eyes and listened to the names of the sea areas, names he had heard through forty winters of harbor life. The gale was coming across the water, bringing sleet and high seas that would batter the stone piers before dawn. He replaced the lid of the tea caddy, turned the gas tap on the wall until the mantle hissed and died, and stood in the dark, watching the red lights of the dock cranes blinking through the sleet.

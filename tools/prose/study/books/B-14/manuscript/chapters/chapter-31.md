# Chapter 31: The Architecture of Doors

The dusk on Sunday had no heat in it. A thin drizzle had drifted inland from the Tail of the Bank at five o'clock, cooling the sandstone until the ashlar facing turned dark and greasy. Arthur Cross stood on the top landing of Baltic Buildings, forty-eight feet above Albert Quay, with his palm flat against the wire-reinforced glass of the roof cupola. The pane was cold, sweating moisture on the inside where forty years of warm human breath had once gathered before escaping through the cast-iron roof vents.

He was the only living thing in the block.

Below him, the spiral well plunged down through forty-eight steps of dressed sandstone, four landings, and twelve doorways. The stairwell was not quiet in the way a field or an empty room is quiet. It possessed a dense, cavernous resonance, like the inside of a disused church or an iron ship hull hauled up on the patent slip.

Arthur turned his back to the glass.

The top landing had two flats: 4A and 4B. Flat 4A had belonged to old Mrs. Garrow, who had died in nineteen-ninety-six, and after her to a succession of young shipyard apprentices who stayed six months and left their milk bottles to sour on the windowsill. The door was solid pitch pine, painted battleship grey, its lower panel kicked in during a burglary in two thousand and two and patched with a square of galvanized iron fastened with clout nails.

Arthur reached out and laid his fingers against the grey timber.

The wood felt hollow. When he knocked twice with his knuckle, the sound did not stop at the panel; it rang through the empty three-room flat inside, echoing off bare plaster walls and bare floorboards before dying out in the cold hearth of the rear bedroom. In Mrs. Garrow’s day, that door had possessed a distinct acoustic signature. It had closed with a dry, papery *snick*, because she had nailed four layers of green baize around the rebate to stop the draughts off the river. Every evening at nine, you could hear the brass chain slide into its slot from two flights below: *ch-ch-ch-clink*.

He moved to Flat 4B across the landing.

Flat 4B was where the Murrays had lived until three weeks ago. They had three boys, all under ten, who ran up and down the stairs four times a day like a team of pit ponies. The doorframe of 4B was splintered around the latch, repaired with steel mending plates screwed into the masonry. In the center of the door, a plastic sticker of a Celtic football club crest was peeling at the corners, curling up like a dry leaf.

Arthur touched the peeling sticker. The plastic was brittle, snapping off beneath his fingernail and fluttering down into the well.

When the Murrays were here, their door was never shut properly. It stood ajar half an inch on a brass night-latch, leaking the noise of commercial television, frying beef dripping, and the high-pitched arguments of the children over their football boots. The slamming of that door had shaken the entire fourth-floor landing twenty times a day. You could time your watch by it: eight-fifteen in the morning, twelve-fifteen for school dinner, three-thirty at the final bell, and six in the evening when John Murray came off the container crane at the terminal.

Now Flat 4B was sealed with twelve coach screws driven through a sheet of corrugated galvanized steel. The steel was cold, smelling of fresh zinc and machine oil. Arthur laid his cheek against the perforated screen. Inside, in the total darkness of the Murray living room, a radiator pipe ticked once: *tock*. Then silence.

Arthur began his descent.

His right knee took the weight on the first step. The pain was sharp and narrow, stabbing into the joint like an awl driven between the bones. He ignored it. He kept his left hand on the dark pitch-pine rail, feeling the deep gouges where the Murray boys had dragged their metal toy trucks down the timber forty years ago.

Step-by-step. Down sixteen stairs to the third floor.

On the third landing, Flat 3A had belonged to Mr. Henderson, the retired marine fitter who had lost three fingers to a lathe in nineteen-sixty-eight. Mr. Henderson’s door was unique on the close: it had a heavy brass letterbox with a spring so stiff that the postman had to use a wooden clothes peg to keep the flap open when delivering the pension cheques. The door had closed with a slow, hydraulic sigh, because Henderson had salvaged an overhead brass door-closer from a scrapped passenger liner and mounted it on the transom with four coach bolts.

Arthur stood before Henderson’s door. The brass letterbox was tarnished dark brown, green verdigris crusting around the hinge pins. The steel security plate covered it entirely now, bolted directly into the stone architrave.

Arthur touched the steel.

He realized with sudden, absolute clarity that the building had never been the stone.

The stone was just three thousand tons of ashlar quarried from the hills behind Paisley, cut by Irish masons in eighteen-ninety, and stacked in courses four feet thick at the plinth. The stone had no voice. It had no memory of the ships that had been built on the slipways, no memory of the sugar boats, no memory of the men who had carried their wooden sea-chests up these forty-eight stairs after three years in the South Atlantic. Left to itself, the stone was merely an obstruction to the harbor wind, a cold cliff face gathering moss and soot.

The life of Baltic Buildings had resided entirely in the friction of its doors.

It had been the sixty different ways twelve doors interacted with their casings: the brass rim latches clicking into their keeps; the heavy mortise bolts scraping through iron staples; the spring-closers groaning against dry leather washers; the storm doors rattling against their rubber stops when a westerly gale blew up the firth; the soft, muffled thud of seasoned Douglas fir sealing a family into its warm, lit box for the night.

Every door had possessed a voice that every tenant on the close recognized without looking.

When Mary was alive, Arthur could sit in his armchair in Flat 2B with the evening paper and name every person who entered the building by the acoustics of the street door and the rhythm of their ascent. Danuta walked like a clock: *tap-drag, tap-drag*, eighty beats to the minute, her key clicking in her lock with two short turns. Callum’s father had taken the stairs three at a time, his heavy rigger boots ringing on the sandstone like sledgehammers, slamming his storm door with a violence that made the gas brackets quiver. Old Mrs. Garrow had dragged her wicker shopping trolley behind her, the solid rubber wheels bumping over each riser with a rhythmic, hollow *thump-thump-thump*.

All of that was gone.

Now there was only the dead, monolithic weight of the stone, stripped of its hinges, its latches, and its voices.

On the half-landing beneath Flat 3A sat the old gas meter press.

It was a shallow timber cupboard recessed into the sandstone wall, five feet high, its double doors framed in three-inch pitch pine and fastened with a brass swivel turn-button. Arthur turned the button. The brass was cold, resisting until it gave way with a dry screech.

Inside hung twelve brass tags on iron cup-hooks, each stamped with an apartment number: *1A through 4B*. Beneath the hooks, an old cardboard notice from the Scottish Gas Board had been pinned with four copper drawing pins in the winter of nineteen-seventy-three, instructing tenants in four-point serif type on the operation of prepay shilling coin meters. The paper was yellow as mustard, brittle at the edges, nibbled by sixty generations of cellar mice.

Next to the notice, someone had written a pencil ledger of communal wash-house rotas in nineteen-sixty-nine: *Week 1: Cross. Week 2: Reid. Week 3: Kowalski. Week 4: Garrow.*

Arthur ran his dry thumb across Mary’s name. The graphite was faint, pressed into the pine grain forty years before, but still legible under the weak twilight from the cupola. Four families sharing one brick wash-boiler, six galvanized tubs, two cast-iron mangles, and thirty yards of tarred clothes line stretched between green poles in the back court. If a wash was missed, someone knocked on your door with a zinc bucket: *Mary, the water's on the boil, hen. You take the first turn.*

He pushed the cupboard doors shut. The latch caught with a hollow, wooden *clack* that bounced down the stairwell.

Arthur descended to the second landing.

He stood between Flat 2A and Flat 2B. Across from him, Callum’s door was a grey iron tomb. Arthur laid his palm on the perforated steel sheet. He could feel the cold draught being sucked through the three-quarter-inch hexagonal punch holes by the low pressure in the empty stairwell. The air smelled of damp chipboard and the sour residue of baby milk that had dried into the carpet underlay before it was torn up.

"Callum," Arthur said aloud.

The word was instantly extinguished. The steel screen did not resonate; it deadened the sound like a slab of lead.

Arthur looked down at his own door, Flat 2B. His door was the only timber leaf remaining on the close. Its four panels were painted bottle green, the brass Yale rim latch polished bright by his own thumb over forty-eight years. Tomorrow morning at eight-thirty, the fitters from Sitex would arrive with their eighteen-volt impact drivers. They would wedge their flat steel pry bars behind the brass hinges, split the old pitch-pine casing, and drop the four-panel door onto the landing flags. Then they would lift another sheet of perforated galvanized steel into the opening and drive twelve coach screws into the stone.

He looked at his hands. The skin was yellow in the dusk, the blue veins standing out like cords across the back of his wrists.

His joints were cold. In forty-eight hours, life would transfer across the street to Flat G-02 of The Meridian. Mornings would begin on carpet resting over concrete heated to twenty-one degrees by electric cables buried in the screed. The bathroom lacked a threshold, its acrylic screen sliding across a porcelain tray beside a chrome mixer tap delivering forty liters of thermostatic water per minute without shaking a single pipe.

No more climbing forty-eight stairs in the damp.

No more greasing his patella with menthol balm before carrying a sack of coal from the cellar.

No more listening to the wind rattling the sash cords or the rain leaking through the lead valley above the scullery.

Warmth was guaranteed. Safety was contracted. What remained was solitude, unbroken and complete.

Arthur stepped onto the middle landing between the first and second floors. He stopped at the landing window. The glass was so crusted with road grime and coal soot that the world outside appeared underwater, a murky grey expanse of asphalt, wire fencing, and muddy harbor water.

Across Albert Quay, the long-reach excavator stood poised against the dark sky like a praying mantis, its heavy steel boom angled over the roofline of the old cooperage. Tomorrow the hydraulic shear would arrive on an articulated low-loader from Grangemouth. The shear could cut through twelve-inch rolled steel joists like an old woman snipping thread with embroidery scissors.

A harbor tug sounded its horn twice—two long, low blasts that vibrated in Arthur’s chest. The sound drifted out across the wide water of the firth, answered only by the hollow hum of the empty stairwell behind him.

He stood on the middle landing and heard only the distant whine of harbor traffic.

# Chapter 25: The Breaker

The heat began at six in the morning, rising off the river like grease from a griddle. By seven, the yellow Caterpillar excavator with the hydraulic breaker attachment was clattering off the low-loader at the dock gates, its steel tracks tearing long white scratches into the soft tar of Albert Quay. Arthur Cross stood at the bay window of Flat 2B with a roll of brown parcel tape hooked over his forearm. The vibration through the timber floorboards was not a sound so much as an ache in the soles of his slippers.

Below, in the triangle of vacant ground where the stevedores' canteen had stood until April, two men in orange fluorescent vests planted four red plastic cones across the roadway. The hydraulic ram was painted the color of a fresh bruise. When the operator throttled the engine up, a plume of black diesel soot shot straight into the stagnant morning air, hanging there without a breeze to disperse it. Then the steel chisel dropped.

The first strike hit a subterranean footing of Whinstone. The shock wave traveled up forty feet of solid dressed sandstone, rattling the brass sash weights inside Arthur’s window casing. The pane of glass hummed against his palm. On the sideboard, Mary’s bone china tea service clicked softly inside its cardboard carton.

"Right," Arthur said to the empty room. "Right then."

He had thirty-six cardboard cartons stacked against the lobby wainscoting. They were standard removals boxes, double-walled corrugated craft board, supplied by the relocation contractor under Clause 8 of the voluntary surrender schedule. Each box had four printed tick-boxes on the side: Kitchen, Living, Bed 1, Bed 2. Arthur had already crossed out Bed 2 with a carpenter's pencil.

He went through to the scullery. The room was already stripped of its calendars and tea towels, leaving pale rectangles on the distempered walls where grease and coal smoke had not reached. The drying rack above the pulley hung empty, its wooden laths bleached grey by forty years of steam. On the oilcloth table sat six stacks of old *Greenock Telegraph* newspapers from the winter of 2018, their edges yellowed like dried tobacco.

Arthur took a dinner plate from the dresser. It was Copeland Spode, the Italian pattern, blue transfer on cream earthenware. Mary had inherited twelve of them from her aunt in Gourock when they were married in 1968. Four had broken over the years—one dropped on the hearth by Callum when he was five, two cracked by hot mutton fat during a hard winter in the eighties, one chipped during the ceiling collapse of 1993. Eight remained.

He smoothed a sheet of newsprint across the oilcloth. The headline read: *DOCK UPGRADE MOOTED FOR 2020*. Arthur placed the plate in the dead center of the print. He folded the top corner over the blue pastoral scene—the cattle by the river, the ruins of an arch, the little boatman with his oar raised against a pale sky. Then the left corner, then the right, tucking the excess paper tight against the glazed rim. The newsprint was cold under his fingers, smelling faintly of cellar mildew and lead ink.

He lifted the wrapped plate and set it into the base of carton number four. The cardboard smelled sweet and chemical, like glue heated in an iron pot.

The hydraulic breaker struck again outside. *Thud-crack. Thud-crack.* The impact was regular, roughly eighty blows to the minute, jarring the iron pipes in the wall behind the sink. A brown drop of rust water fell from the cold tap into the porcelain basin with a distinct *tink*.

Arthur did not look up. He took the second plate.

His knees were stiff from standing on the linoleum. When he bent his back to reach the lower shelf of the press, the joint in his right knee gave off a sharp, dry pop that echoed in the bare room. He straightened up slowly, resting both hands on the edge of the oilcloth until the sharp pinch subsided into its familiar dull thrum. In the old days he would have sat on the horsehair chair by the range until the stiffness cleared, but the horsehair chair was already bound with sisal twine in the lobby, waiting for the van.

Outside, the breaker stopped. The sudden drop in sound was almost louder than the pounding had been. Across the street, the diesel engine dropped to an idle, purring with a heavy, wet rhythm. Arthur heard the screech of a seagull over the roof ridge, followed immediately by the high-pitched metallic rasp of Callum’s angle grinder from the close below.

Callum was cutting down the old pipe-rail bicycle rack that had been bolted to the stone flags beneath the stairwell since the war. The landlord’s notice had ordered all tenant property cleared from the common close by noon on Friday, on pain of confiscation and disposal by the site security team. Callum was not leaving the rack for them. He had bought two lengths of heavy steel angle himself three years ago to reinforce it when the youngsters from the housing estate started kicking the padlocks off.

The grinder snarled as it bit into the galvanized iron, showering sparks across the worn sandstone flags. Arthur felt the sharp reek of scorched iron and zinc drift up through the floorboards. It was the same smell that had filled the James Watt Dock when the shipwrights were cutting plates for the bulk carriers in sixty-nine.

Arthur wrapped the third plate. He wrapped the fourth.

He had lived in Flat 2B for forty-eight years, four months, and nine days. The rent book in his pocket showed twenty thousand eight hundred and sixty weekly entries, every stamp signed in blue ink by three generations of factors—first old Mr. MacFarlane with his steel-nibbed fountain pen, then his son with a red ballpoint, and finally the computerized printed receipts on glossy thermal paper that faded to grey after six months in a drawer.

When the eight plates were wrapped and nested, he laid a thick wadding of crumpled sports pages over them. Next came the tea mugs. There were six, none matching: three plain white earthenware from the Co-op, one with a faded red transfer of the 1977 Silver Jubilee, one from the Transport and General Workers Union with its clasped-hands emblem rubbed down to the grey underglaze, and the heavy earthenware mug Mary had used for her chamomile, pale yellow with a green band around the rim.

He wrapped Mary’s mug separately, using three full sheets of paper so the handle would not knock against the others. His fingers were black with newsprint across the knuckles.

Next came the cast-iron Spong mincer. It was a Number 2 size, painted green enamel, with a wooden crank handle of turned beech and a heavy thumbscrew clamp for fixing to the kitchen table. Mary had bought it from a hardware shop in Cathcart Street during the winter of nineteen-seventy-one to mince beef skirt for Sunday pies. The iron was cold, heavy as a ship's shackle in his hands, the cutting blades sharp from forty years of being stropped on an oilstone. He wrapped the mincer in an entire section of the business pages, securing the heavy bundle with sisal twine before laying it in the corner of carton five.

Beside the mincer sat Mary’s stoneware bread crock. It was five gallons of salt-glazed stoneware from the Buchan pottery in Portobello, dark grey on the lower belly, buff on the shoulder, stamped with the imperial thistle device. For fifty years it had sat on the scullery flags beside the larder press, keeping six loaves of plain bread fresh through summer damp and winter gales. It weighed twenty-six pounds empty. Arthur could not lift it into a carton without his right knee giving a sharp, hot spasm, so he left it sitting on the linoleum, a blue chalk mark *MOVE AS IS* scrawled across its glazed lid.

On the shelf above the sink stood two stone ginger beer bottles from a defunct bottling works in Greenock, their stoneware necks fitted with porcelain swing-stoppers and perished red rubber washers. He wrapped each bottle with two layers of corrugated card.

A knock sounded at the outer storm door. It was three sharp, rhythmic raps, followed by the scrape of a shoe on the coir mat.

Arthur walked into the lobby. His slippers made a soft shuffling sound on the bare floorboards. He drew the brass Yale latch back.

Marcus Ward stood on the landing. He was not wearing his navy suit today; instead he had on a tailored charcoal fleece with the Vanguard crest stitched in silver thread above the left pocket, clean stone-colored chinos, and brown suede work boots that looked as if they had never touched mud. In his hand he held a clip-board with an aluminum cover.

"Morning, Arthur," Ward said. He looked over Arthur’s shoulder into the hallway, his grey eyes cataloging the stack of taped cartons against the wall. "Making steady headway, I see."

"Ten boxes done," Arthur said. He stayed on the threshold, his hand resting on the edge of the timber door. "Twelve to go in the scullery."

"Excellent. The haulage contractors are scheduled for zero-eight-thirty on Monday. Two men, five-ton tail-lift. They’ll take everything marked for the ground-floor unit directly across the road. No stairs for you to negotiate on the receiving end. Ground level, flush threshold, wide doors." Ward tapped the aluminum clipboard against his thigh. "The breaker outside bother you?"

"It shakes the press," Arthur said.

"They're taking out the old slipway retaining wall," Ward said smoothly, glancing down toward the quay. "Mass concrete from nineteen-twelve. It has to come out before the piling rig arrives on Thursday. Forty-foot pre-cast concrete piles. Once they’re in, the ground stabilizes completely. We’re on schedule. Ahead of schedule, in fact."

"Danuta isn't packed," Arthur said.

Ward’s expression remained entirely neutral, the trained composure of a surveyor noting a defective gutter on an elevation. "Social Services have assigned a designated care team for Mrs. Kowalski. They arrive tomorrow at nine to assist with her personal effects. The Greenock facility has a room ready for her in the maritime wing. Full central heating, twenty-four-hour nursing attendance, communal dining. She will be far more comfortable there, Arthur. You know that as well as I do. Three flights of stairs with congestive heart failure is an untenable proposition."

"She’s lived on this close since fifty-four," Arthur said.

"And the building has reached the end of its structural life," Ward replied without hesitation. His voice was soft, reasonable, carrying the weight of a dozen municipal committee minutes. "The lintels on the south elevation have dropped three inches since the January frost. Water ingress behind the ashlar facing is washing the lime mortar out of the rubble core. Another five years and the council would have condemned it over your heads with fourteen days' notice and no rehousing provision. You’ve got a brand-new home fifty yards from where you stand, Arthur. Warm, dry, fully accessible."

Arthur looked at the small silver crest on Ward’s fleece. A stylized compass needle pointing due north between two parallel lines.

"Callum’s cutting the bike rail out," Arthur said.

Ward smiled faintly, a thin tightening of the lips that did not reach his eyes. "Mr. Reid is entitled to remove fixtures he installed himself, provided the common masonry is not structurally compromised. He has until Friday noon to vacate the premises. The enforcement officers from Paisley Sheriff Court have their instructions. We’d all prefer it to conclude amicably."

"He has a bairn," Arthur said.

"Which is precisely why an unheated flat with dry rot and rising damp is not an appropriate residence for him," Ward said. He checked his wristwatch, a slim chronograph with a black silicone strap. "I’ll see you Monday morning, Arthur. Eight-thirty sharp. Leave the keys on the mantelpiece when the movers finish."

Ward turned and started down the stone stairs. His suede boots made no sound on the worn steps; he walked with a light, balanced tread, his hand hovering an inch above the iron handrail without gripping it.

Arthur stood in the doorway until the street door downstairs clicked shut.

He went back into the scullery. The breaker had started again, its heavy rhythmic pulse vibrating through the soles of his slippers. He picked up the roll of parcel tape. He laid the brown sticky strip along the top seam of box four, pulled it taut until the plastic film whistled, and severed it with the edge of the brass dispenser.

He folded the packing tape across the cardboard flaps with a loud tearing screech.

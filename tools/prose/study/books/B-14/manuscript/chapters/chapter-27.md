# Chapter 27: The Salt Pier

The hire van was a white Ford Transit with a dented nearside wing and the logo of a rental firm in Paisley stenciled in blue on both doors. It stood crooked across the pavement of Albert Quay, its rear barn doors propped wide with a wooden chock. The exhaust pipe gave off a sharp, greasy smell of unburnt petrol that mixed with the sea mud along the low-water line.

Arthur Cross stood by the stone doorway of Baltic Buildings, holding the heavy brass latch in his hand.

Callum Reid came down the forty-eight stairs carrying a double mattress wrapped in grey polythene. His face was red, the sweat glistening on his forehead where the strap of his tool apron had left a white welt. He did not look at Arthur. His boots scraped across the worn Caithness flags of the vestibule, each heel strike sounding like a mallet on wet timber.

"Give us a hand with the tail, Callum," Sophie said from the street.

Sophie was standing by the rear bumper of the Transit. She was wearing her padded winter jacket despite the July humidity, the hood thrown back across her shoulders. In her arms she held Maya, wrapped in a fleece blanket with yellow ducklings stamped across the wool. The child was eight months old, red-faced from crying, her small fists clenched against Sophie's collarbone. The sound of her crying was thin and hoarse, rising and falling with the rhythm of the hydraulic drill working across the quay.

Callum backed out into the daylight. He swung the mattress into the metal bed of the van. The poly film crackled loudly against the corrugated steel floor.

"That's the bed," Callum said. He wiped the back of his hand across his nose. "Couch next."

Arthur stepped forward one pace onto the threshold stone. "Callum," he said.

Callum did not turn his head. He reached into the cargo bed of the Transit, shoving a red plastic laundry basket between two cardboard boxes of kitchenware. His knuckles were raw, skinned red at the joints from stripping copper pipe.

Twenty-two years before, in the autumn of nineteen-ninety-nine, Arthur had stood with Callum on the round granite stones of the Albert Quay pierhead. Callum was six years old then, wearing an oversized woolen jersey knitted from Mary's leftover green Aran wool. His grandfather, old John Reid who had spent forty-two years on the platers' bench at Scotts' Cartsdyke yard, had given the boy a six-foot split-cane rod with a brass center-pin reel that clicked like a grasshopper whenever the spool turned.

"Hold it by the butt, Callum," Arthur had told him that morning.

The river had been running high and dark on the ebb tide, carrying bits of driftwood, yellow foam from the paper mill upstream, and the oily sheen of bilges pumped by an outbound coaster. Old John Reid had been too short of breath with the shipyard asbestosis to climb the seawall ladder, so he had sat on a wooden cable drum fifty yards back with his thermos of sweet tea, watching them through the sea mist.

"Keep your thumb on the rim of the spool, Callum," Arthur had instructed. "If a saithe takes the bait and you let the drum spin free, you'll have an overrun that will take forty minutes to pick out with a needle. A bird's nest of nylon sixty yards long. Thumb light against the flange. Feel the metal turning."

The boy had looked up at him with grey eyes, his teeth chattering in the cold maritime wind. His small hands had been red from handling frozen sprats out of a greasy wax carton. Arthur had stood behind him, wrapping his larger hands over the boy's fingers, feeling the thin bone of Callum's wrists beneath the damp green wool. The split cane had smelled of old copal varnish and dried river weed. Together they had swung the tip back past the bollard, then brought it forward in a clean, smooth arc, sending the two-ounce torpedo lead and the two small mackerel hooks arching thirty yards out into the green water of the deep channel.

The lead had struck the water with a sharp *plip*.

"Now wait," Arthur had said, kneeling on the cold stone. "You don't jerk the tip. You let the lead hit the mud, and you wait until you feel three sharp taps through the cork handle. Not two. Three. The third tap is when the hook sets itself into the lip."

They had stood there for two hours while the tide dropped two fathoms against the green weed on the masonry. Across the water, the hammer head cranes at the James Watt Dock had moved against the grey Argyll sky like giant iron mantises, loading steel pipe sections onto a Dutch pontoon. They had caught four coal-fish, none larger than a herring, tossing them into a white plastic lard tub filled with sea water. When Callum had dropped one onto the wet granite, the fish had flapped vigorously against Arthur's boot leather. The boy had laughed, a high, clear shout of childish triumph that startled the gulls off the iron mooring rings.

Old John Reid had walked over from his cable drum, leaning on his hazel stick, coughing that dry, rattling cough that sounded like gravel shaken in an empty bucket. *Good lad, Callum,* the old plater had wheezed, resting a scarred palm on the boy's wool cap. *Crossie’s teaching you the right way. No snatching. Patience on the Clyde, son. If you don't have patience on this river, the water will drown you and the factor will take your boots.*

Now, on the sun-baked pavement outside Baltic Buildings, Callum turned around.

His eyes were not grey like his grandfather’s; they were bloodshot from four nights without sleep, set deep beneath dark hollows that made his cheekbones look sharp as iron wedges. His mouth was set in a tight, bitter seam that made him look forty years older than the lad who had repaired the close bicycle rack with welded angle iron.

"You need help with the wardrobe?" Arthur asked.

Callum looked down at the brass buttons on Arthur's cardigan. "I don't need your help."

"It's two flights down," Arthur said gently. "The landing turn at the first floor is tight. Your dad and I had to take the crown molding off the corner in eighty-two to get your mother's three-piece suite through the stairwell."

"I took the doors off it this morning," Callum said. His voice was flat, dried out by plaster dust and diesel smoke. "The frame goes down in one piece. I can handle twelve stone of laminated pine without assistance from the management's friend."

Sophie shifted the crying baby to her other shoulder. "Callum, don't," she murmured. Her hair was pulled back in a loose elastic band, strands of brown hair clinging to the damp skin of her neck. "Just get the stuff in the van. The hire company wants it back in Renfrew by six, or they charge forty quid for an extra day. We haven't got forty quid."

Callum pushed past Arthur into the close without another word. His work jacket brushed Arthur's sleeve, smelling of damp masonry dust, stale tobacco, and sweat. The sound of his boots on the sandstone stairs was fast, angry, taking two steps at a stride despite the weight of the morning.

Arthur remained on the pavement beside Sophie. The sun had broken through the cloud cover over the river, heating the black asphalt until the smell of fresh road tar was suffocating. Across the street, the pile driver rig had arrived, a tall steel lattice tower mounted on a tracked crawler. Four workmen in yellow helmets were rigging a heavy steel wire rope to a thirty-foot concrete pile that lay on wooden skids beside the fence.

"Where are you going?" Arthur asked Sophie.

Sophie looked down at the child's blanket. She smoothed the corner of the fleece with her thumb, where the yellow duckling pattern had frayed at the hem.

"Erskine," she said. "A council maisonette. Third floor. No lift, but Callum says he’ll put a stair-gate on the internal flight so Maya doesn't fall down when she starts crawling. Two bedrooms. Electric storage heaters."

"Electric," Arthur said.

"They're seventy pounds a week on the key meter," Sophie said. "Callum's sister gave us two oil radiators for the winter. In case the storage ones don't throw enough heat. The living room looks out on the motorway bridge. It's loud with the trucks, but at least the roof doesn't leak into the light fittings."

"It has an enclosed close?" Arthur asked.

"A concrete balcony," Sophie said. Her voice had no bitterness in it; it was simply tired, the flat resignation of someone who had spent four months filling out housing benefit appeal forms in an unheated council waiting room. "Open to the wind from the river. You have to carry the pram up forty steps every time you come back from the chemist. Callum tried to get the ground-floor flat in the block behind the clinic, but the housing officer said priority goes to medical cases with mobility impairment."

She looked directly at Arthur then. Her brown eyes were steady, without anger, but with an absolute clarity that made Arthur’s right knee ache in its socket.

"Like you, Mr. Cross," she said quietly. "Priority rehousing."

Arthur did not answer. He looked down at the pavement where a trail of yellow sandstone crumbs marked Callum’s path from the close door to the rear of the van.

Callum came down the steps again. This time he carried his grandfather’s wooden tool chest, three feet of solid ash reinforced with hand-beaten iron corner straps. The chest weighed eighty pounds empty; with John Reid’s heavy ball-peen hammers, cold chisels, brass-bound rules, and steel drift pins, it took both of Callum's hands to carry it, his back bent double under the strain.

Arthur stepped to the edge of the curb. "Let me take the rope handle, Callum."

"Keep clear," Callum spat. His shoulder struck the van flank as he swung the chest onto the tailgate. The iron corners bit into the painted floor with a sharp screech of metal. "This chest came out of Scotts' yard in forty-eight. It was carried through the gate the day my granddad finished his time on the slipway. It doesn't get touched by someone who signs for the developers."

"Your granddad was my friend, Callum," Arthur said quietly. "We drank in the Argyll Bar every Friday night for thirty years."

"Then you should have remembered him when Ward put the voucher on your table," Callum said. "Granddad would have put his boot through that fancy glass screen across the road. He would have told the factor that forty years on the river gave a man the right to die under his own roof."

Callum turned back toward the dark entrance of the close, his boots taking the bottom steps with violent, deliberate thuds.

Arthur stood on the pavement. Beside him, Sophie rocked the child in a slow, rhythmic circle, her sneakers scuffing on the grit.

"He doesn't mean it all, Mr. Cross," Sophie said softly, without looking up from the blanket. "He's just frightened. When you're twenty-four and they put you in a scheme ten miles from where you were born, you feel like the ground's been cut out from under your feet. He has no mates in Erskine. There's no work on the river there. Just the shopping center and the bus turning circle."

"He's a good joiner," Arthur said.

"Nobody wants joiners for old timber now," Sophie murmured. "It's all dry lining and plastic clips. He spent three days applying for maintenance jobs at the new marina. Ward's firm told him all facility staff had to be sub-contracted through a security contractor in Leeds. Minimum wage. Zero-hour contracts. He tore the application up on the kitchen table."

A heavy crash sounded from inside the tenement stairwell. It was followed by the loud splintering of wood and a short, venomous curse from Callum.

Arthur turned and walked back into the close.

On the half-landing between the ground and first floors, Callum stood over the wardrobe. It was a modern flat-pack carcass of dark walnut-effect chipboard, six feet high by three feet wide. In negotiating the narrow turn where the stairwell curved around the old coal chute, the top corner of the wardrobe had jammed against the protruding stone corbel. When Callum had wrenched it free, the particle board had split along the dowel line, exposing a raw, yellowish interior of glued sawdust and broken plastic fittings.

Callum stood against the wall, his hands dangling at his sides. His chest was heaving under his flannel shirt.

"Damn it," he whispered. "Damn the whole lot of it."

Twenty years ago, when the boy had tangled his fishing line around the iron ladder of the quay, Arthur had sat beside him on the wet granite for forty minutes with an open penknife, patiently working out the snarled loops of monofilament line, one coil at a time. *Patience, Callum. Metal will cut if you force it, but nylon just snaps and you lose your trace. Easy does it.*

Now Arthur stepped up two steps. His knee gave off its familiar dry crack.

"We can carry it flat," Arthur said. "Take the bottom rail. I'll take the top corners. We lift together and tilt it thirty degrees to clear the lintel."

Callum looked up. His face was twisted with fury, his eyes glittering in the gloom of the half-landing.

"Get your hands off it," Callum snarled. His voice cracked, the boy's voice breaking through the man's throat. "Don't you touch my things. Don't you dare touch one stick of my family's furniture."

"Callum, it's just a wardrobe—"

"It's my house!" Callum shouted. The shout bounced off the forty-foot sandstone well of the stairwell, rattling the glass in the roof skylight four floors above. "It was my home! My granddad lived in that flat before you even had a pension! He worked forty years on the fitting berths! And you sold us out to that slick bastard in the fleece for twenty square yards of underfloor heating and a wet room!"

Arthur stood three steps below him. The boy's breath was hot, smelling of black coffee and raw anger.

"The council had the demolition order signed in March, Callum," Arthur said, his voice dropping into the quiet, steady rhythm he had used on drunk passengers on the midnight bus from Gourock. "Ward didn't invent the order. The foundations are moving. You've seen the crack in the cellar wall. You could put your arm into it."

"He bought the order!" Callum yelled. "He sat in the planning office with his maps and his glossy brochures, showing them the luxury flats and the marina berths, and he told them we were slums! He told them twelve working-class families were holding back forty million pounds of dockland development! And when we stood up to him, when we got the petition together, when we were ready to fight the sheriff officer at the door—you took his bloody deal!"

Callum stepped down one step. He towered over Arthur, his work boots coated in white lime dust.

"You took his key," Callum said, his voice dropping to a harsh, shaking whisper. "You signed your name on his register. The moment you signed, the factor told the sheriff that the senior tenant had accepted alternative accommodation, and the resistance fell apart. Danuta was too old to fight alone. The Murrays took their two thousand pounds and ran. And now look at us."

He pointed his thumb toward the open doorway where the white van stood in the sunlight.

"Sophie's twenty-three," Callum said. "The bairn has bronchitis every winter. And we're going to a concrete box in Erskine where the wind comes through the window frames, because you didn't want to walk up two flights of stone stairs."

Arthur did not move. He did not drop his eyes. The words hit him like the cold spray off the Albert Quay pierhead forty years ago, freezing his skin, leaving a taste of salt and iron in his mouth.

"I have four years left, Callum," Arthur said. "Maybe five."

"Then you should have died on your own stairs," Callum said.

He bent down, gripped the splintered particle-board wardrobe by its center rail, and lifted it with a savage, grunting heave. The broken chipboard groaned as he hauled it down the remaining steps, dragging the lower edge across the sandstone flags of the lobby without looking back.

Arthur stood on the half-landing. Through the stairwell window, he watched Callum wrestle the wardrobe through the doorway and into the back of the white van. Sophie climbed into the passenger seat, holding Maya against her chest. Callum kicked the wooden chock out from under the rear wheel, tossed it into the gutter, and pulled the van's heavy barn doors shut.

The latch caught with a hollow, double thud of cheap sheet steel. Callum climbed into the driver's seat. The starter motor whined three times, coughing, before the petrol engine caught with a rough, clattering idle.

The van pulled out from the curb into the lane of Albert Quay, its nearside tire clipping the granite curbstone as it turned east toward the motorway junction.

The van door slammed shut and the exhaust smoke drifted across the empty street.

# Chapter 35: Yellow Paper

The excavator was a Komatsu PC490 with a three-piece demolition boom that reached eighty feet into the morning sky. It was painted canary yellow, spattered to the cab roof with dried grey concrete slurry. In place of a digging bucket, the end of the boom carried a heavy hydraulic pulverizer—two four-foot steel jaws lined with replaceable tungsten-carbide teeth that could exert three hundred tons of crushing force at the tips.

Arthur Cross watched it from the wing-back armchair in Flat G-02. He had a mug of instant coffee on the glass side table.

The day was clear and sharp, an October Tuesday with the wind blowing straight off the snow on Ben Lomond, forty miles up the firth. Across Albert Quay, the demolition contractor’s water bowser was spraying a fine mist of river water over the roof of Baltic Buildings to suppress the lime dust. The spray caught the morning sun, forming a faint, shimmering rainbow that arched between the chimney stacks and the telephone poles.

The Komatsu operator swung the cab ninety degrees.

The long steel boom rose with a high, hydraulic whine that was completely inaudible behind Arthur’s triple-glazed window. Arthur saw only the movement: the twin rams extending, gleaming silver rods six inches thick sliding out of their black steel cylinders. The pulverizer jaws opened wide like the bill of a pelican.

The steel teeth clamped down on the stone coping of the south chimney stack.

It was the stack that served Flat 2B. Four flues of firebrick encased in twelve inches of dressed sandstone ashlar, tied together with lime mortar mixed with horsehair and river sand in eighteen-ninety.

The jaws closed.

There was no sound in Arthur's room, but the visual impact was immediate. The sandstone did not shatter; it crushed into a fine, salmon-pink meal that cascaded down the slate valley like flour poured from a sack. The four terra-cotta chimney cans—two tall octagonal pots and two round roll-top cans—wobbled, tipped inward, and disappeared down the vertical brick shafts with an explosion of black soot that erupted from the bedroom fireplaces forty feet below.

In July of nineteen-eighty-four, Arthur had stood in the scullery of Flat 2B on a pair of wooden stepladders with a six-inch paperhanger’s brush in his right hand.

The temperature in the tenement had been ninety degrees under the slates. Mary had been five months pregnant with the baby they would lose in the autumn; she had been sitting on an upturned sugar box by the open window, fanning herself with an issue of the *Sunday Post*, her cotton apron pulled tight over her swollen belly.

"You're getting paste on the woodwork, Arthur," she had said. Her voice had been light, laughing at him. "Mr. MacFarlane will deduct ten shillings from the deposit if there's cold-water paste on the architraves."

"The paste washes off with a damp rag, Mary," he had told her.

He had bought six rolls of wallpaper from the decorator's shop beside the central station in Greenock. It was an embossed vinyl paper, washable, patterned with a dense lattice of yellow cowslips and green ivy leaves over a pale butter-colored ground. It had cost three pounds fifty a roll, an extravagance on his conductor’s wage, but Mary had wanted the scullery bright. *Tenement kitchens are too dark in the winter, Arthur. You spend all December looking at soot and grey clouds over the timber ponds. I want something that looks like the sun is shining even when the rain's coming down the chimney.*

Arthur had pasted each length on a trestle table made of two scaffold planks laid across the horsehair chairs. He had folded the wet paper into thirds, carried it up the ladder with the edges balanced on his wrists, and laid the top edge against the lath-and-plaster ceiling. He had smoothed the paper down with a horsehair smoothing brush, working from the center outward to push the air bubbles toward the seams.

When the seam had refused to butt flat against the uneven stone corner, Mary had stood up, taken a wooden seam roller from his tool tray, and pressed the damp vinyl into the plaster with her small thumb.

"There," she had said, her thumb white from the pressure. "That won't move until Kingdom come."

Now, across Albert Quay, the Komatsu’s pulverizer bit into the second floor of Baltic Buildings.

The machine’s teeth tore through the front wall of Flat 2B with the ease of an iron spade cutting through wet peat. A six-foot section of blonde ashlar broke free, tumbling through the floorboards of the living room and crashing into the flat below.

The interior of Arthur’s kitchen was suddenly exposed to the open air.

Arthur leaned forward in the armchair, his coffee mug forgotten in his hands.

There it was.

The scullery wall behind the gas cooker. The yellow wallpaper with the pattern of cowslips and ivy leaves was still there, bright and clean against the grey masonry. Forty years of boiling potatoes, frying mutton chops, and Mary’s chamomile tea had left a faint yellowing of grease near the ceiling, but the paper itself had held. It had not peeled. It had not rotted. It clung to the hundred-year-old lath-and-plaster with the stubborn grip of cold-water starch mixed with carpenter's glue.

Beside the wallpaper hung the four-inch cast-iron gas pipe that had fed the cooker, severed at the floor, swinging loose in the morning wind. On the plaster above the press, the pencil mark Arthur had made in nineteen-eighty-five to mark the height of Callum’s father was still visible as a small, dark cross: *J. Reid, 5ft 10in, May 85*.

The Komatsu operator paused. The machine idled, its long boom poised above the roofline like an insect inspecting a carcass.

A gust of October wind swept in from the river, catching the water mist from the contractor’s bowser. The cold spray drifted directly across the exposed kitchen of Flat 2B.

The yellow wallpaper began to darken.

As the moisture soaked into the embossed vinyl, the paper loosened from the horsehair plaster behind it. A three-foot strip along the chimney breast tore free, peeling downward in a slow, elegant curl, like birch bark curling off a dead log in the woods. The wind caught the loose end, whipping it back and forth against the red brickwork of the flue.

Arthur remembered the afternoon they had finished the room.

They had sat on the bare floorboards with their backs against the newly papered wall, drinking cold tea from enamel mugs because the stove had not been reconnected. Mary had rested her head on his shoulder, her dark hair smelling of vinegar and lavender water.

"It looks like a garden, Arthur," she had murmured, her eyes half-shut against the afternoon light. "It doesn't look like a tenement at all. It looks like a cottage in Perthshire."

"It's a tenement on Albert Quay, Mary," he had answered, smiling. "The coal dust will have the yellow grey by November."

"No it won't," she had said, turning her face into his neck. "I won't let it. I'll wash it every Saturday morning with sugar soap."

She had washed it every Saturday for thirty-four years, until her fingers grew too crooked from the arthritis to hold the sponge.

Across the road, the Komatsu cab swung sharply.

The pulverizer moved aside, and the operator switched attachments, dropping the heavy steel jaws onto a timber pallet and picking up a four-ton hydraulic hammer with a hardened steel chisel bit.

The chisel dropped into the bay window of Flat 2B.

The impact tore through the three sandstone mullions simultaneously. The lintel—a single six-foot block of dressed ashlar that had supported the roof load since the reign of Queen Victoria—cracked down its center with a violent, jagged fracture.

The window casing, the pine boxing that had held the brass sash weights, and the four remaining sheets of antique crown glass dissolved into a cloud of red dust, splinters, and flying lead.

Arthur Cross sat in his armchair behind his triple-glazed window.

His knees were warm. The floor beneath his feet was warm. His coffee cup was warm against his fingers.

The excavator bucket bit into the sandstone architrave, turning the bay window into rubble.

# Chapter 21: The Frozen Close

The cold came into Baltic Buildings within four hours of the gas shutoff.

Without the steady, low blue flame of twelve gas cookers and parlor fires warming the masonry flues, the red sandstone acted as a giant cold sink, drawing the raw sea damp out of the harbor and trapping it between the walls. By five o'clock that evening, a fine skin of ice had formed on the inside of the sash windowpanes in Arthur's kitchen.

Arthur sat in his overcoat, his hands tucked inside his driving gloves, breathing through his woollen scarf.

His electric bar fire was plugged into the single wall socket beside the scullery door. The fire was an ancient twin-rod heater with a dented tin reflector, throwing a narrow beam of orange warmth that barely reached three feet across the linoleum. When he stood close to it, his shins baked while his shoulder blades froze.

He thought of Danuta on the ground floor.

He picked up his walking stick, took a small plastic torch from the dresser, and walked out onto the landing.

The close was pitch-black. A fuse had blown in the communal lighting circuit on Tuesday, and Vanguard's emergency repair line had played a recorded message stating that routine electrical maintenance was suspended pending structural survey reports. Arthur shone his yellow torch beam down the spiral stair. The red sandstone steps were glistening with a treacherous glaze of moisture that was already turning to ice near the arched windows.

He took the stairs one by one, his walking stick clicking on the stone, counting each riser out loud: "One... two... three..."

At the bottom of the well, he tapped on the door of Flat 1A.

"Danuta?" he called through the timber.

The latch clicked. The door opened two inches, restrained by the brass chain. Danuta’s face appeared in the torchlight. She was wrapped in three woollen coats, one over the other, her head tied with a thick black babushka scarf, her thick spectacles fogged white with frost. Behind her, the hallway of Flat 1A was dark and freezing, smelling of damp wool and cold cabbage.

"Arthur," she whispered, her teeth chattering audibly. "The gas is gone."

"I know, Danuta. Let me in."

She unhooked the chain with stiff, trembling fingers. Arthur stepped across the cold tiles into her small front room.

In the center of the room, on a tin baking sheet on the floor, sat a single two-bar electric radiator that Callum had brought down from his flat that afternoon. The element glowed faint orange, struggling against the freezing draft that whistled under the skirting boards from the boarded-up wash-houses below. On the table sat a plastic electric kettle connected to an extension lead running from the bedroom socket.

Danuta sat down in her wooden rocking chair, pulling an old horse-hair blanket over her knees.

"I cannot sew, Arthur," she said, holding up her right hand. The fingers were swollen, blue at the tips, the skin cracked across the knuckles. "When the cold gets into the joints, the needle drops. I dropped three needles on the carpet. I cannot find them in the dark."

"You need to go to your sister's house, Danuta," Arthur said gently, sitting on the wooden stool beside her.

"My sister died four years ago in Nottingham, Arthur. You know this." She looked at him with watery eyes behind her thick lenses. "I have no sister. I have no one in this country. St. Mary’s church is cold too; Father Stanislaw says the boiler broke on Wednesday. Where would an old woman go?"

Arthur looked at her shivering shoulders. He felt the white plastic keycard in his waistcoat pocket, burning cold against his ribs.

Flat G-02 was running right now. Geothermal underfloor heat at twenty-one degrees. Warm oak floors. A walk-in shower where steam filled the air at the turn of a brass lever.

He reached into his overcoat pocket and pulled out his tin of wintergreen balm.

"Give me your hands, Danuta," Arthur said.

He scooped a thick lump of the white ointment onto his palms, took her swollen, frozen fingers in his own, and began rubbing the balm into her knuckles with slow, firm strokes. The medicinal smell flared in the freezing room, sharp and clean. Danuta closed her eyes, a single tear escaping her gray lashes and running down her cheek into the wool of her scarf.

Arthur sat on the low wooden stool in Danuta's scullery for an hour, watching the orange glow of the two-bar electric heater fade as the voltage fluctuated on the old communal wiring circuit.

Every ten minutes, the single electric bar would hum with a low, vibrating buzz, its orange coils dimming to a dull cherry red before slowly brightening again. The wiring in Baltic Buildings had not been re-cabled since nineteen sixty-two; when three electric heaters were switched on simultaneously across the flats, the voltage dropped throughout the block.

Danuta’s hands were warmer now, the skin softened by the wintergreen balm, but her breathing remained shallow and rapid.

"My grandmother had a tiled stove in Gdynia," she whispered, her voice wandering in the dark room. "Green ceramic tiles, six feet high, built into the corner of the parlor. We burned birch logs and brown coal from Silesia. When the stove was hot, you could lean your back against the tiles and sleep like a child in the sun. The warmth stayed in the clay until morning."

"We had cast-iron grates in Maryhill when I was a lad," Arthur said softly, his voice keeping her company in the cold. "Burned hard anthracite from the pits at Hamilton. My mother kept a flat-iron on the hob all day; at night she wrapped it in a piece of old flannel and put it at the foot of our bed. If your toes touched the iron, it would blister the skin, but you didn't care because the sheets were like ice."

Danuta smiled weakly, her spectacles reflecting the dull orange bars on the floor.

"We are old people, Arthur. We remember the stoves and the flat-irons. But young Callum, he has only the electric meter. He puts two pound coins in the slot, and in two hours the meter clicks and the room goes dark."

She looked at her Singer sewing machine by the window. The black cast-iron frame sat motionless beneath the gray light from the steel hoarding outside.

"Tomorrow I go to Father Stanislaw," she said, her voice firming slightly. "I ask him if the parish can lend two oil heaters from the church hall. The church has the paraffin heaters they use for the bazaar."

"Paraffin smells, Danuta," Arthur warned. "In a closed room with no gas flue, the fumes will get on your chest."

"Fumes are better than ice, Arthur," she said simply.

Arthur stood up from the stool. His own knees gave a loud, dry crunch as he straightened his legs, the pain shooting up into his lower back like a hot wire. He pulled his overcoat tighter around his chest, checking the flashlight in his pocket.

"Lock your door behind me, Danuta," Arthur said. "Put the chain on."

"Goodnight, Arthur," she murmured.

He stepped out onto the freezing landing. The stone well was dark as a coal mine, the wind whistling through the cracked panes above with a thin, continuous screech that sounded like an animal trapped in the roof.

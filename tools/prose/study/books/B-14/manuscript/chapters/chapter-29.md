# Chapter 29: The Hollow Well

The dusk fell late on Saturday, lingering over the firth until after ten o'clock like an unwashed window. Arthur Cross did not turn on the ceiling pendant in the front room. Electricity was forty-two pence a unit on the Scottish Power tariff, and the meter in the scullery had only three pounds twenty remaining on the electronic key. He sat by the bay window in the wing-back armchair, his feet propped on an overturned fruit crate he had salvaged from the grocer’s bins behind the post office.

Outside, Albert Quay was dead. The demolition crews had knocked off at noon, leaving the yellow Caterpillar excavator parked against the perimeter fence with its hydraulic arm resting on a heap of crushed granite road metal. The site floodlights had not been switched on; only a single orange sodium vapor lamp burned above the harbor master's slipway, casting long, greasy amber streaks across the low-water mud.

In the whole four-story tenement block of Baltic Buildings, twelve flats in all, only two windows showed life: Arthur’s bay on the second floor, and the narrow scullery pane of Flat 1A directly below, where Danuta Kowalski was boiling water on her gas ring.

Arthur leaned his head back against the horsehair antimacassar.

The acoustic behavior of the building had altered completely in forty-eight hours. When ten families had lived within its forty-foot walls, the sandstone had acted as a vast, porous sponge, soaking up eighty years of radio broadcasts, frying lard, crying children, coughing men, and the soft, rhythmic scrape of linoleum slippers on timber floors. Sound had never traveled freely; it had leaked through floorboards, muffled by three inches of horsehair pugging and lath-and-plaster ceilings, emerging in neighboring rooms as a comforting, ambient hum that told you precisely where everyone was between dawn and bedtime.

Now the pugging had dried to dust, the doors were sealed with steel plates, and the ten empty apartments functioned as resonant chambers.

Every sound produced in the stairwell was amplified fourfold. At half-past ten, a loose piece of zinc flashing on the roof cupola flapped in a sudden gust off the water. The vibration carried straight down the cast-iron rainwater downpipe that ran through the scullery wall, sounding like a muffled pistol shot against the masonry. Arthur started, his right hand gripping the arm of the chair. His heart gave two heavy, stumbling thuds before settling back into its slow rhythm.

Ten minutes later, the pipes in the building began to drain.

The council water department had cut the main three-inch lead riser to the upper floors at noon, capping the branch to Flat 3A and 3B with brass compression plugs to prevent freezing damage during the autumn demolition. With the pressure gone, the water remaining in the attic cisterns was siphoning back down through the vertical stacks.

In the wall behind Arthur’s armchair, the lead waste pipe let out a long, shuddering gurgle. It was a wet, hollow rattle, like air being sucked through a straw into an empty tin, ending in three sharp, rhythmic metallic knocks as an air lock hit the cast-iron trap under Danuta’s sink.

"Just water," Arthur said into the darkness of the room.

His voice sounded strange to his own ears. It did not sound like the voice he used when speaking to Callum or the bus depot supervisor. Stripped of the soft furnishings, with the carpets rolled and the curtains down, the bare floorboards bounced his words back at him with a thin, reedy echo that felt entirely detached from his chest.

He stood up from the armchair. His knee joint crackled sharply in the stillness, the sound traveling across the bare pine boards like dry twigs snapping underfoot.

He walked into the hallway and opened the storm door an inch.

The stairwell was pitch black. The seventy-five-watt bulb on the second-floor landing had burned out on Thursday, and the factor’s office had refused to dispatch an electrician for a property subject to an active removal order. The only light came from the top cupola, where the pale northern twilight filtered through four panes of wire-reinforced glass, illuminating the suspension of lime dust that hung permanently in the air.

Arthur stood on the threshold, listening.

Down on the ground floor, forty feet below, a door latch scraped. It was not the crisp click of a Yale lock; it was the heavy, dragging friction of an old mortise bolt being drawn back against a warped pine casing.

Footsteps followed.

They were slow, hesitant, dragging. *Scuff-tap. Scuff-tap.*

In the cavernous emptiness of the stairwell, each contact of shoe leather against sandstone rang with terrifying clarity. The acoustics turned Danuta’s small, sixty-kilogram frame into the tread of a giant. The stone risers gathered the scrape of her felt slippers, funneling the frequency upward through the spiral well until it reached Arthur’s landing with the volume of someone walking three feet away in heavy work boots.

Arthur leaned over the iron banister.

"Danuta?" he called down into the blackness.

The name ricocheted off the blonde ashlar walls. *Danuta. Danuta. Nuta.*

The footsteps stopped instantly.

Silence fell back over the stairwell, thick and suffocating, smelling of damp cellar lime, cold soot, and the bitter tang of the zinc screens bolted across Callum’s door.

For nearly thirty seconds, neither of them moved. Arthur could hear the faint, wet whistle of Danuta’s breathing rising from the first landing—the shallow, rapid panting of congestive heart failure struggling against twenty steps of stairs.

"Arthur?" her voice came back. It was small, cracked at the register, carrying that slight Polish lift she had never lost in sixty-five years on the Clyde. "You are awake?"

"Aye," Arthur said. "I’m awake."

"The water," she said, her voice echoing off the flags. "The cold tap in the scullery. It makes a crying noise. Like a cat inside the wall."

"It's just the cistern draining, Danuta. They capped the upper riser at lunchtime. It's pulling air through the traps."

"It is dark," she whispered. The sound of her hand running along the pitch-pine banister drifted up—the soft, papery friction of dry skin against smooth timber. "The council boys took the bulb from my door. They said the wiring is condemned."

"They're not allowed to touch the lighting until Monday," Arthur said, though he knew the words were meaningless. The council boys did whatever Marcus Ward’s schedule instructed them to do.

"It doesn't matter," Danuta said. "I am going to bed now. The ambulance comes at eight. The social lady told me eight o'clock sharp."

"Do you have your tea?"

"I have two cups of milk left," she said. "The social lady brought two plastic cartons. They have blue foil lids. You peel them with your fingernail. They taste of tin."

"Get some rest, Danuta."

"Good night, Arthur."

Her slippers dragged across the stone landing below. The heavy timber door of Flat 1A creaked on its brass hinges, swung into the frame, and closed with that deep, familiar *thump* that Arthur had heard forty thousand times since nineteen-sixty-eight. Then the brass bolt slid home: *snick*.

Then nothing.

Arthur remained at the banister for a long time. The pale twilight from the cupola was fading now, the glass turning from slate grey to a deep, bruised purple.

Across from him on the landing, the perforated steel screen over Callum’s door was an absolute void. No light leaked around its edges. When Callum had lived there, a line of yellow electric light had always burned under the door until midnight, accompanied by the tinny murmur of the television in the front room, the rhythmic squeak of Sophie’s rocking chair, or the sudden, furious wail of Maya demanding milk at two in the morning.

Arthur had complained about that crying three winters ago. He had met Callum on the half-landing with an empty coal scuttle and said, *The bairn’s lungs are fair healthy, Callum, but she doesn't half make a din through the floorboards when I’m trying to catch six hours before the morning run.*

Callum had looked embarrassed then, ducking his chin into his woolen scarf. *Sorry, Mr. Cross. Her teeth are coming through. Sophie’s walking the floor with her till three.*

Arthur would have given fifty pounds tonight to hear that child cry through the floorboards.

He stepped back into Flat 2B. He did not lock his storm door; there was no point in locking a door in an empty building protected by three tons of galvanized steel screens and an orange warning notice from the sheriff officer. He walked through the dark hallway into the scullery.

The scullery was stripped to its bones. On the floorboards, four round grease marks showed where the feet of the gas stove had rested since nineteen-seventy-one. The cold-water tap above the porcelain sink gave off a faint, rhythmic drip that fell into the dry waste pipe with a hollow *plink*, followed by the hiss of air being sucked into the empty lead trap.

He walked through to the back bedroom.

The room smelled of old horsehair plaster, camphor balls, and the damp wallpaper behind where Mary’s wardrobe had stood for forty years. Through the uncurtained sash window, the lights of the container terminal across the river flickered through the rain, casting long, pale bars across the floorboards. On the picture rail, three rusty wire hooks hung empty, their brass picture wire severed with pliers when the framed prints of Loch Lomond had been packed into carton seven.

Arthur touched the wall. The plaster was cold, sweating moisture under his fingers where the salt from the maritime atmosphere had leached through the stone ashlar. In the chimney breast, the cast-iron bedroom grate was choked with soot that had fallen from the flue when the council roofers had removed the lead chimney flashing.

He returned to the living room.

The room smelled of stale parcel tape, cold hearth tiles, and the menthol ointment he had rubbed into his knees after dinner.

He did not light a candle. He sat down in the wing-back armchair by the window. Across the road, The Meridian stood entirely black against the night sky, its six stories of steel and smoked glass rising like a monolithic wall between the street and the river. Not a single light burned in its eighty luxury apartments; the marketing suite on the ground floor was dark, its plate-glass windows reflecting only the distant amber flare of the harbor slipway.

Somewhere down in the harbor basin, a buoy bell chimed once on the swell—a faint, metallic *clink* carried on the south wind.

Arthur folded his hands in his lap. The skin across his knuckles was dry and rough, catching on the woolen weave of his trousers.

He sat in his armchair listening to the absolute, unnatural quiet of the building.

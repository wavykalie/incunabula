# Chapter 32: The Handover

The removals lorry was a six-ton DAF with a hydraulic tail-lift, painted in the cream and maroon livery of a family firm from Johnstone. It stood backed into the courtyard of Baltic Buildings at seven-thirty on Monday morning, its diesel exhaust puffing blue smoke against the wet sandstone archway. Two men in green polo shirts—one older with grey sideburns, the other a lad of twenty with a silver hoop in his ear—were carrying Arthur’s wing-back armchair down the stairs on a padded webbing strap.

"Mind the corbel on the first turn, Stevie," the older driver said.

"Got it, Tam," the lad answered from below.

Arthur Cross stood on the second-floor landing. His two suitcases sat beside his boots: one brown compressed-fiber case that had belonged to his father, and one plaid zippered holdall Mary had bought at the Arnotts summer sale in nineteen-eighty-seven. In his left hand he held the polished copper kettle by its horn handle.

Behind him, Flat 2B was an empty box.

The floors were bare pine boards, grey with the dust of fifty years of walking, showing the dark rectangular rings where the hearth rugs and runner carpets had protected the timber from the light. On the chimney breast, the pale oval of unpainted plaster showed where the oval bevel-edged mirror had hung since nineteen-seventy-one. The copper gas pipe that had fed the gas fire stuck out of the floorboards like an amputated thumb, its end sealed with a brass compression cap.

Arthur walked back into the living room one last time.

His footsteps had no weight in the room; without furniture to baffle the sound, every shoe click bounced between the bare walls with a dry, hollow snap. He looked at the bay window. The view had not changed: the grey expanse of the Clyde, the distant green hump of Rosneath peninsula, the red navigational buoy marking the edge of the dredged channel.

He reached into his cardigan pocket and drew out his keys.

There were two keys on the split ring: the small, nickel-plated Yale key with the notched teeth that operated the night-latch on the inner storm door, and the heavy, six-inch Victorian mortise key of solid turned brass that threw the double bolt into the stone doorpost.

He walked out onto the landing. He pulled the inner timber door shut behind him. The spring-loaded latch engaged with that crisp, oiled *clack* he had heard every evening after the late bus shift.

He inserted the heavy brass key into the lower escutcheon. The iron lock mechanism was stiff; it had not been turned with the key from the outside in twenty years, because Arthur had always used the night-latch when popping down to the baker or the post office. He leaned his shoulder against the timber to ease the pressure on the bolt, and turned the key clockwise.

The internal levers lifted one by one: *click-click-clunk*.

The heavy brass bolt shot home three inches into the seasoned oak socket mortised into the stone wall.

Arthur withdrew the key. The metal was cold against his palm, carrying the greasy residue of three-in-one machine oil.

He picked up his two suitcases. Carrying a suitcase in each hand with the kettle hooked over his left wrist, he walked toward the stairs. His right knee gave its customary dull crunch on the first step, but his mind did not register the pain; his attention was fixed entirely on the balance of the load and the forty-eight steps remaining between his boots and the pavement.

Step-by-step. Down the sixteen steps to the third floor.

Past Henderson’s bolted door.

Down the sixteen steps to the second landing.

Past Callum’s perforated steel screen.

Down the sixteen steps to the ground floor.

In the courtyard, the tail-lift of the DAF lorry hummed as the electric motor hoisted the armchair into the cargo box. Tam lashed the chair to the internal timber rail with a ratchet strap, the ratchet clicking three times: *zip-zip-clink*.

"That's the lot, Mr. Cross," Tam called down, wiping his palms on his trousers. "Table, four chairs, bedstead, mattress, chest of drawers, and twenty-two boxes. Stevie’s already walked the small stuff across to G-zero-two. He says the management left the keys in the letterbox."

"Thank you, Tam," Arthur said.

Across the courtyard, by the site hoarding, Marcus Ward stood talking to the Vanguard demolition supervisor. The supervisor was a thickset man named Henderson—no relation to the fitter upstairs—wearing an orange weatherproof jacket and steel-toed wellingtons coated in grey slurry. Between them, on an easel made of two timber battens, stood the architectural site plan for The Meridian Phase Two: eighty luxury units, underground parking, private pontoon moorings.

Ward saw Arthur approaching. He touched the brim of his hard hat, said a brief word to the supervisor, and walked across the asphalt.

"Morning, Arthur," Ward said. He had on a spotless yellow hard hat and a high-visibility vest over his fleece. His face was fresh, scrubbed clean, his breath smelling faintly of peppermint lozenges. "Punctual as ever. The removals team says the transfer went smoothly."

"They're good lads," Arthur said.

"They're professionals," Ward replied. "Vanguard only uses accredited logistics contractors. Flat G-zero-two is ready for occupancy. The power is live, the broadband router is connected, and the underfloor heating has been commissioned to twenty-one degrees. You’ll find the welcome documentation on the kitchen island."

Arthur looked across the street. The Meridian looked massive in the morning light, its grey composite panels and tinted glass balconies reflecting the dull grey sky over the river. On the ground floor, three large picture windows marked Flat G-02. Through the glass, he could see Stevie setting a cardboard box on the laminate kitchen counter.

"The building is empty," Arthur said.

"I know," Ward said. He produced an aluminum clipboard from under his arm. "Mrs. Kowalski was collected at eight-fifteen by the Inverclyde patient transport service. I spoke to the liaison officer; she’s already settled in her room in Greenock. Very comfortable. Garden view."

Ward unclipped a printed form from the clipboard: *FORM 4B: CERTIFICATE OF VACANT POSSESSION AND TERMINATION OF RESIDENTIAL OCCUPANCY*. He held out a black ballpoint pen.

"Just your signature on the bottom line, Arthur. To confirm that all personal effects have been removed and vacant possession has been surrendered to Vanguard Urban Regeneration Limited."

Arthur set down his father’s fiber suitcase on the wet asphalt. He took the pen.

The paper was stiff, moisture-resistant bond. Arthur pressed the tip against the dotted line beside the black cross: *A. Cross*. His signature was small, compact, the handwriting of an old clerk who had spent thirty years recording running numbers in a bus conductor's waybill.

He handed the pen back to Ward.

Ward checked the signature, nodded with satisfaction, and clipped the form back onto the board. He reached out his right hand, palm upward, fingers slightly curved.

"And the keys, Arthur."

Arthur reached into his pocket. He brought out the brass mortise key and the small nickel Yale key on their split ring. The brass key was long, its bit heavy with four stepped notches cut into the metal seventy years ago.

He held it for a second over Ward’s outstretched palm.

The excavator across the quay fired its engine with a throaty roar of diesel exhaust. The hydraulic breaker rose four feet into the air, its steel point poised over the stone corner of Baltic Buildings.

Arthur let go of the ring.

The brass key dropped into the manager's palm with a small, dull clink.

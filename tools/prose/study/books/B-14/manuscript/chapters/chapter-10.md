# Chapter 10: The Ground-Floor Plan

Marcus Ward laid the iPad on the low glass table between them. With a flick of his index finger, he zoomed in on the ground-floor architectural blueprint of The Meridian.

"This is Flat G-02," Ward said.

The digital blueprint expanded across the screen in crisp blue lines. The entrance door opened off a wide, carpeted interior gallery directly into an open-plan living room with a bay of triple-glazed glass facing east into a landscaped private courtyard. Off the living room lay a double bedroom with built-in cedar wardrobes, a scullery with integrated laundry appliances, and a bathroom that occupied twenty square feet of tiled floor space.

"Notice the threshold, Arthur," Ward murmured, his finger tracing the line between the communal gallery and the apartment hall. "Level. Completely flat. Not an eighth of an inch of a lip. You step from the carpeted entrance directly onto wide-plank oak flooring with zero elevation change."

Arthur leaned forward, his hands resting on his walking stick. His eyes moved over the blueprint, reading the dimensions: *Living Room: 6.2m x 4.8m. Bedroom: 4.5m x 3.6m. Bathroom: 3.2m x 2.4m.*

"No steps," Arthur said.

"Zero steps," Ward repeated. "From the pavement at Albert Quay, through the security airlock in the main foyer, right into your kitchen. If you take a taxi back from the city centre, the vehicle drops you under a heated porte-cochère ten yards from the lift core. If you go to the postbox, it’s twenty feet along an insulated, carpeted corridor kept at twenty-one degrees year-round."

Ward tapped the screen again. The bathroom blueprint expanded into a three-dimensional rendering.

"Look at the shower enclosure," Ward pointed out. "No plastic cubicle. No step-up rim to trip over with bad knees. It’s a wet-room design: continuous porcelain non-slip tiles running straight into a stainless linear floor drain. Thermostatic mixer valve with lever controls—easy to grip even if your fingers are stiff on cold mornings. Heated towel rails connected to the geothermal loop. And the floors throughout the entire apartment are underfloor hydronic heating. No iron radiators choking on air bubbles. The warmth rises evenly from the oak planks."

Arthur stared at the digital rendering. He thought of his kitchen in Baltic Buildings: the tin draught-excluder nailed to the bottom of the door, the cold linoleum that chilled through two pairs of woolen socks, the two-ring gas plate that smelled of coal gas, the lead cold-water pipe that coughed rust into Mary’s copper kettle every morning.

"What is the rent?" Arthur asked. His voice was gravelly, quiet.

Ward did not blink. He smiled, a warm, professional expression that seemed to acknowledge the blunt practicality of a working man.

"The commercial market rent for a ground-floor courtyard one-bedroom in The Meridian will be eighteen hundred and fifty pounds per calendar month," Ward said smoothly.

Arthur gave a short, dry chuckle in his throat. "My pension is seven hundred and twenty pounds a month, Mr. Ward. Out of that I pay council tax, gas, electric, and my union dues. I couldn't pay eighteen hundred pounds if I lived to be a hundred."

"I am not asking you to pay market rent, Arthur," Ward said. He rotated his gold pinky ring once, then leaned forward, resting his forearms on his knees. "I am proposing an elder statesman tenancy agreement. Vanguard will grant you a lifetime protected occupancy of Flat G-02 under a bespoke private covenant. Your rent will be fixed at exactly what you currently pay the harbor trust in Baltic Buildings: three hundred and eighty pounds per month, indexed to the basic state pension increase only. No service charges. No maintenance fees. Vanguard covers the building insurance, the window cleaning, and the heating loop standing charge for life."

Arthur looked at Ward's face. The man’s eyes were calm, gray-blue, showing neither malice nor hesitation.

"Why?" Arthur asked. "A property developer in Edinburgh doesn't give away fourteen hundred pounds a month out of Christian charity."

"Because time is money, Arthur," Ward answered without missing a beat. "In property development, litigation is the most expensive line item on the balance sheet. If Baltic Buildings forms a hostile tenant committee, if you tie us up in the sheriff court with legal aid appeals and delay our ground-breaking by twelve months, the interest on our acquisition loan costs Vanguard sixty thousand pounds a month. That’s seven hundred and twenty thousand pounds a year gone in bank interest."

Ward picked up his bone-china espresso cup, took a tiny sip, and set it down without a sound on the glass table.

"I can spend forty thousand pounds on court fees fighting you, or I can invest that capital into ensuring that the oldest, most respected tenant in the building is housed in comfort and safety for the rest of his days. It isn't charity, Arthur. It is intelligent commercial containment."

Arthur looked back at the blueprint. The blue lines of Flat G-02 seemed to glow on the dark glass of the iPad. Level thresholds. Underfloor heating. Zero steps.

Ward leaned across the glass table, his forearms resting on his knees, his gold signet ring catching the light from the recessed spotlight above.

"Look at the entrance door details, Arthur," Ward said, pointing his pen at the architectural schedule on the iPad screen. "A solid core timber-composite fire door. Forty-four millimeters thick, with double perimeter acoustic compression gaskets set into an extruded aluminium frame. When you close that door from the inside, the sound of the corridor is cut by forty-two decibels. If someone drops a suitcase on the carpet outside, you don't even hear a dull thud."

Arthur remembered the four-panel pitch-pine door of Flat 2B. When Callum Reid came home at midnight and dropped his steel-toed work boots onto the landing tiles, the concussion ran down the door stiles and through the brass Yale latch like a pistol shot.

"What about the windows?" Arthur asked.

"Triple-glazed laminated glass," Ward said. "Two panes of eight-millimeter toughened safety glass with a six-millimeter acoustic inter-layer, separated by sixteen-millimeter cavities filled with ninety percent pure argon gas. The exterior pane has a low-emissivity pyrolytic coating that reflects eighty-five percent of solar infrared while keeping internal heat inside the room. You can stand right against the glass in a February gale and feel no cold draft whatsoever."

"And the floor?"

"Cross-laminated timber floorboards over an acoustic isolation mat, resting on twenty-five millimeters of high-density insulation. Embedded in the screed are continuous loops of cross-linked polyethylene pipe circulating warm water at thirty-five degrees Celsius. The heat doesn't gather at the ceiling like it does with iron radiators; it radiates upward from the floor surface, warming your feet, your ankles, your knees. It's the ideal therapeutic climate for degenerative joint disease."

Arthur looked at Ward's face. The developer spoke with the quiet, authoritative precision of a physician explaining a clinical diagnosis.

"You know my medical history, Mr. Ward," Arthur said.

"We do our homework, Arthur," Ward said without hesitation. "When Vanguard enters a community, we make it our business to understand the specific needs of the long-term stakeholders. We know you drove the municipal buses for thirty-five years. We know you looked after your wife until her passing eight years ago. We know you have grade-four osteoarthritis in both knees. We know that climbing forty-eight sandstone steps twice a day is destroying your remaining cartilage."

Arthur sat back in the gray wool armchair. His hands gripped the horn handle of his walking stick.

"A businessman in Edinburgh doesn't care about a bus driver's knees," Arthur said.

"A businessman cares about certainty, Arthur," Ward replied softly. "A protracted eviction dispute in the sheriff court introduces commercial risk into our capital timeline. Certainty has a monetary value. By ensuring your personal welfare and providing you with a superior home, we eliminate that risk. It is an honorable, mutually beneficial arrangement."

Ward reached into his pocket and placed the magnetic keycard on the table. It sat beside Arthur's empty bone-china cup, pristine and white on the clear glass.

# Chapter 26: The Inventory of Small Things

The brass ticket punch weighed two pounds four ounces. It had a nickel-plated steel trigger and a heavy circular punch head stamped with the serial number 412. Arthur Cross laid it on the hall carpet runner next to three empty cardboard cartons. When he squeezed the handle, the internal spring clicked with a dull, oiled snick, exactly as it had at seven-fifteen on the morning route between Gourock Pier and the sugar refinery in nineteen-seventy-four.

Inside the leather ticket holster behind the punch lay a wad of stiff paper chits: two-pence child fares, four-pence adult singles, six-pence return transfers printed on pink card stock. The ink on the earliest tickets was faded to a faint sepia line, but the punched numbers remained crisp, punched clean through the cardboard by Arthur’s thumb fifty years before. He laid the punch into the middle box on top of a wool cardigan.

Beside the punch sat three round tins of Quality Street, their painted lids scratched down to bare tinplate at the rims. Arthur opened the first.

Inside lay thirty-two assorted brass keys on split rings, none of them labeled. There was a barrel key for an old mortise lock in a tenement in Port Glasgow where his parents had died; four identical brass padlock keys from his locker at the corporation bus depot; two small steel keys that had belonged to Mary’s singer sewing machine cabinet; and a heavy, square-ended key that operated the cast-iron gas meter box under the scullery sink. He picked up the gas key. The iron was cold, carrying the faint, sweet smell of coal gas from forty years ago.

He dropped the keys back into the tin with a sharp, tinny rattle. He pressed the lid down until the flange clicked home.

"Keep or toss," he muttered to the empty hallway.

He had three piles on the faded carpet runner. The left pile was for the refuse sacks: old bills from the South of Scotland Electricity Board, gas appliance manuals from nineteen-eighty-one, torn plastic rain capes, shoe brushes with bristles worn down to the wooden backs. The right pile was for the removals boxes: clothes, bedding, dishes, the brass carriage clock from the Corporation for twenty-five years of unblemished service. The middle pile was the problem. It contained items that possessed no commercial value, no present utility, but which could not be dropped into a black plastic refuse bag without a distinct physical spasm in the throat.

There was Mary’s tin of horn buttons, gathered from coats discarded before the shipyard closures. There was the small wooden box containing Arthur’s union cards: Transport and General Workers Union, Branch 7/114. The blue card from nineteen-seventy-two was stamped with forty-eight red ink squares, one for every week he had paid his four-shilling dues at the branch office above the baker's shop in Cathcart Street.

Arthur picked up the card. The card stock was stiff, worn soft at the corners where his wallet had pressed against it. His own signature from age twenty-eight was written in fountain pen across the header: *A. Cross, Conductor*. The ink was blue-black, leaning slightly to the right, confident, without the wobble that had entered his handwriting after the tremor began in his thumb three winters back.

He laid the union cards into the middle box beside the ticket punch.

He stood up. The movement produced a grinding click in his right patella, followed by a hot, narrow spike of pain that radiated up his thigh muscle toward the hip. He gripped the edge of the hall press until the muscle relaxed.

"Right," he said aloud. "First load."

The removals men were not coming until Monday, but Ward had given notice that the ground-floor storage vestibule in The Meridian was unlocked for early transfer of non-furniture items. Arthur did not want strangers handling Mary’s sewing tins or the tea service. He lifted the smallest carton, labeled *Books & Papers*, holding it against his stomach with both forearms. The carton weighed nineteen pounds.

He opened the storm door with his elbow, walked onto the stone landing, and pulled the door shut behind him. The brass latch caught with a firm *clack*.

The stairwell was cool, smelling of damp lime and the sharp reek of stone dust from the drilling outside. Arthur walked to the edge of the first step.

The stairs in Baltic Buildings were monolithic blocks of polished blonde sandstone, ten inches wide across the tread, seven inches high at the riser. In the center of each step, fifty years of iron-shod work boots, clogs, and rubber soles had scooped out a shallow, saucer-shaped hollow two inches deep. Arthur placed his left foot into the hollow of the top step. His knee accepted the weight with a familiar, dull complaint. Then he brought his right foot down to the same step.

He did not walk step-over-step anymore. He had not walked step-over-step down these forty-eight stairs since his sixty-fifth birthday. Every step required two beats: left foot down, right foot down to meet it; weight transferred, balance settled; then the next step down.

Step two. Left foot into the saucer hollow. Right foot following.

The banister rail on his left was four-inch pitch pine, rounded smooth as an egg by thousands of hands gripping it in the dark. It had been painted dark brown in nineteen-sixty and never touched since; the paint was rubbed down to the pale honey grain along the entire upper curve. Arthur pressed his left hip against the rail to take a fraction of the box's weight off his joints.

Step three. Left foot down. Right foot down.

The box of books pressed against his breastbone, making his breathing shallow. Through the thin cardboard he could feel the square corner of the *Scottish Bus Group Rulebook, 1965 Edition*.

On the third landing, outside Flat 3A, the court notice hung from two brass tacks in the doorframe. The paper was stiff legal vellum, eight inches by eleven, printed with the crest of the Sheriffdom of North Strathclyde. Arthur did not read it. He had read his own copy three days ago, before Marcus Ward had taken it away and replaced it with the relocation agreement. The words were identical on all four: *Notice to Remove under Section 19 of the Housing (Scotland) Act*.

He paused on the third landing, resting the bottom edge of the box on the wide wooden rail of the stairwell window. His pulse was thumping in his neck, a heavy, quick beat that felt like a fist knocking against the inside of his collar.

Through the dirty glass of the landing window, the river was flat and grey, the color of wet slate. A tugboat from the Clyde Port Authority was churning down-channel toward the Tail of the Bank, its low diesel growl muffled by the double brick of the stair wall. On the opposite bank, the hills above Helensburgh were lost in a low bank of maritime haze.

"Rest a minute," Arthur told himself.

His right knee felt hot under his woolen trousers, as though a mustard plaster had been applied directly over the cap. The cartilage was gone; Dr. MacIntyre had shown him the X-ray plates two years ago on the light box in the clinic. Two white shapes of bone grinding against each other with no daylight between them. *Bone on bone, Arthur. It’s what forty years on a Leyland bus platform does to a man.*

He picked up the carton again.

He descended the second flight, from the second landing to the first. Step-by-step. Left foot, right foot. Sixteen steps to the first floor.

Outside Danuta Kowalski’s door at Flat 1A, a smell of boiled cabbage and caraway seed leaked through the keyhole. It was the same smell that had greeted him on Saturday mornings for forty years. Arthur stopped outside her door. He considered knocking with his knuckle, but the box was slipping in his damp palms, the lower corner digging into the fat of his forearm. He moved past.

Sixteen more steps down to the street level.

The final flight was the steepest. The stone steps here had weathered more than the upper landings; decades of rain blown through the open storm doors had dissolved the lime from the mortar beneath the bottom three treads, giving them a slight, imperceptible tilt toward the street.

Arthur placed his left foot on the forty-sixth step. His foot settled into the depression. When his right foot came down, his boot heel caught the worn edge of the stone with a dry scrape.

His body lurched forward an inch. The carton shifted against his chest. For a split second his left knee buckled under the sudden lateral strain, a sharp tear of pain shooting from the ligament into the calf.

Arthur slammed his left shoulder against the damp stone wall of the stairwell. The rough plaster bit through his woolen cardigan, catching his tweed sleeve. The carton tipped, but he caught the underside with his right wrist, wedging the cardboard against the wall.

He froze. He did not breathe.

The stairwell was silent except for the distant thud of the hydraulic breaker across the road. A cold bead of sweat formed on his hairline and rolled slowly down his temple, tracking into the grey stubble along his jawline.

He stood pinned against the wall for twenty seconds, waiting for his knee to give way or hold.

The knee held. The joint felt loose, swollen with heat, but the bone remained in its groove.

Arthur took a deep breath. He took the forty-seventh step. Then the forty-eighth.

His feet reached the level stone flags of the ground-floor vestibule. The flags were broad slabs of Caithness slate, cool and dark, washed clean by Danuta every Monday morning with water from a zinc bucket.

He pushed open the heavy street door with his shoulder. The humid July heat hit him immediately, smelling of diesel exhaust, pulverized mortar, and the brackish mud exposed by the low tide along the dock wall.

Arthur did not carry the box across the road to The Meridian. His legs were shaking with a fine, rapid vibration that he could feel all the way to his hips. He set the carton of books down on the smooth Caithness flag inside the outer threshold.

He sat down on the bottom stone step, wiping sweat from his eyes with his handkerchief.

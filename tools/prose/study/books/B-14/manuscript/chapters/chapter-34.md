# Chapter 34: The Geometry of Drywall

The living room had four walls, four ninety-degree corners, and no fireplace.

Arthur Cross stood in the center of the room with a six-foot wooden folding rule unfolded to thirty-six inches. The floor was covered in pale grey broadloom carpet that smelled of synthetic latex and scotchgard. Every wall was constructed of twelve-millimeter plasterboard screwed to galvanized light-gauge steel studs at six-hundred-millimeter centers, skimmed with plaster, and rolled with matte emulsion.

He measured the south wall.

The wall measured seven meters twenty from the aluminum window frame to the doorway of the kitchen vestibule. There were no skirting boards of five-inch molded pitch pine; instead, a two-inch strip of white-painted MDF was bonded to the plasterboard with construction adhesive, forming a sharp, dust-free rebate against the carpet pile.

Arthur walked to the oak sideboard. It had taken Tam and Stevie twenty minutes to maneuver the sideboard through the door on their webbing slings. It was solid Scottish oak, made in Beith in nineteen-thirty-five, seven feet long, three feet high, with two carved lion-head corbels supporting the central drawer and heavy brass drop handles on the four cupboards.

Arthur pushed the sideboard three inches to the left.

The oak feet slid across the carpet with a dull, muffled friction. When the back of the sideboard touched the plasterboard, the drywall gave off a hollow, drum-like thump: *tump*.

Arthur stepped back.

In Flat 2B, the sideboard had occupied the recess between the chimney breast and the front bay window. The wall there had been eighteen inches of solid blonde sandstone, lined with lath-and-plaster and covered in three layers of embossed Anaglypta paper painted deep burgundy. The sideboard had nestled into the stone as though the house had been grown around the timber; the dark varnish of the oak had picked up the glow of the open coal grate, reflecting the red embers in the beveled glass of the upper gallery.

Here, against the vast, flat plane of brilliant white drywall, the Beith oak looked absurd.

It looked massive, dark, and clumsy, like an old traction engine parked on the deck of a fiberglass yacht. The carved lion heads looked down at the pale grey carpet with blind, scowling eyes. The brass handles were tarnished brown, out of place beneath the recessed LED ceiling downlights that cast sharp, circular cones of neutral white light across the top surface.

Arthur reached into his cardigan pocket, drew out a clean cotton yellow duster, and rubbed the beveled edge of the sideboard’s crown molding. The wood was cold. It did not have the familiar warmth it gathered from the flue tiles behind it.

He walked through to the kitchen area.

The kitchen was open-plan, separated from the living room by an island clad in grey slate-effect laminate. The worktops were solid white quartz with grey veining, cold and unyielding under the fingers. Behind the worktop sat an array of integrated appliances behind matte anthracite cabinet doors: an eye-level multifunction fan oven, a concealed dishwasher with a stainless-steel interior, and a four-zone induction hob flush-mounted into the quartz surface.

Arthur had never used an induction hob.

He picked up Mary’s polished copper kettle from the cardboard carton on the floor. Danuta had cleaned it with salt and vinegar before handing it to him on Sunday; the metal shone like a fresh penny, catching the strip lights concealed under the wall units.

He set the kettle down on the circular cooking zone marked with four white corner crosses.

The kettle sat level on the quartz. Arthur looked at the touch-sensitive control panel printed directly onto the black ceramic glass. There were no knobs. No brass taps to turn with a dry squeak of graphite packing. No blue crown of town gas hissing around an iron burner ring.

A small icon showed a stylized power symbol.

Arthur touched the glass with his index finger.

A red digital numeral *0* illuminated beneath the glass. He pressed the plus symbol three times until the display showed *9*.

A high-pitched electronic tone chimed: *ping*.

Arthur waited. He stood with his hands resting on the quartz worktop, looking at the base of the copper kettle. In his scullery in Baltic Buildings, you put the kettle on the gas ring and within twenty seconds you heard the iron trivet expand with a metallic tick, followed by the faint hum of water molecules heating against the bottom tinplate, then the low, vibrating rattle that built into a steady whistle through the spout.

The induction hob remained completely silent.

No heat rose from the black glass. The air around the kettle was cold.

After thirty seconds, the red digital display began to flash: *F - 0 - 1*.

The hob emitted four sharp, aggressive electronic bleeps: *beep-beep-beep-beep*. Then the display went dark.

Arthur frowned. He pressed the power symbol again. The numeral *9* appeared, followed immediately by the flashing error code and the four bleeps.

He reached into the kitchen drawer where Marcus Ward had left the document folder: a black ring binder stamped with the Vanguard logo, containing seventy pages of operating instructions for the fixtures of Unit G-02.

Arthur turned to section four: *Neff Induction Hob Installation and Operating Guide*.

He ran his finger down the troubleshooting table until he found the heading: *Error Code F01: Vessel Not Recognized*.

The text was printed in eight-point sans-serif type:

*Induction cooking surfaces operate by generating an alternating high-frequency electromagnetic field that induces eddy currents directly within ferromagnetic cookware. Non-ferromagnetic materials, including pure copper, aluminum, terracotta, and non-magnetic stainless steel, will not couple with the induction coil and cannot be heated. Ensure all cooking vessels possess a flat base fabricated from ferromagnetic cast iron or induction-compatible magnetic composite.*

Arthur closed the black ring binder.

He looked at the kettle. The heavy copper base, rolled by a coppersmith in Greenock in nineteen-thirty, was non-ferromagnetic. It would never heat water on this surface. It would sit on the white quartz worktop as a decorative object, inert and useless, while tea was made in the brushed-steel electric kettle Vanguard had supplied in the welcome basket.

Arthur lifted Mary's kettle by its horn handle. He opened the anthracite cabinet beneath the sink, set the kettle behind the plastic waste separation bins, and closed the door. The concealed soft-close hinges pulled the door shut with a silent, hydraulic whisper.

He returned to the living room.

The removals men had placed his wing-back armchair against the inner drywall partition, facing the television bracket.

Arthur gripped the arms of the chair. It was twenty-eight pounds of seasoned beech frame, horsehair stuffing, and green moquette wool worn smooth at the elbows. He lifted the front legs two inches and dragged the chair across the carpet, turning it ninety degrees until it sat directly before the triple-glazed picture window.

He knelt to adjust the brass castors.

His right knee made no sound on the eighty-twenty wool carpet. The joint bent smoothly, without resistance, cradled by the soft heat rising from the screed.

He straightened the antimicrobial antimacassar across the headrest. He picked up his pair of brass-rimmed reading glasses from the sill and set them on the small teak side table beside the chair.

Through the clear, cold glass of the window, Albert Quay lay three feet below eye level. Across the road, the demolition team was hooking a heavy steel wire sling to the cast-iron balcony of Flat 3B. A man in an orange jumpsuit was cutting through the masonry anchor with an oxyacetylene torch, sending a shower of white and yellow slag cascading forty feet into the courtyard.

Arthur did not turn on the radio. He did not turn on the television.

He sat down in the soft cushions and looked out across the wet dockland asphalt.

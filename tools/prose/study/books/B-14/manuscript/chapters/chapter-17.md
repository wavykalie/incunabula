# Chapter 17: The Galvanised Hoarding

The heavy diesel flatbed lorries arrived at six o’clock on Tuesday morning, their air brakes releasing with sharp, echoing gasps that shook the bedroom windows of Baltic Buildings. Arthur Cross was already awake, sitting on the edge of his mattress with his woollen trousers on, rubbing wintergreen balm into his right knee. Through the floorboards, he felt the rhythmic thrum of the idling diesel engines transmitting directly through the foundation masonry into the joists.

He pulled back the green cotton curtain in the kitchen.

Down in the muddy lane between the tenement and the old grain sidings, four men in orange waterproof suits and yellow hard hats were unloading eight-foot sheets of corrugated galvanised steel. The metal sheets were stacked on timber pallets, their zinc coatings glistening with cold sea drizzle. Each time two workers lifted a sheet, the steel flexed with a loud, tinny boom that echoed off the damp sandstone facade like distant thunder.

Arthur went to the sink, splashed freezing water onto his face, and walked down the forty-eight stairs.

The close smelled of diesel exhaust and damp sawdust. At the street entrance, a contractor with a red beard was driving six-inch timber posts into the tarmac with a hydraulic fence post driver mounted on a miniature tracked digger. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* Every impact sent a shockwave through the stone threshold under Arthur’s boots.

Danuta Kowalski was standing in the close doorway, her quilted housecoat buttoned to her neck, holding an empty milk jug in her hand. Her thick spectacles were fogged from her breath.

"Pan Cross," she whispered, her voice trembling. "They are putting up a cage. Look at the entrance."

The contractors had already bolted two rows of treated pine rails between the posts. The corrugated steel sheets were being secured with battery impact drivers, the self-tapping screws screeching as they bit through the steel into the timber. The open courtyard where Danuta had hung her sheets for twenty-four years was being swallowed behind a continuous gray wall. The close entrance was reduced to a narrow corridor three feet wide, flanked on both sides by chain-link mesh and steel hoarding.

"Where is the light?" Danuta asked, her hand clutching Arthur’s forearm. "They are taking the light from my kitchen."

Arthur looked at the steel fence. The top of the hoarding rose four feet above Danuta’s ground-floor windows, completely cutting off the morning sky, leaving her scullery in perpetual amber shadow.

A supervisor in a white hard hat and a high-visibility vest stepped through the gap in the hoarding, holding a digital tablet in a rubberized case. Across the back of his vest ran the logo: *VANGUARD DEMOLITION & SITE REMEDIATION*.

"Morning, folks," the supervisor said briskly, his pencil tucked behind his ear. "Site perimeter establishment. Required under the Health and Safety at Work Act. Demolition exclusion zone for the adjacent warehouse structures."

"This is our entrance," Arthur said, his voice flat and steady.

"Pedestrian access corridor remains fully operational," the man replied, tapping the chain-link gate with his pencil. "Keypad security lock will be installed on the outer gate by Friday. Emergency services have the master code. Keep to the marked walkway at all times. Hard-hat area begins on the other side of the corrugated line."

Callum Reid came down the stairs behind them, his work jacket unzipped, his eyes wild with morning fury.

"What is this?" Callum shouted, shoving past Arthur to face the supervisor. "Who gave you permission to fence off the close?"

"Municipal permit number 44-B," the supervisor answered without looking up from his screen. "Issued by the city planning department on Friday. Public safety mandate during deep ground-works. Step back behind the yellow line, mate."

"This is our home!" Callum gripped the chain-link mesh, shaking the fence until the galvanized clamps rattled against the pine posts. "You’re fencing us in like cattle!"

The supervisor did not answer. He turned his back, waved his clipboard at the digger operator, and walked toward the timber yard as the machine began driving another six-inch post into the pavement with a deafening, rhythmic crash.

Arthur stood by the inner chain-link gate, looking back at the red sandstone arch of the entrance. The iron letters of the old building name were now half-hidden behind the gray galvanized ridge line of the contractor's fence. A yellow warning sign had been zip-tied to the wire mesh: *DANGER · HARD HATS AND HIGH-VISIBILITY CLOTHING REQUIRED BEYOND THIS POINT*.

Down in the lane, the second lorry was backing into position, its reversing alarm beeping with a piercing, continuous chirp that drove gulls screaming into the air from the warehouse roofs.

Danuta stood beside him on the cold entrance tiles, her fingers clutching her woolen apron.

"Pan Cross," she whispered, her spectacles catching the glare from the excavator's halogen work lights. "They are cutting the air. In Gdynia, when they built the new dry docks, they put up a fence like this around the workers' cottages on ulica Portowa. At first, they said it was for the falling stone. Then they brought the security men with the Alsatian dogs. After two weeks, you could not walk to the baker without showing your shipyard identity pass to a boy with a rifle."

"There are no rifles here, Danuta," Arthur said quietly, leaning his weight onto his walking stick.

"The fence is the same, Arthur," she replied, her voice dropping into a flat, unshakable conviction. "A fence does not keep stone from falling. A fence keeps people from looking. When they put up the steel, it means the building is already dead in their books."

She looked at her milk jug, then at the three-foot corridor between the chain link and the wall.

"How does the coal lorry deliver the sacks?" she asked suddenly.

Arthur looked at the turn in the corridor. The passage was too narrow for a handcart, let alone a two-hundredweight sack of house coal on a carter's shoulder. The coal merchant on Duke Street would refuse to carry fuel down a three-foot tunnel lined with sharp steel wire.

"We have the electric fires, Danuta," Arthur said, though the words tasted like dry ash in his mouth.

"Electric fires," she muttered, turning back toward her door. "Ten pence an hour on the coin meter, and the room stays cold as a tomb."

She stepped into Flat 1A, her slippers shuffling over the linoleum. The brass latch gave its familiar scrape, but the sound was swallowed instantly by the roaring diesel engine of the excavator driving another steel post into the street.

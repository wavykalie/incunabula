# Chapter 28: Perforated Steel

The two security fitters arrived in a silver Volkswagen Transporter with *SITEX PROPERTY PROTECTION* lettered in orange reflective vinyl along the flanks. They wore navy blue bib-and-brace overalls with reinforced Cordura knee pockets, steel-capped rigger boots, and clear safety goggles pushed up onto their foreheads. Each man carried a rechargeable eighteen-volt impact wrench in a molded plastic holster slung from a leather webbing belt.

Arthur Cross watched them from the threshold of Flat 2B. His storm door was open six inches, held by his hand against the pine casing.

The younger fitter, who had a shaved head and a tribal tattoo coiling up the side of his neck into his hairline, backed up the stairs carrying two sheets of twelve-gauge galvanized steel. Each sheet was eight feet high by four feet wide, perforated with a dense grid of three-quarter-inch hexagonal punch holes to permit airflow while preventing glass breakage or human entry. The raw steel had a dull, greasy zinc sheen that caught the weak landing light from the roof skylight.

"Watch the riser, Gary," the older man said from below.

The older man had grey hair clipped down to the scalp and carried a heavy canvas kit bag bulging with coach bolts, steel angle brackets, and battery packs. Between them, the steel sheets chimed with a heavy, industrial resonance whenever the corners touched the stone treads.

They dropped the first sheet onto the timber landing outside Flat 2A. The impact shook the floorboards beneath Arthur’s feet, sending a puff of grey lime dust from the skirtings.

Flat 2A was Callum and Sophie's flat. The door stood wide open, showing the stripped floorboards of the lobby, two rolls of discarded underlay, and a broken plastic light switch hanging from two red wires.

The older fitter set his canvas bag on the landing. He pulled a printed work order from his breast pocket, clipped it to an acrylic clipboard, and ran his thumb down the itemized schedule.

"Job reference VP-ninety-four-stroke-eight," he recited. "Unit two-A, Baltic Buildings, Albert Quay. Full perimeter hardening. Main ingress leaf: one steel security door, two five-lever deadlocks, internal shoot-bolts. Fenestration: four standard three-bay sash windows to front elevation, two rear kitchen openings. All fixings through-bolted where brick permits, or coach-screwed into sound structural timber using anti-tamper shear-nuts."

He looked up, seeing Arthur standing in the opening of Flat 2B.

"Afternoon," the fitter said. His voice was neutral, the flat professional cadence of a gas engineer reading a meter or a roofer pricing lead flashing. "You the occupant across?"

"Arthur Cross," Arthur said.

"We'll have this leaf hung in twenty minutes, Mr. Cross. Then we're doing the bay windows inside. If the rattle of the impact driver disturbs you, you might want to step out for an hour. It rings a bit on the stone."

"I have nowhere to step to," Arthur said.

The fitter nodded once, without curiosity. "Fair enough."

He turned back to the younger man. "Right, Gary. Let's take the timber leaf off first. Don't bother with the hinge screws; the timber's rotten around the butt plates anyway. Just pop the pins with the drift."

Gary produced a flat steel pry bar and a four-pound lump hammer from his belt. He slid the chisel edge of the pry bar behind the top brass hinge of Callum’s door. The hinge had been installed in nineteen-forty-eight, solid cast brass with four steel counter-sunk screws bedded in old pitch-pine casing. Gary swung the hammer.

The sound was explosive in the confined stone well of the stairwell. *Clack. Clack. Crack.*

The dry pine split with a sound like a rifle shot. A long splinter of seasoned wood, six inches long and jagged as a dagger, flew across the landing and struck Arthur’s doorframe, dropping to the floor. Gary gave the pry bar a vicious sideways wrench. The brass screws tore out of the timber, trailing white plugs of ancient lime plaster.

He repeated the action on the bottom hinge. With a dry, tearing groan, the four-panel Victorian door came free. It was solid Douglas fir, painted in six layers of gloss paint—cream, then hospital green, then brown, then white, and finally the slate blue Callum had painted it three years ago when he brought Sophie home from the maternity clinic.

Gary dragged the door out onto the landing and propped it against the iron banister.

"Heavy old bastard," Gary muttered, wiping his brow with his forearm.

"Douglas fir," the older man said, kneeling on the floor to unpack the steel security door. "They don't make them like that now. Modern doors are just cardboard honeycomb with a six-millimeter veneer. These old tenement doors would stop an axe."

He lifted the Sitex steel door leaf from the stack. It was fabricated from two-millimeter folded zinc-coated sheet steel, stiffened internally with three horizontal box sections. Unlike the old wooden door, it had no handle, no brass letterbox, and no peep-hole. In the center sat two square apertures designed to receive heavy brass padlocks protected by hardened steel cowl boxes that prevented bolt-cutters from reaching the shackle.

"Frame brackets first," the older man said.

He picked up the eighteen-volt impact wrench. He fitted a three-quarter-inch magnetic socket over the hex head of a four-inch galvanized coach screw. He set the heavy steel angle bracket against the inside face of the sandstone doorway, right where Callum's grandfather had driven a brass coat hook fifty years before.

The trigger clicked.

The high-pitched, metallic whine of the impact driver tore through the landing. *Zzzzz-tat-tat-tat-tat-tat-tat.* The torque mechanism hammered at thirty blows per second, driving the thick steel thread directly into the ancient masonry. The stone resisted, screaming under the friction, before the screw bit deep into the lime-and-rubble mortar. A cloud of pungent, alkaline dust billowed from the borehole, smelling of burnt stone and sulfur.

Arthur stepped back inside Flat 2B, but he did not close the door.

He watched the older fitter drive twelve screws into the frame—four on the hinge side, four on the lock side, two top, two bottom. Each screw went home with that same deafening mechanical stutter: *tat-tat-tat-tat-tat*. The vibrations traveled through the stone wall directly into Arthur’s apartment, rattling the tea mugs on the dresser and making the brass pendulum inside Mary’s mantel clock flutter against its stop.

When the frame was rigid, the two fitters lifted the steel security leaf into place. The steel was cold, dull, reflecting none of the soft afternoon light that drifted down from the cupola.

"Lift your side half an inch, Gary," the older man commanded. "Drop the pivot pin."

Gary grunted, heaving upward with his steel-toed boot under the lower edge. The steel hinge sleeve slipped over the welded pin with a dry, screeching slide of metal on ungreased metal.

The door swung shut.

It did not close with the soft, cushioned thud of seasoned timber against draught-stripping. It slammed into the steel frame with a massive, ringing clang that reverberated down the stone stairwell like a heavy iron gate dropped in a prison yard.

"Clear," Gary said.

The older man reached into his canvas bag and produced two heavy rectangular brass padlocks. They were Chubb high-security brass padlocks, each weighing nearly three pounds, with hardened molybdenum steel shackles. He inserted the first padlock into the upper protective cowl, slid the shackle through the steel staple, and snapped it shut.

The click of the cylinder was heavy, greasy, final.

He did the same with the lower cowl.

Now the doorway of Flat 2A was gone. In its place stood a grey, perforated steel slab, cold and impervious, stamped in the center with the black stenciled warning:

*WARNING: THESE PREMISES ARE PROTECTED BY SITEX SECURITY SYSTEMS. TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED UNDER SECTION 9 OF THE CRIMINAL LAW ACT 1977. 24-HOUR MONITORING IN OPERATION.*

"Right," the older man said, wiping his palms on his bib. "That's the ingress sorted. Let's get the window screens inside."

Gary picked up his pry bar and hammer. The older man unlocked the lower cowl with a master key, pulled the steel leaf open on its squealing hinge, and both men stepped into the vacant hallway of Flat 2A, pulling the door closed behind them.

Inside, the work began on the front windows.

From his own living room, Arthur could hear every stroke of the work. The layout of Flat 2A was identical to Flat 2B, mirror-reversed across the chimney breast. The fitters were in Callum’s front room, the room where the baby’s cot had stood three days ago.

Arthur heard the splintering crack of the wooden sash parting beads being torn out with the pry bar. He heard the heavy sash weights crash down inside the timber boxing as the cords were severed with a utility knife. Then came the rattle of the perforated steel screens being lifted against the window frames, blocking out the light of the river.

Then the impact driver started again.

*Tat-tat-tat-tat-tat.*

Through the chimney flue, the noise was amplified, booming into Arthur’s hearth as though the tools were operating inside his own fireplace. Soot sifted down the iron register plate, falling in a dry, powdery black shower onto the glazed hearth tiles.

Arthur knelt by the hearth with his brass-handled fire shovel and hearth brush. His right knee hit the carpet runner with a dull thud.

He swept the soot into the pan. The soot was old, smelling of bituminous coal from the nineteen-seventies, dense and greasy. As he swept, the impact driver drove six more screws into the window architrave on the other side of the brick wall. The vibration traveled through the cast-iron grate into the shovel handle, numbing Arthur’s fingers.

He stood up, holding the pan of soot.

Across the landing, the noise stopped.

Arthur walked to the hall. The two fitters stepped out of Flat 2A into the landing. Their navy overalls were grey from stone dust and horsehair plaster across the chest and knees. Gary was carrying their canvas bag and the cordless drivers, the batteries hot and smelling of ozone.

The older man pulled the heavy steel security door shut. The steel edge struck the stop with that same dead, hollow ring.

He inserted the master key into the lower lock cowl, turned the brass cylinder ninety degrees, and snapped the heavy shackle into the body.

The heavy steel sheet was locked with a master padlock, sealing Flat 2A into darkness.

# Chapter 33: The Warm Floor

The waking was wrong because there was no cold.

For forty-eight years in Flat 2B, Arthur Cross had woken to the state of the weather through his collarbones. If a frost had come down from the Argyll hills, the air above his wool blankets was needle-sharp, smelling of frozen river mud and the dry, powdery soot of the chimney flue. You did not swing your legs out until you had gathered your socks under the duvet to take the stiffness out of the wool. You planted your feet on the linoleum, and the shock traveled straight up the shinbones into the patella like an electric jolt.

In Flat G-02 of The Meridian, Arthur woke at six in the morning with his hands lying warm and dry on top of the sheet.

The bedroom had no cornices. The ceiling was a flat, flawless plane of plasterboard painted in *Dulux Trade Vinyl Matt Pure Brilliant White*, uninterrupted by ceiling roses, gas pipe scars, or hairline settlement fissures. The room was illuminated by a faint, amber illumination leaking around the edges of the blackout roller blind—not street light from the quay, but the reflection of the harbor entrance beacon against the double layer of low-emissivity glass.

Arthur sat up.

His right knee did not grind. It did not pop. It felt loose, fluid, resting inside the joint without the burning pressure that had accompanied every morning of his seventy-fourth year.

He swung his legs over the edge of the bed.

His bare soles touched the carpet. It was an eighty-twenty wool twist in oatmeal, half an inch deep, resting on a heavy acoustic underlay over three inches of anhydrite screed. Beneath the screed ran sixty meters of cross-linked polyethylene pipe circulating hot water at thirty-five degrees from the district biomass boiler in the basement.

The heat was instantaneous.

It did not feel like the radiant scorch of an open hearth or the dry, metallic sizzle of a two-bar electric fire. It felt like standing on a flat rock in the afternoon sun—a soft, pervasive warmth that enveloped his heels, his arches, and the balls of his toes. The small muscles in his feet, cramped into hard knots by decades of cold leather bus boots, softened within twenty seconds.

"God," Arthur murmured.

He stood up. His weight settled on both heels simultaneously. For the first time since the winter of nineteen-ninety-four, he did not have to pause with both hands on the mattress edge to allow the synovial fluid to lubricate the eroded joint. His knees carried his twelve stone of bone and flesh without a single click.

He walked to the ensuite bathroom.

The bathroom door was a solid-core timber leaf faced with light oak veneer, closing against a continuous grey neoprene gasket in the frame. Inside, the floor was covered in large, slip-resistant porcelain tiles the color of wet river sand. The floor was warmer here than in the bedroom, regulated by an independent digital thermostat set into the tile work beside the towel rail.

Arthur looked for the threshold.

There was no threshold. The bedroom carpet met the bathroom tile at a perfectly flush aluminum dividing strip, recessed half an millimeter below the pile. No six-inch step of painted pine to haul a stiff foot over; no draft leaking from the cellar below; no rot at the skirting.

He walked into the shower enclosure.

The shower was not a tin cubicle or an enameled cast-iron tub with four-inch sides to climb over. It was a level-access wet room, two yards square, draining toward a narrow stainless-steel channel set into the rear wall. A single panel of ten-millimeter toughened safety glass separated the stall from the basin.

On the wall sat a Grohe thermostatic mixer valve with two chrome cylindrical controls: one for volume, one calibrated in degrees Celsius from twenty to forty-five.

Arthur turned the volume control ninety degrees.

There was no clank of water hammer. No lead pipe thudding behind the lath-and-plaster. A wide, circular showerhead mounted flush in the ceiling discharged twenty liters of water per minute in a wide, aerated cone. The water hit the tiled floor with a soft, steady hiss, exactly thirty-eight degrees.

Arthur stepped under the flow.

The water fell across his shoulders, his neck, and his back. It was hot, soft, treated by the central water softening plant in the utility pod. The ache in his lumbar vertebrae—the ache that had lived with him since he fell off the bus platform during an icy turn at Gourock in seventy-six—dissolved under the heat like salt in broth. He leaned his forearms against the tiled wall, letting the deluge pound into the base of his skull.

His knees supported him. They did not shake. They did not burn. He could have stood there for an hour without leaning his shoulder against a doorframe.

After ten minutes he turned the chrome cylinder off. The water stopped instantly, cut dead by the ceramic disc cartridge inside the mixer.

The steam did not hang in the air. An extractor fan set into the ceiling grill hummed with a low, whisper-quiet drone, sucking the moisture out through a plastic duct before it could condense on the large frameless mirror above the basin. Within thirty seconds, the glass was bone dry.

Arthur took a white bath sheet from the heated chrome rail. The towel was thick, warm as fresh bread from the oven.

He dried his chest, his arms, and his thighs. He walked into the living room.

The living room occupied the south corner of The Meridian, twenty-four feet long by sixteen feet wide. One entire wall was taken up by an aluminum-framed triple-glazed picture window looking directly across Albert Quay toward the river.

Arthur walked to the glass.

Outside, the October weather was brutal. A Force 8 gale was blowing up the Firth of Clyde from the Atlantic, driving sheets of grey maritime rain sideways across the dock road. Down in the harbor, the salt water was churned into yellow, greasy froth that piled up against the stone retaining wall of the quay. The tops of the gantry cranes at the container terminal were shrouded in low, racing scud.

A seagull drifted past the window three feet away, struggling against the headwind, its wings beating frantically.

No sound reached him. The wind was silent behind the glass. Neither the driving rain nor the diesel engines of the container lorries turning out of the dock gates fifty yards below penetrated the seal.

The triple-glazed acoustic units, thirty-six millimeters thick with argon gas fill and laminated acoustic interlayers, cut forty-eight decibels of external noise. The storm raged outside like a silent film projected onto the wall of a darkened room. The glass remained completely cold to the touch on the outside pane, while the inner pane was room temperature, untouched by the condensation that had run down the windows of Baltic Buildings every winter since eighteen-ninety.

Arthur laid his flat palm against the center of the glass.

There was no vibration. The six-story concrete frame of The Meridian, anchored thirty feet into the Whinstone bed by one hundred and twenty steel-cased piles, cushioned the kinetic energy of the gale without a tremor.

Across the asphalt roadway, behind a six-foot perimeter fence of green galvanized mesh, Baltic Buildings stood in the rain.

The roof was already half gone. The slate masons had stripped the West Highland slates on Friday, leaving the bare sarking boards exposed to the weather like the grey ribs of a stranded whale. The window openings were black, rectangular holes, their frames ripped out for salvage, the stone architraves jagged where the crowbars had levered the pine boxes free.

A yellow sheet of polythene fluttered from the second-floor bay window of Arthur’s old flat, snapping violently in the wind.

Arthur watched it. His skin was warm. His feet were warm on the carpet. The digital thermostat on the living room wall clicked softly, its liquid-crystal display glowing a steady *21.0°C*.

He dried his hands on a clean linen towel and listened for sounds from the hallway.

# Chapter 8: The White Envelope

At half past eight on Wednesday morning, the white envelope arrived.

It did not come through the communal letterboxes downstairs. It was slipped directly under the bottom of Arthur’s front door, sliding across the brown linoleum runner with a soft, whispery hiss that stopped three inches from his slippers.

Arthur had been pouring his second cup of black tea from the brown earthenware pot. He set the pot down on the cork mat, picked up his walking stick, and walked down the short hallway.

He bent down, bracing his left hand against the doorframe to keep his knees from buckling, and picked it up.

The envelope was heavy, textured wove paper, pure white, with no stamp and no postmark. On the front, his name was written in black fountain-pen ink in an elegant, sloping hand: *Mr. Arthur Cross, Flat 2B*.

He carried it back to the kitchen table. He used his pocketknife to slit the top edge.

Inside was a folded card of thick cream cardstock, accompanied by a single sheet of heavy correspondence paper:

*VANGUARD URBAN REGENERATION*
*The Meridian Project Office · Albert Quay Pier*

*Dear Mr. Cross,*

*I hope this note finds you well. As the longest-standing resident of Baltic Buildings, you hold an important place in the history of Albert Dock. We are very aware of your thirty-five years of dedicated service to the municipal transport department and your four decades in the close.*

*We recognize that urban regeneration can be unsettling, particularly for senior residents who have given their lives to this community. We are keen to ensure that this transition is handled with the personal dignity and respect you deserve.*

*I would be honored if you would join me for breakfast and an informal, private discussion at our project suite on Albert Quay tomorrow, Thursday morning, at nine o'clock. We have several options regarding your future housing needs that I believe you will find of considerable interest.*

*There is no commitment whatsoever. A car will be waiting for you at the entrance to Baltic Buildings at eight-forty-five.*

*Yours sincerely,*
*Marcus Ward*
*Development Director*

Arthur read the letter twice.

The language was smooth, polite, perfectly oiled. Not a single legal term. No mention of Section 33, no mention of eviction warrants, no mention of demolition dates. Just *respect*, *dignity*, and *future housing needs*.

He picked up the cream card.

On one side was a small, high-resolution architectural photograph of the interior of a ground-floor apartment. The room was flooded with morning light through floor-to-ceiling double-glazed windows looking out onto a private courtyard garden with birch trees and stone planters. Inside the room, the floor was wide-plank pale oak, clean and dustless. A walk-in shower was visible through an open door, with level tiled thresholds and a brushed chrome grab rail set discreetly into gray porcelain tiles.

Across the bottom ran a single printed line:
*The Meridian: Flat G-02. Ground Floor Courtyard Residence. Zero Steps.*

Zero steps.

Arthur let his fingers rest on the card. The paper felt cool, smooth, and expensive, completely unlike the rough, damp newsprint of his morning paper or the crumbly plaster of his hallway walls.

From the street below came the sound of a horn.

Arthur stood up and walked to the bay window.

Down in the lane, parked against the wet sandstone wall of Baltic Buildings, sat a black saloon car, its engine idling silently, its polished paintwork reflecting the gray dock sky. A young man in a dark overcoat stood beside the driver's door, checking the display of a mobile telephone.

Arthur looked down at his kitchen table.

Callum’s action committee petition lay beside the sugar bowl, rumpled and stained with tea rings, with only four signatures scrawled in blue biro at the bottom. Beside it lay Marcus Ward’s white cream card with its photograph of the birch trees and the level shower.

He pulled his pocket watch from his waistcoat pocket and pressed the winding stem. The gold lid popped open with a faint click.

Eight-thirty-eight.

He slipped the watch back into his pocket, took his heavy tweed overcoat from the peg behind the door, and reached for his walking stick.

Arthur stood by the bay window of Flat 2B, looking down at the black saloon car parked in the lane below.

The driver had finished his cigarette and climbed back behind the wheel. The car's twin exhausts breathed thin, pale plumes of white vapor into the freezing harbor drizzle, the engine running with a quiet, rhythmic hum that was barely audible through the sash glass. It was an expensive machine: polished German engineering, tinted glass, alloy wheels clean of mud or salt grime. A vehicle built for private equity directors and commercial lawyers who drove between Edinburgh and London on motorway bypasses, never seeing the damp sandstone tenements or the rusty container cranes of the working docks.

Arthur looked back at the kitchen table.

On the left lay Callum Reid’s crumpled yellow petition, its lines scrawled in angry black ink, its edges stained with grease and tea circles. On the right lay Marcus Ward’s heavy cream card, its embossed blue crest catching the morning light, showing the pristine white lines of Flat G-02: the walk-in shower, the private birch trees, the level threshold with zero steps.

He picked up the card once more, turning it over in his rough, callused fingers.

*Zero steps.*

The words seemed to burn through the glossy paper into his palms. He thought of his knees: the bone-on-bone grind, the cold ache that kept him awake until four in the morning, the terror of slipping on the frost-covered stone steps when carrying his milk bottles up from the street. He thought of Mary’s final year, when every step was a separate trial of breath and agony.

If Mary were alive today, what would she say?

*Take the warm flat, Artie,* a quiet voice seemed to whisper in the room. *Don't die on the stairs like I did.*

He walked to the scullery, took down his woolen overcoat from the wooden peg, and slipped his arms into the heavy tweed sleeves. He took his nickel pocket watch from the saucer on the dresser, wound the stem four turns until it resisted, and checked the time: eight-forty.

He picked up his walking stick from beside the door, opened his brass Yale rim latch, and stepped out onto the cold stone landing of Baltic Buildings.

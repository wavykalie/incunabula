# Chapter 12: The Housing Lawyer

On Thursday afternoon, a blue plastic table was set up in the damp entrance vestibule of Baltic Buildings.

Callum Reid had dragged it down from his second-floor kitchen. Beside it sat a young woman in a gray woollen duffel coat, her damp hair pulled back into a ponytail, a battered leather briefcase resting on her knees. Her name was Fiona Macleod, a solicitor from the Albert Quay Community Law Collective.

Arthur was coming down the stairs with his empty milk bottles when he found half the close gathered on the lower landing.

Callum stood beside the table, wearing his high-visibility work fleece, his arms crossed over his chest. Beside him stood Sophie, holding three-year-old Maya wrapped in a pink fleece blanket. Danuta Kowalski sat on the bottom stone stair, her quilted housecoat tucked around her shins, holding her four-page Vanguard legal letter in both hands like a prayer book.

"Arthur," Callum called out as Arthur reached the turn of the spiral. "Come down here. Listen to this. Fiona’s going over the legal position."

Arthur paused on step four, resting both hands on his walking stick. His right knee was throbbing from the descent, a hot, pulsing wire of pain that made his calf tremble.

"Afternoon," Arthur said to the lawyer.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Cross," Fiona Macleod said. She had sharp, intelligent dark eyes and ink stains on her right index finger. "Callum asked me to review the Section 33 notices served by Vanguard on Tuesday. I’ve gone through the paperwork."

"What does it mean?" Danuta asked from the bottom step, her voice thin, trembling.

"It’s a standard notice to quit served under short assured tenancy agreements," the solicitor explained, tapping the paper with her ballpoint pen. "Because Vanguard bought the superior freehold from the municipal harbor authority, they stepped into the landlord's position. Under current Scots law, a landlord does not have to prove tenant fault under Section 33; they only have to give two months' formal notice and establish that the tenancy contract has reached its natural ish date."

"Our contracts rolled over every six months," Callum said. "For six years."

"Yes," Fiona said. "That’s what makes you vulnerable. Rolling six-month leases give you no long-term security. However, because this is a comprehensive demolition warrant involving more than six occupied residential units, the developer must submit an Environmental Impact and Relocation Assessment to the city planning committee before the sheriff will grant a warrant of ejection."

She looked around the cold stone stairwell, her eyes taking in the flaking lime plaster, the damp patches around the cornices, and the cracked glass of the window above.

"Vanguard is relying on divide-and-conquer," she said plainly. "They offer voluntary relocation disturbance payments to individual tenants—three thousand or five thousand pounds—on condition that you sign a deed of surrender and vacate quietly. If half the tenants take the money and leave by May, the building becomes partially vacant. Once a tenement is more than fifty percent empty, the council’s building control department can declare it an unsafe structure due to lack of communal maintenance. At that point, the sheriff grants summary warrants for the rest of you without compensation."

Sophie Reid drew in a sharp, shuddering breath, pulling Maya tighter against her shoulder. "They can just declare it unsafe?"

"They can," Fiona said. "Unless the tenants form a registered collective association. If all twelve tenants sign a binding constitution and agree to act as a single bargaining unit, Vanguard cannot negotiate with you individually. Every communication must go through the collective. We can challenge the demolition warrant in the sheriff court on procedural grounds, which gives us at least twelve months to demand alternative council rehousing within the same postal district."

Callum turned to face the stair, his eyes landing straight on Arthur.

"That's why we need you, Arthur," Callum said, his voice dropping into a tense, earnest whisper. "You're the senior tenant. If you lead the collective, Vanguard can't break us. If you refuse their private offers, the whole close stays solid."

Arthur looked at the young woman with the baby in her arms. Maya’s small, pale face was smudged with biscuit crumbs, her dark eyes wide and frightened by the tension in the adults' voices. Then he looked at Danuta, sitting on the cold sandstone step with her spectacles slipping down her nose.

"Did Mr. Ward mention anything to you about individual offers, Arthur?" Callum asked suddenly.

The question fell into the entrance hall like an iron bolt dropped onto stone tiles.

Arthur kept his face impassive, his jaw set in that hard, immovable mask he had worn for thirty-five years when angry passengers complained about cancelled buses in the rain.

"I got the same envelope you got, Callum," Arthur said quietly. "Four pages of legal notice and a shiny brochure."

He did not mention the private car. He did not mention Marcus Ward’s white card or the blueprint of Flat G-02 with its zero steps and underfloor heating.

Fiona Macleod picked up a blue rubber stamp from her briefcase, breathed onto the ink pad, and stamped the corner of a clean printed document on the table.

*ALBERT QUAY TENANTS ASSOCIATION · CONSTITUTION.*

"The signing deadline for the collective constitution is Monday at noon," Fiona said, looking at the residents. "If all twelve flats sign, we file with the sheriff clerk on Tuesday morning. If anyone breaks ranks, the collective cannot be registered."

Arthur set his milk bottle on the bottom step, turned slowly, and began the forty-eight steps back to his attic.

Fiona Macleod leaned back in her folding chair, her hands resting flat on her leather briefcase.

"The title deeds for Baltic Buildings go back to eighteen eighty-four," she said, looking around at the gathered residents. "The original superior was the Albert Harbour Commission. In the founding feu charter, the commissioners inserted a protective covenant: the land was granted for the purpose of housing maritime and industrial workers engaged in the public service of the port. That covenant stood for ninety years."

"What happened to it?" Callum asked.

"The municipal reorganisation in nineteen ninety-six," Fiona explained. "The harbor trust was privatized under the Ports Act. The statutory covenants were extinguished by executive order of the Board of Trade, transferring the freehold to the city council as general municipal assets. That was the crack in the wall. Once the covenant was gone, the land became an asset class to be sold off to balance the municipal budget deficit."

Danuta shook her head, her hands clutching her woolen cardigan. "My parish priest, Father Stanislaw, he says the parish boundary includes Baltic Buildings since the church was built in nineteen hundred. The church records show every wedding and baptism in this close."

"The parish boundary remains ecclesiastical, Mrs. Kowalski," Fiona said gently. "The planning committee doesn't recognize church parish boundaries in commercial demolition orders. The law only looks at who owns the superior interest in the Land Register."

Sophie Reid pulled Maya’s woolen bonnet down over the toddler's ears. "And who owns it now, Fiona? Really owns it?"

"A nominee company called Meridian Quay Holdings," Fiona said, closing her folder. "Registered in Edinburgh, backed by a real estate equity partnership in London and Zurich. They don't have a face, Sophie. They have an investment memorandum and a seven-year yield forecast."

She stood up from the blue plastic table and picked up her briefcase.

"Monday at twelve, Callum. If all twelve tenants sign the collective constitution, we file the caveat in the sheriff court. If not, the Section 33 clock keeps ticking."

Arthur said nothing. He watched her walk through the heavy storm door into the street drizzle, her boots splashing through the brown puddles on the broken pavement.

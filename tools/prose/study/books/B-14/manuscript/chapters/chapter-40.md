# Chapter 40: The Green Light

The digital thermostat on the living room wall was a square of white molded polycarbonate, four inches on each side, mounted five feet above the carpet beside the kitchen opening. Its liquid-crystal screen gave off a faint, permanent emerald backlight that cast a pale green square across the surface of the quartz island.

In the center of the screen, dark segment numbers showed: *21.0°C*.

Beneath the temperature, an icon shaped like three wavy vertical lines indicated that the motorized underfloor manifold in the hall cupboard was currently closed. The room had reached equilibrium. No hot water moved through the polyethylene pipes in the screed; the thermal mass of the concrete slab was holding the heat, radiating it upward through the oatmeal axminster with an efficiency of ninety-two percent.

Arthur Cross sat in his wing-back armchair.

He had a mug of tea resting between his two hands. The mug was Mary’s earthenware mug, pale yellow with the faded green band around the rim. The tea inside was Scottish Blend, brewed with two teaspoons of white sugar and a dash of whole milk from a plastic carton. Steam rose from the surface in a straight, undisturbed plume, vanishing into the dry conditioned air two feet above his knees.

The room had no draft.

Outside, on the other side of the triple-glazed acoustic panes, November had arrived on the Clyde. A gale was blowing forty knots from the south-southwest, driving salt spray across the empty gravel waste of Albert Quay. Arthur could see the yellow sodium lamps along the container terminal pier swaying on their steel poles, their light smearing across the wet black asphalt. He could see the foam tearing off the crests of the waves inside the harbor basin.

Inside Flat G-02, not a hair on his head moved.

The triple-glazed casement was locked with four shoot-bolts operated by a single brushed-chrome handle. The perimeter gaskets—hollow rubber D-sections compressed against aluminum extrusions—cut forty-eight decibels of atmospheric disturbance. The room was quieter than a library. It was quieter than the interior of a private car parked in a garage with the engine off.

The only sound was the ventilation.

Every sixty seconds, the mechanical heat recovery unit inside the acoustic drop ceiling drew a measured volume of stale air through the circular plastic extract grille above the kitchen sink. The grille gave off a faint, breathy sigh—scarcely audible unless you held your breath and pressed your chin against your collar—like a sleeping dog exhaling in the next room.

Arthur lifted Mary’s mug to his lips.

He took a sip. The tea was hot, sweet, and comforting against the back of his palate.

His knees rested side by side, perfectly level, under the green wool of his trousers. The patella in his right joint was cool, unswollen, sitting in its femoral groove without friction. He had not touched the tub of menthol balm since moving across the street. The plastic container sat unopened in the bottom drawer of his bedside table, beside his spare spectacles and his old bus conductor punch.

He looked at the wall opposite his chair.

The Beith oak sideboard stood against the drywall, seven feet of dark Scottish oak beneath two recessed LED downlights. Tam and Stevie had centered it between the electrical socket outlets with a tape measure, leaving sixteen inches of white wall on either side.

On top of the sideboard sat the brass carriage clock from the Corporation.

Arthur had wound it on Sunday morning with its brass key, five half-turns clockwise until the ratchet stopped. The clock had run for twenty-five years on Mary’s mantelpiece in Baltic Buildings, ticking with a bright, silvery *tick-tack-tick-tack* that filled the room.

Arthur leaned his head forward, listening.

The tick of the clock was inaudible.

The eighty-twenty axminster carpet, the acoustic plasterboard, and the heavy rubber underlay drank the sound before it could travel six feet across the floor. To hear the clock, you had to stand three inches from the beveled glass case with your hand cupped behind your ear.

He looked at his front door.

The solid ash leaf stood flush in its frame, sealed by its twin perimeter gaskets and the automatic drop-seal at the threshold. Beyond that door lay sixty meters of carpeted hallway, where eight ash doors concealed twelve human beings who lived without names, without voices, and without footsteps.

Nobody would knock on the door tonight.

Danuta Kowalski was twelve miles inland, asleep in room twelve of the maritime wing, her electric bed raised forty-five degrees, her plastic water jug resting on her melamine table.

Callum and Sophie Reid were in their council maisonette in Erskine, listening to the lorries crossing the high bridge in the gale, feeding seventy pounds of electricity into the key meter to keep the baby warm.

Henderson was gone to his sister’s bungalow in Dunoon.

The Murrays were in a four-in-a-block in Port Glasgow.

Arthur was here.

He was the last man on Albert Quay. He had the best flat in the development, guaranteed by covenant, signed by the developer, stamped with the corporate crest of Vanguard Urban Regeneration PLC. His tenancy was absolute. His heating was free. His floors were warm.

He looked at the digital thermostat.

The segment numbers shifted. The room temperature dropped one tenth of a degree, from *21.0°C* to *20.9°C*.

Inside the hall cupboard, an electric relay clicked: a tiny, sharp *tink* of copper contacts meeting inside a plastic housing. A low hum traveled through the floorboards as the circulation pump started, sending sixty meters of warm water coursing through the polyethylene pipes beneath Arthur’s slippers.

The warmth rose through the soles of his feet, cradling his heels, his arches, and his toes in a soft, unbroken embrace.

Arthur finished his tea.

He set the mug on the glass table and turned off the reading lamp, sitting alone in the warm dark.

# Chapter 30: The Copper Kettle

The smell in Flat 1A was different from Arthur’s flat. Arthur’s flat smelled of seasoned pine, cold soot, and tobacco smoke from the pipes he had smoked until nineteen-ninety; Danuta’s flat smelled of beeswax, dried bay leaves, and the sweet, medicinal spirit she used to clean the framed image of Our Lady of Częstochowa above her bed.

Arthur stood in her lobby on Sunday afternoon at three. He had his tweed cap held in both hands across his belt buckle.

Danuta sat on a small kitchen chair with a wicker seat. She had her black wool coat on, buttoned to the chin with five large horn buttons, although the sun was shining through the bare bay window and the room was warm. On her head she wore a black velvet beret with a small silver pin shaped like an anchor. Around her feet were four cardboard cartons, each sealed with grey duct tape, and two battered brown leather suitcases bound with leather dog leads where the brass clasps had broken.

"Sit down, Arthur," she said. She pointed to a wooden potato crate turned upside down against the skirting. "The chairs are gone. The social lady took the table yesterday in her estate car. She said the dining room in Greenock has twenty-four tables with plastic tops. White plastic. Easy to wipe."

Arthur sat down on the potato crate. His knees protested, bending beyond ninety degrees to reach the low wooden slats, but he kept his boots flat on the floorboards and his hands clasped over his cap.

"They treating you all right?" he asked.

"The girl was very polite," Danuta said. Her hands rested in her lap, fingers intertwined. The skin of her knuckles was spotted with brown liver spots, the skin stretched tight like dried parchment over swollen arthritic knuckles. "She brought me a cardboard cup of tea from the garage. Very sweet. Two sugars. She asked if I had religious preferences for the meat. I told her no. In Gdynia, during the winter, we ate turnip peelings fried in machine tallow. Meat is meat."

"Greenock is twelve miles," Arthur said. "Two buses from Albert Quay. The thirty-six to Port Glasgow, then the nine down to the esplanade."

"You will not come on two buses, Arthur," she said gently. Her voice had no reproach in it; she stated the fact with the simple certainty of a harbor timetable. "Your knees cannot do two buses in the rain. You will go to your new flat across the street. You will have your warm floors and your bath with the low door. That is right for you. You are seventy-six."

"Seventy-four," Arthur corrected quietly.

"Seventy-four," she repeated, nodding once. "A young man. My Stefan was seventy-four when he died on the dry dock. A crane dropped an iron plate on his foot in fifty-nine. He walked with an ash stick until the end. He never complained. He said: *In Poland they shot my two brothers behind the brickyard. An iron plate on a foot is nothing.*"

She reached down between her feet. From the gap behind the largest cardboard box, she lifted an object wrapped in a faded yellow tea towel.

She unfolded the cotton cloth with slow, precise movements of her thumbs. Inside sat a heavy copper kettle with a gooseneck spout, an acorn finial on the lid, and a black turned-horn handle fixed between two curved brass brackets. The copper was polished bright as a new halfpenny, reflecting the bare yellow walls of the room and the two old people sitting opposite each other.

Arthur looked at it. His throat tightened.

"Mary's kettle," he said.

"She gave it to me in eighty-four," Danuta said. "When my Stefan died. You remember that winter? The frost was three feet in the ground. The water pipes in the close froze solid from the street main to the roof. You and Callum’s father spent two days on the stairs with blowlamps, thawing the lead bends. Mary came down to my kitchen every morning at six with this kettle full of boiling water from your scullery range, because your range was on the coal and mine was on the gas, and the gas pressure had dropped to nothing."

She held the kettle out by its brass brackets, offering it with both hands.

"I kept it on the shelf above my cooker for thirty-eight years," she said. "Every Saturday morning I cleaned it with vinegar and salt, exactly as Mary told me. You don't use Brasso on copper, she said. Brasso turns it green around the rivets. You take half a lemon and table salt, and you rub it until the metal gets warm."

Arthur reached out. His fingers took the cold brass brackets. The weight was familiar—four pounds of solid rolled copper, balanced perfectly so that the water would pour in a steady stream without splashing over the rim of the cup.

"You should take it to Greenock," Arthur said. "They have tea in the afternoons."

"In Greenock they have an electric urn," Danuta said. Her pale lips curved in a dry, faint smile that barely moved her cheeks. "A large stainless-steel cylinder with a red light and a plastic tap. You press the lever with a polystyrene cup and hot water comes out. If you put a copper kettle on the floor in Greenock, the health inspector puts it in the bin for contamination. The social lady told me: *No personal cooking appliances in the maritime wing.* Clause twelve of the resident safety handbook."

Arthur rested the kettle on his knees. The copper was cold through the thin cloth of his trousers.

"I signed the paper, Danuta," he said. The words came out before he could stop them, rough and sour like vinegar on an open scrape. "Ward came with the lease on Wednesday. Ground floor. No stairs. Level threshold. I signed it."

Danuta looked at him. Her eyes were very pale blue, faded by age and eighty years of Scottish rain, but they were completely steady.

"Of course you signed it, Arthur," she said.

"Callum called me a traitor," Arthur said. "He stood on the stairs on Friday with his bairn's cot in pieces, and he told me I sold the close for underfloor heating."

"Callum is twenty-four," Danuta said softly. "At twenty-four, a boy thinks stone is eternal. He thinks if he shouts loud enough and waves a red flag, the council will change its mind, the shipyards will open again, and his father will come home from the grave to eat mutton broth on Sunday. Callum has never seen a city burned to the ground. He has never seen a street where twelve houses stood on Monday and only a hole in the earth remained on Tuesday."

She reached out and placed her small, dry hand over Arthur's knuckles where they gripped the kettle handle. Her hand felt light, like the wing of a dead sparrow, but the grip was firm.

"This building was finished forty years ago, Arthur," she said. "When the sugar boats stopped coming. When the dry docks filled with weeds. We stayed because we were old and our dead were buried in the cemetery on the hill. But the stone does not love you. Marcus Ward does not hate you. You are an old man with bad knees, and winter is coming. Do not let Callum Reid make you feel ashamed for wanting to sleep in a warm bed."

Arthur looked down at her small black shoes. They were lace-up orthopedic shoes with thick crepe soles, polished clean with black wax.

"I don't know who I am without the stairs," Arthur said.

Danuta gave a small, dry laugh—hardly more than a puff of air through her nostrils.

"You are Arthur Cross," she said. "The man who punched the tickets on the Gourock bus. The man who sat with my Stefan when the blood came up from his lungs in seventy-nine and held his hand so he wouldn't be afraid in the dark. That is who you are. A warm floor does not change that."

Outside in the street, an engine sounded.

It was not the heavy growl of an excavator or the rattle of Callum’s hire van. It was the smooth, high-pitched diesel purr of a Mercedes-Benz Sprinter ambulance. The vehicle slowed, its tires crunching on the loose gravel outside the door, and came to a stop.

Danuta withdrew her hand from Arthur's. She smoothed the skirt of her black coat over her knees.

"That is my car," she said.

Two knocks sounded on the outer door of Flat 1A. They were brisk, professional knocks, delivered with knuckles against timber without hesitation.

Danuta stood up. She did not use the arms of the chair; she rose with a straight back, her chin lifted, her velvet beret perfectly level above her grey hair. She picked up her small black leather handbag by its strap and hung it over her wrist.

"Come," she said to Arthur. "Help me down the three steps to the street."

Arthur stood up from the crate, holding Mary’s copper kettle against his chest with his left arm. He offered Danuta his right elbow.

She took his arm. Her fingers hooked into the woolen weave of his tweed jacket, pressing tight against the bone.

Together they walked through the bare lobby of Flat 1A, out onto the stone flags of the ground floor. The ambulance driver, a young woman with blonde hair tied in a neat ponytail and wearing a green high-visibility fleece, stood in the vestibule holding a folding aluminum wheelchair.

"Mrs. Kowalski?" the girl asked cheerfully. "Ready for the road? The heating's on in the back. Nice and toasty."

"I am ready," Danuta said.

Before she stepped toward the wheelchair, Danuta turned to Arthur. She took his face between her two cold, dry palms. She stood on her toes, pulled his head down, and kissed his left cheek, then his right cheek, leaving a faint scent of lavender soap and dried peppermint on his skin.

Danuta kissed both his cheeks and whispered a quiet blessing in Polish.

# Chapter 24 — The Sister's Anger

The private shelter Bram had constructed inside the rear gallery of the cavern was small, paved with flat river slate and warmed by a secondary stone flue that drew heat from the central hearth. A single beeswax candle burned in an iron bracket, casting long, wavering shadows across the shelves of stone chisels, surveying chains, and dried botanical specimens. Marta stood by the workbench, her fists clenched inside her coat pockets. The initial joy of their reunion had receded, leaving behind the raw, jagged ache of ten months of abandonment.

"Why didn't you send word, Bram?"

The question struck the quiet room like a dropped hammer.

Bram was kneeling by his wooden sea-chest, checking the leather straps of a travel pack. He stopped, his shoulders tensing under his sheepskin coat. He did not turn around immediately.

"You let me believe you were dead," Marta said, her voice trembling with a fury she could no longer hold back.

"For ten months, I sat in that stone hut alone. I tended forty skeps by myself in the freezing wind. Every time the muster horn blew, I thought they had found your frozen bones in the scree. I had to listen to Garek call you a traitor in the village square. I had to watch Osric sneer at our father's memory."

Bram slowly rose to his feet. He turned to face her, his scarred hands hanging empty at his sides.

"I had to protect you, Marta," he said softly.

"Protect me?" She took a step toward him, her eyes blazing with tears.

"By leaving me to starve behind an oak wall? By letting the council treat me like the sister of a criminal? If Kesta hadn't risked his life to leave the gate unbolted, I would be standing on the rampart walk watching thirty thousand bees choke on sulfur smoke!"

"If Osric knew I was alive, he would have burned the apiary ten months ago!" Bram said, his own voice rising with suppressed anguish.

"You don't understand how deep his madness runs, Marta. Forty years ago, when the raiders attacked, Osric wasn't just a wounded sentry. He was the one who left the gate unlatched to trade with a northern hunter! He caused the breach!"

Marta froze.

"What?"

Bram stepped closer, the firelight catching the harsh lines of pain around his mouth.

"My father knew the truth. He found Osric's private trading tokens in the ash of the loom house. Osric traded village grain for northern furs in secret, and the raiders followed his trading track back to the gate. That is why Osric built the quarantine, Marta. Not to keep the raiders out, but to bury his own guilt! If anyone crosses that pass and proves the north is full of peaceful traders, Osric's forty years of tyranny are revealed as a monstrous lie to hide his own cowardice!"

The revelation hung in the warm cavern air like a heavy curtain.

Marta looked at her brother—at his frostbitten thumb, his weathered skin, the desperate resolve in his eyes. The puzzle of Osric's fanatical terror fell into place with sickening clarity. The closed gates, the sulfur warrants, the refusal to allow fresh seed or sheep into the valley—it was all the desperate defense of an old man terrified that his neighbors would discover who had brought the fire upon their cradles.

"Then you should have brought me with you," Marta whispered, the anger draining from her voice, leaving only the hollow ache of loneliness.

"I was your sister, Bram. We were all that was left of our family. You shouldn't have made me carry that wall alone."

Bram reached out and took her hands. His rough, calloused fingers closed over her deerskin gloves, trembling slightly.

"I thought I would be back in three weeks, Marta," he confessed, tears glistening in his beard.

"I climbed the pass to find the wild queens. I thought I could capture two swarms, walk back through the postern gate, and present the council with living proof that the bees could be saved. But the rockfall took the path. When the ledge collapsed, I was trapped on this side of the mountain with broken ribs and a shattered knee."

He looked down at his right leg, where a thick leather brace was laced around his knee joint.

"Master Vorn and the villagers found me half-dead in the scree. It took three months before I could put weight on the foot. And by then, the winter blizzards had sealed the gorge three fathoms deep. Every night I sat by that hearth, looking south toward the ridge, wondering if you had enough firewood to survive the freeze."

Marta drew a ragged breath. She leaned her forehead against his chest, feeling the steady, rhythmic beat of his heart beneath the sheepskin coat.

"I have the nucleus box, Bram," she said softly.

"And I have Hive One's failing queen. If we can reach the postern gate before sundown, we can save the apiary."

Bram turned to the shelf where his stonecutter's tools lay in neat rows: three point chisels, two claw chisels, and his heavy ball-peen hammer. 'When the ledge collapsed beneath my boots, Marta, I lay in the scree for two days with a broken knee. The torrent roared fifty feet below me. I thought of our father dying under the rockfall at Hive Twenty-three, and I thought I would share his bed on the mountain. But then a worker bee landed on my bloody knuckle. A single dark bee from the caldera swarms, drinking the moisture from my skin. It showed me that life was ten paces ahead, if I had the stomach to crawl.'

'You should have trusted me with the truth before you walked away,' Marta whispered, her tears finally drying on her cheeks. 'You made me carry the council's hatred alone.' In the dim light beneath the overhang, Marta inspected the condition of the drone brood comb she had extracted from Hive Four before their flight. She held the fragment toward the daylight, peering into the large hexagonal cells.

"Look at these caps," she said, handing a jeweler's magnifying lens to Bram.

"A normal worker cell is capped with flat, porous wax that allows the nymph to breathe. But drone cells are domed, rounded like miniature copper rivets. And here—see these tiny pinhole perforations?"

Bram squinted through the glass, his brow knotting in concentration.

"What causes the holes?"

"Varroa mites," Marta answered quietly, her voice tinged with the diagnostic coldness of an anatomist.

"The female mite slips into the cell just before capping, hiding beneath the brood food until the larva pupates. Then she pierces the soft exoskeleton of the bee nymph, feeding on its fat bodies and vectoring wing-deforming viruses. If a colony has too many mites entering winter, the emerging bees are crippled and short-lived. The winter cluster collapses in January, leaving behind full supers of untouched honey and a handful of dead workers surrounding a frozen queen."

"Did Vance know about this?" Bram asked, his jaw clenching.

"Vance believes bee blight is a divine retribution for clerical disobedience," Marta said with a dry, bitter edge.

"He poured sulfur into the infected hives and held prayer vigils in the square. He burned the combs, destroying centuries of bred resistance, while the wild swarms in the cliffs survived because they groom each other with aggressive hygiene. These mountain bees bite the legs off the mites. They uncap infested cells and drag the diseased larvae into the snow before the parasites can breed."

She carefully scraped one of the perforated caps with her bone bodkin, exposing a dead drone nymph with crumpled, vestigial wing buds—a silent, physical proof of the microscopic war that determined the fate of empires. 'I was afraid Osric would take your hives,' Bram confessed, his voice cracked with emotion. 'If he thought you were my accomplice, he would have evicted you from the apiary hut and handed the forty skeps to Garek. I had to let you stay innocent of the survey. But every night I sat by that cavern hearth and carved forty notches into my chisel, praying you were holding the wall.'

Bram tightened his arms around her shoulders.

"Then we don't wait for the snow to melt. We take the Roman conduit."

# Chapter 15 — Midnight Scent of Heather

Night fell inside the Screaming Gorge like an iron shutter dropped over an oven mouth. The cliffs on either side rose twelve hundred feet into the darkness, blotting out the stars until the sky was reduced to a narrow ribbon of deep indigo. Marta sat wedged into a shallow crevice eighty feet above the roaring torrent. She had unrolled her wool blanket over a bed of dry pine boughs, tucking her deerskin travel pack between her shoulders and the rock face. Between her knees rested the cedar nucleus box, its ventilation holes emitting the steady, faint hum of eight hundred living bees.

She drew her small copper pipe from her pocket.

She had filled it with dried sage and wild clover leaves gathered from her apiary stores. Striking a tiny spark from her flint against a piece of charred linen, she blew the ember into life and puffed a gentle draft of cool, fragrant smoke into the air around her knees.

The smoke calmed her trembling hands.

In the dark, her senses sharpened.

The mountain had its own voice, composed of fifty distinct tones: the deep bass rumble of boulders rolling in the glacial riverbed; the sharp, rifle-crack snaps of limestone sills fracturing under freezing ice; the high, whistling scream of the gale cutting through the cleft notches.

And beneath those sounds, faint as a heartbeat, drifted a scent.

Marta lowered her copper pipe. She closed her eyes, tilting her face toward the northern draft that swept down from the upper gorge.

The air was cold, but it carried an impossible perfume.

It was the rich, spicy musk of mountain rhododendron, blended with the dry, honeyed warmth of subalpine heather in full bloom. It was not the memory of flowers; it was the living fragrance of thousands of blooming corollas releasing their volatile oils into the mountain breeze. A memory broke through her exhaustion: Bram kneeling beside her in the orchard seven autumns ago, his fingers gently guiding her small nine-year-old hand to cup an active worker bee without crushing its wings.
*Listen to the beat, Marta,* Bram had told her, his voice gentle as the autumn sun. *A bee doesn't fight the wind. She angles her body into the draft and lets the air lift her over the ridge.

When the valley runs cold, trust the wing.*

Marta touched the iron chisel in her pocket.

She opened the small bronze mesh of the nucleus box and dipped her finger into a pot of honey jelly. She pressed the sweet droplet against the wire. Immediately, three tiny worker tongues extended through the mesh, lapping the food with frantic energy. The heat radiating through the cedar wood was steady. The cluster was holding ninety degrees, burning sugar to generate the biological furnace that defied the freezing mountain air.

High above the gorge, an owl hooted twice from the stunted pines.

Marta looked up at the towering silhouette of the Eagle's Beak. The crag resembled the hooked beak of an enormous raptor jutting out over the gorge. Beyond that crag lay the second marker stone, where Bram had chiseled his survey coordinates. She set a small tin cup of snow onto a flat stone near the embers of her sage pipe. As the snow melted into water, she added three dried rosehips from her pouch, watching the water turn a deep, fragrant crimson. She drank the hot broth in slow, deliberate sips, feeling the heat spread through her frozen chest and down into her trembling thighs.

Beside her knees, the nucleus box hummed with life.

The eight hundred worker bees were clustering in a dense mantle over the center frame, their tiny bodies interlocking like shingles on a roof to hold the heat. She slipped her thumb through the small feeding port and checked the sugar candy plug. The workers had eaten a third of the sweet paste, their honey stomachs full, their metabolic furnaces humming at ninety degrees despite the subzero mountain wind.

She looked at the dark silhouette of the Screaming Ridge above her.

Her father had always told her that bees were the only creatures on earth that lived entirely for tomorrow: they gathered nectar they would never taste, built wax combs they would never inhabit, and reared daughters that would never know their mothers' names. In an enclosed valley, that instinct turned into a slow, agonizing prison. Out here in the mountain wind, it was the only compass that mattered. Marta melted a piece of clean snow in her tin cup, dropping in a pinch of dried chamomile and two drops of elderberry spirit. She sipped the hot infusion slowly, feeling the heat loosen the knotted muscles in her shoulders. Her deerskin coat was stiff with rime, but inside, the nucleus box was an island of ninety-degree warmth.

The damp cold of the cleft smelled of wet slate and stagnant seepage. Marta set her copper scraper against the lower tier of wild comb, feeling the vibration of crowded bees behind the thin wax partition. In the high valley, comb was not built with the symmetrical pale fragility of valley hives; here the bees worked dark propolis into every margin, binding dry pine needles and pulverized granite into the mortar until the cells resembled miniature mortared bastions.

"Feel that," she whispered to Bram, taking his rough stonecutter's thumb and pressing it against the lower rim of the cleft.

Bram leaned his shoulder against the damp outcrop. His broad palm rested flat against the stone.

"The stone is warm," he muttered, brow furrowing under his matted beard.

"Not sunlight warm. Blood warm."

"Thirty-five degrees," she said, watching the moisture bead on his knuckles.

"Brood heat. Even without a laying queen, the cluster maintains the core temperature of a newborn animal. They burn their own muscular reserves, shivering their flight muscles in place without moving their wings. Every degree of warmth in this frost costs them ten grains of honey an hour. Look at their abdomens."

He squatted lower, his breath frosting white against the black shale.

The bees clung in overlapping shingle-layers, their wings tucked tight against their carapaces. Unlike the lazy summer bees of Oakhaven, these workers possessed dense, soot-colored pelts across their thoraxes, thick as mountain vole fur. When Marta drew her breath softly across them, they did not sting; they merely hummed with a low, bronze frequency that vibrated through Bram's boot soles.

She checked the bronze ventilation wire with her fingertip.

Two worker bees touched her skin through the mesh, their antennae stroking her cuticle in calm, steady recognition. They were not afraid of the mountain night; they were protected by their communal heat, their shared sugar stores, and the presence of their sovereign queen resting quietly in her cedar frame.

She blew out the glowing ember of her sage pipe.

She wrapped her wool blanket tight around her chest, resting her chin on the lid of the nucleus box, listening to the soft, resilient hum of the bees until the mountain dawn began to silver the eastern cliffs.

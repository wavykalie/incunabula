# Chapter 32 — Bram's Testimony

The assembly on the high rampart grew as word traveled down the stone bridle path to the village square. Weavers, bakers, grain millers, and young apprentices crowded along the narrow walkway, their cloaks pulled tight against the falling sleet. In the center of the gathering, Bram stood upon the stone mounting bench beside Hive Two. Over his shoulder hung a burlap sack that he unrolled upon the slate flags.

From the sack, Bram drew three sheaves of wheat.

The stalks were golden, four feet long, their heads heavy with plump, amber grains that rattled like dry peas when shaken. Beside the wheat he laid a fleece of raw sheep's wool—thick, long-stapled, white as mountain snow, without a trace of the yellow scab or parasite grease that plagued the damp flocks of St. Jude's Cleft. Finally, he set down an earthenware jar stoppered with cork and pitch. He pulled the cork with his teeth. A rich, sweet perfume filled the freezing air: the scent of wild mountain honey pressed from rhododendron and subalpine heather.

"Look at this harvest, people of St. Jude's," Bram said, his voice ringing across the crags.

"This was grown three miles from our boundary gate, in a caldera warmed by geothermal springs. While we divided half-loaves of smutty rye flour and watched our children cough from damp lung-rot, three thousand acres of fertile mountain soil have lain waiting across the pass."

Master Corin the baker stepped forward. He reached into the earthenware jar with his thumb, brought the dark amber honey to his tongue, and closed his eyes.

"It is the same honey the girl brought to the press," Corin said in a hushed whisper, looking at his fellow villagers.

"It has the heat of mountain thyme and cloves. One barrel of this flour would feed our children through the hardest winter we have ever seen."

Old Hal the stonecutter knelt beside the fleece. He rubbed the raw wool between his calloused palms, testing the fiber strength.

"No fluke," Hal muttered, shaking his head with bitter wonder.

"Not a trace of scab. Our weavers have had to card fleece that was thin as spider silk for twenty years because Osric told us the northern sheep carried plague."

"The plague was in our own fear," Bram said. He looked down at the crowd of familiar faces—men and women who had watched him grow from a stonecutter's boy into a master craftsman.

"For ten months, I lived among the highland people," Bram continued.

"They took me into their stone cottages when my knee was shattered in the rockfall. They dressed my wounds with wild honey and spruce pitch. They asked for nothing in return except the friendship our elders stole from them forty years ago. They have no iron. Their smiths have to rework old ploughshares because they have no bog ore. We have three tons of iron bar sitting in Master Arn's cellar that we cannot eat. Why should two valleys starve on opposite sides of a four-hundred-foot wall?"

"Because the council made a covenant!" shrieked Goodwife Elspeth from the rear of the crowd.

"A covenant sealed in blood! If you break the quarantine, God will send the frost to kill our cattle!"

"The frost is already killing your cattle, Goodwife," shouted Master Arn the blacksmith, his heavy voice drowning her out.

"Ten heifers died in the lower bog last week because the grass was rotten! We have eaten our own misery for forty years to satisfy Osric Vance's nightmares! I say the boy is right! I say we open the pass!"

A roar of agreement surged from the crowd. Craftsmen raised their work-tools in the air; young apprentices shouted approval; even the older militiamen lowered their spears and murmured their assent.

Master Corin the baker broke off a piece of the golden wheat stalk, rubbing the ear between his flour-dusted palms. The plump grains tumbled into his hand, hard, amber-colored, without a trace of the black ergot fungus that had blighted the valley rye for three seasons. He chewed the raw grain, his eyes widening. 'This is hard winter wheat,' Corin declared to the crowd. 'High-protein grain that will bake loaves light as sheep's fleece. With three wagonloads of this seed, our south terraces could feed the cleft through five blizzards.'

Old Hal the stonecutter knelt beside the raw fleece Bram had displayed.

He ran his calloused fingers through the staple, marveling at the crimp and elasticity of the wool. 'No rot,' Hal whispered, looking up at the weavers who pressed close. 'No liver fluke. Look at the grease on it—clean lanolin that smells of sweet clover and mountain birch. For twenty years, our women have carded brittle fleece that snapped on the spindles because the sheep were starved in the lower bogs.' Young apprentices pushed to the front of the circle, gazing with wide, hungry eyes at the earthenware jar of dark heather honey. Bram dipped a wooden splinter into the jar and held it out to a seven-year-old boy named Toby, the son of a widowed spinner. The boy licked the honey from the wood, his eyes lighting up with astonished joy as the spicy sweetness of wild rhododendron and mountain thyme burst on his tongue.

'The mountain is not our enemy, people of St.

Jude's,' Bram said, his voice ringing across the silent slate roofs of the village. 'The mountain is a storehouse waiting for our hands. The people of the caldera are our brothers. They have sheep and wheat and wild bees, but their smiths have to forge scythes from old horse shoes because they have no iron. We have three tons of Swedish iron sitting in Master Arn's cellar that we cannot eat. Let us open the road! Let us trade iron for wheat, wool for honey, and fear for fellowship!'

A mighty cheer rose from the craftsmen and weavers.

It rolled down the narrow streets of the cleft, striking the granite walls and startling the jackdaws from the church belfry. Men embraced each other in the snow; women wept openly into their aprons; young boys tossed their wool caps into the mountain air. That evening, the shelter held four fugitives around the hearth. The presence of Master Corin added both burden and strength to their company; though his legs were stiff with rheumatism, his hands possessed forty years of master carpentry skill.

He sat by the fire, examining the frames Marta had constructed with Bram's stone tools.

"The joints are good," Corin observed, testing the corner lap of a cedar frame with his thumbnail.

"Bram cuts wood like he cuts granite—solid, heavy, and square. But you need tenons on the top bar, Marta. When the honey fills the combs, each frame will weigh six pounds. If the top bar rests only on a butt joint, the wax weight will shear the timber right off the shoulders."

He reached into his pocket and produced a small, leather-wrapped package. Inside were two dozen forged iron nails, three bone awls, and a spool of annealed brass wire.

"I took these from my shop before I slipped past the sentries," Corin said, a fierce spark in his rheumy eyes.

"Vance's constables were banging on my front door while I went out the cellar grating. A craftsman doesn't leave his tools to be melted down into crossbow bolts."

Marta took the spool of brass wire, running the smooth, cold filament through her fingers.

"Brass wire is what we needed most," she said with deep gratitude.

"With this wire strung horizontally through the frames, we can embed the wax foundation sheets using heat. The combs won't sag or warp even if we transport the hives on pack mules across rough mountain scree."

"Show me how you embed the wax," Corin requested, leaning closer to the fire.

Marta took a thin sheet of pure beeswax foundation, embossed with the hexagonal bases of worker cells. She laid the wire across the wax, took a small stone heated in the embers, and ran it lightly along the wire. The wax softened instantly under the heated iron, welling up around the brass filament and cooling into a permanent, structural bond.

"The wire becomes the spine of the comb," she said.

"Just as the ribs support the muscle of a beast. With this, the bees don't waste energy building heavy drone comb to brace the structure. Every cell will be worker brood."

Corin nodded in deep appreciation.

"Stone, wood, and iron. The church preaches that grace comes from the sky, but survival has always come from the joiner's bench."

Master Corin sat near the hearth, carefully shaving spruce laths with an antique drawknife. The curls of sweet-scented wood accumulated around his boots like pale ribbons.

"I made hundred-gallon fermentation vats for the abbey brewery forty years ago," the old man recounted, his rheumy eyes reflecting the ember-glow.

"The monks thought beer was a gift of Saint Jude. They would sprinkle holy water over the mash tuns and chant three psalms while the yeast was pitching. But if the brewer's apprentice forgot to scald the oak paddles with boiling water, the whole batch turned to sour vinegar in five days."

"Microbes," Marta said, looking up from her wax-melting pan.

"The wild yeasts live on the skins of juniper berries and rowan fruits. In the forest, bees gather wild yeasts along with pollen, packing them into the bee bread cells. The yeast ferments the pollen inside the comb, breaking down the tough cellulose shells so the nurse bees can digest the proteins."

Corin nodded vigorously, his drawknife biting into the spruce with a clean, satisfying shhh-shhh.

"The monks called it the breath of the angels. They couldn't admit that tiny invisible creatures in the air were doing the work. They wanted the villagers to believe that without the abbot's blessing, bread wouldn't rise and beer wouldn't brew."

"And Vance used the same trick with the bees," Bram added, setting down his whetstone.

"He told the farmers that the queen's fertility came from the parish altar. He claimed that if a man didn't pay his Easter tithes, the queen would turn barren and lay only drone eggs."

"A clever lie," Marta said.

"A queen lays drone eggs when she runs out of sperm in her spermatheca, or when the comb is damaged by cold. It is biology, not divine displeasure. But fear is easier to sell than botany."

Goodwife Elspeth Vance stood alone by the mounting block, her wimple askew, her thin lips working silently as she realized that her forty years of bitter gossip had lost its audience. The fear that had sustained her had dissolved into the morning sun. On the granite bench by Hive One, Elder Osric sat collapsed against the stone wall, his head buried in his hands, weeping silent, bitter tears into his frost-covered mantle as the world he had spent forty years constructing dissolved around him like morning mist.

# Chapter 5 — The Honey Press Gathering

The village square of St.

Jude's Cleft was packed with people by mid-morning. The annual autumn pressing was traditionally the feast of the valley's wealth—the day when the yield of the thirty lower farm hives was brought to the communal beam press beside the parish mill. A massive beam of squared white oak, forty feet long and thick as a bull's brisket, rested in heavy iron trunnions anchored to the granite mill wall. Beneath the center of the beam sat the great slate pressing vat, six feet across, its stone lip grooved to guide the liquid honey into three-gallon oak casks.

The air in the square was thick with the scent of crushed honeycomb, fermenting apple pomace, and the sharp tang of spruce beer. Children in homespun smocks darted between the handcarts, scraping the wax dregs from wooden tubs with alder spoons. Two village fiddlers played by the tavern porch, their tunes sawing through the hum of talk and the creak of wooden wagon wheels. Marta stood by the edge of the pressing vat, her willow basket on the ground between her boots. Inside the basket were three comb sheets taken from the lower supers of Hive Three and Hive Four, the only border hives that had produced surplus honey this autumn. The wax was dark, capped with thin cappings that crumbled like autumn leaves when touched.

Old Master Garek, the village apiarist who managed the sheltered orchard hives in the valley basin, stood on the wooden press platform. He was seventy, with a bulbous red nose and ears tufted with gray hair. His coarse wool apron was stiff with layers of dried honey and propolis from twenty harvests.

"Pour them in, lads!" Garek shouted, waving a wooden paddle.

"Let us see what the clover gave us before the frost locks the barrels!"

Two farm boys dumped an oak tub filled with comb into the pressing crib. The honey was pale amber, thin and watery, running sluggishly across the slate grooves.

"Watery stuff this year, Garek," called out Master Corin, the village baker, leaning against his flour sacks.

"Look at the run on it. It will spoil before midwinter without turning sour in the cask."

"The summer was dry as an old skull, Corin," Garek grunted, leaning his weight against the press screw.

"The clover dried up by August. What do you expect? Wine from flint?"

"The border hives have dark comb," a woman's voice cut through the murmur of the crowd. Goodwife Elspeth Vance, Osric's widowed sister-in-law, pointed a finger at Marta's willow basket. Her sharp face was framed by a stiff linen wimple.

"Look at the wax Varek's girl brought down from the rampart. Black as peat smoke. That is not cleft honey."

The chatter around the press subsided. Several farmers turned to look at Marta. The circle around her widened by two paces, as if her deerskin coat carried the chill of the high pass.

"That is heather honey from the high ridge," Marta said clearly, meeting the woman's suspicious glare.

"It carries more mineral in the nectar. It does not spoil in the comb."

"It is bitter as gall," Garek spat, stepping down from the platform. He peered into Marta's basket, poking a crusty finger into the dark comb. He brought his finger to his mouth, tasting it with a smack of his lips. He scowled, wiping his mouth on his apron sleeve.

"Tastes of wild spruce and bog iron. That comb was gathered outside the boundary."

"It was gathered from the stone skeps on the parapet," Marta retorted.

"The same skeps that have sat on that wall since your grandfather was a boy."

"The bees are not staying on the wall," Garek said, raising his voice so the whole square could hear.

"We all heard what Elder Osric said in the hall yesterday. Hive One is dead. The queen has ceased her lay. The brood has gone cold. If the border colonies turn wild, what stops the mountain beasts from smelling the honey and climbing the cleft?"

A murmur of agreement rippled through the crowd.

"The girl's father was the same," muttered a stonecutter in a leather apron, resting his mallet on a barrel hoop.

"Always talking about the soil souring. Always wanting to clear the north pastures. And then young Bram walked away."

"Leave Bram out of this," Marta said, her voice dropping into a hard tone.

"Why should we leave him out?" the stonecutter sneered, stepping closer.

"Bram took his mason's kit and walked into the Screaming Gorge like he owned the mountain. He left his sister to tend forty hives alone, and left the village without its master stonecutter when the south bridge needed rebuilding. And now the girl comes down with black honey and expects our praise!"

"The honey is sound," Marta said. She picked up a piece of the dark comb, breaking it open with her thumbs. The thick liquid inside held its shape like dark amber jelly, gleaming in the morning light.

"Taste it, Master Corin. It carries the body of mountain thyme and summer sage. One spoon of this will keep a child from chest rot when the snow is six feet deep outside your doors."

Corin hesitated, glancing nervously at Goodwife Elspeth. Then, tempted by the rich, spicy aroma rising from the broken comb, he stepped forward and took a piece between his flour-dusted fingers. He chewed slowly.

His eyes widened slightly.

"It has heat in it," he admitted in a low voice.

"Like cloves and roasted nuts."

"It is wild mountain forage," Goodwife Elspeth snapped.

"Gathered from the forbidden rocks beyond the gate. Do not eat it, Corin. It carries the mountain rot."

Before Corin could answer, the crowd parted at the edge of the square.

Elder Osric Vance walked into the circle. He wore his black fur-trimmed mantle, his heavy oak staff striking the cobbles with measured, rhythmic thuds. Behind him walked two council wardens carrying iron-tipped pikes.

The square fell silent. The boys at the press stopped turning the screw.

Osric stopped before the slate vat. His gaze swept over the pale, watery honey dripping into the casks, then settled on the dark, jelly-like comb in Marta's hands.

"The gathering is for the village harvest, Marta," Osric said quietly. The cold authority in his voice made the baker step back into the crowd.

"We pool what the cleft produces by the labor of our hands and the grace of our soil. We do not press comb that carries the taint of the forbidden country."

"This comb was gathered by our bees, Elder," Marta said, refusing to lower the honeycomb.

"If the cleft soil cannot feed them, the bees have done what any sensible creature does: they have found food where it grows."

"And in doing so, they have breached the covenant," Osric said. He turned to Garek.

"Take the girl's basket. Dump the comb into the offal pit behind the tannery."

"No!" Marta took a step back, clutching the basket against her chest.

"This is three months of work for twenty thousand bees. You have no right to throw it to the dogs."

"The council has the right to preserve the purity of this valley," Osric said, his eyes hard as flint.

"We slaughtered thirty cattle forty years ago when the northern rot touched their hooves. We will not hesitate to destroy three pounds of honey."

He made a sharp motion with his hand. The two wardens stepped forward, their pikes held at the ready.

Marta looked around the circle of faces.

Master Corin looked down at his boots; Garek grinned with petty triumph; the women clutched their children's shoulders, their eyes wide with superstitious dread. Not one person moved to speak for her. Not one person stepped between her basket and the council's pikes. They were afraid. Their fear was not of the honey, but of the mountain itself. They had lived in their narrow cleft for so long that the sky beyond the crags seemed like an open furnace ready to consume them.

"You need not throw it in the tannery," Marta said, her voice shaking with contained rage.

"I will take it back to the rampart."

"The warrant stands, Marta Varek," Osric said, ignoring her words.

"Two days remain. If Hive One does not show normal worker brood by tomorrow's sunset, the garrison wardens will bring the sulfur cauldrons to the parapet. You will not bring that comb down to this square again."

Master Arn stepped up to the press platform, his leather sleeves rolled to his elbows. He laid his broad palm against the oak beam, feeling the shudder of the trunnions as the boys pulled the rope tackle. 'The wood is sound, Garek,' Arn said, his deep bass rumbling over the clatter of the crowd. 'White oak from the lower copse, felled three winters ago. But an oak beam cannot squeeze honey from dry comb. Look at the crib.

You're crushing straw and dead bees instead of cappings.'

Garek's face darkened with anger. 'The bees did their work, blacksmith.

It's the frost that did the damage. If we had three weeks of warm sun in September, the clover would have yielded ten casks.' He spat onto the gravel. 'Instead, we get Varek's girl bringing down black comb that smells of mountain sulfur, trying to make fools of the elder council.'

A knot of young weavers had gathered beside the tannery wall, their gray wool aprons dusted with lint. They looked at Marta's willow basket with quiet, hungry eyes. Everyone in the valley knew the winter rations would be thin. The communal storehouse held barely sixty bushels of meal and forty jars of honey to sustain two hundred people through five months of snow. A single cup of thick mountain honey could save a weaning infant from croup when the frost coated the inside walls of their cottages.

Goodwife Elspeth Vance stood with her arms crossed over her flat bosom, her gaze fixed like an iron hook on the girl. 'Her brother had that same wild look in his eye,' she muttered to the women beside her. 'Always wandering the upper scree with his chisel. Talking of old roads and northern orchards. And look where it brought him. Frozen in a crevasse before his beard was full grown. The girl needs a husband with a heavy hand to keep her by the loom instead of climbing cliffs with a smoker.'

Marta kept her eyes on the oak press screw.

She watched the dark honey oozing from the broken cappings in her basket. Each drop was a tiny sphere of concentrated sunlight, gathered by thousands of delicate tongues from mountain blossoms that bloomed where no sheep had ever trodden. She knew what the honey was worth. It was not mere food; it was medicine, light, and living memory distilled into wax.

Master Corin the baker wiped his floury hands on his apron, his gaze darting from Osric's stern face to the dark honeycomb resting in Marta's willow basket. 'The flour this year is gray, Elder,' Corin said, his voice dropping into a hesitant plea. 'The rye had black smut on the ear. The loaves come out heavy as lead, and the children get stomach gripes from eating the crusts. If we had three casks of that dark mountain honey to sweeten the dough, the bread would keep through the winter without turning sour.'

Old Garek snorted in contempt, wiping his bulbous red nose on his sleeve. 'Let them chew dried plums, Corin! The cleft has lived on rye bread for forty winters, and we haven't died of it yet. A child who grows up on wild honey will grow up with wild notions, wanting to climb cliffs and hunt northern deer instead of staying by the plough.' Inside the stone storehouse, Bram leaned over the map table where Marta had unrolled an old parchment survey of the High Boundary. The map had been drawn eighty years earlier by an imperial topographer, its contours faded to pale sepia and water-stained along the margins.

"See this cleft here?" Bram pointed with his square, calloused thumbnail.

"The map calls it the Red Gap. The surveyor marked it impassable because of rockfalls. But twenty years ago, when we quarried the pink granite for the abbey altar, my father found an old Roman mining cartway cut into the western ledge. It is narrow—too narrow for a wagon—but a pack mule or two men carrying a skep can walk it without ever showing their heads above the skyline."

Marta traced the faint brown line with the tip of her bodkin.

"And the wind in the Gap?"

"It blows north by northwest from sunrise to midday," Bram said with absolute certainty.

"Then at two in the afternoon, the thermal drafts reverse as the sun heats the eastern scree. If you light a smudge pot after noon, the smoke gets sucked straight up into the summit clouds instead of sinking into the Oakhaven valley. Vance's watchmen on the lower tower won't see or smell a thing."

Marta looked from the parchment to the quiet bee box resting on the workbench.

"The bees navigate by the sun's polarization," she remarked.

"Even in the shadow of the Gap, the light reflected from the quartz veins in the cliff will be enough for their compound eyes to fix their orientation. Once we get them through the Gap, they will recognize the southern slope within three flight circles."

The women around the press murmuring softly among themselves, their hands tucking stray wisps of hair beneath their linen wimples. They knew the taste of hunger. In February, when the snowdrifts piled five feet high against the north doors, honey was not a luxury; it was the difference between a coughing toddler surviving the croup or being carried to the churchyard before the thaw. Marta turned on her heel. She walked with slow, deliberate steps across the cobbles, her head held high, carrying her basket of black, fragrant honey out of the square and up the winding stone path toward the silence of the high peaks.

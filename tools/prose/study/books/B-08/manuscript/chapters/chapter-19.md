# Chapter 19 — The Gorge of Screaming Winds

The ascent through the Screaming Throat was a trial of cold, wind, and crumbling limestone.

Marta climbed the narrow ledge path with her head down, leaning her shoulder against the vertical rock wall to keep the violent canyon gusts from pitching her into the abyss. Below her left boot, a sheer drop of one thousand feet fell into the churning white foam of the White Torrent. The river looked like a thread of spun glass winding between the black fangs of the canyon floor.

The wind here had earned the gorge its name.

Trapped between two converging limestone massifs that rose fifteen hundred feet into the gray sky, the gale was compressed into a screaming funnel of air that roared with the pitch of an iron whistle. The sound was so deafening that Marta could not hear her own boots striking the rock.

Snow began to fall in earnest.

It was not gentle valley snow; it was granular mountain rime, hard and sharp as crushed glass, driving horizontally through the gorge at forty miles an hour. It stung her cheeks where the deerskin hood did not cover her skin, freezing her eyelashes and crusting her woolen mittens in a shell of ice.

She stopped at a narrow shelf where the path was blocked by fresh rockfall.

A slab of limestone the size of a hay wagon had sheared away from the cliff above, shattering into jagged boulders across the roadbed. To cross it, she had to crawl on her hands and knees over slippery, ice-glazed rock with a thousand-foot drop yawning six inches from her elbows.

Marta unslung the nucleus box. She checked the bronze ventilation mesh.

The bees inside were agitated, their high-pitched hum cutting through the roar of the gale. The sudden drop in barometric pressure and the violent rocking of the climb had triggered their defensive instincts. Several workers thrust their abdomens against the wire, attempting to sting the freezing air. Marta unbuttoned the top two toggles of her deerskin coat and slid the cedar box inside, holding it firmly against her woolen tunic.

The warmth of the living cluster pressed directly against her ribs, thawing the cold that had begun to numb her core.

"Hold fast, little sisters," she murmured into her collar.

"Just a little further."

She dropped to her knees on the icy limestone.

Crawl by crawl, she dragged herself across the shattered rockfall.

Her fingers, even through the deerskin mittens, went numb as they sought purchase in freezing crevices. Twice her boot slipped on the rime, sending showers of loose shale tumbling into the chasm below. She did not look down. She kept her eyes on the yellow lichen that clung to the inner cliff face, measuring her progress stone by stone, breath by breath.

When she reached the far side of the rockfall, she found a sign that stopped her breath.

Lying on a flat shelf of limestone, frozen in the snow, were six worker bees from Hive One.

They lay with their legs curled tight beneath their bodies, their wings spread flat against the rock. They had flown into the teeth of the gale, carrying their crop full of honey, until the subzero temperature had crystallized their hemolymph and stopped their wing muscles in mid-flight.

Yet their heads were all pointed in the same direction: north-northwest, toward a narrow fracture in the cliff face forty paces ahead.

Marta followed the trail of fallen bees.

Every ten paces, another dark body marked the way—a biological beacon laid down by thousands of insects that had preceded her into the mountain pass. They had flown as far as their tiny flight engines could carry them, dropping into the snow one by one, drawing a line of dead sentries that pointed straight to safety.

She rounded the angle of the fracture and stepped through a narrow rock portal.

Suddenly, the screaming wind died.

The path entered a deep, dry limestone cavern that cut straight through the shoulder of the mountain. The air inside the cave was warm, smelling of dry dust, sulfur minerals, and the unmistakable, rich perfume of active honeycombs.

The blizzard in the Screaming Throat was a living wall of ice.

Marta crawled on her belly over the limestone ledge, her fingers wedged into frozen crevices, her teeth clamped tight to prevent them from shattering in the subzero gale. Every gust of wind threatened to peel her from the cliff face and drop her a thousand feet into the churning white foam of the White Torrent. Inside her deerskin coat, the cedar nucleus box was an island of life. The eight hundred worker bees pressed against her chest, their tiny thoracic flight muscles vibrating at maximum frequency to produce metabolic heat. Through the cedar wood and her woolen tunic, she felt the steady ninety-degree pulse of the cluster—a tiny, heroic furnace that burned honey to keep their failing queen from freezing in the storm.

'Hold fast,' she chanted aloud, the words swallowed instantly by the roaring wind. 'Hold fast, little sisters.'

She reached the corner where the rockfall had blocked the ancient roadbed.

A limestone boulder the size of a hay wagon hung suspended over the abyss, held in place only by frozen gravel and ice. To bypass it, she had to crawl along an eight-inch ledge of crumbling shale with nothing beneath her right elbow but empty air and tumbling snow.

She unfastened her deerskin mittens, letting them dangle from their cords.

Her bare fingers touched the freezing limestone. The rock burned like liquid fire against her skin, numbing her nerves in five seconds. She found a horizontal fissure, jammed her fingertips into the crack, and dragged her body forward inch by inch.

Her left boot slipped on a patch of black ice.

Her leg swung out into the abyss, dislodging a shower of shale that rattled into the darkness. She hung by her fingertips for three agonizing heartbeats, her chest pressed against the cedar box, her breath catching in her throat as the gale tried to tear her from the cliff. With a desperate heave of her back muscles, she swung her knee onto the ledge, jamming her toe into a crevice and dragging her torso onto the solid rock of the far side. She lay flat on the snow, gasping for air, her heart pounding like a stonecutter's mallet against her ribs.

When she raised her head, she saw the trail of dead worker bees. They lay in the snow like dark bronze studs, frozen in mid-flight, their heads pointing steadfastly toward the rock fracture fifty paces ahead. They had flown into the teeth of the blizzard until their blood crystallized, dropping one by one to mark the road to the sanctuary. The limestone ledge was six inches wide, coated with black ice where glacial seepage had frozen in the subzero gale. Marta pressed her forehead against the freezing rock wall, her deerskin gloves clawing for purchase in a horizontal fissure. Below her left heel, a thousand feet of empty air fell into the howling darkness of the White Torrent.

She took a deep, shuddering breath, holding the cedar nucleus box tight against her ribs with her right elbow. She found a notch in the rock, jammed her iron-shod boot into the crevice, and hauled her body forward three feet. The wind tore at her deerskin hood, but her grip was iron. She was a Varek; her grandfather had carved stone on this cliff, and she would not surrender her bees to the chasm. Marta leaned against the cavern wall, her legs trembling with exhaustion, her chest heaving as she pulled the nucleus box from her coat. She had cleared the Screaming Throat.

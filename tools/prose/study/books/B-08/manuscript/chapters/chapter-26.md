# Chapter 26 — The Smoke Signals

The high lookout ledge of the northern caldera overlooked the southern crest through a narrow notch in the limestone crags. From this vantage point, three miles away and fifteen hundred feet below, the jagged notch of St. Jude's Cleft was framed by the dark walls of the Screaming Ridge.

Marta stood beside Bram on the icy platform, her brass sighting alidade resting on a stone tripod.

The sky above the valley had cleared of the initial blizzard clouds, leaving a cold, brittle canopy of pale gray slate. Through the clear air, rising from the stone rampart of St. Jude's Cleft, rose a thin, greasy plume of black smoke. It was not the fragrant blue smoke of dried sumac and birch punk. It was the thick, tarry smoke of burning brimstone and pine pitch.

"The sulfur cauldrons," Marta whispered, her hands gripping the cold brass of the alidade until the metal bit into her fingers.

"They have lit the fires."

Bram leveled his surveyor's spyglass at the distant notch. His jaw was clenched so hard the muscles in his cheek twitched beneath his brown beard.

"They haven't touched Hive One yet," Bram said, his voice flat with concentrated tension.

"Look at the smoke vector. The plume is rising from the northern end of the rampart—from Hive Thirty-six and Thirty-seven. Osric is burning the outer stations first to clear a fire lane."

Marta took the spyglass from his hand and pressed the brass cylinder to her eye.

Through the circle of ground glass, she saw the miniature figures of the garrison wardens moving along the rampart walk. Two men in leather armor were using ten-foot ash poles to lever a straw skep off its granite bench. The skep tumbled sideways over the iron tripod. A burst of yellow flame erupted from the brimstone grate, followed by an enormous billow of foul, suffocating yellow vapor that enveloped the dome. Even at three miles distance, Marta could imagine the horror inside the straw: thirty thousand worker bees choking on sulfur dioxide gas, their wings dissolving in the heat, their honey stores bursting into flame as the pitch cauldrons were overturned.

"He is burning forty years of breeding," Marta said, her voice shaking with rage.

"He is destroying the only defense the village has left."

"He has two hours until sundown," Bram said, checking the angle of the winter sun against his shadow stick.

"He will work his way along the wall from the north to the center. Hive One will be the final sacrifice when the council gathers at dusk."

Beside them on the ledge stood Master Vorn, holding a heavy iron pry-bar and a coiled hemp climbing rope.

"The Roman conduit is clear as far as the second siphon, Bram. My lads took pickaxes to the river ice this morning. You can walk the underground flume beneath the Screaming Throat, but when you reach the lower gorge, you will have to wade through two feet of glacial runoff before you reach the postern arch."

"We can wade," Marta said, turning to the old smith.

"The water is thirty-four degrees, girl," Vorn warned grimly.

"Ten minutes in that current and your legs will turn to wooden posts. If you lose your footing, the river will sweep you under the boundary gate like a drowned rat."

"I have the nucleus box," Marta said. She patted the cedar box lashed firmly inside her deerskin coat.

"The bees are holding ninety degrees against my ribs. As long as the queen is breathing, I can walk through ice."

Bram reached into his pocket and drew out his father's touchstone. He pressed it into Marta's hand, closing her cold fingers around the smooth agate. From the lookout ledge, the smoke from the valley parapet grew thicker, a dark, greasy smudge against the white snowfields of the Screaming Ridge. Marta adjusted her sighting alidade, noting the azimuth. The smoke was drifting south-southeast, carried by the mountain draft straight down into the village square.

'He is burning Hive Thirty-six now,' Marta reported, her eye pressed to the spyglass. 'I can see the sulfur cauldrons smoking on the third bench. Garek is holding the canvas shroud over the dome.' Bram's hand tightened on his iron mallet until his knuckles turned gray. 'Garek is a coward who never raised a queen in his life. He would burn the entire mountain if Osric gave him two bushels of barley.'

Inside the shelter, Marta opened the transport box to perform the evening feed. The bees clustered tightly between three movable bars of cedar wood, forming a dark, pulsating sphere the size of a human head. She warmed a thick cake of candy board—made from boiled beet sugar, elderberry juice, and a pinch of ground sea salt—over the embers of the hearth until it became pliable as baker's dough.

"Why the salt?" Bram asked, watching her press the sweet slab directly onto the top bars above the cluster.

"A bee's metabolic hemolymph requires sodium and potassium to conduct nerve impulses," she explained, smoothing the edge of the candy cake so it did not crush any crawling workers.

"In deep winter, bees cannot fly to mineral springs or wet dung heaps to gather minerals. If they consume pure honey without trace salts, their flight muscles cramp when spring arrives. Old beekeepers in the lowlands thought feeding salt brought the rot, but wild colonies always seek out deer licks and mineral-rich seeps in the rocks."

The bees responded immediately to the scent of the warm candy.

Dozens of antennae quivered in unison, reaching upward between the cedar slats. Slender proboscises began probing the sugar paste, transferring dissolved carbohydrates from tongue to crop, and then sharing it mouth-to-mouth with their sisters through trophallaxis.

"See that?" Marta whispered, pointing to two workers touching heads.

"That is the communication network of the hive. When one bee takes in food, she shares it with five others within sixty seconds. Along with the sugar, they pass chemical messages—pheromonal signals that tell the entire cluster that the food supply is secure, that the queen's scent is present, and that there is no need to panic and break formation."

Bram watched in silent fascination, his rough face illuminated by the amber glow of the lantern.

"A city without words," he murmured.

'We have less than two hours before the sun hits the western notch,' Marta said, collapsing the brass tripod. 'If we aren't through the Roman conduit before the fourth bell, Hive One will be next.'

"We go now," Bram said. He picked up his heavy iron mallet and his mason's tool roll.

"If Osric wants to burn the border wall, he will have to strike the flint through my chest."

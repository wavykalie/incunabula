# Chapter 30 — At the Postern Gate

Marta walked down the center of the rampart walk, her boots striking the slate flags with slow, deliberate rhythm. With every pace she took, the crowd of villagers parted before her, their eyes wide with reverence and wonder.

She stopped ten paces from Hive One.

Before her stood Elder Osric Vance, his heavy oak staff planted like an iron stake in the stone, his torch still sputtering above the yellow sulfur cakes of the tripod. Between them lay the ruined, smoking remains of Hive Thirty-seven and Thirty-eight—blackened straw, melted wax, and thousands of dead worker bees floating in puddles of sulfur sludge.

"Lower the torch, Elder," Marta said. Her voice was quiet, but it filled the narrow rock throat of the cleft with the force of an ancient decree.

"You have broken the quarantine, Marta Varek," Osric said, his voice shaking with a fury born of forty years of buried terror.

"You have unbarred the sacred gate. You have consorted with an exile in the wild country. The law of the covenant demands your life and the destruction of your apiary."

"The covenant was made to preserve the life of this valley, not to ensure its starvation," Marta answered. She took one step forward, setting her cedar nucleus travel box upon a flat granite bench beside Hive Two.

She unlatched the leather strap of the box. She slid the pine lid open two inches.

A wave of rich, warm perfume rose from the interior: the unmistakable scent of wild mountain rhododendron, subalpine thyme, and the sweet, powerful royal pheromones of a healthy, fertile queen.

The effect on the surviving border hives was immediate and miraculous.

Along the rampart walk, from Hive Two to Hive Ten, the shivering, listless worker bees on the landing boards raised their heads. Their antennae quivered. They turned their bodies toward the cedar box, lifting their abdomens and fanning their wings in a joyous, vibrating roar of recognition. The cold paralysis that had gripped the wall for two weeks was broken by the living scent of a northern sovereign.

"She is a mountain queen," Marta said, looking around the circle of villagers.

"Daughter of the wild colonies that have nested in the northern caverns for two hundred years. Her workers have thick thoracic velvet that allows them to fly in freezing fog. Her blood carries natural resistance to the brood blight that has starved our valley for twenty seasons."

She reached into her coat pocket and drew out her father's touchstone. She laid the smooth, polished agate upon the flat slate sill of Hive One.

"And she was gathered from the northern caldera," Marta continued, turning her eyes upon Osric.

"A valley of warm geothermal springs, blooming heather, and peaceful weavers who remember our father's name. A valley that has sent messengers of peace three times in twenty years, only to have their ambassadors driven off by your crossbows."

A murmur of shock and outrage broke from the crowd. Master Corin the baker stepped forward, his fists clenched at his sides.

"Is this true, Elder? Have you kept the pass closed against our own kinsmen?"

"They are raiders!" Osric screamed, his face contorted with madness, raising his torch high above Hive One.

"They slaughtered my Sarah! They burned the loom house! I will burn this entire wall to black dust before I let them cross that threshold again!"

He lunged forward, thrusting the blazing pitch torch directly toward the dry straw skirt of Hive One.

Bram moved like a striking viper.

His heavy ash-handled stone mallet flashed through the blizzard air.

The iron head struck the pine shaft of Osric's torch two inches below the elder's fist, shearing the burning pitch head away and sending it spinning over the parapet into the four-hundred-foot abyss of the White Torrent.

Osric stumbled backward, clutching his bruised wrist, his oak staff clattering onto the slate flags.

"The fire is out, Osric," Bram said quietly, towering over the broken elder.

"The forty years of silence are ended."

Bram turned to the crowd of villagers, raising his hand toward the open postern gate where the mountain wind blew clean and sweet from the high peaks.

Marta stepped to Hive One.

The straw dome was silent, cold, its landing board littered with dying worker bees that had huddled against the entrance to defend their home from the sulfur smoke. She slipped her hive tool beneath the hazel hoop, breaking the brittle propolis seal.

She set her cedar nucleus travel box beside the stone sill.

She slid the pine lid open, revealing the three frames of living mountain comb. The young queen, magnificent in her bronze velvet coat, walked proudly across the wire mesh, her royal retinue fanning their wings and releasing a potent cloud of sovereign pheromones that drifted across the rampart walk. On the fifth morning, the wind shifted to the southwest, bringing a subtle softening to the air. The temperature rose just enough to soften the crust of the snow, turning the drifts into heavy, wet slush that clung to their boots.

Marta approached the hives with cautious optimism. A slight rise in temperature during midwinter was both a blessing and a peril.

"If it warms too much," she warned Bram, who was clearing drainage channels around the stone terrace, "the bees will think spring has come. The queen will start laying hundreds of eggs a day. But if another arctic cold front slams down three days later, the cluster cannot cover that large brood area. They will be forced to choose between abandoning the brood to freeze or freezing to death themselves trying to cover it."

She gently lifted the outer stone lid of the experimental hive, peering through the inner glass pane she had salvaged from the apiary window frame.

Below the glass, the bees hung in a dense, quiet cluster.

At the center, deep within the warmest core, a subtle movement caught her eye. The old queen, recognizable by her elongated, golden-amber abdomen and scarred right wing, was moving slowly across a circle of cleaned cells.

"She hasn't started laying yet," Marta whispered with relief.

"She is testing the cell bottoms with her antennae, but she senses the cold rock behind the comb. She is waiting."

"A patient monarch," Bram murmured behind her shoulder.

"A wise one," Marta corrected him.

"In the valley, beekeepers stimulate their queens with warm sugar syrup in February to get early spring swarms. But in these mountains, patience is the only virtue that keeps blood moving. A queen who lays before the alder trees flower kills her people before the thaw."

The effect on the surviving border hives was electric.

Along the stone benches, twenty thousand worker bees stirred from their cold stupor, raising their heads and turning toward the living queen. The dark nightmare of the quarantine was ended. In its place, the living song of forty thousand wings rose into the winter sky, claiming the mountain pass as their own.

"Bring the smoker," Bram commanded.

"Bring the wax knives and the clean frames. Today, we supersedure the queen of St. Jude's Cleft."

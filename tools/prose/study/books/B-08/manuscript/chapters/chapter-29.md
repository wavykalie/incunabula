# Chapter 29 — Kesta's Signal Horn

The blast of the ox horn echoed against the vertical cliffs of the Screaming Ridge, its brass resonance vibrating through the granite battlements with the authority of an iron bell. On the watchtower roof, sixty feet above the stone walkway, Kesta Holm stood with his legs braced against the howling blizzard. His boiled-leather armor was crusted with rime, his bear-fur mantle whipping in the gale like a dark banner. In his right hand he held his ash spear, its leaf-shaped iron tip polished bright; in his left, he held the polished horn of an ox, pressing the brass mouthpiece to his lips for a second, deafening blast.

Below him on the rampart walk, the council bailiffs panicked.

"The clans!" shouted a young warden, dropping his sulfur shovel into the snow.

"The northern tribes are on the wall!"

"Stand your ground, you cowards!" Elder Osric roared, striking his oak staff against the flags with furious violence.

"There are no clans! It is the traitor on the tower! Bailiffs, take the stairs! Bring him down in chains!"

Two guards turned toward the watchtower door, their crossbows leveled.

Before they could take three paces, Kesta cast down his spear. The heavy ash shaft clattered onto the slate flags twenty feet below, striking the stone between Osric and the bailiffs with a ringing crack that made the men jump back.

"Look to the postern gate, Osric!" Kesta's voice boomed down from the battlements, cutting through the blizzard with the clarity of a trumpet.

"The border is not dead! The living have returned!"

Every eye on the rampart turned toward the north arch.

Through the narrow wicket door of the postern gate stepped two figures in snow-covered deerskin and mountain sheepskins.

The first was Marta Varek.

Her face was pale, smudged with soot and river silt, her dark hair plastered to her temples with frozen spray. But her head was held high, her step was unyielding, and in her hands she carried a cedar nucleus travel box, held forward like an imperial standard. Beside her towered a broad-shouldered man in a heavy elk-hide coat, an iron stonecutter's mallet in his belt, his bearded face set in lines of grim, unshakeable authority.

The villagers along the walkway fell dead silent. Even the whistling wind through the crenellations seemed to die into an astonished hush.

Old Hal the stonecutter dropped his grain shovel onto the flags. He took three trembling steps forward, his mouth opening in disbelieving awe.

"Bram?" the old man whispered.

"By the grace of the stone... it is Bram Varek!"

"A ghost!" shrieked Goodwife Elspeth Vance, crossing herself with frantic hands.

"The girl has brought an exile's phantom back through the gate!"

"I am no phantom, Goodwife," Bram's voice rang across the rampart, deep, resonant, carrying the unshakeable weight of a master craftsman who had dressed the foundation stones of their village.

"I am Bram Varek, son of your master beekeeper. And I have returned from the north valley with the cure for our dying hives."

Osric Vance stood motionless before Hive One.

The torch in his right hand trembled, its red flame hissing against the falling sleet. His face was gray as ash, his bloodshot eyes staring at Bram as if the mountain had opened its granite roots and cast up his deepest, most terrifying nightmare.

"Seize them!" Osric rasped, his voice cracking with desperate hysteria.

"Wardens! Crossbowmen! Shoot them where they stand! They carry the northern pestilence into our cleft!"

Not a single bailiff raised a weapon.

On the high watchtower battlements, Kesta Holm sounded his ox horn a third time—a long, triumphant blast that rolled across the valley cleft like the cry of a mountain eagle. He lowered the horn, wiped the snow from his brow, and looked down at the astonished crowd below. Old Master Corin the baker was the first to speak. 'It is Bram!' he cried, his flour-dusted hands trembling in the air. 'Bram Varek has returned from the mountain! He is alive!'

Night fell with sudden, arctic swiftness. The stars above the gorge appeared unusually large and piercingly bright in the thin mountain atmosphere, glittering like scattered diamonds on black velvet. Inside their bivouac, Bram rekindled the hearth with dried pine roots that burned with a hot, steady flame and smelled of rich turpentine. Marta sat cross-legged near the heat, carving a new queen cage from a scrap of mountain elderberry wood.

She used a narrow spoon-gouge to hollow out the soft white pith from the center of the twig, leaving a thin, durable wooden cylinder with a three-eighths-inch bore.

"Why elderberry?" Kesta asked, watching the wood curls fall into Marta's lap.

"Elderberry wood has a natural chamber," Marta explained, holding the cylinder up to the firelight.

"The pith pushes out easily with a wire, leaving a smooth interior that won't snag a queen's legs or wings. Then I drill five tiny air holes through the wall with a heated iron needle, and plug one end with a cork made of beeswax and powdered sugar."

"The queen candy," Bram noted.

"Yes. When you introduce a new queen to a strange hive, the workers want to kill her because her pheromone smells unfamiliar. If you release her loose, they ball her—clumping around her in a suffocating mass until she cooks to death from their collective body heat. But inside this wooden cage, she is protected. The workers must chew through the sugar plug to reach her. It takes them two days to eat the candy away. By the time they finish the plug, they have smelled her pheromones through the air holes for forty-eight hours, and they accept her as their mother."

Bram shook his head, poking the hearth log with an iron rod.

"A calculated truce negotiated through sweet sugar. Men could learn a lesson from that."

The crowd surged forward against the bailiffs' picket line.

The guards, unnerved by the sight of their former master stonecutter standing tall and formidable beside his sister, lowered their crossbows and refused Osric's frantic orders to fire. The authority of forty years of fear was evaporating like mist before the morning sun. The guards stood frozen, their crossbows pointing at the slate floor, their eyes moving from Bram's living face to the ancient mason's mark carved into the stone mallet at his belt.

# Chapter 13 — The Watchtower Negotiation

The upper chamber of the north watchtower was octagonal, paved with rough slate and pierced by eight narrow embrasures that looked out over the cleft, the village roofs, and the jagged gap of the Screaming Gorge. An iron brazier burned in the center of the room, fueled with bitter coal that sent a foul yellow smoke drifting toward the timber roof beams.

Kesta Holm stood at attention before the heavy oak table.

His spear stood propped against the stone corner, its ash shaft polished bright with neatsfoot oil. His helmet rested on the bench beside him, leaving his dark hair damp with sweat despite the draft blowing through the arrow slits.

Across the table sat Elder Osric Vance.

Osric had unclasped his charcoal mantle, draping it over the back of the high chair. On the table lay Kesta's iron key ring, the heavy brass ox horn, and a leather-bound sentry log open to the previous night's watch.

"You have served on this wall for seven years, Kesta," Osric began, his voice deceptively mild.

"Your father is an honorable stonecutter. He laid the foundation course for the south granary with his own hands. When the raiders burned our farms forty years ago, your grandfather stood beside me at the lower ford and took three spear wounds in his thigh."

"I know our family's service, Elder," Kesta answered steadily.

"Then explain to me how the North Postern Gate came to be unbolted while you held the keys."

Osric tapped the sentry log with the flat of his fingernail.

"The log records your signature at the second bell, at the midnight change, and at the fourth bell. At no point do you record unbarring the oak balks. Yet the gate was found four inches ajar, the grease on the iron slides fresh as lard from this morning's skillet."

Kesta kept his eyes fixed on the central arrow slit, where the gray peaks of the northern range met the swollen snow clouds.

"The frost works the stone jambs, Elder. In autumn, when the cold strikes the gorge, the granite sill contracts. The lower bolt has slipped its socket twice this month. I reported the fault to Master Garek three weeks ago."

"Do not lie to me, boy," Osric said, his voice dropping into a harsh gravelly rasp. He leaned across the table, his scarred face inches from Kesta's breastplate.

"Garek knows nothing of iron bolts. Marta Varek is gone from her hut. Her sea-chest was rifled. Her brother's tool roll is missing. And five thousand bees from Hive One streamed through that gate twenty minutes before the alarm horn sounded."

"The swarm took flight on its own, Elder," Kesta said.

"You heard the alarm horn yourself. I sounded three blasts from the watchtower roof the moment I saw the dark cloud rise above the parapet walk."

"You sounded the horn after she was already through the gate," Osric snarled. He stood up, towering over the table, his heavy hands gripping the oak edge until the wood creaked.

"You gave her twenty minutes of grace. In twenty minutes, a mountain girl with deerskin boots can clear the lower scree and reach the shelter caves behind the Eagle's Beak."

"If you believe I aided an exile, Elder, put me in irons," Kesta said, his jaw tightening with stubborn pride.

"Call the council wardens. Strip my boiled leather and lead me to the granary vault. But you have no witness, and you have no confession."

Osric stared at him for a long, suffocating minute. The charcoal brazier hissed as a snowflake drifted down through the roof vent and vanished upon the red coals.

"You think you are noble, Kesta," Osric said softly, returning to his chair. He picked up the heavy brass horn and turned it over in his scarred hands.

"You think you are protecting a brave girl who chases a phantom brother. You believe the elders are cruel old men who hoard the valley's grain out of spite."

He set the horn down with a dull thud.

"Listen to me, boy," Osric whispered.

"Forty years ago, I stood on the rampart walk where you stood last night. I was four-and-twenty. I was strong, faster with a spear than you will ever be, and I believed the mountain was a garden waiting to be claimed. When the border hives drifted north that autumn, I laughed. I told the council the bees were seeking sweeter clover. I left the postern gate unlatched for three young hunters who wanted to chase red deer in the high basin."

Kesta's gaze flicked to Osric's face. The elder's eyes were clouded with an ancient, incurable agony.

"The hunters did not come back," Osric said.

"The northern raiders came back. Three hundred men in boiled elk-hide and iron caps, armed with fire-hardened pikes and pitch pots. They came through that open gate in the dead of a winter night, while the sentries slept beside the braziers. They burned seventeen houses before we could muster the militia. My young wife, Sarah, was trapped in the loom house. When I broke through the burning timber to reach her, there was nothing left to bury but her silver wrist ring."

The chamber was utterly silent, save for the howling wind outside.

"The only thing that saved this valley from total slaughter was the border apiary," Osric continued, his voice shaking with a terrible intensity.

"My father ran along the parapet with a timber balk, overturning the forty stone skeps into the rock throat. The bees rose in the darkness. Two hundred thousand venomous mountain bees filled the narrow cleft, stinging men and horses until the raiders threw down their swords and leaped into the gorge to escape the agony. That venom is what bought your life, Kesta. That wall of bees is the only reason your mother did not burn in her cradle."

Kesta swallowed hard. The brass horn on the table seemed to gleam with the reflected light of forty years of fire.

"I do not hate the bees," Osric said, leaning back in his chair.

"I revere them. But a hive that drifts into the wild country is a beacon to the wolves. If the northern clans see bees crossing the gap, they know the pass is open. They know the boundary is unkept."

"Bram told me there are no clans in the high basin, Elder," Kesta said in a low voice.

"He said the north holds only geothermal springs and wild meadows."

"Bram was a boy blinded by his own tools," Osric snapped.

"And now your little beekeeper will freeze in the scree beside him. The blizzard will strike before sundown. No living soul can survive forty-eight hours on the Eagle's Beak in an autumn blizzard."

Osric stood up and buckled his mantle. He reached for his oak staff.

"You are relieved of duty, Kesta Holm," Osric commanded.

"Hand over your dagger. You will remain confined to this guardroom until the council renders its judgment. If Marta Varek does not return before the storm breaks, you will be tried for treason at the winter solstice."

Kesta looked at the heavy brass key ring lying on the oak desk between them. The keys were iron, four inches long, stamped with the heraldic bee-wing mark of the border guard. His father had polished those keys with fine river sand every Sunday evening, hanging them above the family hearth as a symbol of their sworn trust.

Now those keys had become an indictment of treason.

'You think my father will curse me for leaving the bolt free, Osric?' Kesta asked, his voice steadying. 'My father watched Bram walk into the gorge ten months ago. He sat by our hearth and carved a stone memorial for him, dressing the slate with his finest chisel. When he finished, he told me that Bram had more courage in his little finger than the whole council had in their five gray beards.'

Osric's face darkened with terrible fury.

He rose from his chair, his tall, lean frame casting a menacing shadow across the octagonal chamber. 'Your father is a stonecutter who works with dead granite! He knows nothing of governance! He knows nothing of the night when Sarah screamed from the loom house while forty raiders held the street with fire-hardened pikes!'

He stepped to the narrow arrow slit that looked north into the gorge. The wind whistled through the stone aperture, spraying fine rime across his gray beard. 'Look out that window, Kesta. Look at the black limestone of the Screaming Throat. Do you think those cliffs care for a boy's romantic dreams? The mountain is a beast of frost and stone that devours everything that crosses its ridge. If the girl is out there in this wind, her fingers are already black with frostbite. In two hours, her blood will stop moving in her veins.'

'Marta knows the mountain better than you, Elder,' Kesta answered, his eyes locking with the old man's stare. 'She carries the smoker and the nucleus box. She knows how to read the bee flight. If anyone can find the northern pass, it is old Varek's daughter.' 'And if she finds it?' Osric whispered, his voice dropping into a harsh, trembling rasp. 'If she finds the north valley and brings those people to our gate, what then? Do you think they will bring loaves and honey? They will bring the reckoning of forty years of hatred! They will burn our cottages to the ground and dance on the ashes of our church!'

'Then why did they send messengers?' Kesta demanded, taking half a pace toward the table. 'Why did they send two pack mules with smoked salmon three autumns ago, begging for an audience? You ordered the sentries to loose crossbow bolts from this very window before their men could speak! You were not defending the valley, Osric.

You were defending your own lie!'

Osric walked to the iron brazier in the center of the watchtower floor, holding his scarred hands over the red coals. 'My father told me that a valley without walls is like a sheepfold without hurdles, Kesta,' the old man said, his voice dropping into a solemn, archaic rhythm. 'When you open the gate to the wild country, you invite every predator on the mountain to feast upon your flock. We built St. Jude's Cleft with our blood, and we preserved it with our silence.' The wind through the gorge tore at their sheepskin mantles, carrying the sharp, flinty dust of the scree. Bram stopped beneath the overhang of a split dolomite block, sheltering the transport skep behind his broad back while Marta adjusted the vent louvers.

"Look at the lichen on the north face of this boulder," she pointed out, brushing a dusting of rime from the rock.

"It is bright orange xanthoria. Xanthoria only grows where the air is rich in nitrogen. That means sheep grazed this valley sixty years ago, before the boundary was sealed by the first quarantine edicts. The old herdsmen knew this pass wasn't cursed. They brought their ewes up here every July to escape the liver flukes in the valley bottoms."

Bram touched the rough crust of lichen.

"My father spoke of the summer pastures," he said, his voice dropping to a gravelly murmur.

"He said the cheese made from high mountain milk was yellow as marigolds, with a sharp, peppery rind that kept for three winters in the cellar. The guildmasters in the lowlands hated it because it didn't spoil during transit. They couldn't force the herdsmen to sell at harvest prices when the mountain cheese could sit in a cool cave until the winter market opened."

"Control of food is control of obedience," Marta said. She took a pinch of dried thyme leaves from her pouch, rolled them between her palms, and dropped them into the smoker chamber.

"If a village cannot store its own food through the frost, it will kneel to whatever lord holds the grain elevators. That is why Vance burned the wild apiaries. A peasant who harvests eighty jars of wild honey has no need to buy sugar from the parish storehouse at twenty silver groschen a pound."

Marta pulled her wool mittens on over her calloused fingers, testing the tension on the pack ropes. The cedar transport box sat securely between two ash-wood pack frames, padded with thick pads of dried moss and wrapped in oiled canvas.

"A bee carries a drop of honey in her honey crop that weighs half as much as her own body," she explained to Bram as they secured the last hemp hitch.

"During normal flight, that fuel lasts her for four miles. But in the cold, she burns that honey not for propulsion, but to keep her thoracic temperature above ten degrees. If her temperature drops below eight, her flight muscles seize, her wings lock, and she drops like a stone."

"Then we keep them warm," Bram said simply, hauling on the cinch until the cedar frame groaned against the ash stay.

"I packed four hot river stones wrapped in sheep's wool into the false bottom beneath the floor screen. The heat will radiate into the lower air space for five hours without scorching the comb."

Marta placed her palm against the lower vent. A faint, gentle warmth rose from the mesh, fragrant with the smell of warm wax, cedar resin, and sweet honey.

"Five hours is enough to reach the first rock shelter at the timberline," she said.

"If the snow holds off, we will have them in the cliffs before nightfall."

'And look what your silence has bought us, Osric,' Kesta replied, pointing through the arrow slit at the village below. 'Forty families sharing two hundred acres of sour bog. Sheep with liver rot. Barley bread that tastes of smut. We are not living in a sheepfold, Elder. We are living in a slaughterhouse where the butcher takes forty years to swing the mallet.' Kesta unbuckled his iron dagger and laid it upon the table beside his horn. He did not speak. He stood rigid by the window, watching Osric's dark mantle disappear down the stone stairs, his eyes searching the white mist of the Screaming Gorge for the last trace of the girl he loved.

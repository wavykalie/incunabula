# Chapter 10 — The Drifting Swarm

The first autumn frost fell from the gray sky not as soft flakes, but as hard, granular pellets of rime that hissed against the dry straw of the forty skeps. By three hours after noon, the temperature on the high rampart had dropped ten degrees below freezing. Marta stood before Hive One, her small travel sack slung over her shoulders. Inside the sack rested her cedar nucleus travel box, lined with sheepskin fleece and fitted with three empty comb frames, beside Bram's mason's chisel and the glass vial of dried rhododendron blossoms. She carried two spare tallow candles, a tinderbox of dry charred linen, and a small copper flask of elderberry spirit to warm her blood in the mountain night.

She knelt upon the icy flags to listen.

Inside Hive One, the silence had broken.

It was not the steady, rhythmic hum of a contented winter cluster, nor the high-pitched buzzing of an angry colony preparing to defend its stores. It was a strange, undulating song: the collective piping of thousands of worker bees sounding a migratory alarm.

The hive was swarming.

Swarming in mid-autumn was biological madness.

An ordinary swarm departed in the lush warmth of May or June, when flowering trees provided abundant nectar and warm sun allowed worker bees to draw new wax combs within forty-eight hours. To swarm in late October, on the eve of a mountain blizzard, was an act of desperate suicide.

Yet the bees had no choice.

A colony that remained inside Hive One faced the certain death of Osric's sulfur cauldrons at sundown. The worker bees, attuned to the dying pheromones of their matriarch and the rich, foreign scent of alpine pollen carried home by the scouts, were executing the ancient emergency protocol of their species: absconding.

The flight board suddenly boiled with dark bodies.

They poured from the wicker entrance in a thick, living flood.

Thousands of dark mountain bees spilled over the slate bench, tumbling over one another in their haste to reach the open air. Their bodies were heavy, gorged to maximum capacity with the remaining honey stores from the center combs. Each worker carried three days of flight fuel stored in her abdomen.

Marta stepped back, shielding her face with her deerskin sleeve.

A cloud of five thousand bees rose from Hive One, climbing into the freezing mountain gale. The sound was deafening—a deep, vibrating roar that echoed against the granite walls of the Screaming Ridge like a distant avalanche. They circled twice above the stone rampart walk, forming a dark, whirling funnel that blocked out the pale light of the afternoon sky. The wind caught the edge of the swarm, shredding the outer veil of bees, but the core held tight, bound together by the irresistible pull of their queen's flight path.

From the watchtower roof, an alarm horn sounded. Kesta's warning blast tore through the valley air: three short, panicked notes signaling a breach of the perimeter.

Marta did not look back toward the watchtower.

The swarm straightened its flight.

Guided by the polarization of the dim winter sky and the invisible scent trail of the north gap, the five thousand bees surged forward in a dense, humming spearhead. They swept over the battlements of the North Postern Gate, cutting through the freezing mist, and plunged directly into the sheer limestone depths of the Screaming Gorge.

Marta ran along the parapet walk, her boots slipping on the rimed slate.

At the iron-banded postern gate, she threw her weight against the oak timber. The center bolt, greased with lard by Kesta's hand, slid back with a dull, heavy thump. The lower bolt yielded under her boot.

She hauled the massive gate open four inches—just wide enough for her slender frame to slip between the iron-plated timbers.

She stepped through the threshold.

Behind her lay the enclosed cleft of St. Jude's, with its forty chimneys, its sulfur cauldrons, and its fearful elders huddled behind their walls. Ahead rose the Screaming Gorge: a howling wilderness of black limestone, crashing glacial water, and tumbling snow.

She checked the cedar nucleus box inside her pack one final time.

The sliding lid was locked tight with a rawhide thong. Inside, the three frames of empty worker comb rested securely in their rebates, ready to receive whatever mountain queen she might find in the high passes. She had packed three strips of dried venison, a small bag of parched barley, and a cake of pine tallow for her boots.

She looked back once toward the village of St.

Jude's Cleft. The stone cottages huddled together in the gloom like sheep before a storm. In an hour, the wardens would muster with their pitch cauldrons. They would climb the rampart stairs, kindle their sulfur fires, and find forty empty stone benches and an open postern gate. Marta unrolled her wool blanket and wrapped it tightly around her cedar nucleus box, tying the bundle with braided rawhide to keep the cedar frames from rattling against each other on the descent. She checked her emergency kit: three tallow matches wrapped in bladder skin, a small vial of clove oil to soothe stings, and a cake of hard barley bread baked with elderberries.

She looked at Hive One.

The five thousand absconding bees had formed a dense, vibrating cloud that swept like a living river over the stone merlons of the postern gate. In hive biology, absconding was the final roll of the dice: when a colony faced certain destruction from starvation or fire, they abandoned their wax, their brood, and their honey stores, staking the survival of their species on a single desperate flight toward a new frontier. Marta paused beside the frozen runnel, checking the canvas lashings on the pack frames. Cold wind tore at the fringe of her hood, carrying the faint, sharp smell of ozone from the crags above.

"The air pressure is falling," she said to Bram.

"Feel how the smoke clings to the dwarf juniper rather than rising? When the barometric column drops this fast, the bees inside the core tighten their ball by another two inches. Every worker draws her wings in flat against her abdomen, shingling together like slate tiles on a steep roof. If we move them while the cluster is that tight, a sudden jolt can dislodge the outer crust."

Bram nodded, his wool mitten testing the knot on the cedar crossbar.

"I padded the runners with sheepskin fleece. Even over rough schist, the transport box rides smoother than a hay cart on the lower turnpike. We have two hours before the wind turns to sleet."

She swung her pack onto her shoulders, tightened the chest strap, and stepped onto the threshold of the open wicket door.

High above the gorge path, the dark cloud of drifting bees flew into the mountain gale, drawing a golden line across the winter sky. Marta pulled the hood of her deerskin coat tight around her face, gripped Bram's chisel in her mitten, and followed the swarm into the dark.

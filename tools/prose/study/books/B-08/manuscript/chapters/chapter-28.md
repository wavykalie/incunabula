# Chapter 28 — The Descent in Snow

The exit of the Roman conduit was a narrow arch obscured by heavy icicles that hung like iron portcullis bars across the mouth. Beyond the ice, the lower gorge was enveloped in a swirling twilight fog of snow and mountain spray. Bram used the butt of his stone mallet to shatter the icicles. The frozen spikes crashed onto the shale rocks below, chiming like church bells in the winter wind.

They stepped out onto the open mountain shoulder, fifty paces from the high defensive battlements of St. Jude's Cleft.

The scene before them was a nightmare of fire and smoke.

Along the upper rampart walk, three of the stone skeps had already been destroyed. Their straw domes had collapsed into mounds of blackened, glowing embers that hissed against the falling snow. Dense, greasy clouds of sulfur vapor drifted along the parapet wall, carried by the northern wind toward the village roofs below.

Near the center of the wall, surrounded by eight armed bailiffs holding pitch torches and iron-tipped pikes, stood Elder Osric Vance.

Beside Osric stood Master Garek, holding a smoking brazier of sulfur cakes on an iron shovel. In front of them, lashed to its granite bench by heavy hemp rope, sat Hive One. A crowd of fifty villagers stood fifty yards back along the rampart walkway, held at bay by four guards with cocked crossbows. Marta saw Master Arn the blacksmith, his heavy leather apron flapping in the wind, shouting furious protests that were swallowed by the roar of the waterfall. She saw old Hal the stonecutter, his hands raised in desperate supplication, begging the council to stay the fire.

"They are kindling the tripod beneath Hive One," Bram whispered, his hand going to his mason's chisel.

"The postern gate is twenty paces ahead," Marta said.

The massive iron-plated oak gate stood shut, but the small wicket door at its base—the postern escape hatch—stood unlatched, held in place only by a light iron hasp. They moved across the snow-covered scree with swift, silent strides. Their wet trousers had frozen into stiff boards of icy leather, crackling with every step, but the adrenaline surging through their veins drove away all feeling of exhaustion.

At the wicket door, Bram paused. He reached for the iron latch, then stopped.

High on the watchtower battlements above the gate, a lone figure stood silhouetted against the gray snow clouds. It was Kesta Holm.

He had broken his confinement. He had slipped past the guardroom bailiffs and climbed the outer stairs to the high roof. In his hands he held his brass-mounted ox horn, its bell pointed directly into the teeth of the gorge wind.

Kesta raised the horn to his lips.

A single, piercing note tore through the blizzard: the ancient battle call of the valley militia, sounding the alarm that the border was breached.

The sound shattered the council's assembly on the rampart walk.

Osric turned sharply, his staff raised in fury toward the watchtower roof. The bailiffs lowered their torches, looking up in terrified confusion, believing that a northern war party had scaled the cliffs under cover of the storm.

"Now!" Marta whispered.

They scrambled through the shattered icicles at the conduit mouth, stepping into the freezing mist of the lower gorge. The cold air hit their lungs like a blade of ice, but the sight of the smoking battlements twenty paces ahead drove away all weariness. Along the parapet walk, three stone benches stood empty, their straw skeps reduced to piles of steaming black soot. A crowd of fifty villagers stood gathered near the watchtower, their voices raised in angry protest against the council bailiffs who held the line with crossbows.

By midday, Kesta had returned from her scout run along the eastern ridge, her snowshoes kicking up diamond-bright rooster-tails of dry powder. She carried a bundle of spruce boughs and three fat grouse she had taken with blunt-headed bird arrows.

"Vance has placed two pickets at the old tollgate," she reported, sitting on a flat stone and unlacing her gaiters.

"They built a timber shelter and lighted a smudge fire. They aren't trying to climb; they are waiting for us to starve or freeze."

Bram laughed, a dry, booming bark that echoed off the rock face.

"They think winter is their ally! They forget that a stonecutter lives all his life with cold hands. In January, the quarry stone is harder than cold iron. We used to split rock with fire and ice while the guildmasters stayed huddled around their porcelain stoves."

He took one of the grouse and began plucking the feathers with quick, experienced tugs of his thumb. The grey down drifted across the snow like dandelion fluff.

"Save the wing feathers," Marta instructed him.

"The large primaries make the finest bee brushes in the world. A horsehair brush is too stiff; it ruffles the workers and makes them defensive. But a grouse quill is soft as down. You can sweep five hundred bees from a brood frame with a single stroke of a grouse wing and not a single bee will raise her sting."

Kesta watched Marta collect the long, mottled brown feathers, bundling them neatly with a strip of waxed twine.

"You find a use for every scrap of this mountain," the scout said with genuine respect.

"A beekeeper wastes nothing," Marta replied.

"Wax for candles and waterproofing; honey for food and antiseptic; propolis for medicine; pollen for winter strength; and feathers to keep peace with the hive. In nature, there is no garbage. Everything that dies feeds another life."

Master Corin reached into his leather pouch and pulled out a small horn bottle containing refined mutton tallow mixed with clove oil.

"For the frame runners," the old wheelwright explained, smearing a thin film along the rebate where the frame ears rested.

"A hive glued together with propolis is impossible to inspect without jarring the combs. A drop of sheep tallow on the rebate stops the workers from gumming the joint. You can lift a eight-pound honey frame with two fingers without shaking a single bee."

Marta watched the oil soak into the dry grain of the cedar.

"My father used walnut oil," she said with a faint smile.

"He claimed clove oil masked the queen's scent. But with mountain bees, the clove keeps parasitic mites from tracking the bees' respiratory spiracles. The mites navigate by scent; clove confuses their olfactory pits."

'The wicket door is unlatched,' Marta whispered, pointing to the small iron-plated hatch at the base of the Great Postern Gate. 'Kesta left it ready.' Bram threw his shoulder against the wicket door. The iron hasp snapped with a loud crack, and the heavy oak timber swung inward onto the stone walkway of St. Jude's Cleft.

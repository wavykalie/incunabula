# Chapter 17 — The Carved Benchmark

The granite waymarker stood at the junction where the ancient roadway branched toward the high caldera. The stone was gray, speckled with flakes of black mica, weathered by centuries of winter blizzards but standing plumb and true on its chiseled limestone footing.

Marta dropped her pack onto the dry roadbed and knelt before the marker.

The original inscription, carved in archaic capital letters fifty generations ago, had been worn smooth by the mountain gales: *VIA SEPTENTRIONALIS — PASSUS APERTUS.* The Northern Way — The Open Pass.

Beneath the ancient Latin letters, freshly chiseled into the mica granite, was a modern benchmark.

Marta ran her bare fingertips over the groove.

The incision was deep, three-quarters of an inch wide, chiseled with the sharp flat of a three-quarter-inch stonecutter's chisel. At the center of the benchmark was Bram's personal mark: the stylized bee wing crossed with a square rule. Beneath the mark were three lines of coordinates chiseled into the rock:
*2,100 paces to Screaming Throat.*
*1,300 paces to Thermal Caldera.*
*The hives are on the terrace. Follow the smoke.*

Beside the lettering, pressed into a natural hollow in the granite, was a circular seal of dark beeswax.

Marta leaned close to inspect the wax. It was dark heather wax from the valley, identical to the comb of Hive One. Embedded in the center of the wax seal was an object: a polished river agate, cut and ground into an oval cabochon. It was her father's touchstone—the stone he had used to test the purity of gold coin and silver brooches at the annual honey fair. When their father had died of rockfall at the rampart hives six years ago, Bram had taken the stone and worn it on a leather thong around his neck.

Bram had pressed his father's stone into the wax before climbing toward the caldera.

The wax was not brittle with ten months of winter frost. It was pliable, yielding slightly to the pressure of her thumbnail. A thin smear of fresh propolis had been rubbed around the perimeter of the seal to protect it from moisture.

The seal had been maintained within the last seven days.

A gasp caught in Marta's throat. She pressed her forehead against the cold granite of the waymarker, her tears breaking through her reserve and falling hot upon the carved bee wing.

"He is alive," she whispered to the mountain.

"He is alive."

She drew Bram's octagonal chisel from her coat pocket.

She laid the steel blade against the chiseled benchmark. The width of the blade matched the groove with absolute mechanical precision; the tiny flaw in the left corner of the cutting edge mirrored the microscopic ridge inside the carved rune. Bram had stood on this very stone, his heavy ball-peen hammer striking this chisel, sending granite chips flying into the gorge as he marked the road for her to follow.

From the high saddle above, a sudden gust of wind swept down the canyon. It carried a thick, unmistakable plume of woodsmoke—not the bitter peat smoke of the valley hearths, but the sweet, fragrant aroma of seasoned birch logs burning in a clean hearth. Marta took Bram's cold chisel and held it beside the carved bee wing on the waymarker. Her brother had chiseled his initials—B.V.—in the lower right corner, using the same three-pointed stop he had learned from their father. Below the initials was a tiny diagram etched into the granite: three parallel lines representing comb frames, with a sovereign star above the center bar.

The message was unmistakable. Bram had not only found the northern caldera; he had established an apiary. He had built nucleus boxes, captured wild mountain swarms, and reared fertile queens on the geothermal terraces.

She looked at the beeswax seal holding her father's touchstone.

The propolis coating was still sticky, smelling of fresh birch resin and mountain pine. Someone had stood beside this waymarker within the last three days. Bram was maintaining his markers, checking the road, waiting for the sister he had left behind to follow the pollen trail across the divide.

She touched the freshly chiseled groove of Bram's mason mark.

The granite stone dust in the bottom of the cut was still bright white, unwashed by rain or snowmelt. Her brother had carved this benchmark within the last seventy-two hours. He was alive, working the upper pass, maintaining his survey lines with the deliberate precision of a master builder who knew his sister was coming. Bram laid out three iron chisels on a flat slab of mica-schist, their cutting edges freshly honed against water-slick quartzite. He lifted a four-pound mallet with a hornbeam handle worn smooth by thirty years of mountain masonry.

"Granite splits along the rift," Bram said, tapping the flat face of the stone.

"If you strike against the grain, you shatter the bed and end up with rubble. Stone remembers the pressure that cooked it five leagues under the roots of the earth. Bees are no different. You force them into an square spruce box with zinc nails, and they gnaw the corners raw trying to round it into a belly."

Marta watched him score a straight guide groove across the slab. A powdery drift of grey stone-meal gathered beneath his thumbnail.

"The Elder thinks a boundary can be cut with an ink pen on vellum," she remarked.

"Vance never cut anything harder than an apple," Bram scoffed softly, his mallet striking with measured, rhythmic thuds—tink, tink, tonk. The ring of iron against granite sounded clear across the frozen scree.

"He believes rock stays where a surveyor places a wooden peg. But you see that fault line running through the eastern bluffs? That crack opened when my grandfather was a boy. The mountain creeps four inches every twenty winters. Water gets into the seam, freezes into wedges, and splits a ten-ton boulder clean as a wheel of cheese. The valley cannot stay shut because the mountain itself refuses to stand still."

He set his chisel into the groove, struck once with full shoulder weight, and the thick slab parted with a clean, muffled snap, revealing a dry interior crystalline surface glistening with flecks of hornblende. Beside the benchmark, embedded in the beeswax seal, her father's touchstone gleamed like a polished drop of river amber. She laid her own palm over the stone, feeling the smooth, cold mineral surface that her father had carried in his pocket for thirty seasons. The boundary was not a wall; it was a road that their family had marked across forty miles of mountain wilderness.

Marta stood up, her weariness vanishing like mist before the sun. She shouldered her travel pack, tightened the straps of her nucleus box, and stepped forward onto the open roadway toward the high saddle.

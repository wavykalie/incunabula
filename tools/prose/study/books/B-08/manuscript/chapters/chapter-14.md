# Chapter 14 — Whispers at the Mill

The grain mill of St. Jude's Cleft stood where the White Torrent tumbled from the high gorge into the valley floor. Its massive timber waterwheel groaned under the weight of icy spray, turning the granite millstones that ground the village rye into gray winter flour. Around the central grain hopper, a dozen men gathered out of the cold wind. The air smelled of damp meal, wet fleece, and hot axle grease. A copper kettle of spiced cider simmered over an iron brazier, filling the drafty shed with the scent of cloves and roasted apples.

Master Corin, the baker, sat on a meal sack, scraping flour dough from beneath his fingernails with an iron bodkin.

"They rang the muster horn twice at noon," he said, glancing toward the open mill door.

"Osric has put young Kesta under guard in the high watchtower. They say the postern gate was found open."

Old Master Garek spat into the millrace.

"The girl has gone into the mountain. Took half of Hive One in a willow basket, like an idiot girl going to a Sunday fair. She'll be frozen stiff before the moon clears the peaks."

"Bram went the same road," remarked an old stonecutter named Hal, resting his gnarled hands on a wooden grain shovel.

"Ten months ago. Everyone said Bram was mad, but Bram was the best mason this cleft ever saw. He dressed the arch stones for the bridge without a single chisel flaw."

"Bram was an exile who broke his oath," Garek muttered, ladling cider into a horn mug.

"He defied the council. Osric told him to stop carving those heathen runes on the boundary cairns, and Bram spat on the hall floor."

"They were not heathen runes," Hal retorted, his gray eyes narrowing.

"They were surveyor marks. Bram was tracking the old road—the highway that ran through the pass before the great quarantine. My grandfather told me that sixty winters ago, pack mules from the north valley came through that cleft every autumn loaded with smoked salmon, dried plums, and copper pans."

The men around the brazier went quiet. To speak of trade with the outside was dangerous talk in St. Jude's Cleft.

"That was before the slaughter, Hal," Corin whispered, looking nervously over his shoulder.

"We all know what happened when the gates were open. Twenty cottages burned. The elders closed the road to keep our blood clean."

"The elders closed the road to keep their hold on the grain bushels," Hal said in a harsh, dry whisper.

"Look at the flour in that hopper, Corin! It's dark as river silt, full of smut and rust fungus. The land inside this cleft has been cropped four times forty years without rest. The sheep have rot in their hooves. If we don't open that pass and bring in clean seed and fresh ram stock, the winter will kill more children than forty raiders ever did."

"Osric says the north is full of plague," Garek said defensively.

"Osric sees his wife's ghost in every snowflake," Hal replied.

"A man cannot rule a living valley from the ashes of his wedding bed. The girl had the right of it. Look at the honey she brought to the press yesterday—thick, fragrant, dark as walnut oil. That honey came from wild flowers blooming in the snow. If the bees can find life in that gorge, why should our children starve behind oak balks?"

The mill door creaked wide, letting in a gust of freezing mist.

The men turned. Master Arn, the village blacksmith, stepped through the doorway, shaking snow from his heavy leather apron. His face was grim, his thick forearms crossed over his chest.

"They're hauling pitch to the rampart," Arn announced, his deep voice cutting through the hiss of the waterwheel.

"Osric has ordered the sulfur cauldrons kindled at sundown. If the girl doesn't walk back through that gate by the fourth bell, Hive One burns. And Garek—you're to take the smoker to the other thirty-nine skeps tomorrow morning."

Garek smiled nervously, but the other craftsmen looked down at their boots.

"Burning the mother hive," Hal muttered, shaking his gray head.

"My father helped weave that first straw dome with old Varek sixty years ago. They daubed it with dung from the first spring heifers. If Osric burns that skep, he burns the heart out of this valley."

"Then go tell him so, Hal," Arn said quietly.

"He's sitting on the council bench with his staff in his fist and two dozen bailiffs at his back. Who among us has the stomach to tell the Chief Elder that his quarantine is a tomb?"

Master Arn the blacksmith picked up an iron tongs and lifted a glowing coal from the brazier, lighting his short clay pipe with a steady puff of blue smoke. 'My father remembered the Great Highway before the quarantine, Corin,' Arn said, leaning his heavy shoulders against the timber post. 'He told me the stone bridges through the gorge were three wagons wide, paved with dressed limestone that didn't heave in winter frost. Pack trains came through every week with copper kettles from the north and wool cloth from the southern sea.'

'And the raid?' Corin asked, clutching his cider mug with trembling hands. 'What of the forty raiders?'

'The raid was twenty horse thieves who followed a private trade track!' Arn spat, his deep voice rumbling over the clatter of the waterwheel. 'Everyone in the cleft over fifty winters knows the truth, but no one has the belly to speak it before Osric's staff! Osric traded council grain for northern furs, cheated the dealers on the weight, and brought their kin down upon our roofs to settle the score! And then he locked the gates and called it a holy covenant to keep his shame buried in the loom house ashes!'

The men around the mill hopper fell into a stunned, fearful silence. Even old Hal the stonecutter looked down at his shovel, his weathered hands tightening on the ash handle. 'You speak treason, blacksmith,' Garek whispered, his bulbous nose red with cider and fear. 'If Osric hears those words, he'll seize your smithy and take the bellows from your apprentices.'

'Let him try,' Arn growled, slamming his fist onto a grain sack with such force that a cloud of white rye dust exploded into the air. 'I have three sons in the militia and forty pounds of iron bar in my cellar. If the girl brings living bees back through that gate, I'll take a sledgehammer to the postern bolts myself!'

Master Arn the blacksmith laid his heavy iron calipers on the grain hopper, tapping the oak rim with a steady, rhythmic beat. 'My father took five pack horses through the Screaming Gorge fifty winters ago, Hal,' Arn said, looking around the circle of listening craftsmen. 'He brought back three hundredweight of copper plate from the northern caldera, and twenty casks of sweet heather honey that didn't sugar in the cold. The northern people were our cousins. They spoke our tongue; they sang our hymns. Osric built his wall to hide his own guilt, and we were cowards enough to let him do it.'

The younger mill hands listened with wide, solemn eyes.

They had grown up believing that beyond the postern gate lay only barren ice, plagued beasts, and the ghosts of dead raiders. To hear that a fertile caldera of warm thermal springs and blooming orchards lay three miles up the canyon was like hearing that their dead ancestors had built a second paradise in the snow. No one answered. The heavy granite millstone ground on with a dull, rhythmic rumble, crushing the dark mountain rye into dust as the shadows lengthened across the valley floor.

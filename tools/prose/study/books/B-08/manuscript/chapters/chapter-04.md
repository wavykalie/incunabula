# Chapter 4 — The Flight of the Scouts

The apiary hut was built from dry-stacked shale, wedged into a hollow of the cliff twenty paces below the rampart walk. Inside, the air smelled perpetually of dried lavender, sumac bobs, linseed oil, and old smoke. Strips of tallow-soaked linen hung from the ceiling rafters beside bundles of sweet woodruff and dried tansy. A small hearth of rounded creek stones warmed the single room, its embers fed with split birch and dry pine cones.

Marta sat on her three-legged ash stool by the single unglazed window.

A horn lantern burned on the low workbench, its yellow flame flickering behind thin plates of scraped cow horn. On the bench lay her inspection notebook, a sheaf of rag paper bound in coarse sheepskin, filled with columned tallies of brood frames, honey supers, and winter losses written in her father's neat, angular script.

Her father had kept the book for thirty seasons.

Every frost, every swarming week, every honey harvest was recorded in iron gall ink. On page forty-eight, dated nine autumns ago, her father had written:
*Hive One drew seven frames of worker comb in ten days. Queen vigorous. Her brood is solid as dressed ashlar.* At the bottom of the last page, the ink differed from the rest. Her brother Bram had written three lines ten months ago:
*The clover is souring. The soil in the lower basin has given all it has. If the queen cannot find heather, the blood of the hive will turn to water.*

Marta touched the dried ink with the tip of her forefinger.

The paper was rough, manufactured from linen rags boiled in lye. Bram had sharpened his quill with his stonecutter's knife, leaving the strokes thick and deliberate. Beside the notebook lay her small wax-carving knife and three wooden spools of copper wire used to reinforce comb frames against mountain winds. Through the window, she watched the silhouette of the watchtower against the violet sky of dusk. Below the rampart, the village of St. Jude's Cleft lay curled like a sleeping dog in the fold of the valley. Smoke rose straight up from forty stone chimneys, blue and fragrant with birch firewood. The villagers were locking their goat sheds, barring their heavy timber gates, retreating behind the perimeter walls that had protected them for four generations.

They believed the walls were salvation. They trusted that by sealing every crack in the valley's granite rim, they could keep out the winter, the plague, and the ghosts of old wars.

A faint scratching sound drew her attention to the window sill.

A single honeybee was crawling across the dry pine sill.

It was an Italian dark worker from Hive One, recognizable by the broad bronze bands across its third abdominal segment. The insect's wings were spread wide, trembling at a high frequency that generated a faint hum. Its front legs were busy grooming its long antennae, wiping away microscopic grains of dust.

Marta leaned closer, holding her breath so the moisture of her lungs would not chill the bee.

On the worker's hind legs, tucked into the concave pollen baskets surrounded by stiff hairs, sat two pellets of bright, golden-orange pollen.

Marta froze.

The pollen from the valley clover was dull brown, almost gray, like river silt. The pollen from the village heather was a pale, powdery purple that smelled of damp moss. This pollen was brilliant, saturated with color, glowing like crushed marigold petals under the horn lantern's light. Clinging to the bee's hairy thorax was a distinct, unmistakable perfume: wild mountain rhododendron, a flower that grew only in deep, sheltered alpine valleys where warm thermal water nourished the limestone soil.

"Where did you find it?" Marta whispered to the bee.

The insect finished grooming its antennae. It turned its body around on the sill, orienting itself toward the open window. It paused, lifting its abdomen into the evening draft, then launched itself into the twilight air.

Marta jumped up from her stool, thrusting her head out into the cold wind.

The worker bee climbed steeply into the darkening sky. It did not circle. It did not hesitate. It flew in a straight, unswerving bee-line north-northeast, passing twenty feet above the crenellations of the watchtower, cutting directly through the narrow cleft of the Screaming Gorge.

She grabbed her deerskin coat from the peg beside the door.

Her hands shook as she pulled on her boots. She required no elder's permission to understand what the bee was doing. Fifty thousand years of apian memory lived inside that tiny brain. When a valley turned barren, the bees remembered the mountain passes that human fear had forgotten. They had found flowers in the snow. Marta reached into her wooden sea-chest under the bed. Beneath her spare wool tunics and her mother's silver marriage brooch lay Bram's leather-wrapped tool roll. Her fingers found the cold iron buckle. She pulled the strap free and rolled back the oiled canvas.

Inside lay three stonemason's chisels, a heavy ball-peen hammer, and a brass compass fitted with a steel sighting needle.

Beside the tools rested a small glass vial, stoppered with dry pine pitch. In the bottom of the vial lay six dried rhododendron blossoms, their crimson petals brittle as parchment, preserved from a journey Bram had taken before he vanished into the dark.

The apiary hut was quiet save for the crackle of a dry pine cone in the hearth. On the stone shelf above her bed sat three glass jars containing wax comb samples from previous years. She took down the middle jar: comb drawn in her twelfth summer, when the south clover had bloomed waist-high and Hive One had cast three prime swarms in seven days. The wax was pale, aromatic as clover blossom, so thin she could read her father's script through the hexagonal cell walls. Now the comb on the parapet was black, tough as shoe leather, built from old wax chewed and reworked ten times over by starving bees.

On the corner of her workbench stood her wax-melting crucible—a small iron pot fitted with a copper spout and a turned birch handle. Beside it lay three cakes of clarified yellow beeswax, cast in wooden molds carved with the shape of an apple blossom. When melted at one hundred and forty-five degrees, this virgin wax was used to paint foundation strips onto the wooden top-bars of her observation hives, guiding the bees to build straight, parallel combs rather than wild, crisscrossed bridges. She dipped her finger into the cold wax, feeling the smooth, silken grain beneath her nail. Beeswax was the most expensive material in the mountain: it required the workers to consume eight pounds of honey for every single pound of wax secreted from their ventral abdominal glands. To burn forty hives of mature comb, as Osric threatened, was to destroy three thousand pounds of honey that had been converted into stone-hard wax by sixty generations of wings.

Marta uncorked the vial. The dry scent of high mountain summer drifted out into the cold stone room. She held the vial against her breast, listening to the north wind scream through the gorge like a living horn.

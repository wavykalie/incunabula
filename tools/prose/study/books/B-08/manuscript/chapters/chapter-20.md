# Chapter 20 — The First Blizzard

Outside the cavern entrance, the sky turned the color of an iron anvil. Within thirty minutes of Marta's arrival on the upper ledge, the full fury of the mountain blizzard struck the northern peaks. The wind shrieked against the limestone portal like a choir of furious horns. Snow fell not in flakes, but in blinding sheets of white that obliterated the Screaming Gorge, the valley trail, and the distant peaks in a dense, swirling fog. Within an hour, four feet of snow had drifted across the narrow ledge path she had just traversed, sealing the pass behind her with an impassable wall of ice and avalanche debris.

The cleft of St. Jude's was cut off.

Even if Elder Osric and forty bailiffs had mustered with iron shovels and ropes, no human soul could cross the Screaming Throat until the spring thaw melted the drifts five months from now. She was trapped on the high mountain shoulder, two thousand feet above her home, alone in the northern wilderness.

Marta walked deeper into the cavern, her boots crunching on dry limestone dust.

The temperature rose with every ten paces she advanced.

The cold that had threatened to freeze her marrow on the ledge faded, replaced by a gentle, soothing warmth that radiated from the rock floor. Deep beneath the cavern, subterranean channels of hot volcanic water flowed through the mountain's roots, heating the limestone like a Roman hypocaust.

In the ceiling above, sixty feet up in the darkness, hung dozens of stalactites.

And from those stalactites, suspended in magnificent golden tiers, hung the combs of the mountain sanctuary.

They were massive, ancient colonies that had nested in the dry cavern for centuries. Hundreds of thousands of dark mountain bees covered the wax sheets, their velvety bodies humming in a harmonious, resonant chord that filled the cavern with living music. They required no straw skeps. They needed no human daubing. Protected from wind, snow, and rain by the stone roof, warmed by the geothermal floor, they had built a subterranean kingdom of wax and honey that had flourished while human villages rose and burned. In the distance, at the far end of the cavern where the rock opened toward an inner mountain valley, a flickering yellow light caught her eye.

It was not the cold glow of starlight or the flash of lightning.

It was the steady, warm flame of a stone hearth fire. And beside the fire, framed by the dancing shadows of the cavern wall, stood the silhouette of a man in a sheepskin coat, holding an iron stonecutter's chisel in his hand.

Marta stepped forward into the golden firelight, her travel pack falling from her shoulders, her voice breaking the ancient silence of the cave.

The silence inside the cavern was profound, broken only by the gentle, musical dripping of warm condensation from the stalactites and the deep, steady hum of one hundred thousand mountain bees. The air smelled of sulfur minerals, warm limestone, and the intoxicating perfume of wild mountain heather in full bloom. Marta unbuckled her travel pack, letting it slide from her bruised shoulders onto the dry limestone dust. Her fingers were stiff with frost, her deerskin trousers caked in ice, but the warmth of the subterranean floor radiated through her boots like the heat of an oven hearth.

She looked up at the ceiling.

Hanging sixty feet above her in parallel tiers were the great wild combs of the mountain sanctuary—magnificent sheets of deep golden wax four feet wide, their cells gleaming with dark mountain honey that had never seen an iron frame or a sulfur flame. Hundreds of thousands of velvety bronze bees moved across the comb faces in a rhythmic, harmonious dance of life. At the far end of the gallery, framed by the dancing yellow flames of the hearth fire, stood her brother Bram, holding his stone mallet in his scarred hand.

Inside the great geothermal cavern, the warmth was an intoxicating embrace that thawed the marrow of her bones. The air smelled of sulfur steam, warm honey, and blooming rhododendron. High in the vaulted limestone ceiling, sixty feet above the stone floor, hung the magnificent golden tiers of the wild mountain combs, humming with the vibrant music of one hundred thousand dark bees. Inside the rock shelter, the small hearth sent up a thin coil of blue birch smoke that escaped through a narrow fissure overhead. Marta sat near the ember bed, scraping propolis from her fingernails with the flat edge of a bone bodkin. The resin smelled intensely of poplar resin, crushed spruce needles, and bitter mountain herbs.

"This is second-harvest propolis," she said, showing Kesta the reddish-brown ball of gum she had rolled between her palms.

"Notice how dark it is? Valley bees collect resin from alder buds and willow shoots. It is pale and sticky. But this high-altitude resin has antimicrobial oils ten times more potent. The bees varnish every square inch of the cleft wall before the queen lays a single egg. It seals out damp, suffocates mold spores, and keeps the hive sterile even in a cave surrounded by melting snow."

Kesta leaned against the dry-stone windbreak, adjusting the leather straps of her snowshoes with methodical pulls.

"The wardens use spruce pitch on rope bindings," the scout said quietly.

"It cracks when the frost bites hard. But this gum remains supple."

"Because the bees chew it with glandular saliva," Marta explained, kneading the ball until it softened under body heat.

"They mix it with digestive enzymes that prevent oxidation. In Oakhaven, the apothecaries would pay twenty silver bits for a jar of this salve to treat gangrene and frost ulcers. Vance kept it locked in the parish coffer, calling it consecrated wax. He told the villagers it was holy grease blessed by the Synod, when it was simply wild gum gathered by mountain insects from the bark of wind-twisted pines."

Kesta touched the dark pellet, her calloused finger picking up the sharp, medicinal fragrance. She shook her head once, her lips tightening with a silent, bitter recognition. At the far end of the cavern gallery, framed by the yellow flames of the hearth fire, stood Bram. He had dropped his stone mallet onto the sand; his arms were open, his gray eyes wide with astonished wonder as his sister stepped out of the blizzard into the golden light of the sanctuary.

"Bram."

# Chapter 21 — The Cave of Amber Combs

The heat inside the limestone cavern rose in gentle, pulsing waves from the stone floor. It was not the dry, scorching air of a forge, but a humid, mineral warmth that carried the scent of volcanic sulfur, wet slate, and wild mountain blossoms. Marta stood in the arched mouth of the cave, her travel pack resting against a limestone boulder. Her deerskin mittens hung from their cords at her wrists. The cold that had bitten through her boots on the Screaming Throat was already thawing from her toes, prickling her skin with needle-sharp warmth.

Above her, the vaulted ceiling of the cavern arched sixty feet into the gloom.

From the creamy limestone stalactites hung the combs.

They were massive, curved sheets of deep golden wax, some ten feet long and four feet broad, suspended in parallel ranks that followed the natural fissures of the rock. Thousands of worker bees covered the wax in dense, shimmering carpets. They were the dark mountain race she had glimpsed on the lower terraces—large, robust insects covered in thick bronze velvet, their thoracic hair dense as beaver fur. They did not cluster in the tight, shivering balls of the starving valley skeps. They moved across the comb faces with vigorous, deliberate steps, fanning their wings, polishing cell rims, and packing fresh pollen into hexagonal storage pits.

Marta approached the nearest hanging comb, holding her breath.

The honey stored in the upper margins was not the pale, thin syrup of the over-grazed cleft clover. It was dark as roasted molasses, gleaming with ruby highlights where the light of the distant hearth fire struck the wax cappings. The cappings themselves were pure white, constructed of virgin wax scales without the propolis stains that marked old valley hives. A worker bee detached itself from the comb and buzzed toward her face. It hovered three inches from her nose, inspecting her eyes with rapid, darting movements of its antennae. Marta remained motionless, her pulse steady, allowing the insect to taste the vapor of her skin. The bee settled on her collarbone, brushed its front legs over its eyes, and returned to the comb.

At the base of the cavern wall ran a natural channel of steaming water, two feet wide, bubbling out of a deep rock fissure and winding across the floor before vanishing into a subterranean sinkhole. Along the margins of the warm stream grew lush cushions of emerald moss, wild watercress, and miniature alpine ferns that had never felt a snowflake. Marta knelt by the warm stream. She dipped her fingers into the water; it was seventy-five degrees, tasting lightly of limestone calcium and iron salts.

Here was the secret engine of the mountain sanctuary.

The geothermal water heated the limestone floor, maintaining a steady subalpine microclimate of sixty-five degrees year-round inside the cavern. While blizzards buried the outer passes in ten feet of ice, the wild bees lived in perpetual spring, foraging the thermal basins of the northern valley and returning to their stone vault to rear brood through the darkest months of the year.

Behind her, the crunch of gravel warned of footsteps.

Marta turned. Standing twenty paces away, beside a massive limestone pillar carved with mason's marks, stood Bram.

He wore a long sheepskin coat tied with rawhide cords, his boots fashioned from smoked elk-hide and lined with mountain hare fur. His face was leaner than she remembered, his jaw darkened by a full brown beard, but his gray eyes beneath thick brows were unmistakably the eyes of her brother. His hands were scarred with the white lines of stonecutter's work, but his right thumb was blackened by frostbite at the knuckle. In his right hand he carried an ash-handled stone mallet. In his left rested a cedar nucleus box identical in design to the one she carried in her pack.

For five long heartbeats, neither of them moved. The cavern hummed with the song of one hundred thousand bees, a deep, steady chord of living vitality that resonated through the limestone walls.

Bram set the nucleus box down upon a flat boulder. His throat worked as he swallowed.

"Marta," he said. His voice was husky, cracked with ten months of solitary mountain wind.

She inspected the mineral walls of the cavern gallery.

The rock was not the cold, moisture-dripping slate of St. Jude's Cleft; it was a porous, warm calc-tufa formed by centuries of geothermal mineral deposition. The thermal vents maintained a continuous draft of dry, warm air that rose along the limestone fissures, bathing the hanging wild combs in a sixty-degree current that prevented honey fermentation and brood chill.

Her father had taught her to judge the thickness of natural wax: in wild colonies nesting in dry limestone caves, the comb sheets were twice as thick as those drawn in straw skeps, reinforced with propolis gathered from subalpine birch buds. She reached up and touched a lower comb margin. The wax was firm, resilient, smelling of wild thyme, spruce resin, and warm royal jelly. The worker bees clinging to her sleeve did not buzz with the sharp, nervous pitch of starving valley colonies; their hum was a deep, resonant vibration of complete health.

The trail ascended through a jagged defile known to the herdsmen as the Devil's Flue. The wind through the chimney roared with the acoustic weight of an iron foundry, whipping spindrift across the black limestone ledges. Marta kept her sheepskin mantle drawn tight beneath her chin, her knuckles tucked inside her woollen sleeves around the wooden transport box. Every hundred paces, she paused in the lee of a boulder to inspect the air holes. A fringe of frost formed around the copper mesh, born of the moisture in the bees' collective breath. With the tip of a dry spruce twig, she gently cleared the ice, ensuring the ventilation remained open.

"If the mesh freezes over," she told Bram as he caught up, panting under the weight of his tool pack, "their own carbon dioxide will suffocate them in less than an hour. A hive in transit is a delicate furnace. Too little air and they smother; too much wind and the cluster chills past recovery."

Bram glanced back down the gouged snow chute. Below them, the tree line had shrunk to a dark, bristling pelt of firs, while beyond the ridge, the familiar valley of Oakhaven lay buried beneath a low fleece of winter cloud.

"No horse could climb this scree," he observed, leaning heavily on his iron-tipped ash stave.

"Vance's constables wear boots with flat valley soles. If they try to pursue us up the Flue, half of them will break their ankles before they reach the treeline."

"They won't come up on foot," Marta said, watching the frozen vapor trail from her mouth dissipate into the gale.

"Vance uses the warning beacons. If he spots smoke from our cleft, he will summon the district bailiff from the garrison."

Bram spat into the snow, the dark fleck instantly freezing on the crust.

"Let him summon an army. An army cannot cut stone without fuel, and you cannot eat iron spears when the blizzard closes the gap." He hoisted his sack higher on his broad shoulder, his boots biting steadily into the crusted drift.

Along the eastern wall of the cave, Bram had constructed an apiary workshop: three sturdy benches of dressed pine, stacks of cedar top-bars, and wooden cages fitted with bronze wire screens. On the floor sat six earthenware jars sealed with pitch, filled with dark mountain honey and fermented bee bread stored for queen rearing. Marta took three running steps across the warm limestone floor and threw her arms around his neck, burying her face in the coarse wool of his sheepskin coat as the tears broke hot against his shoulder.

# Chapter 27 — The Decision to Return

The descent into the Roman aqueduct began at a sunken stone portal carved into the limestone bedrock beneath the caldera waterfall. The arch was Roman work from four centuries before the founding of St. Jude's Cleft: massive ashlar blocks of travertine limestone held together without mortar, fitted so tightly that a knife blade could not penetrate the joints. Bram carried a pine pitch torch in his left hand, its orange flame throwing long, dancing shadows across the vaulted stone ceiling. Over his right shoulder hung his surveyor's chain and his heavy tool roll; strapped securely across his chest was a secondary travel crate carrying six frames of capped honey comb from the caldera sanctuary.

Behind him walked Marta, her nucleus box secured inside her deerskin coat, her hands gripping a five-foot hazel walking staff shod with an iron spike. The conduit was six feet wide and seven feet high, designed to carry overflow water from the volcanic basin during spring snowmelts. The stone floor was covered in a slick coating of calcium deposit and fine river sand that crunched beneath their boots. Every hundred paces, an arched ventilation shaft opened to the sky above, letting in a draft of subzero air and a column of pale, powdery snow that sifted down like flour through a baker's sieve.

"Keep to the center rib, Marta," Bram called back, his voice echoing in the underground vault.

"The side gutters carry seepage from the upper scree. If the ice gives way under your heel, you will twist an ankle in the drainage slots."

Marta adjusted her footing, planting her iron-shod staff firmly with every stride.

The heat from the nucleus box radiated through her woolen tunic, warming her breastbone and filling her nostrils with the clean, spicy perfume of virgin mountain beeswax. Inside the cedar case, the young queen walked across the wire mesh, her royal retinue of mountain nurse bees stroking her antennae and sharing droplets of sugar syrup.

"How far does the conduit run beneath the gorge?" she asked.

"Two thousand paces," Bram answered, holding the torch high to inspect the vaulting.

"It bypasses the entire Screaming Throat. The Roman engineers cut it through the living rock to keep their water supply from freezing in winter blizzards. When we reach the lower end, we will emerge behind the limestone buttress forty paces from the North Postern Gate."

They walked in silence for half an hour, the steady crunch of their boots and the crackle of the pine torch the only sounds in the ancient stone tube. As they approached the midpoint of the tunnel, the floor began to slope downward at a steeper gradient. The air grew colder, and the faint, sulfurous odor of burning brimstone began to filter through the overhead air shafts.

Bram stopped suddenly. He lowered the torch, his eyes scanning the stone floor ahead.

The conduit was flooded.

A hundred yards ahead, an ancient ceiling collapse had partially blocked the drainage channel with limestone rubble. Behind the barrier, glacial water from the White Torrent had backed up into the tunnel, forming a black, subterranean pool fifty yards across. Thin sheets of dark ice floated on the surface, clinking against the stone walls with a hollow, metallic chime.

"The second siphon," Bram muttered, testing the water with his staff. The wood sank three feet before striking the silted floor.

"Can we wade it?" Marta asked, stepping to his shoulder.

"The current is sluggish, but the water is straight from the glacier," Bram replied, turning to look at her.

"It will reach your waist, Marta. The cold will take your breath in five seconds. If the nucleus box dips beneath the surface, the ventilation mesh will flood and the queen will drown in fifty heartbeats."

Marta did not hesitate. She unbuckled the leather straps of her coat, unfastening the deerskin toggles. She pulled the cedar nucleus box free and held it high above her head with both arms.

"I will carry the box on my neck," she said, her voice hard as chiseled flint.

"Keep the torch high, Bram. Don't let the flame touch the ceiling moss."

Bram looked at his sister—at her slender frame, her determined jaw, the absolute lack of hesitation in her dark gray eyes. A slow, proud smile broke through his beard.

"Father would have made you Master Apiarist three years ago," he said softly.

He stepped off the dry ledge into the black pool.

The icy water hit his thighs with the shock of liquid iron.

His breath sucked in through his teeth in a sharp, agonized hiss, his muscles knotting against the subzero cold. He took three long strides into the dark water, holding the blazing torch high above his head with his left hand, his right arm reaching back to steady Marta as she stepped into the flood.

The cold seized Marta's legs like an iron trap.

A bolt of pure pain shot from her ankles to her hips, so intense that her vision flashed white. She gasped, her throat closing against the freezing draft, but her arms remained locked straight above her head, holding the cedar nucleus box steady as an altar.

Step by agonizing step, they pushed through the subterranean pool.

The floating ice scraped against their deerskin trousers, tearing at the seams, while the black water swirled around Marta's waist. She kept her eyes fixed on the glowing yellow flame of Bram's torch, chanting the names of the forty border hives under her breath like a litany: Hive One, Hive Two, Hive Three, Hive Four... When her boots finally struck the rising stone ledge on the far side of the pool, her legs were numb wooden pillars that could barely support her weight. She scrambled onto the dry travertine blocks, dropping to her knees, her teeth chattering so violently she could not form words.

Bram hauled himself out of the water beside her. He threw his dry sheepskin coat around her trembling shoulders, rubbing her arms with frantic energy.

"You held it, Marta," he whispered, his own lips blue with cold.

"The box is dry. The queen is safe."

The underground conduit seemed to stretch forever into the black heart of the mountain. Every ten paces, Bram raised his pitch torch to inspect the Roman vaulting, checking for fresh stress cracks or shifting keystone blocks that might indicate an impending ceiling cave-in. The travertine stone was cool and dry, carved with ancient mason's marks: Roman numerals, running water symbols, and the imperial eagle crest of the legions that had paved the Great Highway four centuries ago. 'The Roman engineers understood water,' Bram explained as they walked, his voice echoing in the stone tube. 'They knew that glacial torrents freeze from the surface down. By channeling the stream through this subterranean flume twenty feet beneath the rock floor of the gorge, they kept the water moving at forty degrees even during the bitterest northern blizzards.'

Marta held the nucleus box firmly against her ribs, her hands tucked inside her deerskin coat to shield the bronze ventilation mesh from cavern drafts. Inside, the young queen walked across the combs, her attendants feeding her droplets of honey candy. 'They are calm, Bram,' she said softly. 'They know where we are going.' Dawn broke over the high peaks in an incandescent wash of apricot and cold rose. The shadows in the dolomite cirque were cobalt blue, long and sharp as spearpoints across the frozen drifts.

Marta stepped out of the shelter to read the weather signs. The smoke from Bram's morning hearth rose straight into the air like a plumb line—a sign of stable, high-pressure cold that would keep storms at bay for at least forty-eight hours. Beside the shelter, the stone skep sat silent, wrapped in layers of thick wool felting and dried straw mats tied down with hemp cord. Marta placed her ear against the stone side, holding her breath.

From within came the deep, contented respiration of the colony—a steady murmur at a pitch near two hundred and fifty hertz, indicating that the internal brood temperature remained stable at thirty-four degrees despite the fifteen-degree frost outside.

"They are holding," she said as Bram joined her, carrying a kettle of boiled spruce needle tea.

"You speak to them as if they were children," he said, handing her a steaming tin mug. The resinous steam warmed her nose and lips.

"They are an organism made of thousands of parts," she answered, sipping the bitter, vitamin-rich tea.

"A single bee has no more will than a single blood cell in your arm. If you separate her from the comb, she dies of cold and grief within two hours, even with a cup of honey beside her. But together, they remember things no single bee ever learned. They remember the geometry of hexagon packing that uses the absolute minimum amount of wax to store the maximum weight of liquid. They remember how to orient their flight by polarized sunlight through dense storm clouds. They have carried that memory through ice ages that flattened mountains."

Bram looked out over the vast expanse of jagged peaks stretching northward toward the horizon.

"And we forgot it in three generations of valley peace," he said quietly.

"We didn't forget," Marta said, watching a single worker emerge onto the landing board, circle twice to defecate in the snow, and dart back into the warm dark of the entrance before her wings could stiffen.

"The Elders just found it easier to rule people who believe survival is a gift from the church rather than a craft of the hands.

The morning light in the amphitheater was diamond-sharp. Marta knelt by Hive Three, running her fingers along the cedar bottom board to inspect the debris that had fallen through the winter screen onto the inspection slide.

"What do you read in the trash?" Bram asked, crouching beside her with a wooden scraper.

"Everything that happened in the dark for seven days," she answered, pointing with her bodkin to the thin streaks of brown dust and white wax cappings.

"Here—these pale, papery wax flakes are the cappings chewed off the honey cells. The bees are uncapping stores on the eastern face of the cluster, moving toward the rear frames. That means their winter feed is progressing in an orderly line from front to back, exactly as it should."

She shifted the lens to a cluster of darker granules near the left margin.

"And this is crystallized honey," she continued.

"Heather honey crystallizes into fine, buttery granules. It contains less glucose than clover honey, so it stays moist in the gut. But see these three dark pellets? Bee droppings. Two workers broke cluster during the midday warm spell yesterday and took cleansing flights. If a bee cannot void her bowel outside the hive, she develops nosema and dysentery within three weeks. The fact that they flew, voided, and made it back to the entrance tells us the colony's vitality is unbroken."

Bram whistled softly.

"A whole book written in crumbs on a board."

"A book of life and death," Marta said, carefully sliding the clean wooden tray back beneath the screen.

"And every line in it is written in wax."

'They are going to save their home, Marta,' he answered. 'And so are we.'

Marta looked down at the cedar box resting upon her lap. Through the bronze mesh, the warm, steady hum of the living bees vibrated against her chilled thumbs, as strong and resilient as the mountain itself.

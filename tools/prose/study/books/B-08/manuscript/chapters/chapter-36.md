# Chapter 36 — Osric's Breakdown

The Chief Elder's chamber in the rear of the Council Hall was cold and dark. The peat fire had burned down to gray ash in the stone hearth, leaving the room smelling of cold soot, old vellum, and the damp mold of unwashed wool.

Elder Osric Vance sat in his high oak chair before his council desk.

On the table lay his oak staff, the heavy iron keys to the village granaries, and a small lead box that had remained locked beneath his floorboards for forty winters.

The lid of the box was open.

Inside rested a single silver wrist ring, blackened by fire, its circular band twisted and pitted where the heat of burning timber had melted the clasp. Beside the silver ring lay four bronze trading tokens stamped with the running wolf crest of the northern clans.

Osric picked up the twisted silver ring with trembling fingers.

The metal was cold as ice against his skin.

For forty years, he had believed he preserved St. Jude's Cleft. By locking the gates, by barring the passes, and by keeping his people ignorant of the world outside, Vance thought he honored the woman who had burned in the loom house.

He looked at the bronze tokens.

He remembered the winter evening when he was four-and-twenty—the secret meeting in the river willow copse, the promise of twenty northern elk-skins in exchange for forty bushels of council seed barley, the refusal to pay when the skins were delivered, and the sudden, terrifying scream of the war horns as three hundred riders came down through the unguarded postern.

Yet the village was never saved. It was starved.

Forty years were spent constructing a prison out of cowardice, terrified that if a single messenger crossed the pass, the trading tokens would be remembered and his shame shouted from the rooftops of the cleft.

A knock sounded on the heavy oak door.

Osric did not raise his head.

"Enter."

The door swung inward with a low creak of iron hinges. Marta Varek stood on the threshold, a small horn lantern in her hand.

She wore her deerskin coat, her deerskin mittens tucked into her belt. Her face was calm, her dark gray eyes reflecting the yellow flame of the lantern as she stepped into the cold chamber.

In her right hand she carried a small earthenware jar, sealed with fresh beeswax.

She walked across the stone flags and set the jar upon the desk, beside the twisted silver wrist ring.

"This is the first honey from Hive One," Marta said softly.

"The bees gathered it from the high ridge before the freeze. It has the scent of wild thyme and rhododendron."

Osric looked down at the jar. He looked at the silver ring, then raised his bloodshot eyes to meet the girl's steady gaze.

"Why are you here, Marta?" his voice was barely a whisper, broken and hollow.

"The village is celebrating on the rampart. Your brother is hailed as a master of the valley. Have you come to watch the old man break?"

"I came to bring you medicine, Elder," Marta answered quietly.

She reached out and laid her hand on his scarred wrist, over the jagged white arrow scar that had marked him for forty years. Her skin was warm, carrying the faint, sweet perfume of virgin wax and living bees.

"My brother has told no one about the trading tokens," she said.

"The tokens will remain in this room. The village will remember you as the elder who kept the wall until the bees found a road home."

Osric stared at her.

His throat worked as he swallowed the bitter lump of forty years of grief. A sob broke from his chest—a harsh, ragged gasp of weeping that shook his lean frame until his forehead pressed against the cold oak desk, his tears falling upon his wife's blackened silver ring. The Chief Elder's chamber was silent as a crypt. Osric sat before his desk, staring at his dead wife's silver wrist ring. The blackened metal seemed to absorb the faint candlelight, reflecting nothing but the cold shadows of the stone room.

He remembered the morning after the raid forty years ago—the smoldering ruins of the seventeen cottages, the crying of orphaned children, and the terrible silence of the loom house. In the ashes he had stood, holding this twisted silver ring, knowing that his private greed had opened the path that brought the slaughter. In that moment of agonized guilt, he had sworn to build a wall so high that no outsider could ever look upon St.

Jude's Cleft again.

Duty was the name he gave it.

Love was the excuse he repeated. But sitting in the cold room, stripped of his authority, he saw the truth: his wall had been an altar to his own shame. Forty seasons of prosperity had been sacrificed, starved their flocks, and poisoned their soil, all to ensure that no messenger could ever arrive to speak the name of the northern clans. When Marta had set the jar of mountain honey upon his desk, her touch had carried no hatred. She had looked at him not as a tyrant, but as a broken old man who had forgotten the taste of sweet clover.

He took a small horn spoon and dipped it into the honey jar. He brought the dark, amber syrup to his lips. The taste was rich, spicy, carrying the heat of high mountain rhododendron and summer sun. It tasted of the meadows he had forbidden; it tasted of the life he had denied his people.

Marta prepared the smoldering mixtures in two copper smudge-pots. She layered glowing birch charcoal with dried pepper pods, pulverized sulfur, and crushed propolis scrapings.

When propolis burns, it produces an aromatic smoke that is pleasant in open air, but when combined with sulfur and capsaicin from dried peppers, it generates an acrid, heavy vapor that hugs the ground and causes violent, uncontrollable coughing and temporary blindness.

"Downwind chimney effect," Marta whispered to Kesta.

"The Flue forms a natural furnace flue. The draft draws straight down the cleft toward the lower gorge."

Bram set the two smudge-pots on the lip of the rock cleft and worked a pair of leather bellows with steady, powerful strokes. Dense, yellow-grey fumes billowed out, catching the downward air current and cascading down the rock funnel like an invisible avalanche.

Below, the cries of the climbers began almost immediately.

"What is that stench?" a voice shouted, muffled by wind.

"Gas! It's mine-damp!"

Violent coughing broke out, followed by the clatter of dropped iron axes on the rocks. The heavy fumes filled the narrow chimney, stinging the eyes and throats of the climbers until they were weeping and retching into their collars.

"Retreat! Cut the rope! Back to the ledge!" the bailiff's sergeant choked out in panic.

In the confusion and blinding smoke, the four men scrambled frantically downward, slipping and tumbling across the lower snow chutes in a disorderly rout. Within ten minutes, they had abandoned their ropes and crampons at the base of the gully and were retreating at a dead run toward the forest boundary below. A single tear rolled down his scarred cheek, splashing onto the cold oak desk beside the silver ring. Osric Vance closed his eyes and breathed the scent of mountain heather, letting forty years of terror melt into the quiet dark.

Marta did not speak. She stood beside him in the silence of the stone room, her hand resting upon his scarred arm until the weeping subsided into the steady, quiet breathing of peace.

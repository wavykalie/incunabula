# Chapter 33 — The Ritual of Introduction

Marta knelt upon the frozen slate before Hive One.

She ignored the shouting of the crowd, the arguments of the craftsmen, and the cold sleet that dusted her shoulders. Her entire world narrowed to thirty thousand dying insects and a two-inch wooden cage of cedar and wire mesh. She pumped her copper smoker gently, sending a cool draft of dried sage smoke into the wicker entrance of the skep. The sharp, aromatic terpenes calmed the agitated guard bees, masking the alarm odors that had begun to circulate through the colony.

With a swift, practiced motion of her hive tool, Marta cut the hemp lashing that bound the straw dome to its bench. She tilted the skep backward, exposing the dark, parallel sheets of wax comb.

The center brood nest was cold. A few hundred worker bees clustered over the dry cappings, their wings vibrating in a feeble, despairing rhythm.

Marta searched the lower comb margins with her jeweler's lens. On the third comb, surrounded by three listless attendants, she found the old queen.

The sovereign was motionless, her withered abdomen dragging on the wax, her antennae drooping toward the empty cells. She had given everything she possessed to the border wall: four seasons of fertile brood, ninety thousand worker bees, and twenty swarms that had anchored the defense of the cleft. But her biological clock had run out. Her spermatheca was dry.

"Thank you, mother," Marta whispered into the dark dome.

With gentle, steady fingers, Marta slipped her thumb and forefinger over the old queen's thorax. She lifted the matriarch from the comb, holding her for one brief second against her palm, before placing her inside an empty wooden matchbox lined with cotton fleece. She would not crush the queen; she would keep her in her apiary hut until her natural death.

The moment the old queen was removed, the chemical equilibrium of the colony collapsed.

Within two minutes, the worker bees detected the absence of queen mandibular pheromone. The pitch of the hive changed instantly—a high, mournful piping sound that vibrated through the straw like the sound of wind through dry reeds. The bees began running aimlessly across the combs, searching for their mother, their movements jerky with the panic of orphanhood.

Marta reached into her nucleus box and drew out the elderberry queen cage.

Inside, the young northern queen walked across the bronze wire, attended by her five mountain workers. Her abdomen was long, plump, banded with bronze down, releasing a potent stream of 9-oxodec-2-enoic acid and royal terpenes that drifted into the dark interior of the skep.

Marta checked the candy plug at the base of the cage.

It was three-quarters of an inch thick, made of powdered sugar kneaded with mountain honey until it formed a dense, firm paste. It would take the worker bees twenty-four to thirty-six hours to chew through the sugar plug from the outside, while the queen chewed from the inside.

That delay was critical.

If a strange queen was introduced directly onto the comb without a cage, the resident worker bees would perceive her as an alien invader. They would cluster tightly around her body in a suffocating ball of twenty or thirty workers, stinging her to death or overheating her thoracic muscles until she died of heat exhaustion.

The sugar candy plug enforced a period of chemical habituation.

As the worker bees slowly chewed away the sweet sugar, they would stroke the new queen's antennae through the wire mesh. They would taste her royal secretions; they would ingest her pheromones and pass them mouth-to-mouth to every nurse bee in the colony. By the time the sugar plug was eaten away and the exit was cleared, the entire hive would have adopted her scent as their own sovereign identity. Marta wedged the elderberry cage firmly between the two center brood frames of Hive One, ensuring the wire mesh faced downward toward the cluster so the workers could feed the queen through the screen.

She tilted the heavy straw skep back onto its granite sill. She took a handful of soft clay daubing mixed with wood ash and resealed the lower hazel rim against the cold mountain wind.

She laid her ear against the straw dome.

Marta touched the wooden matchbox where the old queen rested in her bed of cotton. She felt the faint, feeble scratch of the sovereign's tarsal claws against the cedar wood. The old queen was dying, but she would die with honor, warm by the hearth, not suffocated in a cloud of brimstone smoke.

A good beekeeper never disrespected the mother of forty generations.

She examined the three center combs of Hive One with her brass calipers. The wax was dark, seasoned with years of brood, but the hexagonal cells were clean. In the center of the middle frame, worker bees had already begun polishing the cells with propolis, preparing the nursery for the new sovereign. The instinct for life was unquenchable; the moment the workers smelled the potent mandibular pheromone of the young northern queen, their despair vanished, replaced by the tireless urgency of comb building. Beside her, Bram held the copper smoker, puffing gentle clouds of cool birch smoke whenever the guard bees grew restless. His hands were steady, his breath calm. For the first time in ten months, brother and sister worked together on the border wall, their movements harmonized by years of shared apprenticeship beneath their father's watchful eye.

'She will begin laying before dawn, Marta,' Bram said softly, watching the worker bees cluster around the elderberry cage. 'By midsummer, sixty thousand mountain bees will fly from this bench.' By the end of the first week of their mountain encampment, the stone apiary had taken solid shape. Bram and Corin had completed eight tiered stone niches, each recessed three feet into the granite cliff to provide maximum thermal insulation from the exterior elements.

Marta worked systematically through each hive, checking food consumption by weight. She did not open the hives to expose the bees to the cold air; instead, she used a simple spring-scale balance made from a notched hazel rod and a lead weight.

"By lifting the back of the hive one inch," she demonstrated to Corin, "I can estimate the remaining honey reserves within half a pound. A full winter hive weighs thirty-five pounds. If the weight drops below eighteen pounds before the middle of March, the colony is starving and requires emergency candy board feeding."

"And what do the wild colonies weigh?" Corin asked, gazing up at the inaccessible crevices in the upper dolomite cliffs.

"Wild colonies store forty to fifty pounds because their cavity is larger," she answered.

"They don't have human keepers taking their surplus in autumn. But they also suffer higher winter losses. If a wet rot gets into a wild hollow, the entire colony dies because there is no beekeeper to scrape the bottom board or clear dead bees from the entrance."

"A partnership," Bram remarked, trimming a cedar lath with his adze.

"A domestic symbiosis," Marta affirmed.

"We give them shelter, defense against predators, and emergency rations when the season fails. They give us their surplus wax and honey, and they pollinate every apple tree, bean patch, and clover meadow within two leagues. Without the bees, Oakhaven would have no orchard harvest, no clover for sheep hay, and no tallow for winter light."

Kesta looked down toward the distant grey smudge of the valley floor.

"Vance thinks he can run the valley with prayers and constables. When the apple trees fail to set fruit next May, the farmers will understand who actually fed them."

'They are flying already, Bram,' she answered, looking toward the open gap of the gorge where the first stars were beginning to pierce the twilight violet. 'They are flying home.'

The frantic, mournful piping of the orphaned workers had ceased.

In its place, rising through the coiled rye with steady, mathematical precision, came a new sound: the deep, harmonious hum of thirty thousand bees gathering around the royal cage, lapping sugar candy, and accepting the sovereign life of the mountain pass.

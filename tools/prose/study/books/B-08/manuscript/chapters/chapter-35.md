# Chapter 35 — The Awakening of the Hive

Night returned to the high rampart, but the darkness had lost its terror.

A fire of clean birch logs burned in the iron brazier outside the watchtower door, throwing a warm, golden circle of light across the slate flags. Beside the fire sat Kesta Holm, his armor polished, his brass-mounted ox horn resting across his knees as he shared a trencher of hot barley bread with Bram.

Marta sat alone upon the granite sill of Hive One, wrapped in her mother's wool shawl over her deerskin coat.

The cold was sharp, eight degrees below freezing, but her hands were bare. She rested her right palm against the lower hazel skirt of the skep, feeling the living rhythm of the colony inside.

The miracle was unfolding in the dark.

Over the past four hours, eight hundred worker bees had chewed their way through the sugar candy plug of the elderberry cage. At the third watch of the night, twenty minutes before midnight, the last grain of sugar had fallen away.

The young mountain queen had walked out of the cage onto the comb.

Marta pressed her ear directly to the straw dome.

The sound was magnificent. It was not the thin, desperate buzz of the starving cluster she had found three mornings ago. It was a deep, resonant, organ-like vibration—a steady forty-cycle hum that resonated through the straw and into the living rock of the rampart bench.

The worker bees were celebrating their sovereign.

Nurse bees were grooming her bronze velvet coat with their mouthparts, distributing her potent queen substance across every comb. Foragers who had huddled in cold stupor on the bottom board were raising their abdomens, fanning their wings to circulate the warmth throughout the sixty thousand hexagonal cells of the dome.

Inside the brood nest, the temperature had surged to ninety-four degrees.

The new queen was already inspecting the center frames.

Her antennae tapped the polished rims of the empty cells, measuring their depth and hexagonal symmetry. Satisfied with the nursery, she backed her long abdomen into the first cell, anchoring a tiny, translucent white egg to the wax base with a droplet of sovereign fluid.

The queen had resumed her lay.

The blood of the border apiary was renewed.

In twenty-one days, the first generation of hybrid worker bees would chew through their cappings—vigorous, cold-hardy insects with thick thoracic fleece and powerful wings capable of flying through the alpine fog to forage the blooming heather of the northern pass. The stars above the high rampart shone with the cold brilliance of polished flint. Marta sat wrapped in her mother's shawl, her back against the granite battlement, her eyes closed as she listened to the music of Hive One.

Every ten minutes, she pressed her palm to the straw dome.

The heat inside was rising steadily: eighty-eight degrees, ninety degrees, ninety-four degrees. The nurse bees were burning honey to generate the precise incubation temperature required for brood rearing. In the center of the cluster, worker bees were busily chewing away the sugar candy plug of the queen cage, their mouthparts softening the hardened paste and sharing the sweet honey crystals mouth-to-mouth. At midnight, she heard a faint, distinct click through the straw—the sound of the wire mesh gate falling open as the last grains of candy yielded.

A sudden, electric tremor ran through the thirty thousand bees.

The pitch of the hum shifted into a deep, vibrating roar of collective triumph. The queen had stepped onto the comb. She was walking across the wax, her antennae stroking the workers, her royal attendants forming a tight, devoted ring around her long, bronze-velvet body.

The following morning, Kesta brought news that caused Bram to lay down his chisel.

"A patrol of four men was seen at the foot of the Devil's Flue," she said, breath steaming as she crouched beside the hearth.

"They carried climbing ropes and iron crampons. One of them was wearing the livery of the district bailiff. They found Corin's tracks in the lower snowfield and are attempting the chimney."

Bram stood up, his massive shoulders squaring.

"Let them climb. The Flue is iced over; one loosened boulder from the top will sweep the whole gully clear."

"No," Marta said firmly, placing her hand on Bram's forearm.

"If we crush them with rocks, Vance has the blood justification he needs to bring regular soldiers from the county seat. He will claim we are bandits and murderers. We must defeat them with the terrain and the cold, not with weapons."

"How?" Bram challenged, eyes dark with tension.

"The bees," she said.

Bram stared at her.

"The bees are clustered! You cannot send bees into an arctic wind; they will freeze before they fly six feet!"

"We don't send bees," Marta replied with quiet certainty.

"We use what the bees produce. Bram, fetch the sulfur pots we used for smoking the wild combs. Corin, bring the dried red pepper stalks from my rucksack. Kesta, guide us to the narrow ledge overlooking the middle choke-point of the Flue."

Within twenty minutes, they were positioned eighty feet above the climbing patrol, concealed behind an overhang of ice and rock. Below them, four men in heavy wool cloaks and iron caps were roped together, struggling up a narrow icy chimney where every step required cutting footholds with hand-axes.

Kesta adjusted the tension on her recurve bow, checking the braided linen serving around the nocking point.

"The wind down in the chimney has settled into an eddy," she reported, looking down from the rock parapet.

"The updraft will catch anything we release and whip it straight into their faces. A climbing man on an ice face cannot take off his mittens to rub his eyes. If he lets go of the rope to wipe away tear-gas or smoke, he falls sixty feet into the talus."

"We give them every opportunity to turn back," Marta insisted, checking the smoldering embers in the copper smudge pot.

"We want them to report that the pass is impassable, not that they were butchered. Fear of the mountain keeps the valley quiet far longer than blood."

Marta opened her eyes.

The northern wind blew clean and sweet through the postern arch, carrying the scent of alpine rhododendron into the valley. She knew that in three weeks, the first hybrid worker bees would chew through their cappings, ready to carry the valley's flight into the mountain sky. Marta closed her eyes, letting the vibration of thirty thousand wings thrum through her palm, her cheek, and her heart. The long night of winter had begun, but inside the stone skep of St. Jude's Cleft, life had won its victory over the cold.

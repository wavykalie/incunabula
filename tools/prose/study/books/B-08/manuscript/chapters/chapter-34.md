# Chapter 34 — The Council's Terror

The wind off the Screaming Ridge began to slacken as the sunset painted the western crags in streaks of cold crimson and violet.

On the rampart walk, Elder Osric Vance stood alone beside the ruined sulfur cauldrons. The four council bailiffs who had accompanied him had moved twenty paces away, standing among the craftsmen and villagers who surrounded Bram and Marta.

"Bailiffs," Osric called out, his voice cracking with a desperate, pitiful authority.

"Arrest the girl. Confiscate the nucleus crate. By the decree of the five elders, the boundary remains under emergency quarantine."

Captain Torren did not draw his sword. He stepped forward, his leather armor creaking in the stillness, and laid his iron key ring upon the granite parapet wall.

"The emergency is ended, Elder," Torren said plainly.

"The boy has returned alive. The northern valley is not a plague ground. If you want the postern gate barred, you will have to throw the oak balks with your own hands."

"You are sworn to the Council of St. Jude's!" Osric roared, stepping toward him with his oak staff raised.

"You took the oath of the wall on your sixteenth winter!"

"I took an oath to protect the people of this cleft from enemies, Osric," the captain replied, meeting the elder's wild glare without flinching.

"I took no oath to starve our children behind a lie. Look at the wall! Hive One has accepted the mountain queen. The bees are humming. If we burn this apiary, we murder our own future."

The villagers murmured their approval. Master Arn the blacksmith stepped to Torren's side, followed by Master Corin the baker and twenty younger craftsmen who had grown to manhood behind the locked gates.

Osric looked around the circle of faces.

He looked for the deference he had commanded for thirty years. He looked for the fearful silence that had greeted his proclamations in the Council Hall. He found only the steady, unyielding judgment of a community that had awakened from a long, suffocating dream. His hand trembled as he lowered his staff. The gnarled oak ferrule struck the slate flags with a dull, hollow thud that sounded like the closing of a tomb.

"You are fools," Osric whispered, tears cutting pale tracks through the soot on his cheeks.

"All of you. You think the mountain will welcome you with open arms. You think the world outside will forgive our forty years of silence. When the snow thaws and the caravans come through that gap, you will find that freedom costs more than grain."

"We are willing to pay the price of freedom, Osric," Bram said quietly, stepping to his sister's side.

"It is the price of fear we refuse to pay any longer."

Elder Mallow and Elder Hobb, the two remaining senior councilors, stepped forward from the shadows of the watchtower arch. Their charcoal mantles were dusted with snow, their faces pale and drawn with the realization that their authority had collapsed. 'The warrant is vacated, Osric,' Elder Mallow said, his voice trembling with age and sorrow. 'We cannot burn the hives that hold our children's food. We cannot maintain a wall that divides brother from sister. The covenant of the quarantine is dead.'

Osric looked at his fellow elders—men who had sat beside him for thirty years, ratifying his decrees and nodding at his warnings. In their eyes he saw no betrayal, only the profound exhaustion of old men who had grown weary of living in a fortress.

'You surrender everything we built,' Osric whispered, his staff drooping toward the flags. 'You give the keys to the mountain.'

'The mountain gave us the bees, Osric,' Mallow replied gently. 'It is time we gave the mountain our trust.'

A brief squall blew across the pass on the eighth day, dumping four inches of dry, powdery graupel that hissed across the rocks like spilled millet. The cold snap that followed was severe, dropping the nighttime temperature to a level where iron tools stuck painfully to bare skin.

Inside the hives, the bees reacted with instinctual precision. Through the inspection port, Marta observed the cluster compressing itself into a hyper-dense ball.

"The outer mantle," she pointed out to Bram, who watched through the lens.

"The bees on the outside don't move. They pack their bodies together with their heads pointing toward the center, interlocking their legs and thoracic hairs to form a wind-tight insulating shell three layers deep. Only the bees inside the core consume honey and flex their muscles to generate heat. Every few hours, the cold bees on the outer shell rotate inward, and warm bees from the core take their place on the perimeter. No single bee is left to freeze on the outside."

"Sacrifice and rotation," Bram noted quietly.

"No one stays in the cold forever."

"If our council worked like that," Master Corin muttered from the hearth, "we wouldn't have seventy-year-old elders sitting by the fire while nineteen-year-old boys freeze on the border pickets."

The wind shrieked through the chimney of the gorge, but within the shelter, the temperature stayed well above the freezing mark, tempered by the thermal mass of the mountain granite and the steady hearth coals. Marta adjusted the vent louvers, feeling the warm, propolis-scented exhaust air escaping from the hives—a fragrant reminder that life flourished in the teeth of the mountain gale. Corin sat upon a three-legged stool he had fashioned from split birch limbs, using an iron rasp to shape the edge of a new bottom board.

"In the lowlands, they make bottom boards from soft white pine," he said between rasps.

"Pine rots in three winters from bottom moisture. But here, we use mountain larch heartwood. Larch has so much natural resin that water beads on it like grease on an oiled boot. A larch floor will last fifty years beneath a beehive without rotting through."

Marta examined the slope of the board.

"The slope must angle outward by one quarter of an inch," she reminded him.

"Any condensation that gathers on the inside of the stone walls must run out the front door, away from the winter cluster. Wet bees are dead bees; cold bees stay alive so long as they remain dry."

The bailiffs stepped back, opening a wide path along the rampart walk.

The villagers did not mock the old Chief Elder. They stood in solemn, respectful silence as Osric walked past them, his head bowed, his heavy oak staff dragging across the snow like an unhitched plow. Osric Vance did not answer. He turned on his heel, his charcoal mantle dragging across the soot-stained slate, and walked slowly down the rampart stairs toward the darkening village, his lonely footsteps echoing in the silence until the shadows swallowed him whole.

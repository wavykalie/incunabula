# Chapter 22 — The Fire in the Dark

The hearth fire burned in a circular ring of dressed limestone blocks near the rear wall of the cavern. It was fueled with split mountain birch and dried peat bricks that Bram had cut from the thermal bogs of the upper caldera. A low stone bench covered in cured deer hides provided a seat, sheltered from cavern drafts by a woven screen of hazel wicker. Marta sat beside the fire, an iron mug of steaming water between her hands. Bram had steeped crushed juniper berries and dried peppermint into the water, sweetening it with a thick spoonful of mountain heather honey that dissolved in dark amber swirls.

Across the hearth, Bram sat with his elbows on his knees, his scarred hands outstretched toward the flames. His fingers were stiff, the frostbitten joint of his thumb red and swollen, but he moved them with slow, disciplined care.

"I thought you were dead, Bram," she whispered. Her voice sounded fragile in the warm stone chamber.

"Kesta found your cloak ten months ago. Torn on the thorn bushes above the torrent. Stained with blood."

Bram gave a quiet, rueful shake of his head.

"I slipped on the shale crossing the Screaming Throat. A rockfall sheared away twenty paces of the ledge behind me. The cloak caught on an ironwood branch as I tumbled into the gully. I had to cut the neck cords with my chisel to keep from being strangled."

He touched the scarred skin along his collarbone.

"The blood was sheep's blood, Marta. I had dressed a mountain ewe that had broken its leg in the scree. I needed the tallow to grease my surveyor's chains. When I saw the cloak hanging over the abyss, I knew the garrison sentries would find it. I let it hang. I knew if Osric thought I was dead, he would stop sending search parties into the gorge."

"He didn't send search parties to rescue you," Marta said, her anger flaring through her relief.

"He sent them to confirm you were gone. And then he declared the postern gate permanently quarantined. He passed a decree forbidding anyone from speaking your name in the Council Hall."

Bram looked into the glowing birch embers.

"I know Osric. He has been dead since his twenty-fourth winter, Marta. The raiders took his wife, and in return he turned our entire valley into a stone fortress. He thinks if he locks the gate and starves the sheep, the world will forget we exist."

"The hives are dying, Bram," she said, leaning forward. She reached for her travel sack and pulled out the cedar nucleus box.

"Hive One stopped laying three days ago. The brood is cold. The old queen is a drone-layer. Osric brought the sulfur cauldrons to the parapet this morning. If I hadn't stolen the swarm through the postern gate, Hive One would be black cinder by now."

Bram reached out and laid his palm on the wooden lid of her nucleus box. He closed his eyes, feeling the faint, rapid vibration of the eight hundred worker bees inside.

"The diploid drone blight," Bram murmured, his brow furrowing.

"Our father warned the council twelve years ago. If you breed the same forty queens to their own brothers for fifty generations, the genetic alleles collapse. The eggs turn sterile. The nurse bees eat the dead larvae to survive. It isn't a curse from the northern mountains, Marta. It's the biology of inbreeding."

He stood up and walked to a low shelf chiseled into the cavern wall. On the shelf sat four wooden nucleus hives, each fitted with clean cedar frames and bronze wire screens.

"Look here," Bram said.

Marta joined him at the shelf. She peered through the bronze screen of the first box.

Inside, attended by three hundred worker bees, walked a magnificent young queen.

Her abdomen was long, pointed, and banded with dark bronze velvet that gleamed like burnished copper in the firelight. Her thorax was broad, covered in thick down, and her antennae moved with vigorous, confident strokes across the comb.

"She emerged six days ago," Bram said with quiet pride.

"Daughter of the great wild comb on the central arch. I mated her on the high caldera ridge with drones from three separate mountain colonies. Her spermatheca is full of wild, cold-hardy genetics. One frame of her brood will produce worker bees that can fly in forty-degree fog and resist the brood rot that is killing St. Jude's."

Marta touched the bronze mesh. The young queen walked directly toward her fingertip, vibrating her wings in a low, resonant note of sovereign vitality.

"We have to take her back," Marta said.

Bram reached into his pocket and pulled out a small piece of dark honeycomb. He broke it in two, handing the larger half to Marta. 'Taste this, little sister. This is autumn comb drawn from high subalpine rhododendron. It carries three times the mineral of the cleft clover. When you eat a spoon of this in the morning, your hands don't freeze on the smoker bellows.'

Marta chewed the thick, fragrant wax.

The honey was spicy, dark as roasted chestnuts, bursting on her tongue with notes of cloves, dried plums, and wild mint. It was the very essence of mountain summer, distilled by thousands of dark worker bees into pure liquid warmth. For ten months, she had eaten thin, sour clover honey that tasted of river mud; now the living vitality of the mountain pass surged through her blood. She looked at Bram's hands—the thick, scarred fingers that had carved the boundary benchmarks. 'You built the benchmarks so I could follow you,' she whispered. 'You knew I would come.'

The upper valley was an amphitheater of grey dolomitic crags, untouched by pastoral clearing. Giant blocks of detached rock lay strewn across the valley floor like the ruins of a cyclopean fortress, tumbled by ancient glaciers and covered in pale crustose lichens that smelled of dry flint. Marta scouted the sheltered side of a south-facing limestone amphitheater. The stone here absorbed the low winter sun, radiating a faint residual warmth even as the air temperature lingered below freezing.

"Look here," she pointed out to Bram and Kesta, kneeling before a dry overhang where wild juniper bushes clung to clefts in the rock.

"The wild bees chose this hollow three centuries ago. See the darker discoloration along the upper lip of the cave?"

Bram stepped closer, peering at the rock ceiling. High above their heads, ancient combs had blackened with age, hanging in fossilized sheets like pleated stone drapery.

"Those combs are old as the empire," he said in awe.

"Older," Marta murmured.

"The bees abandon a comb when the cell walls become thick with cocoons. Every generation of larvae spins a silk sheath inside its cell. After fifty cycles of brood, the cell diameter shrinks by a fraction of a millimeter. The bees stop using it for brood and turn it into honey storage, or abandon it entirely to start fresh comb three inches further down. This rock has harbored thousands of generations. They don't recognize our boundaries, our county lines, or Vance's quarantine markers. Their territory is defined only by nectar sources and southern exposure."

Kesta unslung her recurve bow, checking the bowstring against dampness before laying it carefully in a dry crevice. She surveyed the narrow neck of the gorge leading into the basin.

"One watchman with a sling or a bow could hold this defile against twenty men," she said, assessing the tactical geometry of the cliffs.

"The approach is a single file scramble over loose shale. If Vance's bailiffs come up here, they will be silhouettes against the snow, while we remain invisible in the shadow of the overhang."

Marta set down the transport skep upon a flat slab of mica.

Inside, the quiet, synchronized vibration of the workers continued—a steady, warm hum that defied the frozen wilderness. She took a vial of concentrated sugar syrup from her pocket, warmed it against her collarbone, and tipped three drops through the wire mesh. Immediately, dozens of slender orange tongues reached through the apertures, greedily lapping the sweet fuel to feed their shivering thoracic muscles. 'I knew you wouldn't let Hive One die, Marta,' Bram answered, his gray eyes reflecting the firelight. 'Our father gave his life to that wall. When the old queen stopped laying, I knew you would look at the flight vectors. I knew you would find the rhododendron pollen.'

Bram turned to her, his face shadowed by the flickering flames.

"The pass is sealed, Marta. The blizzard broke thirty minutes after you entered the cave. The Screaming Throat is drifted with six feet of snow. No one walks back until spring."

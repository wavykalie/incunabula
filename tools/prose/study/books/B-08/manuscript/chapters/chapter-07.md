# Chapter 7 — Bram's Carved Chisel

The loose pine floorboard beneath Marta's cot in the apiary hut lifted with a dull creak. Dust motes rose into the pale afternoon light as she reached into the dark space between the joists, her fingers brushing against cold stone and rough canvas. She pulled out Bram's secondary tool chest—a box of river alder bound with hand-forged hoop iron. The wood was stained with old linseed oil and powdered limestone. On the lid, deeply gouged with a gouge chisel, was the mason's mark her brother had used on every boundary marker he dressed: a stylized bee wing crossed with a square rule.

Marta opened the iron hasp.

Inside lay three cold chisels of varying widths, a heavy iron compass, a plumb-bob of cast lead, and a leather tally roll. She picked up the tally roll and untied the rawhide cord. The sheepskin parchment was covered in Bram's tight, deliberate script. It was not a mason's ledger of ashlar blocks and mortar bushels. It was a secret survey of the mountain boundary.

*First Marker, High Saddle:* 1,200 paces from Postern.

Granite sound. No fault lines.
*Second Marker, Screaming Throat:* 2,100 paces. Limestone shale. Sward moss blooming in rock fissures. Air temperature four degrees warmer than valley rim.
*Third Marker, Caldera Shoulder:* 3,400 paces. Thermal mist rising from lower scree. Wild thyme and alpine heather in flower beneath the cliffs. The bees of Hive One are foraging here.

Marta read the lines three times.

Her breath came fast and shallow. Bram had not wandered into the wild country in a fit of grief or rebellion. He had surveyed it link by link, measuring the distance with his surveyor's chain, recording the temperature of the air and the species of plants thriving in the sheltered gaps.

Beneath the parchment roll lay an iron chisel wrapped in an oiled rag.

She unwrapped the tool.

The steel was heavy, eight inches long, with an octagonal shank and an edge ground sharp as a razor. Near the butt of the tool, where the hammer struck, Bram had carved a series of tiny notches. Marta counted them: forty notches, arranged in four neat rows of ten.

The forty stone skeps.

Beside the notches was a smear of dried beeswax. Embedded in the yellow wax were five grains of orange rhododendron pollen, pressed into the metal like tiny golden jewels. Bram had marked the chisel with the wax of Hive One before he departed.

Footsteps crunched on the gravel outside the hut.

Marta quickly folded the sheepskin roll, tucked the chisel into the inner pocket of her deerskin coat, and slid the wooden chest back beneath the floorboards. She had barely dropped the pine board into place when the latch lifted and the heavy oak door swung inward.

Elder Osric Vance stood on the threshold. Behind him in the afternoon mist stood two council wardens, their iron pikes held across their chests.

Osric did not step into the room. He remained in the doorway, his gray eyes scanning the interior—the dried herbs hanging from the rafters, the smoking copper canister on the hearth, the cot with its rumpled wool blanket.

"Tomorrow is the third day, Marta Varek," Osric said. His voice was flat, carrying the cold authority of an executioner.

"The council has prepared the brimstone cauldrons. They sit in the gatehouse vault."

Marta stood by the workbench, her hand resting inside her coat pocket, her fingers clenched around the octagonal shank of Bram's chisel.

"The deadline was sundown tomorrow, Elder."

"The sentries report that sixty more bees crossed the north rim at noon," Osric said. His gaze flicked to her pocket, then back to her eyes.

"A diseased limb does not wait for sundown to poison the body. If the queen does not show capped worker brood by the morning bell, the wardens will carry the cauldrons to the parapet."

"You gave your word in the Council Hall," she said, her voice steady.

"My word is to the village of St. Jude's Cleft," Osric replied.

"Not to a stubborn girl harboring the memory of an exile. Keep your hut locked tonight. The garrison has orders to turn back anyone who approaches the rampart after the twilight horn."

He turned and walked away down the gravel path, his oak staff tapping against the stones until the mist swallowed his footsteps.

Beside the chisel lay Bram's brass dividers, their tips ground sharp as needle points. Marta picked them up, spreading the legs across the scale etched into the cedar box lid. Each division represented one furlong—forty rods of surveyor's measure. Her brother had calculated the mountain gradient with the precision of an architect planning a cathedral spire. He had noted every spring, every dry overhang, and every rock shelf capable of supporting a stone apiary bench.

She rubbed the dried beeswax between her thumb and forefinger.

The wax softened under her body heat, releasing the faint perfume of high-altitude summer. Bram had marked the steel so that anyone who found his kit would know what he was doing. He was not running from duty; he was scouting a frontier for his sister's hives. Beside the cold chisels lay Bram's stone-dressing hammer—a three-pound mallet forged from Swedish iron, its handle turned from seasoned mountain ash and wrapped in waxed hemp cord to cushion the shock of the blows. Her brother had notched the handle with fifty tiny marks, recording the key arches he had cut for the south bridge and the parish mill race.

Marta picked up the leather tally roll and held it close to the horn lantern. Bram had drawn a tiny sketch in the margin of the third entry: a natural limestone arch with an arrow pointing through the opening, labeled *Aqueduct flume — dry in autumn.* The note was written in his neat stonecutter's hand, using lampblack ink mixed with walnut oil to withstand mountain damp. She realized that Bram had not simply explored the pass; he had engineered an escape road. Every marker, every notch, and every compass heading was designed as an instruction manual for someone carrying a nucleus box on foot.

Marta pulled Bram's chisel from her pocket. The cold steel had warmed against her skin. She looked out the window toward the high north crags where the afternoon clouds were gathering, dark and heavy with the promise of snow.

# Chapter 25 — The Genetics of the Cold Queen

The working bench in Bram's cavern workshop was illuminated by two oil lamps filled with clarified mutton tallow. Between the lamps rested a three-frame observation case constructed of clear mountain mica plates set into grooved cedar rails.

Marta leaned over the mica pane, her father's brass calipers in her right hand.

Inside the case, surrounded by a royal retinue of forty dark mountain workers, walked the virgin queen Bram had reared on the high caldera terrace. She was an extraordinary specimen of apian biology. Her body was nearly an inch in length, her abdomen long, tapering, and densely clothed in fine bronze pubescence that provided insulation against freezing air currents. Her wings were clear, strong, extending two-thirds the length of her body, their veins dark and robust.

"Look at the wing venation, Bram," Marta said, adjusting her jeweler's lens.

"The cubital index is three-point-two."

Bram nodded, peering over her shoulder.

"The Italian bees of St. Jude's have a cubital index of barely two-point-one. That extra vein margin gives these mountain workers fifteen percent more lift in thin alpine air. They can fly against a twenty-knot wind without stalling their flight muscles."

Marta gently tapped the mica pane with the blunt end of her calipers.

The queen did not panic.

In an ordinary, inbred valley hive, a virgin queen was nervous, darting across the comb faces and hiding in empty drone cells when exposed to light. This mountain sovereign moved with majestic, unhurried grace. She paused, extending her forelegs to touch the worker bees that crowded around her, offering her antennae for mutual grooming.

"Her mother came from the high limestone arch," Bram explained, pointing toward the cavern ceiling.

"That colony has nested in this rock for at least two hundred years. They have survived sixty winters without human feeding, without straw skeps, and without disease."

"How do they resist the chalkbrood?" Marta asked, studying the worker bees that attended the queen.

"Hygienic behavior," Bram replied, his finger tracing a row of capped worker cells on the center frame.

"In our valley hives, when a larva dies of fungus or foulbrood, the nurse bees ignore it until the comb turns black and rotten. These mountain bees have an acute olfactory sense. The moment a pupa dies or becomes infected, the workers detect the chemical change within two hours. They uncap the cell, remove the dead larva, and carry it fifty yards out of the cave entrance into the snow."

He drew a small glass vial from his apron pocket. Inside the vial lay three worker bees preserved in clear elderflower spirit.

"I sent samples to Master Vorn's microscope," Bram said.

"The thoracic hair on these bees is nearly twice as dense as our valley stock. When an ordinary bee drops below fifty degrees, her flight muscles seize up; she falls into a cold chill coma and dies on the snow within ten minutes. These mountain bees can generate metabolic heat down to thirty-eight degrees. They can fly in late autumn heather bloom when our bees are frozen stiff on their landing boards."

Marta picked up her travel nucleus box. She eased the sliding lid back, checking the eight hundred worker bees from Hive One that had clustered inside. The valley workers were listless, their bodies small and pale compared to the northern race. But as the scent of the young mountain queen drifted through the ventilation mesh of the observation case, the valley workers began to stir.

Their antennae quivered in unison.

They turned their bodies toward the scent, raising their abdomens and rapidly fanning their wings in the unmistakable posture of Nasonov pheromone dispersal. They recognized the sovereign odor of a fertile, healthy matriarch. Their biological distress—the despair of an orphaned, failing colony—was instantly replaced by the instinct of adoption.

"They will accept her," Marta said, her eyes shining in the lamplight.

"We don't need a three-day introduction cage. The valley bees are starving for a queen who can lay."

"Then we cage her now," Bram said.

He took a small wooden queen cage—two inches long, fashioned from elderberry wood and fitted with a wire mesh front and a sugar-candy plug at the base. With steady, practiced fingers, he caught the young queen by her thorax, gently guiding her into the cage. He added five young mountain nurse bees to attend her during the journey, then sealed the entrance with a plug of hardened honey candy.

Marta slid the queen cage between the center frames of her nucleus travel box.

She examined the queen's compound eyes through her jeweler's lens.

The facets were clear, crystalline, without a trace of the cloudy cataract degeneration that blinded inbred valley queens. Her mandibles were sharp, powerful, designed to chew through royal cell cappings without assistance. Her scent was an intoxicating blend of citral, 9-oxodecenoic acid, and wild mountain terpene—a chemical signature so potent that the worker bees in Marta's nucleus box were already clawing at the bronze wire in their eagerness to groom her. The afternoon cold deepened until the moisture inside Marta's nostrils froze into brittle crystals with each inhalation. They worked together to haul granite slabs across the frozen scree, constructing a three-sided bee shelter against the rock wall.

Bram laid the stones without lime mortar, using dry-stone interlocking joints that allowed mountain winds to pass through the seams without catching the wall like a sail.

"A mortared wall is rigid," he explained, lifting a hundred-pound capstone with measured purchase.

"When the frost heaves the ground beneath it, mortar snaps like dried bone. But dry stone yields half an inch, settles into the movement, and locks tighter with every winter frost."

Marta observed the alignment of the shelter's facade. It faced south-southeast, angled to catch the very first rays of dawn across the ridge while blocking the vicious westerly downdrafts from the summit cirque.

"The morning sun is critical," she noted, adjusting the slope of the gravel terrace.

"If bees catch early light on their landing board, they break cluster an hour earlier in early spring to gather hazel pollen. Hazel is the first protein of the year. Without hazel catkins, the nurse bees cannot produce the royal jelly needed to feed the early brood cycle."

Kesta crouched by the corner of the wall, using a flat slate to carve notches into a cedar tally stick.

"We have enough salt pork for nine days," the scout stated plainly.

"Parched oats for twelve. Dried apples for six. If we remain up here past the full moon, we will need to hunt mountain hares across the scree."

"The hares are running in the juniper scrub," Bram said, wiping stone-dust from his forehead with the back of his rough wool mitten.

"I saw their runways in the snow near the spring. We can set wire snares tonight along the runnels."

Marta stood back, regarding the stone shelter. It was low, rugged, and completely invisible from the valley floor below—a fortress of rough-hewn granite that blended seamlessly into the talus slope, sheltering forty thousand fragile lives within its stone womb. 'She is ready for the wall,' Marta said, tightening the leather strap around the travel crate. 'Let us see if Osric's sulfur can extinguish a sovereign who carries the blood of two hundred winters.'

As the lid closed, the eight hundred valley bees surged forward, covering the elderberry cage in a dense, humming ball of warmth, eager to feed their new sovereign through the wire mesh.

# Chapter 23 — The Secret of the North Valley

The northern arch of the cavern opened not onto an icy precipice, but onto a broad, sheltered caldera that stretched three miles between towering limestone walls.

Marta stood beside Bram on the natural stone terrace at midday.

The blizzard that roared against the southern face of the mountain was invisible here; the high peaks of the Eagle's Beak deflected the gale, creating a calm, protected pocket of warm air that hovered over the valley floor.

Below them lay a landscape that defied every story told by the village elders.

Rising from the valley floor were dozens of natural steam vents and hot geothermal pools, their waters turquoise and emerald against the dark volcanic soil. The steam rose in lazy white columns, condensing into a mild, humid mist that drifted through groves of wild hazel, subalpine willow, and ancient apple orchards. Along the terraces of the caldera, where thermal springs bathed the limestone soil in constant warmth, wild rhododendron flourished in vast crimson blankets, blooming in late autumn beside purple mountain heather and golden spurge.

"They told us this was a barren plague ground," Marta said, her voice filled with awe.

"They told us what their fear demanded," Bram replied, leaning against his ash-handled walking staff.

"When the raiders came forty years ago, Osric convinced the survivors that the northern valley was full of demons and pestilence. He didn't want anyone to look over the ridge. He wanted the people locked inside St. Jude's, dependent on the council's grain distribution."

He pointed toward a cluster of low stone dwellings built near the central hot spring.

"There are twenty families living down there, Marta. Outcasts, exiles, younger sons who refused to starve on a fourth of an acre in the cleft. They are not raiders. They are weavers, shepherds, and stonemasons. They have sheep with fleece four inches deep, bred from northern stock that never saw liver rot."

They walked down the winding stone path toward the settlement. The air grew warmer with every hundred paces, smelling of sweet peat, baking rye bread, and the rich, honeyed fragrance of thousands of blooming heather bells. As they entered the village circle, several people looked up from their work. A woman in a dark wool tunic was spinning wool on a high drop-spindle, her hands moving with swift, effortless rhythm. Two boys were netting fat mountain trout from a wooden flume that carried water from a cold tributary into a stone holding basin.

"Bram!" called out an elderly man in a leather blacksmith's apron, wiping his soot-stained brow with his forearm.

"Did you find the stone seam on the upper ridge?"

"I found more than stone, Master Vorn," Bram answered, smiling as he drew Marta forward.

"I found my sister."

The old blacksmith stopped his work. He looked at Marta—at her deerskin coat, her apiary scraper tucked into her belt, the cedar nucleus box slung over her shoulder. His weathered face softened into a look of profound wonder.

"Old Varek's daughter," Vorn murmured, stepping forward to clasp her hand in his rough, calloused palm.

"I remember you when you were knee-high to a sheep, girl. You had honey on your cheeks and wax in your hair. Is your father still tending the forty skeps?"

"My father died six winters ago, Master Vorn," Marta said quietly.

"Rockfall at the high rampart."

The old man bowed his head in a brief, reverent silence.

"He was a good man. He brought three swarms through the pass fifty years ago to seed our first orchard hives. If it wasn't for his bees, this caldera wouldn't have an apple on its trees."

Other villagers gathered around the terrace. They brought wooden trenchers of warm barley bread, goat cheese seasoned with wild chives, and wooden bowls of dark, spiced cider pressed from the caldera orchards. Marta ate with ravenous hunger, realizing she had not tasted warm food since the morning of the council audience. The bread was light, sweet, ground from wheat grown on the warm volcanic terraces—a world apart from the bitter, smut-stained rye of St. Jude's Cleft.

"How many hives do you keep here?" Marta asked between mouthfuls.

"Sixty in the cave, and forty along the lower orchards," an elderly woman named Sarah answered, offering Marta a clay bowl of dried figs.

"They take care of themselves, child. The thermal steam keeps the frost off the combs, and the heather blooms until midwinter. The only thing we lack is iron. The mountain has limestone and timber, but the bog iron in our streams is thin."

"St. Jude's has bog iron," Marta said.

"Master Arn's smithy has three tons of forged bars stacked in the lower cellar. They have iron, but they have no flour that isn't sour, and their sheep are dying of fluke."

The villagers looked at one another.

"We have offered trade three times in twenty years," Master Vorn said, his voice hardening.

"Every autumn, we sent two messengers to the postern gate with pack mules loaded with smoked fish and dried fruit. Osric had the sentries loose crossbow bolts from the battlements before our men could speak a word. He threatened to burn our ambassadors as plague-carriers."

"He will burn the border hives at sundown tomorrow," Marta said, setting down her mug.

The circle fell dead silent.

"He prepared the brimstone cauldrons before I fled," she continued, her voice shaking with urgency.

"He believes the drifting bees will lead your people down the gorge to butcher the village. If I don't return with a fertile mountain queen to prove the north is sound, forty stone skeps will be burned to ashes."

Bram stood beside her, his hand resting on his stonecutter's chisel.

"The Screaming Throat is blocked by avalanche, Marta. You saw the snow. A goat couldn't cross the ledge today."

"Then we find a way that doesn't use the ledge," Marta said, standing to face him.

"You surveyed the mountain, Bram. You told me the old highway had three branches. Is there a lower conduit through the gorge?"

Bram looked at Master Vorn, then out toward the steaming caldera. His jaw tightened as the stonecutter's calculations moved behind his eyes.

"There is the old water tunnel," Bram admitted slowly.

"The Roman aqueduct that channeled the torrent before the rockfall of seventy winters ago. It runs beneath the limestone floor of the canyon. But it is half-choked with river gravel and black ice."

They walked past the communal bread ovens of the caldera settlement.

Two women were sliding loaves of wheat bread into the stone hearth with long ash peels, the aroma of toasted crust and sourdough filling the warm air. A herd of twenty mountain goats grazed peacefully on the terraced meadows above the houses, their long white fleece gleaming in the morning sun. 'There are no raiders here, Marta,' Bram said, resting his hand on her shoulder. 'There are only families who grew tired of watching their children cough in the damp shadow of St. Jude's cliffs. When Osric locked the gates forty years ago, fifty people refused to be walled in. They climbed the Screaming Throat, found this warm caldera, and built a sanctuary where the sun shines twelve months a year.'

Master Vorn led them into the blacksmith's shop, where a small forge was heated by coal gathered from an exposed seam in the upper canyon. 'Look at these plows, girl,' the old smith said, pointing to three wooden frames tipped with rusted iron teeth. 'We have wheat and honey to spare, but our iron is thin. If we had ten bars of Swedish iron from Master Arn's smithy, we could forge six two-horse plows and double the caldera grain harvest.' 'And St. Jude's has three tons of iron bar sitting in cellars while children eat smutty rye porridge,' Marta said, her voice shaking with indignation. 'The elders have starved two valleys to keep one old man's cowardice from coming to light.'

Bram's mallet rang out against the dolomite with rhythmic precision. He was shaping foundation blocks to support the hives, raising them two feet off the frozen gravel to keep the bottom boards clear of drifting snow and hunting voles.

"Voles are the winter curse of the apiary," Marta remarked as she swept snow from the building terrace with a bundle of dried heather twigs.

"A field mouse can squeeze through a gap no wider than my little finger. Once inside a dormant hive, the mouse nests in the lower combs, chewing through the wax and gorging on honey. The bees cannot fly to sting it; the cold outside the cluster paralyzes their wings within three seconds. They can only cling to their cluster and watch their winter stores vanish."

"Then we carve stone entrance guards," Bram said without breaking his stroke. He held up a narrow strip of schist into which he had notched eight neat rectangular teeth, each exactly four millimeters wide.

"Wide enough for a loaded foraging worker to slip through sideways, too narrow for a vole's skull. My grandfather carved guards like this for the old abbey apiaries in the high pass."

Marta took the stone strip, admiring the clean chisel marks.

"Four millimeters is perfect. Even a heavy queen cannot pass through an entrance guard of this gauge. In the spring, when supersedure begins, it keeps the old mother confined to the brood chamber while allowing the virgin swarm to emerge through the upper vents."

Kesta returned from the perimeter ridge, dragging two dead pine branches for firewood.

"No tracks within two leagues," she reported, dropping the resinous wood near the hearth.

"The southern pass is choked with avalanche debris. Vance's scouts won't risk the ridge until the crust hardens after the new moon. We have at least three days of clear weather before the next storm."

Marta nodded, her eyes lingering on the pale blue sky framed by the crags. High above, a solitary ravens circled on the thermals, a black speck riding the mountain draughts that carried the scent of distant cedar groves. Inside the stone bivouac, the rhythm of Bram's masonry was as steady as the ticking of an abbey water-clock. He had squared twelve granite blocks for the hive plinths, each dressed with fine claw-chisel striations across the upper face to prevent the wooden bottom boards from slipping.

"Notice the fall of the stone," Bram said, tapping the corner of the third plinth with his level.

"A stonemason never cuts a surface perfectly flat. If you leave it true to the horizon, melting snow sits in the micro-fissures, freezes at night, and pops the surface into gravel. You give it a fall of one-eighth inch toward the front. Water runs off before it can freeze; ice has no tooth to bite."

Marta watched him brush the stone-flour from the bed.

"The bees know that geometry," she said.

"When they build brood comb, they don't draw the cell walls perpendicular to the midrib. Every hexagon tilts upward at an angle of nine to fourteen degrees. If the cells were horizontal, the raw nectar would run out onto the floor before the bees could evaporate the moisture and cap it with wax. Nature never uses a flat line where liquid is concerned."

Bram paused, his mallet resting against his thigh. He looked at the tiny hexagonal cells of the comb fragment in her hand, then down at the massive granite plinths he had shaped with iron and muscle.

"We are doing the same work," he murmured.

"One with stone, one with wax."

"Both against the mountain winter," Marta agreed.

"And neither of us can afford a mistake."

Bram set his heavy iron crowbar under the corner of the fifth hive plinth, testing the balance of the stone bed against the natural slope of the terrace.

"The bedrock here is mica-schist," he explained, pointing his chisel toward the fractured seam in the cliff face.

"It looks solid, but water gets between the mica layers. When the spring thaw comes, the surface layers can slough off like wet cardboard if you don't anchor the foundation into the underlying granite core."

He tapped a four-inch iron drift pin into a drilled hole in the rock. The iron rang true, a sharp, singing note that reverberated against the dolomite walls of the amphitheater.

"That pin goes six inches into the heart-rock," Bram said, wiping his brow with his rough sleeve.

"A winter gale can blow eighty miles an hour through this gorge and it won't budge this plinth by the width of an ant's leg. Your bees will sleep as soundly as monks in a vaulted crypt."

Marta smiled faintly, checking the spirit level she had fashioned from a sealed glass vial of mineral oil and a bubble of air.

"Stability is peace for the hive," she said.

"In the valley, farm carts rattling past on the turnpike sent micro-vibrations through the apiary benches. Every heavy wagon made the bees murmur and consume extra honey to brace themselves. Up here on the solid mountain bedrock, there are no wagons, no bells, and no church wardens shouting curfews. Just the silence of the stone."

'Then we end it today,' Vorn said firmly, his heavy fist striking his anvil with a hollow boom. 'Take the mountain queen through the Roman tunnel, Marta. Prove to the council that the north is full of bread and bees. And if Osric tries to strike you with his staff, tell him that Master Vorn has sixty mountain men ready to march through the postern gate with iron pry-bars.'

"It leads to the postern gate," Marta said.

"That is all that matters."

# Chapter 16 — The North Postern Gate

Dawn revealed the true scale of the wilderness beyond the boundary wall. As the morning mist burned away, Marta stood upon a narrow shale ledge eight hundred feet above the White Torrent, looking back toward the south.

Two miles behind her, the great defensive rampart of St.

Jude's Cleft appeared as a thin, jagged scar of dark stone wedged into the mountain gap. The forty straw skeps were invisible at this distance, mere dots of gray along the battlements. Smoke rose from the village chimneys, but from this height, the valley seemed impossibly small—a narrow, claustrophobic crevice of sour bogs and over-grazed meadows hemmed in by suffocating cliffs.

She turned her face toward the north.

Here, the Screaming Gorge broadened into an ancient hanging canyon carved by forgotten glaciers. The rock was no longer dark granite; it had shifted to warm cream-colored limestone, terraced in natural steps that rose toward the high shoulder of the mountain. Marta adjusted her travel pack, testing the balance of the nucleus box against her hips. The trail before her was not a wild sheep path.

Beneath the crumbling shale and mountain moss, she saw the undeniable hand of human masonry. Squared blocks of dressed limestone formed the foundation of a broad mountain roadway, four paces wide, banked with stone retaining walls that had withstood centuries of snowmelt. The roadway had been built with deliberate engineering, its gradient steady, winding around the canyon contours to avoid avalanche chutes.

"The old highway," Marta whispered aloud.

Her grandfather had spoken of it in the winter evenings by the hearth: the Great Northern Road that had linked the alpine valleys before the wars of the quarantine. The village elders had taught three generations that the road was an invention of heretics and dreamers, that beyond the postern gate lay only barren scree and savage wilderness.

Yet here was the stone. Here were the drainage conduits chiseled into the rock face to channel runoff away from the roadbed.

Beside the road, sheltered beneath an overhanging limestone bluff, stood the remains of an ancient terraced orchard. Gnarled, stunted plum trees clung to the stone terraces, their bark covered in silver lichen, but their branches were heavy with small, hard purple fruit that had withered in the early frost. Below the trees, thriving in the shelter of the retaining walls, grew thick beds of wild mountain thyme and alpine rhododendron, their evergreen leaves glossy in the morning light.

A buzzing sound drew her attention to a south-facing limestone outcrop.

Marta stopped, holding her breath.

Hanging beneath a natural arch of dry rock, five feet above the roadbed, was an enormous wild bee colony. The combs were built directly against the living stone, without straw skep or wooden box—six magnificent sheets of golden wax, four feet long and two feet wide, curved like the sails of an ancient ship to shed the mountain wind.

The bees covering the comb were unlike any Marta had seen in the cleft.

They were larger than the valley bees, their bodies covered in a thick, velvety fleece of dark brown down that extended over their heads and thorax like miniature fur cloaks. Their flight was swift and powerful, humming with a deep, resonant pitch that carried effortlessly through the cold canyon air. While the valley bees were paralyzed by temperatures below fifty degrees, these mountain workers were actively flying in the forty-degree morning draft, returning to their comb with heavy loads of pollen and nectar from the hanging terraces.

Marta approached the wild comb with slow, rhythmic steps, keeping her arms relaxed at her sides. She held out her bare palm.

A foraging worker landed on her skin.

The bee was heavy, warm, its compound eyes dark and alert. It did not raise its abdomen to sting. It walked across her palm, extending its proboscis to taste a drop of honey sweat on her knuckle, then stroked its antennae in a calm, deliberate gesture of greeting.

"You don't fear the cold," Marta whispered, her heart pounding with wonder.

This was the biological prize Bram had sought: a cold-hardy mountain race of honeybees adapted to subalpine survival, possessing thoracic insulation that allowed them to forage in frost, and natural resistance to the brood blight that was suffocating the inbred colonies of St. Jude's Cleft. If she could graft larvae from this colony into Hive One, or introduce a fertile queen from these mountain terraces, the border apiary would be reborn. The valley would have bees that could thrive on alpine bloom, breaking the forty-year cycle of starvation.

She looked up the roadway.

The paved road continued to climb, winding toward the great saddle behind the Eagle's Beak. Embedded in the roadbed fifty yards ahead stood a granite waymarker, its squared face rising three feet above the limestone cobbles. The Roman roadway wound through the limestone cliffs with the deliberate geometry of imperial surveying. Every fifty paces, the ancient masons had chiseled drainage conduits through the solid rock, leading runoff water into arched cisterns that once supplied mountain caravans with fresh glacial melt. The pavement stones were fitted so tightly that four centuries of subzero frost had failed to heave the roadbed.

Marta stopped beside an ancient stone fountain carved into the limestone wall.

The water had long since ceased to flow, but in the basin, protected from mountain wind by an overhanging cornice, grew a dense carpet of wild alpine thyme and mountain gentian. The deep blue blossoms were open to the morning sun, their petals glistening with dew that had not frozen in the sheltered microclimate.

She leaned over the basin.

A dozen worker bees from the wild colony on the cliff arch were actively foraging the thyme flowers, their long tongues dipping into the tiny blue corollas to drink the rich, concentrated nectar. Their hind legs were packed with bright orange pollen pellets, their movements brisk and confident in forty-degree air that would have paralyzed a valley bee in sixty seconds.

'The thoracic hair,' she murmured to herself, recalling her father's lessons.

In an ordinary bee, the thoracic insulation was thin, composed of simple unbranched setae that offered little barrier against freezing drafts. In these mountain bees, the setae were plumose—branched like tiny feathers that trapped a layer of dead air against the chitin, retaining metabolic heat generated by the flight muscles. She walked along the terraced stone orchard, admiring the ancient stone retaining walls that banked the mountain slope. The soil behind the walls was rich, dark, composed of decomposed limestone and ancient volcanic ash that held the warmth of the subterranean thermal veins. The stunted plum trees and wild crabapples had survived for generations without human pruning, their roots anchored deep in the rock fissures, their blossoms feeding dozens of wild colonies that nested along the southern cliffs.

Here was the lost heritage of St.

Jude's Cleft. Before the panic of the quarantine, their ancestors had tended these mountain terraces every summer, grazing their flocks in the high alpine basins and trading honey and wool with the northern valleys. Osric's decree had not protected that heritage; it had severed the valley from the very roots that gave it life. She unslung her pack and set the cedar nucleus box upon a flat retaining stone. She opened the feeding port and laid a fresh piece of dried wild thyme against the bronze mesh. The eight hundred worker bees inside surged toward the scent, their antennae quivering with frantic excitement as they recognized the wild mountain perfume of their ancestors.

The ancient Roman roadway was banked with massive dry-laid retaining walls of dressed travertine, four cubits high, designed to hold back the limestone scree of the upper mountain shoulder. Every twenty paces, an arched niche was chiseled into the rock wall, containing the carved stone base of what had once been a waymarker or a travelers' shrine. Marta stopped beside an ancient stone trough fed by a trickle of warm geothermal water. In the steaming basin, surrounded by snowdrifts, grew beds of wild watercress and mountain mint, their green leaves vivid against the gray stone. A dozen dark worker bees were drinking at the water's edge, their plumose thoracic hair keeping them warm in the freezing mountain air as they gathered moisture to dilute the dense winter honey in their cliff combs.

Marta hastened her pace, her boots striking the ancient stones with a crisp, hollow rhythm that echoed through the empty mountain terraces.

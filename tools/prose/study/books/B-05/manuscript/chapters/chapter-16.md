Tess lay on her back in the dark of her bedroom, listening to the dry ticking of the wall clock and the steady, quiet breathing of the dog at the foot of her bed.

It was 2:40 AM.

She had been awake for two hours. Her mind was not running through medication schedules or thoracic radiograph measurements; it was replaying the look in Clara Evans's gray eyes on the reservoir trail.

*The things that were just comfortable stopped being enough.*

Tess rolled onto her side, pulling the heavy wool blanket up to her chin. The room was freezing; she kept the thermostat set to fifty-eight at night to keep Barnaby from overheating.

When Nate had told her in June that he had started seeing Clara—the quiet botanical illustrator who had rented the old carriage house behind the library—Tess had felt a sharp, bitter flare of abandonment. She had spent four years watching her marriage starve on a diet of polite avoidance, convinced that she was too clinical, too guarded, too sharp-edged to ever be truly loved by an ordinary, decent man.

She had expected Clara to be soft, easy, accommodating—the kind of woman who would soothe Nate's bruised ego and laugh at his woodshop jokes without asking hard questions.

She had hated Clara for that imagined softness before they had even spoken three sentences.

And now, lying awake in the mountain dark, Tess realized that every assumption she had built had been a lie designed to protect her own pride.

Clara was not soft. Clara was resilient, observant, forged in the fire of her own family grief. Clara saw through pretense with the effortless precision of a microscope lens. Clara understood the terror of watching a living body fail, and she met that terror not with panic, but with roasted sweet potatoes, neat digital spreadsheets, and warm herbal tea.

Tess pressed her face into the cold cotton of her pillow.

What terrified her now was not resentment. Resentment was familiar; resentment was a heavy wool coat you could wrap around yourself to keep out the winter chill.

What terrified her was the warmth that flared in her throat every time Clara's car turned into the clinic drive. What terrified her was the way her own pulse picked up when Clara touched her hand across an exam table.

It was wrong. It was Nate's partner. It was the woman living in the house Tess had walked away from six months ago.

At the foot of the bed, Barnaby gave a soft, shuddering sigh in his sleep, his paws twitching against the fleece bolster.

Tess reached down through the dark, found the coarse, warm fur of his hip, and held on until the gray light of dawn touched the frost on the glass.

Tess lay on her side in the dark of her bedroom, watching the red digital numerals of the alarm clock tick from 3:15 to 3:16 AM.

At the foot of the bed, Barnaby's breathing was smooth, rhythmic, fifteen breaths a minute.

She let her hand rest on the wool blanket, feeling the quiet pulse of her own blood in her fingertips. For months, she had told herself that her life was complete: her clinic shifts, her diagnostic cases, her clean, quiet apartment with its orderly bookshelves and copper cookware. She had convinced herself that she needed nothing more than professional competence and self-reliance.

And now, lying in the dark, she knew that the fortress she had built was crumbling from the inside.

Every thought circled back to Clara Evans: the sound of Clara's voice in the morning, the smell of mint and chamomile on Clara's wool sweater, the way Clara's eyes softened when she held Barnaby's paws on the clinic floor.

It was terrifying. It was an exposure she had spent seven years avoiding.

Barnaby gave a soft, sleepy whimper in his dreams, his paws twitching against the fleece.

Tess leaned down from the edge of the bed, resting her fingers against his warm fur, and let the quiet truth settle into her heart until the morning light touched the Continental Divide.

At two in the morning, Tess got out of bed and walked quietly down the narrow hall to the front room.

The living room smelled of dried pine needles and the sweet beeswax polish on the floorboards. Barnaby was stretched out on his side across the braided wool rug, his paws twitching as he chased dream rabbits through summer meadows.

Tess stood by the bookshelf, looking at the small framed photograph on the top shelf. It showed Barnaby as an eight-week-old puppy, sitting inside a hollow spruce stump on the Continental Divide, his golden ears ridiculously oversized for his round head. Nate's work boots were visible in the corner of the frame, caked in sawdust and mountain dirt.

She had kept the photograph after the divorce because it belonged to Barnaby, not to the marriage.

Now, looking at the puppy's dark, hopeful eyes, Tess felt the tight knot of anger that had lived in her throat for two years dissolve into nothing.

She didn't hate Nate anymore. She didn't resent the years they had spent trying to force two mismatched lives into the same pine frame.

And she didn't feel a shred of bitterness toward Clara.

Instead, when she closed her eyes, she remembered the warmth of Clara's fingers through the winter mittens by the frozen reservoir, the steady gray light in Clara's eyes, and the quiet courage in the woman's voice when she spoke of starting over.

Barnaby let out a long, peaceful sigh, his ribs settling against the rug with rhythmic precision: twenty breaths a minute.

Tess pulled her wool cardigan tight around her chest, walked back into her bedroom, and fell into a deep, dreamless sleep.

Tess woke at four in the morning to the sound of snow sliding off the metal roof of the clinic downstairs.

The bedroom was cold, the air tasting of frost and dry cedar. On the nightstand, her clock radio glowed green: 4:12 AM.

She pulled the quilt up to her chin, watching her breath form a faint, white vapor in the darkness. Down the hall, Barnaby shifted on his orthopedic bed, his claws clicking once against the floorboards as he turned to face the radiator.

She thought of the way Clara looked at Barnaby's chest during the emergency—not with panic, but with steady, attentive presence.

Most people who brought dying pets into the clinic looked away from the medical instruments. They looked at the ceiling, or at their phones, or out the frosted window toward the highway. Clara had looked right at the barrel of the syringe, watching the level drop as Tess pushed the furosemide into the port.

Tess closed her eyes, listening to the quiet rhythm of the dog's breath until the morning alarm sounded.

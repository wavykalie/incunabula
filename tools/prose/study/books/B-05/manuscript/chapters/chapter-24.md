The industrial woodshop at Silvercreek High School smelled of sweet Douglas fir, scorching pine resin from the table saw blades, and the mineral sharpness of steel tool oil.

Tess pushed open the heavy metal door at three o'clock on a Friday afternoon, holding Barnaby's leash. School had let out twenty minutes earlier; the hallways were empty, and the only sound was the high, whining buzz of an orbital sander at the back of the shop.

Barnaby walked through the double doors with his tail wagging, sniffing the mountain of blond curly shavings piled beside the planer. He loved the woodshop. He had spent his puppyhood sleeping in a cardboard crate under Nate's workbench while Nate built oak book cabinets for the county library.

Nate was standing at the assembly bench, wearing safety goggles, leather work gloves, and a denim apron covered in yellow sawdust. He was sanding the edge of a massive, bevel-cut cedar timber.

When he heard the click of Barnaby's claws on the concrete floor, Nate flipped the sander switch off. The machine spun down into silence.

Nate pulled his goggles down to hang around his neck, wiping a streak of sweat from his forehead with the back of his sleeve.

"Hey, buddy," Nate said. His voice was thick, warm, dropping into the familiar mountain cadence he had used since the day they brought the retriever home from Leadville in 2015.

He knelt down on the sawdust-covered floor, and Barnaby immediately buried his snout under Nate's arm, his tail thumping hard against the base of a cast-iron drill press. Nate wrapped his thick arms around the dog's chest, burying his face in the golden fur, holding him with a tight, fierce stillness that made Tess look away toward the tool racks.

After thirty seconds, Nate patted Barnaby's flank, stood up, and brushed the shavings from his knees.

He looked at Tess.

He looked tired. The lines around his blue eyes were deeper than they had been in October, his brown hair dusted with gray sawdust along the temples. But his eyes were clear, direct, devoid of the defensive, sulking anger he had carried in the fall.

"He's walking better on that back leg," Nate said, nodding toward Barnaby.

"The enalapril helped his circulation," Tess said. "And the joint supplements. He had a good week."

Nate nodded slowly, picking up a block of 220-grit sandpaper and turning it over in his rough hands.

"Clara told me," Nate said simply.

The words hung in the vast, echoing shop between the bandsaw and the lumber racks.

Tess felt her stomach drop, a cold wave of adrenaline washing through her ribs. She kept her chin up, refusing to look down at the floor.

"Nate," Tess began.

Nate held up one hand, palm forward. He wasn't angry. His face was filled with a quiet, melancholy grace that caught her completely off guard.

"You don't need to explain, Tess," Nate said, his voice level and steady. "I'm not blind. I watched the two of you in the kitchen after that dog started coughing in November. I watched the way Clara checked his chest, and the way you looked at her when she handed you his chart."

He set the sandpaper block on the bench, leaning his hip against the cedar timber.

"For four years, Tess, I tried to give you what I had," Nate said softly. "I built tables. I stacked wood. I kept the roof patched. But you were always looking for something deeper, something that didn't hide behind the chores. And I didn't know how to speak that language."

He looked down at Barnaby, who was happily chewing on a small scrap of clean pine molding.

"Clara speaks that language," Nate said. "She understood what you were carrying the second she walked into the clinic. If anyone is going to be there with you when this dog's time comes, I'm glad it's her."

Tess felt the sudden, hot sting of tears spill over her eyelashes, running down her cold cheeks before she could wipe them away.

"I'm sorry, Nate," she whispered. "I never wanted to hurt you."

"I know that, Tess," Nate said, stepping forward and clapping one rough, sawdust-stained hand on her shoulder in a brief, solid clasp. "We're going to be okay. All three of us. Now take that dog over to the bench; I built him something."

Nate led Tess and Barnaby back to the assembly bench at the rear of the high school woodshop.

On the heavy maple surface lay a finished piece of timberwork: a custom-built, raised orthopedic feeding platform made of figured black walnut, with two recessed cutouts holding stainless steel food and water bowls. The timber had been planed to a satin finish, the natural grain of the walnut swirling in rich chocolate and auburn tones.

"I built it nine inches off the floor," Nate explained, running his rough palm across the wood. "I saw him drinking from his floor bowl last week. He had to spread his front legs to reach down, and it put all his weight onto his carpal joints and compressed his trachea. If he eats at chest height, his neck stays straight and he won't gag."

Tess stared at the wooden stand.

The joinery was flawless: hand-cut through-mortise and tenon joints locked with tiny maple wedges, every corner beveled with a block plane. In the center of the walnut face, carved in elegant, deep lettering, was the single word: *BARNABY*.

"Nate," Tess whispered, her throat aching with sudden, overwhelming emotion. "This must have taken you thirty hours."

"Thirty-two," Nate said with a small, self-deprecating shrug, though his eyes were warm with craftsman pride. "I had the walnut stored up in the loft since 2019. Was saving it for a coffee table, but this seemed like a better use of the timber."

He reached into his apron pocket, pulled out a strip of dried venison jerky, and held it out to the dog. Barnaby took it with joyful, slobbery enthusiasm, resting his heavy chin on the walnut platform as if he had eaten from it his entire life.

Nate chuckled, ruffling the golden fur on the dog's head.

"Take it home to Willow Creek, Tess," Nate said softly, looking at her with complete, unburdened generosity. "Make sure he eats good from it."

Tess wrapped her arms around Nate's neck, holding her ex-husband in a fierce, tearful embrace that healed the last lingering wound between them.

Nate led Tess and Barnaby down the concrete ramp into the high school industrial arts wing.

The shop smelled of fresh cedar shavings, walnut dust, boiled linseed oil, and the sharp electric tang of warm saw blades. Overhead, long banks of fluorescent fixtures illuminated twenty heavy maple workbenches, each fitted with a cast-iron quick-release vise and a rack of Japanese pull saws.

Eight teenage students in denim aprons and clear safety glasses were working on their mortise-and-tenon joints, the rhythmic tap of wooden mallets against chisel handles echoing off the high cinderblock walls.

When Barnaby walked through the double doors, two of the students looked up and grinned.

"Hey, Barnaby," a tall boy with sawdust in his curly hair called out.

Barnaby's tail thumped once against Nate's leg, but the dog didn't trot over to the benches. He stayed close to Tess's left knee, his pace slow and deliberate on the sealed concrete floor.

Nate pointed toward the finishing alcove in the corner of the shop. "I moved the project in here so the dust wouldn't settle on the finish."

Tess followed him into the alcove. Resting on two wooden sawhorses was a low, wide ramp built from rough-sawn Western red cedar. It was twelve feet long and three feet wide, rising at a gentle ten-degree incline. Every eighteen inches, Nate had routed shallow grooves into the cedar planks and glued down strips of heavy black rubber tread.

"The rise is only five inches per foot," Nate explained, running his calloused hand over the smooth edge of the cedar railing. "I calculated the slope based on what you wrote in the spreadsheet about his reservoir walk. A five percent grade blew his breathing, right?"

Tess looked from the ramp to Nate's face. His eyes were tired, the skin around his temples creased with sawdust and dry winter air, but his expression was completely free of bitterness.

"You built this for the porch stairs on Pine Street," Tess said quietly.

"Three porch stairs are eight inches each," Nate said, wiping a thumb across his leather tool belt. "Twenty-four inches of vertical rise. When he came over on Sunday, he stood at the bottom step and looked up at the doorknob for two minutes before he tried to jump. When he jumped, his back foot slipped on the ice."

Nate looked down at Barnaby, who had settled onto a canvas drop cloth beside the sawhorses, his chin resting on his front paws.

"I had to carry him up the stairs, Tess," Nate said, his voice dropping low enough that the students couldn't hear. "He weighs seventy-two pounds. He didn't growl, but his whole chest was shaking. A retriever shouldn't have to be afraid of his own front door."

Tess felt an unexpected lump form in her throat. She stepped forward, running her fingers along the cedar handrail. Nate had sanded the wood down to three hundred grit, easing every sharp corner so a dog's flank wouldn't catch on the splinters.

"It's beautiful, Nate," Tess whispered. "The slope is perfect. It won't increase his cardiac preload at all."

Nate nodded once, looking down at his boots. "Clara told me you came over to her studio last week."

The silence in the finishing room became suddenly profound, punctuated only by the distant hum of the dust collector in the main shop.

"She gave me tea," Tess said honestly, her voice steady. "We talked about Barnaby's breathing."

Nate looked up, his brown eyes meeting hers with absolute clarity. "Clara's happier lately, Tess. She hums when she's painting at her easel. She hasn't done that since she moved out here from Oregon."

Tess held his gaze, her breath tight in her chest. "Nate—"

Nate raised one hand, palm outward, cutting her off gently. "You don't owe me an explanation, Tess. We tried for seven years. I'm a carpenter; I know when two pieces of wood don't take the glue. You don't force a joint that doesn't fit. You just plane the edge clean and let it be."

Barnaby let out a long, peaceful breath on the canvas drop cloth, his paws relaxing into the fragrant cedar dust.

Nate smiled faintly, reaching down to scratch Barnaby's gray chin. "Help me load this ramp into the bed of my truck. We'll set it up on Pine Street before the sun goes down."

Before they loaded the ramp into the truck, Nate brought over a block of paraffin wax from his workbench.

"Rub this on the guide rails once a month," Nate instructed, handing the yellow block to Tess. "Keeps the wood from absorbing moisture from the garden hose or the melting snow. Western red cedar naturally resists rot, but paraffin seals the end grain where the water likes to pool."

Tess sniffed the wax, which smelled of clean honey and pine resin. "I'll do it on the first of every month."

Nate wiped his hands on his canvas apron, looking down at Barnaby, who was sniffing a pile of curly maple shavings by the lathe.

"He's a good dog, Tess," Nate said, his voice dropping into quiet sincerity. "Always has been."

"He loves you, Nate," Tess said softly.

Nate smiled, a quiet, wistful expression that touched his eyes. "Tell Clara I'll see her on Tuesday for the pantry shelf delivery."

Nate tightened the ratchet straps across the cedar ramp in the bed of his pickup truck.

He pulled on the webbing twice with both hands to ensure the cargo wouldn't shift on the mountain curves.

"Solid as an anvil," Nate said, wiping his brow with his canvas sleeve. "I'll follow you over to Pine Street."

Tess climbed into the Subaru, clipped Barnaby's seatbelt harness into place, and started the engine.

The morning sun streamed through the south-facing casement windows of the cottage living room, laying a wide, brilliant rectangle of warm golden light across the braided wool rug.

It was nine o'clock on Monday morning.

Barnaby lay on his sheepskin mattress in the center of the sunlight, his coat glowing like burnished honey. Clara knelt on his left side, her fingers buried in his ruff, stroking the soft fur behind his ears. Nate sat on the floor near his hind legs, one large, calloused hand resting steady on the dog's flank.

Tess sat at his head, cradling his muzzle in her lap.

The front door swung open on oiled brass hinges. Dr. Marcus Bell stepped into the living room, carrying his small black leather veterinary call bag. He had left his white clinic coat in the truck; he wore a clean blue chambray work shirt and corduroy trousers, his wire bifocals resting low on his nose.

He didn't make small talk. He looked at the three people on the floor, then down at the dog in the sunlight.

"Morning, folks," Marcus said gently, setting his bag on the floorboards.

"Morning, Marcus," Tess said.

Marcus knelt down beside Tess. He reached out and stroked Barnaby's head with infinite tenderness, his gravelly voice dropping into a low, comforting murmur. "You did good, Barnaby. You did damn good, old friend."

He unzipped the leather bag. He pulled out a butterfly catheter, a roll of white tape, and two syringes: a small three-cc syringe containing clear dormosedan and telazol for deep sedation, and a large twenty-cc syringe filled with bright cobalt-blue pentobarbital sodium.

"I'll place the catheter," Tess said softly.

"No, Tess," Marcus said, laying his hand gently over hers. "Not today, kiddo. Today you're just his mom. Let me do the doctoring."

Tess leaned back, her breath catching in her throat, and let Clara's arm wrap around her waist.

Marcus worked with swift, painless precision. He slipped the butterfly needle into the saphenous vein on Barnaby's right hind leg, taped the hub securely to the golden fur, and attached the sedation syringe.

"First injection is just sleep," Marcus explained quietly. "No pain. No anxiety. Just heavy, peaceful dreaming."

He pushed the plunger.

Within thirty seconds, Barnaby's breathing slowed into a deep, rhythmic slumber. His eyelids closed, his jaw relaxed, and the tension that had gripped his chest for ten months melted away completely. His head lay heavy and peaceful in Tess's lap.

Clara kissed his forehead, her tears falling hot onto his coat. Nate bowed his head, his hand gripping the retriever's hip, his shoulders shaking with silent grief.

Tess looked at Marcus and nodded.

Marcus attached the second syringe to the catheter hub. "Whenever you're ready, folks."

"We're ready," Tess whispered. "Go ahead, bear. You're safe. We love you."

Marcus injected the blue solution in a steady, unbroken stream.

The breathing stopped. The chest remained still. After twenty seconds, Marcus placed his stethoscope against the left thorax, listened for forty silent beats of the wall clock, and pulled the binaurals from his ears.

"He's gone, Tess," Marcus said softly. "Peaceful and clean."

The room was filled with the warm morning sunlight and the quiet weeping of the three people who loved him, while outside the summer wind whispered through the mountain pines.

Dr. Bell packed his black leather bag in the quiet living room, wiping his wire bifocals with a clean handkerchief.

Barnaby lay in the warm rectangle of sunlight on his sheepskin bed, his golden coat gleaming, his face relaxed in eternal, peaceful slumber. The room was silent except for the soft ticking of the mantel clock and the gentle weeping of Tess, Clara, and Nate kneeling beside him.

Marcus walked over to Tess, resting his heavy, comforting hand on her shoulder.

"You did everything a technician could do, Tess," Marcus murmured with deep paternal warmth. "And you did everything a loving mother could do. Be proud of the way you walked him home."

"Thank you, Marcus," Tess whispered, wiping her face.

Marcus nodded to Nate and Clara, picked up his bag, and walked out the front door, closing it softly behind him.

Nate stood up slowly, looking down at the golden retriever. He leaned down, kissed the top of Barnaby's head one last time, and stepped toward the kitchen.

"I'll bring the cedar box up from the truck," Nate said softly, his voice thick with tears.

Tess and Clara remained on the wool rug in the sunlight, holding each other tight against the retriever's flank, their tears mingling in the peaceful morning quiet.

At two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, Dr. Bell's blue pickup truck turned into the gravel driveway.

The living room of the cottage was flooded with warm May sunlight. The double French doors opening onto the garden were propped wide with wooden wedges, letting in the fragrant mountain breeze, the scent of blooming lilac bushes, and the cheerful chatter of mountain chickadees in the pine trees.

Barnaby lay on his orthopedic bed in the center of the room, surrounded by clean towels and fresh fleece.

On a white ceramic platter beside him sat the feast: an entire rotisserie chicken, warm and fragrant with rosemary, carved into bite-sized morsels; two slices of sharp cheddar cheese; and a small porcelain bowl of heavy whipping cream.

Nate sat on the floor by Barnaby's hindquarters, feeding him pieces of roasted chicken breast. Barnaby took each piece with gentle, enthusiastic delicacy, licking Nate's greasy fingers with his pink tongue.

Clara sat beside the dog's head, her arm wrapped around his neck, whispering sweet, continuous words of gratitude into his soft ear.

Marcus walked into the room carrying his black leather veterinary medical bag. He wore a clean white clinic coat over his flannel shirt, his demeanor solemn, respectful, and infinitely gentle.

He set the bag on the floor, crouched beside the bed, and scratched Barnaby's silver jawline. Barnaby thumped his tail three times against the fleece.

"Hello, old friend," Marcus murmured.

Marcus looked up at Tess, who stood beside the bed holding an intravenous catheter pre-flushed with heparinized saline.

"Ready, Tess?" Marcus asked quietly.

Tess knelt beside Barnaby's left front leg. With practiced, tender competence, she clipped a two-inch square of golden fur over the cephalic vein, swabbed the skin with chlorhexidine, and placed the twenty-gauge pink catheter into the vessel on the first attempt. The flash of dark venous blood filled the clear hub immediately. She secured the catheter with two strips of white porous tape.

"Good girl," Marcus said softly.

Marcus drew two syringes from his leather bag: the first filled with milky white propofol for deep surgical anesthesia; the second holding twenty milliliters of deep blue pentobarbital sodium.

"Give him the rest of the chicken, Nate," Marcus said gently.

Nate fed Barnaby the last morsel of chicken, then held the small bowl of heavy cream while Barnaby lapped it clean with rhythmic, contented slaps of his tongue.

"Go ahead, Marcus," Tess whispered, holding Barnaby's paw tightly between both of her palms.

Marcus attached the syringe of propofol to the catheter and pushed the plunger slowly. "This is just sleep, Barnaby. Pure, deep sleep."

Within ten seconds, Barnaby's eyes grew heavy and peaceful. His breathing relaxed, deep and completely unlabored. His chin rested into Clara's lap, his muscles releasing all the tension and pain of seven months of cardiac struggle. He was asleep.

Marcus detached the first syringe and connected the blue pentobarbital.

"This is the release," Marcus said softly, his voice trembling slightly. He depressed the plunger smoothly over forty seconds.

Tess pressed the flat of her palm against Barnaby's left thorax.

Beneath her hand, the frantic, irregular galloping flutter of the mitral murmur began to slow.

Lub-dub... lub-dub... lub... dub...

And then, silence.

The heart stopped. The chest was still. The struggle was over.

"He's gone, Tess," Marcus whispered, setting the empty syringe on the tray.

Clara buried her face in Barnaby's neck fur, her shoulders shaking in weeping grief. Nate bowed his head, resting both of his large hands on the dog's flank, his tears falling silently onto the golden coat.

Tess leaned down and kissed Barnaby's warm, gray muzzle.

"Thank you, my sweet boy," Tess whispered into the quiet, sunlit room. "Run free on the ridges."

Marcus packed his black medical bag with slow, steady movements.

He placed the empty glass vials into his sharps container, sealed the lid with a plastic click, and latched the brass buckles of his bag. He stood up, wiping his forehead with a clean handkerchief.

"He was a noble dog, Tess," Marcus said, resting his hand on her shoulder. "In forty years of veterinary practice, I've seen a thousand dogs cross over. Not one went with more grace or more love in the room than Barnaby."

Tess stood up, leaning her head against Marcus's shoulder for a brief, grateful second. "Thank you, Marcus. For coming to the house."

"Always, doctor," Marcus murmured.

He shook Nate's hand with a firm, silent grip, nodded gently to Clara, and walked out the open French doors into the afternoon sunlight.

Nate remained kneeling by the bed for another ten minutes, his large hands resting on Barnaby's paws. Then he stood up, his eyes red but clear.

"I have the cedar box in the back of the truck, Tess," Nate said in an undertone. "I'll take him down to the crematorium in Salida this afternoon. I'll pick up his ashes on Thursday."

"Thank you, Nate," Tess said softly.

Nate leaned down, lifted Barnaby's seventy-pound body wrapped in his blue fleece blanket, carrying him out to the truck with the easy, reverent strength of a man carrying his own son.

Clara and Tess stood together by the open doors, their arms wrapped around each other's waists, watching the truck drive slowly down the tree-lined lane into the golden afternoon.

After Nate's truck drove away, the cottage was filled with a reverent stillness.

Tess and Clara walked back into the living room. The afternoon sunlight was still streaming through the French doors, illuminating the empty orthopedic bed, the clean fleece blankets, and the porcelain bowl that had held the sweet cream.

On the table by the door sat Barnaby's leather collar and his brass tags.

Clara picked up the collar with both hands. She held the soft, oiled leather against her chest, closing her eyes as the tears fell freely down her cheeks.

Tess stepped behind her, wrapping her arms around Clara's waist, resting her chin on Clara's shoulder.

"He went so gently, Tess," Clara whispered, her voice trembling. "No fear. No pain. Just sweet chicken and sleep."

"That was the greatest gift we could ever give him," Tess said softly, her own tears soaking into the fabric of Clara's shirt.

They stood together in the sunlit room, holding each other tightly, letting their grief wash through them like mountain rain, while outside the lilacs blossomed in the warm May breeze.

Tess walked into the kitchen, carrying the empty cream saucer to the sink.

She turned on the hot water, washing the porcelain clean with the lemon sponge, her hands moving with quiet, automatic rhythm.

Through the window above the sink, the mountain peaks stood bathed in the pure, radiant light of late afternoon.

Clara came into the kitchen, taking the towel from the drawer and drying the saucer without a word.

They set the clean dish on the open shelf, their shoulders touching in the quiet warmth of the room.

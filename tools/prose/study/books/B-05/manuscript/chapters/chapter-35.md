The living room of Clara's cottage on Willow Creek was quiet on a Wednesday evening in late June, the French doors open to the scent of blooming wild roses and cool pine night air.

Tess sat on the sofa, her legs curled beneath her, watching Clara groom Barnaby on a blue wool blanket spread across the fir floorboards.

Clara had an old wooden-backed boar-bristle brush in one hand and a small spray bottle of organic lavender water in the other. She misted the dog's golden coat with the lavender spray, then brushed through the thick fur with long, gentle, rhythmic strokes from the nape of his neck down to the feathery fringe of his tail.

Barnaby lay on his side, his four legs outstretched, his eyes half-closed in pure, unadulterated pleasure. Every five strokes, he let out a long, contented groan that made Clara smile.

"He loves lavender," Clara whispered, her brush moving with practiced, unhurried grace. "When I was little, my grandmother used to put dried lavender under the sofa cushions. She said it settled the mind after a storm."

Tess leaned back against the sofa cushions, watching the fireflies flicker in the dark garden beyond the open doors.

She looked at Clara's profile: the clean line of her throat, the dark curls tucked behind her ears, the tenderness in her hands as she worked through a small tangle behind the dog's ear.

There was no emergency tonight. There was no furosemide to inject, no rhythm strip to decipher, no spreadsheet to analyze. There was only the quiet room, the smell of lavender and summer roses, and the profound, peaceful beauty of an ordinary domestic evening carved out of the shadow of loss.

Tess realized that this was what she had searched for her entire life: not a fortress that kept out the weather, but a home where two people could look straight into the dark and choose to stay gentle anyway.

Barnaby rolled slightly, resting his chin on Clara's knee, his tail giving two soft, rhythmic thumps against the floor.

Tess watched Clara's brush glide through the golden fur, misting the coat with lavender water.

Barnaby let out another deep, shuddering sigh of canine bliss, his paws stretching out until his black pads pressed against the base of the sofa.

"He's lost four pounds this month," Tess noted quietly, her eyes assessing the hollows behind his ribs. "Cardiac cachexia. The failing heart consumes immense amounts of energy, and the systemic inflammation drives muscle wasting."

Clara laid down the brush, stroking the dog's soft flank with her bare palm.

"I know," Clara said softly. "I can feel his hip bones when I lift him onto the ramp. But look at his eyes, Tess. He isn't in pain tonight. He's just resting."

Tess slid off the sofa, kneeling on the blue wool blanket beside Clara. She laid her head against Clara's shoulder, watching the gentle rise and fall of the dog's chest.

"We gave him a good year, Clara," Tess murmured.

"The best year," Clara whispered back, wrapping her arm around Tess and holding her close in the lavender-scented evening air.

At eight in the evening, the cottage was filled with the calming scent of lavender and cedar.

Clara sat on the floor with her legs folded, holding Barnaby's heavy head in her lap. With slow, rhythmic strokes, she drew a natural bristle brush through the thick golden fur along his neck, working out the dried mud from his morning walk in the garden.

Every few minutes, she misted the brush with a light spray of distilled water infused with organic French lavender oil.

Barnaby's eyes were half-closed in pure canine ecstasy. His throat vibrated with a deep, rumbling purr that sounded like a small outboard motor idling in gear.

Tess sat on the sofa above them, holding a clipboard with the monthly medication audit, but her pen hadn't moved in twenty minutes. She watched Clara's hands: the gentle, patient discipline with which she untangled a burr from behind Barnaby's ear, the soft murmur of praise whenever the dog shifted his weight to give her better access to his chest.

"He loves that brush," Tess said softly.

Clara looked up, brushing a strand of dark hair from her forehead with the back of her wrist. "Animals know when you're doing something just for their comfort. They don't have to earn it. It's just grace."

Tess set her clipboard aside on the coffee table. She slid down from the sofa onto the rug, settling beside Clara. She laid one hand on Barnaby's warm flank, feeling the tired, failing heart beat in its steady, fragile rhythm beneath the ribs.

Barnaby reached out with his front paw, hooking his blunt claws gently over Tess's wrist, pulling her hand closer to his chest.

The three of them sat together in the quiet living room while the twilight turned to deep indigo outside the windows.

At nine o'clock, the wind picked up from the north, rattling the cedar boughs against the bedroom window.

Tess went into the kitchen to warm a cup of milk for Clara. On the counter sat the daily medication schedule, marked with Clara's neat checkmarks beside each dose: 8:00 AM Vetmedin, 8:00 AM Enalapril, 12:00 PM Mexiletine, 4:00 PM Furosemide, 8:00 PM Vetmedin.

Every box was checked. Not a single dose had been missed in seven months.

Tess carried the warm mug back into the living room.

Clara had finished brushing Barnaby's coat. The dog lay on his side on the thick sheepskin rug, his breathing soft and even: twenty breaths per minute. Beside him, Clara had gathered the loose golden undercoat into a soft, fragrant ball of fur the size of a grapefruit.

"Look how much he shed," Clara whispered, showing the ball of fur to Tess.

"The summer coat coming in," Tess said, sitting beside her on the rug and handing her the warm milk. "He's getting ready for July."

Clara cradled the warm mug between her palms, looking down at the sleeping retriever with clear, tender eyes.

"We gave him a good winter, Tess," Clara murmured.

"The best winter," Tess agreed, resting her head against Clara's shoulder while Barnaby slept between them.

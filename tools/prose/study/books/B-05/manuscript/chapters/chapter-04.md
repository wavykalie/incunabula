The pine kitchen table at Pine Street was four inches thick, built by Nate from salvaged timber after the old sawmill in Alma burned down. The surface was scarred with pencil scores, chisel marks, and circular water rings from years of tea mugs.

It was noon. The clinic was closed for the lunch hour. Outside, the October sun reflected brilliantly off the dusting of fresh snow on Mount Silverheels, but inside the kitchen, the air was cool and still.

Tess sat on the bench on the north side of the table, her clinic fleece zipped to her chin. Clara sat opposite her, an open spiral notebook and a black rollerball pen laid before her. Nate stood by the woodstove, leaning his shoulder against the brick chimney, his arms crossed over his chest. He hadn't taken off his heavy canvas barn coat.

"We have to divide the weeks differently," Tess said. She laid three white plastic prescription bottles on the center of the pine surface. "The old arrangement was two weeks with me, two weeks with you. That doesn't work anymore."

Nate shifted his boots, the leather creaking. "Why doesn't it work? We've done two weeks since April."

"Because medication compliance has to be exact," Tess said, looking directly at him. "If he misses a single dose of pimobendan, his atrial pressure spikes within eight hours. If he gets an extra dose because someone forgot to mark the calendar, his blood pressure tanks and he faints. We need shorter rotations so neither of us loses track of his baseline."

"Sunday to Sunday," Clara suggested. Her voice was calm, cutting through the edge of Nate's defensive posture. "Seven days here, seven days at Tess's. Handover at five o'clock on Sunday evenings. That way we both see him weekly, and any change in weight or breathing gets caught within seven days instead of fourteen."

Nate stared at the floorboards between his feet. "He's our dog, Tess. Yours and mine. Clara shouldn't have to manage his prescriptions."

The room went cold.

Tess looked at Nate. She saw the familiar, stubborn clench of his jaw, the same expression he had worn during the last year of their marriage whenever the checking account ran short or the roof began to leak. Nate didn't want to fail; he just couldn't bear to look at the machinery of failure while it happened.

"Nate," Clara said gently. She didn't sound hurt. She sounded steady, like a teacher explaining a difficult fraction to a stubborn child. "I live here. I'm the one who's home at seven in the morning when you're at the high school opening the woodshop. I'm the one who makes his sweet potato mash. If I don't give him the pills, who does?"

Nate swallowed. He looked at Clara, then turned his gaze toward the back window where the chickadees were fluttering around the feeder. "I just don't want you stuck with our mess, Clara."

"He isn't a mess," Clara said. "He's Barnaby."

Tess felt an odd, unexpected knot loosen behind her ribs. For four years, every difficult conversation with Nate had ended in this exact deadlock: Nate retreating into quiet shame, Tess hardening into clinical impatience. They had spent forty-eight months dancing around each other's wounds without ever touching the truth.

And now Clara Evans had stepped into the middle of that dead dance and spoken the truth in two sentences without raising her voice.

"Sunday to Sunday," Tess said, pushing the prescription bottles across the table toward Clara. "Seven in the morning, seven at night. We log every dose. If his resting respiration goes above thirty breaths a minute while he's sleeping, you call me. Day or night."

Clara uncapped her pen and wrote *Sunday 5 PM* at the top of the notebook page. "Understood."

Nate pushed off from the chimney, turned without a word, and walked out through the mudroom door toward the woodpile. Ten seconds later, the rhythmic, heavy crack of the splitting maul rang out through the cold mountain air.

Tess stood up, zipped her parka, and took Barnaby's lead to head back to the clinic for her afternoon shift.

Tess sat quietly on the bench as the heavy thuds of the splitting maul continued outside. Across the table, Clara watched the steam rising from her tea, her hands cupped around the earthenware mug.

"He does that when the world gets too loud," Clara said, her voice dropping into a gentle cadence. "When the county road department threatened to condemn the drainage ditch behind the garage in August, he spent four days out there with a mattock, digging out rocks until his palms were raw."

"I know," Tess said softly. "When my grandmother died in Leadville, he built a cedar chest in twenty-four hours. He didn't come to bed. He just planed every surface until it looked like glass."

Clara smiled faintly, a sad, recognizing curve of her lips. She reached into her pocket and pulled out a small plastic pill organizer with seven compartments, labeled Monday through Sunday with black permanent marker.

"I bought this at the pharmacy in Frisco yesterday," Clara said, setting it on the table between the prescription bottles. "Seven compartments for morning, seven for night. I'll load his pimobendan and enalapril for the week. Let me show you how I've been giving him the morning dose."

Tess slid off the bench and knelt on the braided rag rug beside Barnaby. The dog looked up, his tail tapping once against the pine floorboards. Tess placed her hand on his inner thigh, slipping her index and middle fingers into the femoral groove.

"Come here, Clara," Tess said.

Clara knelt beside her on the rug, her wool sweater brushing against Tess's shoulder.

"Put your fingers right here," Tess instructed, guiding Clara's hand to the femoral triangle. "Feel that pulse? It's strong, but watch how quickly the pressure falls off. That's the regurgitant jet stealing volume from the peripheral arteries. If this pulse ever feels weak or thready like a wet string, his blood pressure is dropping and you need to call me immediately."

Clara pressed her fingertips against the dog's groin, her brow furrowing with intense concentration. "I feel it. It beats hard, then it disappears."

"Exactly," Tess said. "That's the bounding character of mitral insufficiency. You're feeling the compensation."

Clara looked at Tess, their faces barely six inches apart in the quiet kitchen. Clara's gray eyes were calm, searching, completely present.

"Thank you for teaching me, Tess," Clara murmured.

Tess felt her throat tighten, an unfamiliar warmth spreading through her chest. She pulled her hands back, stood up, and gathered her keys.

"I'll see you Sunday at five," Tess said.

She walked out through the mudroom into the crisp mountain air, the sound of Nate's maul ringing out through the pines as she started her car.

Twilight fell across the valley of Silvercreek in shades of bruised violet and copper.

The air smelled of warm pine needles, wild sage, and the cold mountain snowfields lingering on the high peaks of the Divide.

Tess, Clara, and Nate sat together on the front lawn of the Willow Creek cottage, their backs resting against the cedar rail fence. Between them, on his sheepskin bed spread across the soft clover, lay Barnaby.

The dog was calm. The late afternoon injection of butorphanol had relieved the anxiety in his chest, allowing his ribs to rise and fall in a slow, peaceful cadence. His head rested across Clara's lap, his paws touching Tess's knee, his tail brushing against Nate's boot.

Nate held a plate with three warm, freshly roasted strips of bacon he had cooked in the cottage kitchen.

"You want some bacon, old bear?" Nate murmured.

Barnaby opened his dark eyes. The golden retriever instinct—the ancient, unyielding love of food that had defined his eleven years—sparkled for one final, glorious moment. He took the bacon from Nate's fingers with delicate, velvet lips, chewed it slowly, and swallowed with a deep, contented sigh.

"Good boy," Nate whispered, his hand trembling as he stroked the dog's graying muzzle.

Clara leaned down, pressing her cheek against Barnaby's golden ear, whispering words of gratitude and love that only the dog could hear.

Tess held Barnaby's front paw between her palms, feeling the familiar calloused pads that had trotted through thousands of miles of Colorado mountain trails. She didn't check his pulse. She didn't count his respirations.

For the first time in ten months, she put down the technician's burden. She was no longer managing a cardiac case; she was simply a woman saying goodbye to the creature that had taught her how to love.

The first star appeared above the granite ridge of the Continental Divide, sharp and cold and clear in the twilight sky.

The twilight deepened over the high peaks, turning Mount Silverheels into a sharp indigo silhouette against the violet sky.

Barnaby lay on his sheepskin mattress on the front lawn, his breathing slow and peaceful under the gentle influence of the butorphanol. He had finished his bacon, and now his heavy golden skull rested comfortably across Clara's lap, his eyes gazing out at the darkening valley.

Nate sat on the grass beside him, his pocketknife carving a small piece of cedar bark into a smooth wooden whistle.

"Remember when he jumped into the beaver pond during our second anniversary, Tess?" Nate asked with a quiet chuckle. "He came out covered in green duckweed, smelled like rotting cattails for a week."

Tess laughed softly, tears glinting in her eyes. "He shook himself right inside the tent, Nate. Blew green mud all over your down sleeping bag."

Clara smiled, stroking the soft velvet behind Barnaby's ears. "He lived a big, wild life. A real mountain dog."

"The best," Nate agreed softly, setting the carved cedar piece beside the dog's paws.

They sat together in the cool mountain twilight until the stars filled the sky, watching over their old friend in peaceful, unbroken love.

They sat together in a wide circle on the grass behind the cottage, under the gold and copper light of the setting May sun.

Barnaby lay in the center of the circle on his blue fleece blanket. Nate had carried the heavy orthopedic mattress out from the living room, setting it gently on the lawn where the dog could look out over the valley toward Mount Princeton.

Nate sat on Barnaby's right, his long legs stretched out in the fresh clover, his calloused hands slowly stroking the dog's golden flanks. Clara sat at Barnaby's head, holding his gray chin in her lap, feeding him small morsels of grilled steak from a ceramic saucer. Tess sat on his left, her hand resting over the left side of his chest, monitoring the fading cadence of his pulse.

The valley below was quiet. In the distance, the bells of the Methodist church in Silvercreek struck seven times, the mellow bronze tones drifting through the evening air like smoke.

"He had a good run, Tess," Nate said, his deep voice thick with emotion. "Ten years. He was on the Continental Divide when he was eight weeks old. He retrieved ducks from the reservoir. He slept on the foot of our bed through every winter storm."

"He was the best dog in the world," Clara whispered, leaning down to press her forehead against Barnaby's soft ears. Barnaby gave a faint, slow thump of his tail, licking a drop of meat juice from her knuckle.

Tess felt the pulse under her fingers: weak, irregular, fluttering like a trapped moth. The left ventricle was failing to generate sufficient systolic pressure to perfuse his kidneys. His paws were cold to the touch.

"Tomorrow afternoon," Tess said, looking from Nate to Clara. "Marcus will come to the house at two o'clock. We'll do it right here in the living room, on his bed, with the sun coming through the window."

Nate looked at Tess, his eyes shining with unshed tears, and nodded once. "I'll be here at one-thirty. I'll bring the roast chicken from the deli he likes."

"I'll buy fresh cream," Clara murmured. "He always wanted the cream from my coffee."

Barnaby sighed, his heavy golden head settling into Clara's lap, his eyes closing in peaceful exhaustion while the twilight wrapped the mountain peaks in purple and rose.

The twilight deepened over the valley, turning the snowfields on Mount Princeton from copper to deep lavender.

The evening breeze carried the cool mountain air down through the aspens, rustling the green leaves with a dry, papery whisper. Barnaby opened his brown eyes once, looking slowly around the circle of three faces that had framed his life for ten years: Tess, who had kept his heart beating; Nate, who had built his ramps and walked him on the ridges; and Clara, who had brushed his coat with lavender and fed him sweet cream from her fingers.

He gave a soft, shuddering sigh, his tail wagging twice against the grass before his head settled back into Clara's lap.

"He's saying thank you," Clara whispered, her tears falling softly onto his golden fur.

"He's telling us he's ready," Nate said, wiping his eyes with the back of his denim sleeve. "A good retriever knows when the hunt is done. You don't make him stay in the blind when the sun goes down."

Tess reached out, taking Nate's rough hand in her left and Clara's hand in her right, forming an unbroken circle over the dog's resting body.

"We gave him a wonderful life," Tess said, her voice steady and clear despite the grief filling her throat. "And tomorrow we will give him a gentle death."

They sat together on the lawn until the first stars appeared above the Continental Divide: the bright white diamond of Vega directly overhead, the steady orange beacon of Arcturus setting in the west, and the cool, silent mountain air wrapping them all in peace.

They sat on the lawn for another hour, long after the stars had filled the mountain sky.

The night was cool and still, carrying the scent of blooming wild plum blossoms and damp earth from the irrigation ditch. Barnaby lay between them, his breathing soft and unlabored in the cool mountain air.

Nate reached into his canvas coat pocket and pulled out Barnaby's favorite leather ball, scuffed and gray from years of teeth marks. He placed it gently between Barnaby's front paws.

"Remember when he jumped into the Arkansas River after this ball?" Nate asked, his voice low in the dark. "He was three years old. The river was running four thousand cubic feet per second in June runoff. I thought he was gone."

"And he came out fifty yards downstream with the ball still clamped in his jaws," Tess remembered, a soft tear rolling down her cheek. "He climbed up the boulder bank and dropped it right at your feet like it was a game."

"He was always brave," Clara said, leaning down to kiss the top of Barnaby's head.

Nate stood up, brushing the grass from his jeans. He looked down at the dog, then looked at Tess and Clara.

"I'll bring the deli chicken at one-thirty tomorrow, Tess," Nate said, his voice thick with emotion. "The whole roasted one with the herb butter."

"Thank you, Nate," Tess said, standing up and hugging him tightly in the dark.

Nate hugged Clara next, a long, quiet embrace that held all the tenderness of their friendship.

"Goodnight, Barnaby," Nate whispered, looking down at his old dog one last time before walking through the side gate to his truck.

Nate's truck lights illuminated the cedar trees along the driveway as he backed out into the quiet country lane.

Tess and Clara walked back onto the porch together, holding Barnaby between them with his nylon chest sling. He climbed the ramp slowly, one paw at a time, his breathing calm and unhurried on the rubber treads.

Inside the living room, Clara spread a fresh flannel sheet over his orthopedic bed.

Barnaby circled once, collapsed softly onto his side, and buried his nose into Clara's fleece slipper.

Tess sat on the floor beside him, resting her hand over his ribcage. His pulse was weak, but the rhythm was smooth.

"He's comfortable tonight," Clara whispered, pulling the blue blanket over his hips.

"He's comfortable," Tess agreed, leaning her head on Clara's shoulder as they watched the candle burn down to the saucer.

The moving boxes stacked in the mudroom of the Pine Street house were heavy corrugated cardboard from the liquor store in Frisco, taped with reinforced nylon filament.

It was a Sunday afternoon in late March.

Nate stood in the kitchen holding a roll of packing tape and a utility knife. Clara was wrapping her ceramic watercolor palettes in sheets of unprinted newsprint, carefully placing them into a shallow wooden crate.

"I found a cottage on Willow Creek Road," Clara said, her voice calm and even. "The old caretaker's cabin behind the Miller place. It has two rooms and a glass sunporch that faces south. Good light for the illustrations."

Nate cut a strip of tape with his thumb, sealing the bottom of a box.

"Miller's a decent landlord," Nate said. "He keeps the road plowed in the winter. The roof is corrugated metal, so you won't have to shovel the snow off in January."

He set the tape gun down on the pine table. He looked at Clara, his expression gentle, honest, completely devoid of bitterness.

"You don't have to rush out, Clara," Nate said. "The ground's still muddy. You could stay until April."

"It's time, Nate," Clara said softly, setting a wrapped palette into the box. "We both know it is."

Nate pulled out a kitchen chair, spun it around, and sat down with his arms resting across the back.

"I was thinking about last summer," Nate murmured, looking out at the bare aspens in the side yard. "When you first moved your plants in here. I was so damn relieved to have someone who didn't look at me like I was a broken joint that couldn't be glued. You were kind to me, Clara."

"You were kind to me too, Nate," Clara said, walking over and placing her hand on the back of his chair. "You gave me a quiet place when my life was screaming. I will always be grateful for that."

"We were a good shelter," Nate said with a faint, wry smile. "A sturdy lean-to in a bad wind. But nobody builds a permanent home in a lean-to."

He stood up, towering over her in his flannel shirt, and pulled her into a warm, brief hug that smelled of cedar shavings and clean laundry.

"Go be with Tess, Clara," Nate said, kissing the top of her head. "She's been lonely for a very long time, and so have you."

Clara rested her face against his chest for one second, then stepped back, picked up the wooden crate of paints, and carried it out to her wagon.

Nate carried the last heavy wooden crate of botanical books out to the tailgate of Clara's wagon, sliding it smoothly across the ribbed bed liner.

He wiped his hands on his canvas pants and turned back toward the house.

The kitchen looked clean, bare, and spacious. Clara's watercolor palettes were gone from the dining table, her glass specimen jars removed from the windowsills, her dried herbs packed away in cardboard boxes.

Clara stood on the porch with her keys in her hand, looking at the cedar bungalow where she had spent eight peaceful months.

"The place looks good, Nate," Clara said gently.

"It's a good house," Nate replied, leaning against the porch railing. "It was good when Tess was here, and it was good when you were here. But it's time for me to find out who I am when I'm not trying to fix someone else's quiet."

Clara smiled, stepping forward to clasp his hand in both of hers. "You're a good man, Nate Gallagher. Don't ever forget that."

"Take care of her, Clara," Nate said softly. "And take care of our dog."

Clara nodded, walked down the cedar ramp, and drove down Pine Street into the early spring evening.

Nate sat down at the heavy oak dining table he had built during his second year of teaching.

The evening light came through the south-facing windows of the Pine Street house, cutting long rectangular blocks of amber across the polished wood floor. On the table between them sat Clara's stoneware mug of mint tea and Nate's enamel mug of black drip coffee.

In the hallway, Barnaby lay on the cedar landing of his ramp, dozing in the warmth of the evening air with his nose resting on his front paws.

"You packed your sketchbooks," Nate said, nodding toward the two cardboard boxes stacked by the front coat closet. His voice was level, quiet, without an edge of anger.

"Yes," Clara said, keeping her hands wrapped around her warm mug. "Mrs. Higgins has the carriage house behind the library ready for me to move in tomorrow morning."

Nate nodded slowly. He picked up his coffee mug, took a sip, and set it down precisely on the cork coaster.

"I knew it was coming, Clara," Nate said. "I think I knew it three weeks after you moved in last summer."

Clara looked up, her gray eyes wide and searching. "Why didn't you say anything, Nate?"

"Because you were safe here," Nate said simply. "And I was lonely. When Tess left two years ago, this house felt like an empty barn. Having you in the studio, hearing your watercolor jars clinking in the sink, having you cook supper on Wednesdays... it made the place feel like a home again."

"You were good to me, Nate," Clara whispered, her throat tightening. "You gave me shelter when I had nowhere else to land."

"You didn't need a landlord, Clara. You needed a partner," Nate said, looking directly across the table. "And I was never going to be that for you. I spent seven years being the man who couldn't talk to Tess about what hurt. I'm not going to spend the next seven years pretending I don't see who you're looking at when she walks into the room."

Clara felt a hot flush of shame and gratitude rise through her chest. "Nate, I never planned this. I never wanted to hurt you."

"You didn't hurt me," Nate said, his deep voice steady and sure. "Tess hurt me when we broke up, and I hurt her right back. That took two years to burn off. But watching you and Tess together with Barnaby... that didn't feel like being hurt. That felt like watching two people who speak the exact same language finally sit down at the same table."

He stood up, walking over to the window that looked out toward the mountain ridge. The sun had dipped behind Mount Princeton, turning the snowfields into glowing ribbons of copper and rose.

"Tess has been alone in that apartment for two winters," Nate said, his back turned to her. "She carries the whole county on her back, patching up ranchers' calving heifers and old dogs, and she never asks for help. She wouldn't let me help her because I didn't understand the medicine. But you understand her, Clara."

Clara stood up and walked over to him, laying her hand on the heavy canvas sleeve of his work coat. "You're a good man, Nate Miller."

Nate turned, a gentle, sad smile touching his lips. He placed his large, calloused hand over hers on his arm.

"Take care of her, Clara," Nate said softly. "And make sure you keep that old dog's water bowl filled with cold tap water. He hates warm water."

"I know," Clara said with a faint, tearful laugh.

Nate walked her to the front door, carried her two boxes of art supplies out to the trunk of her Honda Civic, and waved once as she backed down the gravel driveway into the quiet mountain dusk.

After Nate loaded the boxes into the Civic, they walked back into the empty living room together.

The afternoon sun was sinking behind the western crest of the mountains, casting long shadows of the ponderosa pines across the bare floorboards where Clara's easel had stood for seven months.

Nate walked over to the stone fireplace, leaning his elbow on the cedar mantel. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small brass key on a split ring.

"This is the spare key to the shop," Nate said, holding it out to Clara. "If you ever need to cut framing stock or use the heavy belt sander for your wooden watercolor panels, the shop is always open to you, Clara."

Clara looked at the key in his open palm. Tears welled in her eyes, catching the copper sunlight.

"Nate, I don't know what to say," she whispered.

"Don't say anything," Nate said gently, closing her fingers over the cool metal. "You're an artist. You need good tools. Just because we're not living under the same roof doesn't mean I stop caring whether your frames fit square."

Clara stepped forward, pressing her cheek against his canvas coat. Nate wrapped his heavy arm around her shoulders in a solid, protective embrace.

"You're a good friend, Nate," Clara murmured.

"Go on now," Nate smiled, patting her shoulder. "Tess is waiting for you at the cottage. Don't let that soup get cold."

Nate walked down the gravel driveway beside Clara's car, resting his hand on the driver's side doorframe.

The evening light was fading fast, the mountains turning to deep charcoal silhouettes against the lilac sky. In the woods behind the shop, an owl called three times, the low hoot floating across the quiet valley.

"You have enough firewood at Mrs. Higgins's place?" Nate asked, his practical nature surfacing even at the end of their conversation.

"Two cords of split birch stacked against the carriage house wall," Clara said, smiling through her tears. "And a new pellet stove in the kitchen."

"Good," Nate nodded. "The chimney sweep in Salida cleaned that flue in September, so it draws clean. Just make sure you keep the ash pan emptied every three days."

"I will, Nate," Clara promised, turning the ignition key.

The Honda's headlights flickered on, cutting bright white beams through the twilight dust.

Nate stepped back from the door, raising his hand in a slow, steady wave as Clara backed down onto the asphalt road and drove toward her new life.

Nate watched the red taillights of Clara's Honda Civic disappear around the bend where Pine Street met the river road.

The valley air had dropped below freezing, the frost beginning to sparkle on the gravel driveway under the cold stars.

He stood in the darkness for five minutes, listening to the rushing water of the river over the granite boulders. Then he turned, walked up the front steps, and clicked the deadbolt into place.

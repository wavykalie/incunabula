The phone on Tess's nightstand rang at 11:42 PM on a Thursday.

In veterinary medicine, a late-night call was never good news. It was either a horse tangled in barbed wire up on the pass or a dog that had eaten an entire pan of chocolate brownies off a holiday buffet.

Tess grabbed the receiver on the second ring. "Gallagher."

"Tess." Clara's voice was tight, stripped of its usual fluid cadence, vibrating with controlled adrenaline. "It's Barnaby. He's coughing."

Tess sat bolt upright, throwing the comforter aside. "What kind of cough? Dry or wet?"

"Wet," Clara said. In the background, Tess could hear the harsh, hacking retch of a dog trying to clear fluid from his carina—a rhythmic, suffocating rasp that made her blood run cold. "He stood up from his bed twenty minutes ago and started pacing. His neck is extended. He's bringing up white foam."

"Pulmonary edema," Tess said, pulling her jeans on with one hand while holding the phone with the other. "Fulminant Stage C. Nate there?"

"Nate's in Denver at the forestry curriculum conference," Clara said. Her breathing was fast, but her words were precise. "I have Barnaby in the back of my wagon. I'm backing out of the driveway right now."

"Don't let him jump," Tess ordered. "Meet me at the clinic back door in five minutes. I'm leaving now."

Tess didn't bother with socks. She shoved her bare feet into her work boots, grabbed her keys and jacket, and sprinted down the outside wooden staircase to the Subaru. The engine roared to life with a cloud of exhaust in the twenty-degree air. She threw the transmission into reverse and fishtailed out onto the empty, frozen main street of Silvercreek.

When she swung into the clinic gravel lot, the headlights of Clara's Subaru Outback were already illuminating the rear double doors.

Clara was already at the tailgate. She had draped an old wool blanket under Barnaby's chest and belly, using it as a sling to support his weight so his legs didn't have to carry him. Barnaby was standing on the gravel, his head held low, froth bubbling at the corners of his lips, his sides heaving like a bellows.

"Grab the head," Tess said, dropping her keys into her pocket and taking the front handles of the blanket sling.

"I've got him," Clara said.

They moved together in absolute, unspoken synchronization. Neither of them hesitated. They lifted the seventy-pound retriever between them, taking the weight smoothly onto their thighs, carrying him through the back door into the warm, bright fluorescent glare of the treatment bay.

Tess kicked the crash cart brake off with her heel.

"Table two," Tess said.

They hoisted him onto the rubber mat. Barnaby sank onto his brisket, his elbows abducted, his neck stretched out straight in an orthopneic posture to maximize airway diameter. Every breath sounded like wet paper tearing in a gale.

"Get the oxygen mask from kennel six," Tess directed, her voice dropping into the cool, rapid command tone of emergency triage. "Green tank. Flow meter set to ten liters."

Clara didn't ask questions. She moved across the treatment room with quiet efficiency, returning in ten seconds with the clear plastic conical mask and the green tubing. She slid the mask over Barnaby's muzzle, holding it gently behind his ears, murmuring steady words against his forehead while the cold gas hissed into the chamber.

Tess ripped open a drawer. Butterfly catheter. Twenty-gauge needle. Vial of injectable furosemide—five percent solution, fifty milligrams per milliliter.

She checked his weight on the chart behind the door: thirty-two kilograms. Dose: two milligrams per kilogram. Sixty-four milligrams. One point three milliliters.

She drew the clear liquid into a three-cc syringe, flicked the air bubbles to the top, and snapped off the needle guard.

"Hold his right forelimb," Tess said. "Roll the cephalic vein lateral."

Clara reached across the table. Her hands were covered in dry garden dirt, but her fingers were rock steady. She wrapped her left palm around Barnaby's elbow, her thumb pressing down into the groove over the vein, rolling the vessel onto the dorsal surface of the radius.

The vein popped up under Tess's index finger, tight and bounding with venous hypertension.

Tess slid the needle through the skin at a fifteen-degree angle. A flash of dark venous blood filled the hub.

"Release the roll," Tess said.

Clara lifted her thumb. Tess pushed the plunger home over ten seconds, sending the potent loop diuretic straight into his central circulation to block sodium-potassium-chloride cotransporters in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle. Within fifteen minutes, the kidneys would begin dumping massive volumes of water, lowering intravascular hydrostatic pressure and pulling the fluid out of the alveoli.

"Good," Tess breathed, pulling the needle free and pressing a cotton swab over the puncture site. "Hold that for sixty seconds."

Clara pressed the swab. Her face was pale, a smudge of soot on her cheekbone from Nate's woodstove, her dark eyes fixed entirely on the dog's straining chest.

"Is it going to work?" Clara asked.

"The furosemide works fast," Tess said. "We give it twenty minutes. If he doesn't clear, we give another half-dose and tap his pleural space. But he should respond."

She looked at Clara's hands holding the cotton against Barnaby's leg. They were shaking now, a fine, uncontrollable tremor running through her knuckles as the initial adrenaline began to wash out.

Tess reached across the stainless steel table, laid her palm over Clara's cold fingers, and held them steady against the dog's coat.

The treatment room at midnight was an island of harsh white fluorescence in the middle of a frozen, sleeping mountain town.

Tess kept her palm pressed firmly over Clara's trembling knuckles where Clara held the cotton swab against Barnaby's cephalic vein. Under their hands, the coarse golden fur was damp with sweat and melted snow.

The wall clock ticked: 12:08 AM.

Ten minutes had passed since Tess pushed the sixty-four milligrams of furosemide through the butterfly catheter.

Slowly, almost imperceptibly at first, the frantic mechanical heave of Barnaby's thorax began to settle. The wet, tearing rasp of his respirations lost its harsh, serrated edge. The white froth around his flews ceased bubbling, drying into a thin, sticky crust against his whiskers.

"Look at his chest," Clara whispered, her voice shaking with relief. "It's slowing down."

Tess reached for her stethoscope with her free hand, fitting the binaurals into her ears. She set the diaphragm against his right hemithorax, listening to the dependent lung fields.

The loud, coarse crackles that had dominated the auscultation ten minutes ago were breaking up into softer, scattered rales. The loop diuretic was working, shutting down sodium reabsorption in the thick ascending limb, forcing the kidneys to dump water, lowering the left atrial pressure below the hydrostatic threshold.

"The crackles are clearing," Tess said, letting out a long, shuddering breath. "The fluid is resorbing. He's crossing back over."

Clara dropped her forehead against the edge of the stainless steel table, her shoulders shaking with silent, exhausted sobs.

Tess looked at Clara's dark hair, at the smudge of woodsmoke on her cheek, at the raw, unguarded vulnerability of a woman who had spent the last two hours driving through an icy mountain night with a dying dog in her cargo bed.

Without thinking, Tess reached out and touched Clara's shoulder, her fingers resting gently against the rough wool of her sweater.

"You saved him, Clara," Tess said softly. "If you hadn't brought him right when you did, he wouldn't have survived the night."

Clara lifted her head, wiping her wet cheeks with the sleeve of her cardigan. Her gray eyes were bloodshot, bruised with fatigue, but clear and steady as mountain water.

"I couldn't lose him, Tess," Clara said, her voice catching. "I couldn't let him die alone in the kitchen while Nate was in Denver."

"You weren't alone," Tess murmured. "We're here."

They lifted Barnaby between them, transferring him from the cold exam table into the isolation ward's high-flow oxygen cage. The dog lay down on the thick blue fleece with an audible groan of relief, resting his chin on his front paws as the clear door sealed shut and the compressor began to hum.

Tess turned down the overhead lights, leaving only the amber glow of the desk lamp, and sat down on the floor beside Clara to begin the night watch.

By two in the morning, the clinic treatment bay was quiet.

The primary infusion pump hummed on its chrome stand, metering out normal saline at a slow maintenance rate of fifteen milliliters an hour. On the stainless steel wet table, Barnaby lay on his left side, his chest rising and falling with regular, unlabored rhythm: twenty-six respirations per minute. The pale lavender tinge had left his tongue, replaced by a healthy, warm rose.

Clara sat on the floor with her back resting against the metal supply cabinet, her knees drawn up toward her chest. Her wool cardigan had gray ash from her woodstove smeared across one sleeve, and her fingernails were stained with green watercolor pigment around the cuticles.

"His lungs are clear," Tess said, setting the bell of her Littmann stethoscope on the treatment tray. "The crackles are gone from the dependent lobes."

Clara exhaled a long breath, her shoulders dropping three inches. "When he started coughing at midnight, it sounded like he was drowning on dry carpet."

"He was," Tess said plainly, wiping the rubber earpieces with an alcohol prep pad. "Pulmonary edema is mechanical drowning. The left atrium backs up, hydrostatic pressure exceeds oncotic pressure, and transudate floods the alveoli. The furosemide pulled sixty milliliters of water out through his kidneys in ninety minutes."

"You didn't hesitate," Clara said, looking up at her. Her gray eyes were steady, stripped of social polite pretense by exhaustion. "You knew the exact syringe, the exact drawer, the exact dose."

Tess sat down on the rubber floor mat beside the dog, folding her legs beneath her. "It's my job, Clara."

"No," Clara said quietly. "It's not just your job. You love him. You love him enough to stay clinical so he doesn't die."

Barnaby stretched his front paws outward, his toenails clicking against the steel table leg. He turned his heavy head toward Clara, resting his chin over her knee. Clara reached out, her long, slender fingers curling into the thick ruff of fur behind his ears, scratching the sweet spot where his collar had rubbed the skin bare.

Tess watched Clara's hands. They were capable hands, dusted with graphite and watercolor sediment, gentle in their touch.

"Nate wanted to come," Clara said after a moment, her voice dropping lower. "He had his boots on. But he stood by the back door with his keys in his hand and couldn't walk out to the truck."

"I know that stance," Tess said, looking down at her own thumbs. "When things break past the point where a spoke shave or a wood clamp can fix them, Nate freezes. He doesn't mean to be cold. He just runs out of tools."

"He told me that you two used to divide the world like that," Clara said. "He built the chairs, and you kept the living things breathing."

"It worked until it didn't," Tess murmured.

Clara tilted her head, watching Tess in the harsh fluorescent glare of the treatment lights. "Does it hurt? Having me here with him?"

Tess looked at Barnaby, then looked up directly into Clara's eyes. A cold wind rattled the frosted glass in the clinic door, but inside the treatment room the air was warm, smelling of chlorhexidine and cedar shavings.

"Three months ago, it would have killed me," Tess said honestly. "Tonight, I was grateful it was your truck that pulled into the bay."

Clara didn't answer with words. She simply held Tess's gaze, steady and calm, while Barnaby slept between them under the gentle hum of the oxygen cage.

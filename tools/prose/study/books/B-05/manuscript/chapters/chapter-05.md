The waiting room of the Silvercreek Animal Clinic had four vinyl chairs, a low pine coffee table covered in six-month-old copies of *Western Horseman*, and a cast-iron radiator that hissed whenever the outdoor temperature dropped below freezing. At two o'clock on Tuesday, the room was full.

Mrs. Gable sat by the coat rack holding an elderly calico cat in a plastic laundry basket covered with a bath towel. On the floor by the scale, Pete Henderson, who ran eighty head of black Angus cattle up on the high mesa, sat with his scarred hand resting on the neck of a three-legged blue heeler.

Tess emerged from the treatment bay with a box of disposable syringes. Barnaby was walking beside her, his nails clicking on the linoleum, heading for his favorite rug behind the reception desk.

Pete Henderson looked up from under the brim of his grease-stained Stetson. His pale blue eyes dropped to Barnaby's chest, noting the shaved rectangular patch on his left ribs where the ultrasound probe had left a faint ring of pink skin.

"That your old golden, Tess?" Pete asked, his voice like gravel turning in an iron drum.

"It is, Pete," Tess said, setting the syringes on the counter.

Pete leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees. "Saw him in the back of Nate's truck on Sunday. Thought he looked a little hollow behind the flank. Nate said you two are passing him back and forth like a hot biscuit."

Mrs. Gable lowered her towel, her bird-like face turning toward Tess. "I heard that too, Tessie. My sister saw you at the gas pump on Pine Street handing over his leash. People in town are wondering how that works. Most folks can't share a lawnmower after a divorce, let alone a bird dog."

Tess felt the familiar prickle of heat rise along her neck. In a mountain town of nine hundred residents, privacy was an illusion maintained by mutual agreement, and that agreement dissolved the moment anyone changed their routine.

"We make it work," Tess said, keeping her voice even as she grabbed Barnaby's canvas leash. "Barnaby needs both of us."

Pete gave a low, rumbling chuckle that ended in a dry cough. He spat a shred of chewing tobacco into an empty coffee cup between his boots.

"Well, I'll tell you something, girl," Pete said, nodding toward the dog. "I've been divorced twice and buried three good cattle dogs. Dividing up eighty acres and a tractor is easy. You just call the appraiser, sign the paper, and go drink a beer at the Elk Horn. But sharing custody of an old dog that's running on three cylinders? That takes more guts than marriage ever did."

Tess paused, her hand on the reception counter. "Why's that, Pete?"

"Because when you're married, you can hide behind the housework and the bank notes," Pete said, his gaze fixed on Barnaby's gray muzzle. "When you're keeping an old dog alive together, you can't hide from nothing. Every time you look at that animal, you're looking right at what you couldn't keep whole. And you're doing it on purpose, every seven days, because you love the dog more than you hate your own failure."

Mrs. Gable sniffed, pulling her towel back over her calico. "That's awful bleak, Pete Henderson."

"Ain't bleak, Martha," Pete grunted. "It's just ranching. You don't abandon the stock just because the pasture fence rotted out."

The front door opened with a jingle of brass bells, admitting a blast of freezing wind and Dr. Bell carrying two cardboard boxes of vaccine vials from the delivery truck. He stamped the snow from his clogs and set the boxes on the counter.

"Afternoon, Pete," Marcus said, glancing at the blue heeler. "That dog still chewing his paws?"

"Only when he's thinking about his sins, Doc," Pete said, standing up and hauling the heeler to its feet.

Marcus chuckled, wiping his glasses with a paper wipe. He glanced at Tess, then at Barnaby resting quietly on his mat. Marcus didn't say anything, but his hand rested on Tess's shoulder for a brief, reassuring second before he guided Pete toward exam room one.

"Go on back, Pete," Marcus said. "Let's flush that ear out before it turns into cauliflower."

Tess watched them walk down the hall. Barnaby settled his head on his paws, his tail giving three lazy sweeps across the linoleum, completely peaceful in the dry warmth of the radiator.

She picked up the vaccination log, checked the expiration dates on the rabies tags, and went back to work.

Tess followed Marcus into exam room one to assist with the three-legged heeler. The room was narrow, smelling of isopropyl alcohol and autoclaved instruments. Marcus set the dog on the low rubber mat, kneeling down with his otoscope.

"Hold his muzzle, Tess," Marcus said.

Tess wrapped her forearm under the heeler's jaw, holding the head steady with firm, calm pressure. Pete Henderson leaned his shoulder against the doorframe, watching her hands.

"You've got good hands with stock, Tess," Pete said, rolling his toothpick between his teeth. "Always did. Your daddy had good hands with cattle, too, before he sold the spread up on the pass."

"Thanks, Pete," Tess said, keeping her gaze on the dog's eyes.

Marcus peered through the speculum into the inflamed ear canal. "Nasty yeast buildup, Pete. He's been swimming in the irrigation ditch again."

"He loves that muddy water, Doc," Pete chuckled. "Can't keep him out of it. Just like an old cowboy. He knows it's bad for him, but he does it anyway."

Marcus pulled a syringe of flush solution from the counter, washing the debris from the canal in a steady, warm stream. "Well, you keep him out of the ditch for ten days, Pete, or this ear will swell up like an eggplant. I'm putting him on otomax twice a day."

"Yes, sir," Pete said, taking the tube of ointment. He looked over at Barnaby, who was still visible through the half-open door, resting peacefully in the sunny waiting room. "You take good care of that golden, Tess. A dog like that only comes around once in a man's life."

"I will, Pete," Tess said softly.

After Pete loaded his heeler into the back of his flatbed truck, Tess walked back to the reception area. The waiting room was empty now, the radiator ticking quietly in the afternoon calm.

Marcus stepped up beside her, holding two clean mugs of diner coffee. He handed one to Tess, leaning his hip against the reception desk.

"You handled Pete well," Marcus said.

"Pete speaks his mind," Tess replied, sipping the hot, bitter coffee.

"That he does," Marcus agreed, smiling faintly. "And he's right about one thing. People in this valley notice when someone does something brave. Sharing that dog with Clara and Nate... it shows a lot of heart, Tess. Don't let anyone tell you different."

Tess looked down at Barnaby, who gave a long, contented sigh from his rug, his tail giving three gentle sweeps across the linoleum.

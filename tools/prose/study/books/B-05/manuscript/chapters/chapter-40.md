The late August sun illuminated the garden behind the cottage on Willow Creek in shades of amber and warm honey.

Above the valley, the high granite peaks of the Continental Divide stood sharp against an immaculate cobalt sky, stripped of their winter snowfields except for the permanent glacial cirques along the northern shadows. In the meadows bordering the creek, the wild hay had been cut and baled into golden cylinders, perfuming the afternoon breeze with the sweet scent of dried clover and timothy grass.

Tess knelt in the dark, enriched soil along the southern fence line, wearing heavy leather gardening gloves and a faded canvas work shirt with the sleeves rolled past her elbows.

Beside her sat six wooden nursery flats holding dozens of native alpine seedlings they had collected and nurtured over the summer: mountain columbine, dwarf silky lupine, scarlet gilia, and purple creeping phlox. Each plant sat in its biodegradable peat pot, its roots already pushing white threads through the damp fiber.

Clara knelt three feet away, using a hand trowel to open a trench along the base of the cedar fence. Her sun hat cast a soft shadow across her gray eyes, and her forearms were dusted with peat moss and dry mountain loam.

"Look at this earth," Clara said, turning a trowelful of rich black soil. "Three years of sheep manure and shredded aspen bark. It holds the moisture like a sponge."

"Marcus told me the old rancher who lived here in the sixties kept forty ewes in this paddock," Tess said, picking up a healthy clump of *Lupinus argenteus*. "The nitrogen bank in this dirt will feed perennials for twenty years."

Tess sliced the peat pot with her thumbnail, freeing the root ball. The root system was dense, pale, and dotted with small nitrogen-fixing nodules. She set the lupine into the prepared hole, drawing the dark soil around its crown and pressing down firmly with the heel of her gloved hand.

Clara picked up the heavy copper watering can, tilting its perforated brass rose over the seedling. A gentle, soaking shower of cold creek water settled the soil around the stem, turning the dry loam into dark, glistening mud.

"Silky lupine establishes deep," Clara observed, setting the can down on the grass. "In the wild, it sends a taproot three feet down through granite gravel to reach groundwater. It knows winter is coming, so it invests everything in the foundation before it puts up a single flower spike."

"Practical plant," Tess said, a quiet smile touching her mouth. "Very little decorative nonsense."

"Like somebody else I know," Clara said, leaning over to bump her shoulder against Tess's arm.

Tess laughed, a clear, untroubled sound that rose into the warm afternoon air.

On the cedar fence post directly above the new lupine row, Barnaby's old oiled leather collar hung from an iron square-cut nail. The brass identification tag and the rabies vaccination shield caught the afternoon sun, glinting with a warm, steady golden spark. Nate had cleaned the brass with jeweler's rouge before bringing it over in June, but the leather still carried the dark patina of Barnaby's neck and the faint, enduring scent of woodsmoke and lavender.

Tess looked up at the collar. For months after the euthanasia in May, the sight of his empty tags had caused a sharp, suffocating ache in the center of her ribs.

Now, three months later, the ache had changed. The sharp edges of acute loss had weathered down, replaced by a deep, quiet gratitude that filled her chest like mountain sunlight. He had lived ten full, glorious years. He had died in dignity on his own bed, surrounded by roast chicken, sweet cream, and hands that loved him without reservation.

From the driveway out front came the familiar rumble of a heavy diesel engine, followed by the crunch of gravel tires.

"That'll be Nate," Clara said, setting her trowel on the flat stone border.

Tess pulled off her gardening gloves and stood up, brushing the damp earth from the knees of her canvas trousers.

A moment later, Nate walked through the wooden side gate into the garden. He wore his faded denim work shirt, clean jeans, and heavy leather boots. In his large, calloused arms, he carried a five-foot garden bench built from thick slabs of salvaged Western red cedar.

The bench was a masterwork of joinery. The legs were mortised into heavy curved trestles, pinned with square white oak pegs, and the seat planks were scooped with a hand adze to create a subtle, comfortable saddle. On the center slat of the backrest, Nate had carved a shallow relief of a dog's paw print: three inches wide, with four blunt pads and a triangular heel, identical to the print on Clara's botanical watercolor.

Nate set the bench down on the level flagstones beside the lilac bushes, wiping his brow with the back of his forearm.

"Afternoon, ladies," Nate said, a broad, easy grin breaking across his rugged face.

"Nate," Tess exclaimed, walking over to run her bare hand along the smooth cedar seat. "You finished it."

"Finished it this morning in the high school shop," Nate said, patting the carved backrest. "Four coats of marine spar varnish. It can sit out in the winter snow for ten years and never rot. I wanted you two to have a proper place to sit while you watch these lupines come up next spring."

Clara stepped forward, her eyes bright with emotion. She reached out and wrapped her arms around Nate's neck, pulling him into a warm, fierce hug.

"Thank you, Nate," Clara whispered against his shoulder. "It's magnificent."

Nate returned the hug with a solid, brotherly pat on her back, then stepped back, looking from Clara to Tess.

"I tried the seat out before I loaded it into the truck," Nate said with a chuckle. "Fits two people and a seventy-pound dog, if you squeeze together."

"Barnaby would have claimed forty percent of the middle," Tess smiled, her voice thick with affectionate memory.

"At least forty," Nate agreed. He looked around the sunny garden, his eyes resting on the neat rows of green seedlings and the brass tags glinting on the cedar post. "The garden looks real good, Tess. My grandmother always said a house isn't a home until the perennial bed survives its first winter."

"Come have an iced tea before you drive back to Pine Street," Tess offered, gesturing toward the back porch.

"Can't today," Nate said, checking his wristwatch. "I promised the lumberyard in Buena Vista I'd pick up fifty board feet of kiln-dried walnut before four o'clock. Building a dining table for that new doctor who bought the old ranch up on Chalk Creek."

"Marcus says he's a good surgeon," Tess noted.

"Better be," Nate said. "He's paying cash for the walnut."

Nate walked back to the gate, paused with his hand on the latch, and looked back at them across the blooming garden.

"See you two for Sunday dinner at the diner?" Nate asked.

"Twelve o'clock sharp," Clara confirmed. "Betty promised to make blackberry pie."

"I'll be there," Nate nodded. He gave them a casual wave, clicked the iron gate latch shut, and walked down the driveway to his truck.

The diesel engine fired with its deep rumble, and a moment later the sound faded as he turned onto the county highway heading south.

The garden returned to its afternoon quiet. A yellow swallowtail butterfly fluttered over the flagstones, landing on the purple bloom of a creeping phlox seedling before taking flight toward the willow trees along the creek.

Clara walked over to the cedar bench, sitting down on the smooth wood and resting her back against the carved paw print. She patted the seat beside her.

"Come sit with me, Tess," Clara said softly.

Tess walked across the lawn and sat down beside Clara. The cedar wood was warm from the sun, smelling of resin and linseed oil.

Tess laid her arm across the back of the bench, her fingers resting lightly on Clara's shoulder. Clara leaned into the touch, resting her head against Tess's collarbone, her hand finding Tess's on the warm wooden seat.

They sat together in the quiet mountain light, watching the shadow of the Continental Divide begin its long, slow march across the valley floor.

"We have thirty more seedlings to plant before sunset," Tess murmured, looking at the flats by the fence.

Clara smiled, lifting Tess's fingers to her lips and kissing them softly.

"We have all afternoon," Clara said.

Tess stood up, picked up her hand trowel from the grass, and knelt beside the next empty space in the dark earth.

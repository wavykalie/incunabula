The trail to the Continental Divide ridge began at nine thousand feet and climbed through dense stands of blue spruce and bristlecone pine before breaking out onto the alpine tundra at eleven thousand five hundred feet.

It was mid-July.

Tess, Clara, and Nate walked in single file along the narrow granite switchbacks, their boots crunching on the scree. Nate walked in the lead, carrying an eight-inch wooden urn carved from fragrant western red cedar, its lid fitted with a tight wooden mortise.

The air at the ridge top was thin, cold, and smelled of wild mountain thyme, lichens, and ancient granite.

They reached the crest where the Divide dropped off into the vast, emerald expanse of the Pacific basin to the west and the rolling high plains to the east. The wind swept across the col with exhilarating, clean power, whipping their hair and jackets.

Nate stopped at the edge of the granite outcrop overlooking the alpine cirque below. He knelt down, set the cedar urn on a flat slab of gray rock, and loosened the wooden lid.

He looked back at Tess and Clara.

"You two should do it," Nate said, stepping back into the wind.

Tess walked forward, taking Clara's hand in hers. Together, they knelt by the cedar box.

Inside, the ashes were pale gray, coarse with tiny fragments of bone, smelling faintly of clean mineral stone and cedar shavings.

Tess took the first handful. The ash was cool against her skin. She stepped to the rim of the precipice and opened her palm to the mountain gale.

The wind caught the pale dust, carrying it in a wide, shimmering arc over the green valley below, scattering Barnaby's physical remains across the high meadows, the snowbanks, and the cold mountain streams he had loved with all his retriever heart.

Clara took the second handful, releasing it with a quiet smile into the summer sky.

Nate took the last handful, casting it far out into the updraft with a strong, clean sweep of his arm.

When the urn was empty, they stood together on the spine of the continent in the bright, blinding mountain sunlight, watching the last traces of gray dust dissolve into the summer wind.

Nate turned, wrapped both arms around Tess and Clara in a fierce, silent embrace, then picked up his walking stick and began the descent down the ridge, leaving them alone at the top of the world.

On the Continental Divide, the summer wind whipped across the granite scree with exhilarating, mountain power.

The pale gray ashes of Barnaby's physical form drifted out over the vast, green valley below, glittering like diamonds in the high-altitude sunlight as the updraft lifted them toward the snowfields and the sky.

Tess stood with Clara's hand locked in hers, feeling the wind dry the tears on her cheeks.

Beside them, Nate looked out over the endless mountain ranges stretching toward the horizon, his face serene, his shoulders square and peaceful.

"He's running those high ridges now," Nate said, smiling through his tears. "Chasing ptarmigan and drinking snowmelt."

"Always," Clara whispered.

Nate turned, pulling both women into a warm, fierce hug that held no lingering grief or regret—only the profound, enduring bond of three people who had faced death together and chosen love.

He picked up his walking stick and began the hike down the granite trail, leaving Tess and Clara standing together at the top of the continent, looking out at the endless mountain sky.

They reached the crest of the Continental Divide at ten in the morning, where the timberline gave way to wind-sculpted granite and alpine tundra.

The June sun was brilliant and warm, but the breeze off the snowfields was crisp, carrying the scent of crushed juniper and ancient stone. Below them, the entire valley of Silvercreek was laid out like a green patchwork quilt, dotted with sparkling irrigation ditches and miniature tin roofs.

Nate carried the small square box of Western red cedar he had built in his woodshop, finished with hand-rubbed Danish oil and fitted with brass hinges.

He set the box on a flat granite boulder surrounded by blooming alpine forget-me-nots.

Tess and Clara stood on either side of him, their hands linked together.

Nate lifted the cedar lid. Inside rested the fine, pale gray cremains of their dog, clean and light as mountain dust.

"You want to do it, Tess?" Nate asked quietly.

Tess stepped forward. She took a handful of the pale cedar ashes. They felt cool, soft, like dry river silt between her fingers.

She turned into the west wind, opening her hand and casting the ashes out over the precipice.

The wind caught the ash immediately, carrying the pale cloud in a wide, sweeping arc over the canyon, dispersing it into the vast blue sky above the peaks where Barnaby had run as a young dog.

Clara took a handful next, scattering the dust with a quiet prayer of thanksgiving. Then Nate stepped up to the edge, casting the remaining ashes into the mountain breeze with a solemn, reverent nod.

When the cedar box was empty, Nate closed the lid with a quiet click.

He turned to Tess and Clara. He pulled them both into a tight, fierce embrace, his broad arms wrapping around their shoulders on the high granite ridge.

"I love you both," Nate said, his voice carrying clearly on the wind.

He stepped back, smiled through his tears, and started down the trail ahead of them, leaving them standing together in the brilliant summer light.

They watched Nate's figure grow smaller as he hiked down the switchbacks toward the trailhead parking lot.

The mountain wind whipped across the granite pass, clean, cold, and smelling of ancient snow. Around their boots, the tiny blue petals of alpine forget-me-nots trembled in the gale, anchored firmly into the scree by deep taproots.

Clara turned to Tess, her gray eyes clear and peaceful in the high elevation light.

"Look at the valley, Tess," Clara said, pointing down to where the green ribbon of Willow Creek cut through the hay meadows.

Tess looked. The small town of Silvercreek sat nestled in the mountain shadow, its tin roofs glinting like silver coins in the June sun.

"It looks so small from up here," Tess said.

"It is small," Clara smiled softly, taking Tess's hand and lacing their fingers together. "Small enough to hold everything we need."

They stood on the high ridge for another half hour, holding each other against the cold mountain wind, before turning to follow Nate's trail back down through the pine forests to their home.

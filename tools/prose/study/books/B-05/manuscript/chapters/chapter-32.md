The garden behind Clara's cottage on Willow Creek was enclosed by an old split-rail cedar fence covered in climbing wild hops.

At two o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon in early June, Tess was on her knees in the dark, composted soil, helping Clara transplant seedlings of mountain columbine into the border bed.

Barnaby was lying on the gravel path ten feet away, watching a bumblebee hover over the chive blossoms.

Suddenly, without a bark or a whimper, Barnaby stood up. His front legs locked straight. His head arched backward in a sharp, involuntary extensor spasm, his eyes rolling back until only the whites showed beneath his golden eyelids.

His back legs crumpled beneath him. He hit the gravel path on his left flank with a dull, sickening thud.

"Barnaby!" Clara cried, dropping her trowel.

Tess was on him in two strides.

She didn't freeze; the clinical reflex took over with absolute, cold efficiency. She fell to her knees beside his chest, her hands instantly finding his femoral artery and his chest wall.

No pulse. The artery under her fingertips was completely flat, a hollow thread with zero hydrostatic pressure. Under his ribs, the cardiac apex was silent—no gallop, no systolic rush, just a faint, disorganized flutter like a moth trapped inside a paper envelope.

"Cardiogenic syncope," Tess said, her voice sharp and rapid. "Ventricular arrhythmia. He's thrown a run of VT."

"What do we do?" Clara asked, kneeling beside his head, her face white as bone, her hands trembling over his ears.

"Keep his airway clear," Tess ordered, pulling Barnaby's tongue to the side so he wouldn't aspirate. "Don't compress his chest. The rhythm has to reset itself."

Tess counted the seconds in her head. One one-thousand. Two one-thousand. Three one-thousand.

At eight seconds, Barnaby took a deep, gasping intake of air—a loud, sucking inspiratory snort that shuddered through his entire frame.

Under Tess's fingers, the femoral pulse suddenly kicked back into life: thud-thud-thud. Fast, bounding, irregular, but present. The pupils constricted back to normal diameter. His tongue turned from dark plum back to pale rose.

Barnaby blinked. He lifted his head from the gravel, looked around at the sunny garden with mild, bewildered surprise, and licked the dirt from Tess's knuckle.

Tess let out a long, ragged sob that tore through her throat, burying her face against the dog's warm shoulder.

Clara wrapped both arms around Tess and the dog, holding them both tight against her chest while the tears soaked into Barnaby's fur.

"He came back," Clara whispered, her voice shaking violently. "He came back, Tess."

"He came back," Tess choked out, wiping her eyes with her muddy sleeve. She lifted her head, looking at the dog, then at Clara.

The clinical truth was undeniable. In Stage C mitral valve disease, cardiogenic syncope was the final marker. It meant the myocardial tissue was ischemic, scarred by chronic strain, throwing life-threatening arrhythmias that would eventually fail to convert.

The clock had not just run down; it was in its final minutes.

After Barnaby recovered from the syncope episode, Tess and Clara sat on the garden bench beside the cottage, keeping him between them on the soft grass.

Tess had her fingers resting lightly on his femoral pulse, counting the rate every five minutes: one hundred and ten, dropping to ninety-six, settling at eighty-eight. The rhythm had stabilized back into normal sinus rhythm, but the pause had lasted ten full seconds.

"What causes it, Tess?" Clara asked, her voice still trembling with adrenaline shock.

"Myocardial ischemia," Tess answered, her voice clinical and precise, though her hands were cold. "The left ventricle has become so thickened from chronic pressure overload that the coronary capillary bed can't perfuse the deep muscle fibers. When his heart rate jumps suddenly—even from excitement over a bee—the myocardium runs out of oxygen, and an ectopic focus fires, triggering ventricular tachycardia. The ventricles flutter without pumping blood, blood pressure drops to zero, and the brain faints."

Clara leaned her forehead against Barnaby's neck, her hands buried in his golden coat.

"Will it happen again?" Clara whispered.

"Yes," Tess said, not hiding the truth. "The next arrhythmia could be longer. Or it won't convert back. That's why we have to watch him constantly. When an arrhythmia takes them, it's painless—they just go dark in seconds. But we need to make sure he isn't suffering from chronic pulmonary distress in between."

Clara nodded slowly, wiping a tear from her cheek. "I understand. We stay right beside him."

Barnaby looked up at both of them, gave a soft thump of his tail, and licked Clara's knee, completely unconcerned by the storm inside his chest.

Tess sat on the clinic exam room stool, reviewing Barnaby's digital electrocardiogram tracing on the monitor screen.

Marcus walked in carrying two cardboard cups of takeout coffee from the diner. He set one cup on the counter beside the ultrasound probe and looked over Tess's shoulder at the scrolling green waveform.

"Ventricular premature complexes?" Marcus asked, pointing a blunt finger at a wide, bizarre QRS complex interrupting the rhythm every fourth beat.

"Multifocal," Tess said, her voice tight and flat. "He collapsed in the garden this morning. Total syncope. He was sniffing a dandelion clump and his legs just folded beneath him. Lost consciousness for about eight seconds."

Marcus took a sip of his coffee. "Did he convulse?"

"No tonic-clonic activity. No urination or salivation. Just pure cerebral hypoxia," Tess said, pointing to the tracing. "The ventricular tachycardia dropped his cardiac output to near zero. When the rhythm converted back to sinus, he blinked, stood up, and looked around like he'd dropped his keys."

Marcus sighed, the sound heavy in the quiet exam room. "Stage D, Tess. Refractory end-stage heart failure. The myocardium is stretched paper-thin. When the electrical pathways start fraying like this, the drugs have reached their ceiling."

Tess stared at the monitor. The green line traced its jagged path: P-wave, PR interval, wide aberrant spike, compensatory pause.

"How long, Marcus?" she asked quietly.

Marcus rested his hand on the back of her wooden stool. "A week. Two weeks. Maybe a month if the mexiletine controls the arrhythmia. But we're playing for days now, Tess. You know the score."

Tess closed her eyes, breathing in the familiar scent of antiseptic and cold steel. "I know."

"Go home to Clara and that dog," Marcus said gently. "I'll cover the farm calls tomorrow."

Tess stepped outside into the clinic alley behind the recovery ward to catch her breath.

The afternoon air was thin and dry, smelling of sagebrush and hot asphalt. Above the corrugated metal roof of the clinic, two red-tailed hawks circled in the thermal updrafts rising from the valley floor.

She leaned her back against the cinderblock wall, closing her eyes.

She had managed hundreds of canine cardiac patients in her decade of clinical practice. She had explained ventricular arrhythmias to cattle ranchers, schoolteachers, and retired miners. She had drawn the diagrams on whiteboards: the sinoatrial node, the Purkinje fibers, the ectopic foci firing out of order when the heart muscle stretches past its limit.

She had always been the doctor with the steady hands and the calm, reassuring voice.

Now, standing in the alley with her stethoscope cold against her collarbone, her hands were shaking. She pressed her palms flat against the rough concrete to steady them.

"Tess?"

Marcus stood at the back door of the pharmacy, holding an amber bottle of mexiletine.

"Here's the antiarrhythmic," Marcus said gently, holding the vial toward her. "Fifty milligrams twice daily. Let's get it into him before evening."

Tess opened her eyes, took a deep breath of the mountain air, and reached for the bottle. "Thank you, Marcus."

The porch light on Pine Street was an amber globe set in black iron, throwing a dull yellow circle across the frost-heaved flagstones. Tess parked her Subaru by the stacked cordwood. The smell of cedar sawdust and woodsmoke hung thick in the cold air, coming off the workshop behind the garage where Nate spent his evenings turning bowls on his lathe.

Barnaby jumped down from the tailgate with a soft grunt. His front paws hit the gravel, and his hocks gave a tiny, momentary sag before he caught himself. Six months ago, Tess wouldn't have noticed that hesitation. Now, with the sound of the mitral jet still rattling behind her ears, every hitch in his stride felt like a siren.

"Easy, bear," she said, holding his webbed leash close to the collar.

The storm door creaked open before they reached the third step.

Clara stood in the doorway. She was wearing an oversized wool cardigan the color of unbleached flax, sleeves pushed up past her forearms to reveal smudges of olive watercolor on her wrists. Her dark hair was pinned up with an old wooden paintbrush, loose strands curling against her temples. Behind her, the kitchen smelled of toasted sourdough, beeswax, and rosemary.

"Hey, boy," Clara said. Her voice was quiet, warm, dropping into the low register she used whenever the dog crossed the threshold. She knelt down on the pine floorboards without hesitation, letting Barnaby press his broad skull into the hollow of her collarbone. She buried both hands in his ruff, scratching behind his ears with steady, familiar pressure.

Tess stood on the welcome mat, her canvas tote bag slung over one shoulder. The bag held Barnaby's joint supplements, his rubber grooming brush, and a three-ring binder with his vaccine records.

"Nate's still in the shop," Clara said, looking up. Her gray eyes were clear and direct, taking in Tess's work scrubs, the dark circles under her eyes, the windblown hair sticking to her lip. "He wanted to finish sanding the Douglas fir table before the frost set into the wood."

"That's fine," Tess said. "I don't need to see Nate. I just need to leave the bag."

Clara stood up smoothly, resting one hand on Barnaby's flank. Her fingers lingered over his ribs. Tess watched the way Clara's palm moved, noting how her thumb rested right where the apex beat pulsed against the chest wall.

"He's breathing faster," Clara said.

Tess stiffened. She hadn't said a word. She hadn't texted. "What?"

"Since Sunday before last," Clara said, her tone level and unhurried. She stepped aside, gesturing toward the kitchen table. "When he was sleeping by the radiator, I counted his breaths. Usually he's around twelve or fourteen when he's deep asleep. Tuesday he was twenty-two. Friday night he was twenty-eight, and his ribs had a little hitch at the top of the inhalation."

She walked over to the dry-erase board mounted beside the pantry. In neat, precise script—the careful, deliberate lettering of someone who spent eight hours a day labeling botanical genus and species—Clara had drawn a four-column grid.

Date. Sleeping Respiratory Rate. Appetite. Energy.

Tess stared at the board. The numbers were recorded in dark blue ink. October 12: 16 bpm. October 15: 22 bpm. October 18: 26 bpm. In the margin, in pencil, Clara had written: *Shifts weight off left shoulder after drinking water. Licks paws at 3 AM.*

"You kept a chart," Tess said. The words came out sharper than she intended.

Clara turned around, drying her hands on a striped linen towel. She didn't look defensive. She looked at Tess with a calm, searching patience that made Tess's chest ache with an old, complicated shame.

"I didn't know if it meant anything," Clara said. "I'm not a vet tech. But my mother had congestive failure when I was twenty. She used to breathe with that same dry flutter in her throat before her ankles swelled. I just wanted to make sure I wasn't imagining it before I called you."

Tess felt the floor beneath her boots suddenly lose its solid footing. For six months, she had told herself that Pine Street was Nate's domain, a place of casual masculine neglect where dog bowls went unwashed and toenails grew too long until she stepped in to fix them. She had guarded Barnaby's care like a fortress, convinced that she alone stood between the dog and slow decline.

And here was Clara Evans, standing in wool slippers, tracking respiratory rates on a dry-erase board with the observational precision of a field biologist.

"He has a murmur," Tess said. Her voice dropped low. "A grade five. We auscultated him an hour ago."

Clara's hand went still against the linen towel. "The valve?"

"Mitral valve disease. The left leaflet is collapsing. Blood is washing back into the left atrium." Tess set the heavy canvas tote on the edge of the kitchen bench. "Marcus is running an echo tomorrow morning at seven-thirty. We'll measure the chamber enlargement and start him on Vetmedin."

Clara didn't offer a canned expression of pity. She didn't say *I'm so sorry* or *he's going to be fine.* She simply walked over to Barnaby, who had settled onto his sheepskin rug beside the woodbox, and laid her palm flat against his golden side.

"Does it hurt him?" Clara asked.

"No," Tess said. "Not yet. It just makes him tired. His heart has to beat twice as hard to move the same amount of oxygenated blood. If the pressure builds up in the pulmonary veins, fluid leaks into the lungs. That's when the coughing starts."

"The hitch in his breathing," Clara murmured. "That's the fluid starting?"

"No. That's the atrium pressing against his trachea. The fluid is what happens when the heart can't clear the return." Tess pulled her fleece collar tighter around her neck. "I'll pick him up at seven tomorrow for the clinic."

"I can drive him over with you," Clara offered gently.

"You don't have to do that," Tess replied quickly. "It's clinic business. You're working on the alpine flora prints for the Denver show."

"The prints can wait," Clara said. "Barnaby doesn't like the cold metal of the ultrasound table. He does better when someone holds his ears."

The kitchen door from the mudroom clicked open with a burst of pine-scented air. Nate walked in, stamping his heavy work boots on the coir mat. He was covered in cedar shavings, curls of blond wood clinging to his flannel shirt and the cuffs of his jeans. His face was wind-reddened, his brown hair dusty with sawdust.

"Hey," Nate said, stopping short when he saw Tess standing by the bench. His expression froze in that familiar, clumsy caution he had worn ever since the mediation lawyer stamped the divorce decree. "Tess. Everything okay? Tailgate give you trouble again?"

"Tailgate's fine," Tess said.

Nate looked from Tess to Clara, then down at Barnaby. The dog thumped his tail twice, but didn't stand up. Nate rubbed the back of his neck, his knuckles thick and calloused from the planer.

"He looks tired," Nate said. "I stacked three cords of Douglas fir out back. Thought I'd take him down to the beaver ponds tomorrow afternoon to stretch his legs."

"No ponds," Tess said.

Nate frowned, shifting his weight. "He loves the pond, Tess. It's just mud."

"He has heart failure, Nate," Tess said, the words landing like lead shot on the pine table. "His mitral valve is failing. If you let him run through freezing marsh water, his lungs will fill up with fluid before you get him back to the truck."

Nate stood motionless. A shaving of cedar fluttered from his sleeve to the floor. His mouth opened slightly, then closed. He looked at Barnaby, then at Clara, his face washing pale beneath the outdoor sunburn.

"Heart failure?" Nate whispered. "He was chasing chipmunks last month."

"Dogs compensate until they can't," Tess said.

Clara moved quietly across the kitchen, poured hot water from the kettle into a heavy ceramic mug, and handed it to Nate. Nate took it with both hands, staring into the dark herbal steam without drinking.

"Tomorrow morning," Clara said to Nate, her voice steady and grounding. "Seven-thirty at the clinic. We're going with Tess for the ultrasound."

Nate nodded once, his eyes fixed on the dog's graying muzzle. "Yeah. Okay. Seven-thirty."

Tess zipped her jacket, stepped back through the storm door into the freezing mountain dark, and closed the latch behind her.

Nate came out onto the porch carrying Barnaby's canvas travel bag, filled with clean chew toys and his favorite rubber kong.

"He likes that rope bone you bought him in Salida," Nate said, setting the bag beside the Subaru's rear wheel. "He carried it around the shop all Saturday morning while I was jointing table aprons."

Tess took the bag, placing it on the passenger floorboard. "Did you give him the joint supplements with his breakfast?"

"Two glucosamine chews with his kibble," Nate confirmed. He rested his hands in his back pockets, looking out toward the snow-capped ridge of Mount Antero. "He seemed tired after our walk along the creek, Tess. He lay by the woodstove until three in the afternoon without moving."

Tess nodded, her hand resting on the car door. "That's the valve, Nate. He's working harder to pump the same volume of blood. Rest is good for him."

Nate looked at Barnaby in the back seat, who had his gray chin resting on the fleece armrest, watching them through the glass.

"Take good care of him this week," Nate said softly.

"I will," Tess answered, pulling out onto the road.

The Mountain View Cafe on Monday morning was loud with the clatter of heavy diner mugs and the hiss of the flat-top grill where short-order cook Earl was frying corned beef hash.

Tess sat at the counter beside Dr. Marcus Bell. Marcus was on his second mug of black diner coffee, dunking a glazed cake donut into the dark liquid with practiced efficiency.

"The mare on Willow Creek made it through the night," Marcus said, chewing his donut. "Bowel sounds are returning in all four quadrants. Her owner thought she was a goner, but a good gallon of mineral oil and three hours of walking will cure most stubborn horse colic."

Tess pushed her scrambled eggs around her paper plate with a plastic fork. "That's good, Marcus."

Marcus stopped with his donut halfway to his mouth. He set it down on his paper napkin, pushed his wire bifocals up the bridge of his nose, and turned his weathered, lined face toward her.

"You've been staring at that fork for ten minutes, Tess," Marcus said, his gravelly voice dropping into that quiet, fatherly cadence he used when an intern was about to drop an anesthesia patient. "What's going on?"

Tess looked at the grease-spotted stainless steel backsplash behind the counter. "Nothing. Just tired. Winter call schedule."

"Don't lie to an old country vet, kiddo," Marcus said. "I've been reading body language since before you were born. You look like a horse that's caught between two fences and doesn't know which way the lightning's going to jump."

Tess exhaled slowly through her nose. "People in town are talking, Marcus. Pete Henderson. Martha Gable. Joe Miller down at the road department."

Marcus snorted, picking up his coffee mug. "Pete Henderson hasn't had an original thought since the Carter administration. What are they saying?"

"They think the custody arrangement is weird," Tess said, keeping her voice low so Earl wouldn't hear over the grease exhaust. "They think Clara and I shouldn't be spending time together. They think it's unnatural for an ex-wife and a new partner to share a dog."

Marcus took a long, slow sip of his coffee. He set the mug down with deliberate precision.

"Let me tell you something about this town, Tess," Marcus said, looking right into her eyes. "This town loves an easy tragedy. They love a clean divorce where everyone hates each other and goes to separate churches. It makes sense to them. It fits into the Sunday sermon."

He reached over and tapped the back of her hand with his rough, scarred index finger.

"Caring for a dying animal is the most honest, brutal work there is," Marcus said. "It strips away every polite lie you tell yourself to get through the day. It exposes every raw nerve you've got. When two people stand in that fire together, they don't look at social conventions. They look at who's holding the rope."

Tess felt the sudden, hot prickle of tears behind her eyelids, but she blinked them back, staring fiercely at her coffee cup.

"Marcus," she whispered.

"I'm not asking what's between you two, Tess," Marcus said softly. "That's your business. But don't you dare let Pete Henderson or anyone else in this valley make you feel ashamed for finding comfort while you walk an old friend to the gate. Life is too short, and winter in these mountains is too damn long."

He picked up his donut, finished it in two bites, and tossed three dollar bills onto the laminate counter.

"Come on," Marcus said, sliding off his stool. "We've got a golden retriever due for a thoracic check at eight-thirty, and his technician needs to be ready."

Tess walked out of the Mountain View Cafe with Marcus into the crisp morning air. The sun was brilliant, reflecting off the fresh snowbanks that lined Highway 9 like polished marble.

Marcus stopped by his rusted Ford pickup, leaning his heavy forearms against the tailgate.

"You've got surgery prep at nine, Tess," Marcus said, looking down the valley toward the peaks. "The Labrador with the cruciate tear."

"Already set up, Marcus," Tess said. "Autoclaved the orthopedic pack yesterday. Pre-meds are drawn."

Marcus nodded slowly, pulling a pack of peppermint lifesavers from his coat pocket and offering one to her. Tess took it, the cool mint sharp on her tongue.

"You're a hell of a technician, Tess," Marcus said, his gravelly voice filled with quiet affection. "Your dad would be proud of the way you run this hospital."

"Thanks, Marcus," Tess said softly.

"And don't you worry about Nate," Marcus added, his eyes meeting hers behind his wire bifocals. "Nate's a good boy. He knows what's real. He knows when a season has run its course."

Marcus climbed into his truck, started the engine, and waved through the window as he pulled out onto the highway.

Tess stood on the gravel shoulder for a moment, watching the exhaust plumes rise into the pale blue Colorado sky, feeling the weight of the town's expectations dissolve into the crisp, clean mountain air.

Marcus finished his mug of black coffee and signaled to Betty for the check.

"I've watched three generations of mountain families sit in those booths, Tess," Marcus said, gesturing with his thumb toward the red vinyl banquettes along the front windows. "They worry about the frost, they worry about the beef prices, and they worry about what their neighbors are doing with their ex-husbands."

"Let them worry," Tess said, folding her paper napkin into a tight triangle.

"That's my girl," Marcus said with a dry grin. He stood up, tossing a ten-dollar bill onto the table. "Just remember one thing, Tess."

"What's that?"

"Clara's an artist," Marcus said, looking down at her with a quiet, penetrating gaze. "Artists see things the rest of us step right past. Don't go hiding your heart behind your diagnostic charts. She'll see right through the paper."

Tess looked up at the old veterinarian, her breath catching in her throat.

Marcus gave her shoulder a brief, affectionate squeeze with his heavy hand and walked toward the register, his work boots thumping solidly against the worn linoleum.

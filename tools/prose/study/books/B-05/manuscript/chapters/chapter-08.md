The red digital numerals on the bedside alarm read 3:14 AM.

Tess woke not to a sound, but to the absence of one. Her second-floor apartment was completely silent. The baseboard heaters weren't running; the refrigerator compressor was off.

From the foot of the bed, where Barnaby's orthopedic mattress lay against the dresser, came the faint, rhythmic movement of air.

Tess didn't turn on the lamp. The harsh electric glare would startle him, driving his heart rate up and ruining the resting count. Instead, she slid her legs out from under the down comforter, her bare feet meeting the cold fir floorboards with practiced silence. She reached for the small red-lens penlight she kept on her nightstand—the one she used for checking pupillary light reflexes during emergency night shifts.

She knelt beside his bed, shielding the red beam with the crook of her thumb until only a faint ruby crescent illuminated his chest.

Barnaby was lying on his right side, his four legs stretched out straight, his head resting on the raised bolster. He was deep asleep, his paws giving an occasional, delicate twitch as he chased dream pheasants through imaginary stubble.

Tess focused on the point just behind his shoulder blade where the hair lay thin over his tenth rib.

Up. Down. One.
Up. Down. Two.
Up. Down. Three.

She clicked the timer on her wristwatch. Sixty seconds.

She kept her gaze locked on the rise and fall of the fur, breathing in synchrony with him to keep her own pulse steady. The room smelled of cedar shavings from his collar and the faint, sweet malt scent of his paws.

Nineteen breaths.

Tess let out a long, slow breath of her own. Nineteen was safe. Anything under twenty-five at rest meant the pimobendan was maintaining forward stroke volume and the left atrial pressure hadn't crossed the hydrostatic threshold into overt pulmonary edema.

She clicked off the penlight and sat back on her heels in the dark.

The apartment felt enormous around her, hollowed out by the autumn cold. When she and Nate had separated in April, she had told herself that having her own space would be a relief: no woodchips tracked across the bathroom rug, no half-finished fly-fishing rods drying on the dining table, no heavy silence lingering between two people who had run out of things to say.

For six months, she had called the silence independence.

Sitting on the floor at three in the morning, listening to the frail, mechanical pump of an eleven-year-old dog's failing heart, she knew the truth. It wasn't independence. It was just an empty room where nobody saw her hands shake when she cut a pill in half.

Barnaby shifted, stretching his neck, and exhaled with a soft, fluttering whistle through his nostrils. His front paw flopped over and landed across Tess's bare ankle, heavy and warm.

Tess leaned down, kissed the soft velvet behind his ear, and stayed on the floor until the clock turned four.

Tess remained on her knees on the cold fir floorboards, watching Barnaby breathe in the red penlight glow.

Nineteen breaths a minute. Regular, quiet, unforced.

She clicked off the penlight, slipping it back into the pocket of her flannel pajama bottoms. The darkness in the bedroom was thick, velvety, broken only by the dim blue streetlamp filtering through the lace curtain across the street.

She stood up slowly, her knees cracking in the silence.

She walked out into the kitchen, her bare feet making no sound on the worn floorboards. She filled the copper kettle with tap water, set it on the small gas burner, and turned the valve until the blue ring of flame flared into life with a soft *whoosh*.

She leaned against the counter, waiting for the water to heat.

On the refrigerator door, held by a small magnetic wooden clip Nate had carved years ago, was a photograph from the summer of 2018. It showed Tess and Nate standing on the summit of Mount Democrat, their windbreakers dusted with sleet, their faces sunburned and grinning. Between them sat Barnaby at three years old, his tongue hanging out, a red bandana tied around his neck, looking like the happiest creature on earth.

Tess looked at Nate's face in the faded photograph.

He had looked so young then, his eyes unclouded by the quiet, exhausting years of drifting apart. They had thought that climbing fourteen-thousand-foot peaks together was the same as building a life. They hadn't realized that the descent was where people got lost—in the ordinary valleys of unsaid words, missed connections, and the slow accumulation of emotional fatigue.

The kettle began to whistle, a low, rising hum in the dark kitchen.

Tess lifted it from the flame before the whistle could wake the dog. She poured the boiling water into a ceramic mug over a slice of dried lemon and a teaspoon of raw clover honey.

She carried the steaming mug into the living room, sitting down on the braided rug beside Barnaby's orthopedic mattress. The dog smelled of cedar and clean fur, his body a furnace of living warmth in the freezing room.

He shifted, turning his head, and rested his chin across the top of her bare foot.

Tess took a sip of the hot lemon water, feeling the steam warm her face, and sat watching the snow begin to fall against the windowpane until the clock turned five.

At four in the morning, the wind died entirely.

The sudden silence woke Tess from a light doze on the living room sofa. The electric baseboard heater hummed faintly against the outer wall, clicking every twenty seconds as its thermostat cycled.

Barnaby hadn't moved from the oval braided rug. His golden flank rose and fell in a steady, unbroken cadence: twenty-four breaths a minute. Good. Well below the threshold for pulmonary congestion. The extra dose of enalapril was keeping the systemic vascular resistance down, letting the damaged left ventricle empty without excessive back-pressure.

Tess sat up, running both hands over her face. Her skin felt dry from the woodsmoke and the mountain elevation.

She stood and padded barefoot into the small kitchen. On the counter sat the ceramic salt pig Nate had turned on his lathe three winters ago from a block of spalted maple. The grain ran in dark, stormy lines through the pale heartwood, polished with beeswax until it felt like soapstone under the fingers.

She filled an enamel kettle with cold tap water and set it on the front burner. When she struck a kitchen match, the sulfur smell flare and died, replaced by the blue hiss of propane.

Through the window above the sink, the mountain sky was black and hard, dusted with winter constellations: Orion's belt tilted sharply over the ridge of Mount Princeton, the Pleiades clustered like diamond grit above the frozen gulch.

In the bedroom down the hall, the double bed sat empty, made neatly with the blue wool army blanket she had bought at the army surplus store after the divorce settlement was signed.

For two years, she had told herself that having the entire flat to herself was freedom: no sawdust tracked across the floorboards, no pile of woodworking journals on the toilet tank, no negotiations over whose weekend it was to cook supper.

Barnaby gave a soft, snuffling grunt from the rug, his paws twitching in the rhythm of a phantom chase.

Tess took her mug of hot peppermint tea into the living room and sat down on the floor beside him. She slid one bare foot under his flank, resting her toes against the warm, furred curve of his belly where the heart beat steadily against the ribs: lub-dub, lub-dub, soft and regular.

Barnaby blinked his dark eyes open, lifted his chin half an inch to lick the knuckle of her thumb, and closed his eyes again.

Tess sipped her tea, listening to the clock on the bookshelf tick through the quiet dark until the first gray light touched the frost on the window panes.

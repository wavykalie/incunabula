The reservoir was frozen along the shoreline, a jagged fringe of milky ice sealing the mudflats where the willows grew thick and gray. Above the timberline, the Tenmile Range stood sharp and white against a pale winter sky, the snow plumes blowing off the ridge tops like silver banners.

Tess and Clara walked side by side along the flat dirt access road, matching their strides to Barnaby's slow, measured trot.

The dog wore his red fleece chest protector, the fabric zipped snug over his ribs to keep the mountain chill from shocking his peripheral vasculature. He moved with a steady, rocking gait, his nose down, sniffing the frozen rabbit tracks in the snowbank. He stopped every forty yards to lift his leg or inspect a clump of dried brome grass, his breath puffing in small white clouds against the cold.

"His color is good today," Clara said, her breath fogging in the crisp air. She was wearing a heavy wool pea coat and a knitted mustard-colored scarf wrapped twice around her neck. "Gums were nice and pink when I gave him his broth this morning."

"Pimobendan is doing what it's supposed to," Tess replied, her hands buried deep in the pockets of her down parka. "It keeps the stroke volume up without demanding more myocardial oxygen. As long as the rhythm stays regular, he feels decent."

They reached the wooden bench overlooking the spillway, where the water tumbled over concrete baffles in a dark, roaring torrent that hadn't yet frozen. Clara stopped, resting one mittened hand on the weathered cedar railing.

"Nate went down to Denver this morning," Clara said in a low voice. "School district board meeting. He won't be back until tomorrow night."

Tess looked at the dark water churning below the dam. "Nate hates Denver. Too much concrete. Too many traffic lights."

"He went because he wanted to get out of the house," Clara said.

Tess glanced at her. "Did you two have a fight?"

"No," Clara said. A faint, sad smile touched the corner of her lips. "Nate doesn't fight, Tess. You know that better than anyone. Nate chops wood. He sharpens his chisels until they can shave the hair off his forearm. He sweeps the sawdust off the workshop floor three times in an evening."

She looked out across the gray ice of the reservoir, her mittened hands tightening on the cedar rail.

"Sometimes I walk through that house on Pine Street," Clara continued, her voice low and reflective, "and I feel like a visitor who stayed past checkout time. The coat hooks by the back door still have your old garden trowel hanging on the third peg. The pantry has six jars of peach preserves your mother canned in 2023. Nate never moved them. He just leaves them there, like relics from a church that closed down."

Tess felt a strange, cold pull in the center of her chest.

"I left those preserves because I didn't have room in the apartment pantry," Tess said.

"It's not about the jars, Tess," Clara said, turning to look at her directly. The winter wind caught a loose strand of dark hair, whipping it across Clara's cheek. "Nate is a good man. He's kind, he's decent, he works hard. But when he looks at me, he's looking for someone to fill the quiet spaces you left behind. And when I look at him, I'm looking for a safe harbor where nobody is drinking themselves to death in the kitchen."

The admission was raw, stripped of pretense.

Tess knew about Clara's mother—the quiet, exhausting years Clara had spent in Seattle managing an alcoholic parent's bankruptcies, hospitalizations, and relapses before fleeing to the Colorado high country for clean air and silence.

"You don't owe Nate a safe harbor, Clara," Tess said softly.

"I know," Clara replied. "And he doesn't owe me a home. But when Barnaby got sick, everything got... clear. The things that were just comfortable stopped being enough."

Barnaby walked over to the bench, his tail swishing gently against Clara's wool coat. He pressed his broad golden head against Clara's knee, then turned and rested his chin against Tess's thigh, linking them together with his physical presence.

Clara reached down, stroking the dog's soft ears. Her fingers brushed against Tess's coat sleeve. Neither of them moved away.

"Let's walk back," Clara whispered. "The wind is picking up off the pass."

They walked slowly along the reservoir spillway, the sound of the churning water echoing against the frozen granite cliffs.

Barnaby trotted ahead on the packed snow, his red fleece jacket standing out brilliant against the white landscape. He stopped at a snowdrift, burying his muzzle deep into the powder to inspect a squirrel track, his tail wagging in slow, contented arcs.

"Look at him," Clara said, smiling as she pulled her mustard scarf tighter. "He thinks he's a five-month-old pup hunting grouse."

"He's feeling the cold air on his lungs," Tess said, watching his chest. "Cold air reduces airway inflammation, as long as he doesn't exert himself. His resting rate was nineteen this morning."

Clara stopped on the path, looking across the frozen reservoir toward the jagged peaks of Mount Lincoln.

"Tess," Clara said in a low voice.

Tess paused, turning to face her. "What?"

Clara looked down at her mittened hands, then met Tess's eyes with that calm, unhurried directness that always caught Tess off guard.

"Before I came to Colorado," Clara said, "I thought my life was going to be about managing damage. Taking care of my mother, paying off debts, keeping things from falling apart. I thought love was just... staying upright during a landslide."

She took a step closer, the cold wind whipping her dark hair across her cheek.

"Being with Barnaby... and being with you," Clara continued, her voice soft but steady, "it made me realize that love isn't just surviving the damage. It's choosing what you want to build after the storm passes."

Tess felt her heart thumping against her ribs, fast and hard, an ache of fierce tenderness rising in her throat.

She reached out, taking Clara's mittened hand in her own.

"We're going to build something good, Clara," Tess whispered.

Barnaby trotted back to them, pressing his snowy golden muzzle against their joined hands, his dark eyes bright in the mountain winter light.

They reached the wooden bench near the boat launch at three in the afternoon.

The reservoir had frozen into a sheet of slate-gray ice along the shoreline, ringed by a white collar of fresh powder. Across the water, the north face of Mount Princeton was draped in unbroken winter snow, the jagged granite ribs of the peak standing out dark and sharp against the sky.

Clara unzipped her canvas tote bag and spread a thick fleece blanket over the frozen wooden slats of the bench.

"Sit here," Clara said, gesturing to the blanket. "The wood will pull the heat right out of your hips."

Tess sat down, tucking her hands into her coat pockets. Barnaby walked over to the bench and leaned his shoulder against Tess's shins, his tail wagging twice before he settled onto the snow-dusted gravel at their feet. Clara sat down beside Tess, their shoulders brushing through the thick fabric of their parkas.

"His breathing was good on the flat road," Tess said, looking down at Barnaby's flank. "Twenty-two breaths a minute. No coughing."

"The cold air seems to help," Clara noted, pulling off one knit mitten to pour steaming herbal tea from a stainless thermos into two ceramic mugs. "Back in Oregon, winter was wet and heavy. The air felt thick in the throat. Here, the cold is dry. It tastes clean."

She handed one mug to Tess. The steam smelled of dried mint and mountain honey.

Tess took a sip, the heat warming her throat. "Why did you leave Oregon, Clara?"

Clara looked out across the frozen reservoir, where a flock of common mergansers floated in the open water near the dam spillway.

"My partner and I were together for six years," Clara said in a low voice. "We bought a Victorian house in Eugene. We had a vegetable garden and an art studio in the converted garage. And then one spring she told me she had fallen in love with a ceramicist from Portland and that she had been unhappy for three years."

Tess turned her head to look at Clara. The winter light reflected pale and clean in Clara's gray eyes.

"What did you do?" Tess asked.

"I packed my watercolor paper, my brushes, and my clothes into my Honda Civic," Clara said with a dry, self-deprecating smile. "I drove east until I hit the mountains. Nate was the first person I met in Silvercreek who didn't ask me why a woman in her late thirties was renting a cabin alone."

"Nate never pries," Tess said softly. "He gives people space. Sometimes so much space that they freeze."

"I know," Clara whispered. She looked down at their boots side by side in the snow. "He's gentle, Tess. But when we're together in the evening, I feel like I'm an exhibit in a museum he's curated. He's proud to have me there, but he doesn't touch the glass."

Tess felt a sudden, sharp ache in the center of her chest. She remembered that glass. She had lived behind it for seven years before she filed for divorce.

She reached out and set her gloved hand over Clara's mitten on the wooden bench.

"You're not an exhibit, Clara," Tess said firmly.

Clara turned her hand over beneath the wool, her fingers squeezing Tess's palm through the thick layers of fleece. Neither of them spoke for five minutes while the mountain wind rustled the dried reeds along the reservoir shore.

Barnaby lifted his head, gave a quiet sneeze, and rested his chin across Clara's snow boot, his golden fur catching the last copper light of the setting winter sun.

On the drive back from the reservoir, the snow began to fall in earnest.

Large, lazy flakes drifted down through the bare aspen groves, coating the gravel road in a layer of quiet white. The wipers on the Subaru swept back and forth with a rhythmic, rubber squeak against the windshield.

In the back seat, Barnaby lay curled on his side, his nose tucked into the crook of Clara's wool coat. Clara had one arm draped over his ribs, her thumb idly stroking the soft fur behind his ears.

"Look at the way the snow clings to the pine needles," Clara said softly, looking out the side window. "It creates these strange, architectural shelves. Like little white pagodas in the forest."

Tess glanced at Clara in the rearview mirror. The soft gray light of the snowstorm illuminated Clara's face with calm, serene grace.

"You see everything like a painting, don't you?" Tess asked with a fond smile.

"I can't help it," Clara laughed under her breath. "My brain translates light into pigment values before I even realize I'm doing it. Right now, that ridge is raw umber and titanium white with a touch of cobalt blue."

"Sounds beautiful," Tess murmured, turning the Subaru onto the paved highway leading back to town.

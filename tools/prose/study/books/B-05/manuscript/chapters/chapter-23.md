The clinical pharmacology of cardiac glycosides was an exercise in tightrope walking over a chasm of ventricular fibrillation.

At eight o'clock on a Tuesday morning in January, Tess sat at the stainless steel laboratory bench in the clinic pharmacy, reading the latest serum digoxin levels from the Fort Collins reference lab.

Barnaby's heart had begun to show the subtle, ominous signs of early electrical remodeling: intermittent ventricular premature complexes on his three-minute lead II rhythm strip, and a resting rate that crept toward thirty breaths a minute despite forty milligrams of furosemide twice daily.

Marcus had added digoxin—zero point zero zero five milligrams per kilogram, once daily—to increase vagal tone, slow atrioventricular conduction, and squeeze a fractional increase in contractility out of the tired left ventricular myocardium.

Tess looked at the printed lab value: *Serum Digoxin: 1.1 ng/mL.*

Target therapeutic range in dogs: 0.8 to 1.5 ng/mL. Anything over 2.0 was toxic, triggering nausea, anorexia, severe bradycardia, and fatal ventricular tachycardia.

"Right in the pocket," Marcus said, leaning over her shoulder and tapping the paper with his thumb. "One point one. The kidneys are clearing it."

"His potassium was four point two," Tess noted, pointing to the electrolyte panel. "If the furosemide pulls his potassium below three point five, the digoxin toxicity threshold plummets. The sodium-potassium ATPase pump gets paralyzed."

"Which is why you check his electrolytes every three weeks," Marcus said. He pulled a fresh tongue depressor from the glass jar and broke it in half with a crisp wooden snap. "You're keeping him alive on a chemistry balance, Tess. You know that, don't you?"

"I know," Tess said.

"How is Clara holding up with the dosing?" Marcus asked casually, walking toward the autoclave.

Tess looked down at the lab sheet.

Since the morning after the blizzard three weeks ago, the shared custody routine had undergone an invisible, tectonic shift. Clara had not moved out of Pine Street yet—winter in the mountains made finding an open rental cottage nearly impossible until the spring thaw—but she had spoken to Nate.

Tess didn't know the exact words of that conversation; Clara had kept the details private, respecting Nate's dignity. But Nate had moved his cot into the heated woodshop behind the garage, and Clara spent her days painting in the studio and her evenings walking Barnaby along the frozen creek.

When Clara brought Barnaby to the clinic for his blood draws, she and Tess stood together in the treatment room with a quiet, charged understanding that spoke louder than any public announcement.

"Clara hasn't missed a single dose," Tess said. "She crushes the digoxin tablet in chicken broth at seven every morning. She logs the heart rate twice a day. She watches his appetite like a hawk."

Marcus turned from the autoclave, his wise, lined face softening into a wry smile.

"A good technician is hard to find, Tess," Marcus said. "A good partner who knows how to titrate cardiac drugs is a miracle. Don't let your stubborn pride scare you away from what's standing right in front of you."

He shut the autoclave door, latched the heavy brass wheel, and walked back to exam room one.

Marcus pulled down the three-lead electrocardiogram strip that was printing from the thermal recorder in exam room two.

The rhythmic grid of pink paper unfolded onto the counter, covered in the black ink tracing of Barnaby's cardiac conduction cycle: P wave, QRS complex, T wave. Marcus ran his plastic ruler along the tracing, measuring the PR interval and the QT duration.

"Look at that PR segment," Marcus said, pointing with the tip of his fountain pen. "Zero point one four seconds. Digoxin is doing its job. It's slowing down the AV node conduction velocity just enough to protect the ventricles from supraventricular runs."

Tess leaned over the tracing, her trained technician's eye scanning the baseline. "No ST segment slurring. No ventricular bigeminy. The therapeutic margin is holding."

"For now," Marcus noted with realistic clinical gravity. "Remember, in an enlarged left atrium, stretch creates automaticity. Ectopic foci will start firing as the dilation progresses. But today, the rhythm is organized."

He tore the strip from the machine and handed it to Tess.

"File that in his permanent record," Marcus said. He glanced at her through his bifocals, a wry, affectionate smile touching his weathered lips. "Clara's waiting out front with his leash. Go on and give her the good news. Even country vets know when a woman needs to go home."

Tess took the pink paper strip, smiling warmly at her mentor, and walked through the swinging doors to where Clara stood by the front desk, holding Barnaby's canvas lead in the morning sun.

Tess brought the plastic pill tray over to the small pharmacy counter.

She counted out sixty tablets of digoxin, using her stainless steel spatula to sweep the tiny white discs into an amber prescription vial five at a time. The pills made a faint clicking sound against the plastic sides, like dry corn kernels falling into an empty bucket.

Marcus stood at the wet sink, scrubbing a pair of surgical thumb forceps with an ultrasonic brush. He glanced over his shoulder at the amber vial in Tess's hand.

"Starting the inotrope?" Marcus asked, his gravel voice quiet.

"His heart rate spiked to a hundred and forty at rest yesterday evening," Tess said, snapping the childproof white cap onto the vial. "Mitral regurgitation is driving compensatory sinus tachycardia. The left atrium is stretched to the point of firing ectopic pacemakers. We need to slow the AV node conduction before he goes into atrial fibrillation."

Marcus turned off the water with the back of his wrist. He dried his hands on a clean brown paper towel, tossing it into the step-can.

"Digoxin is an old drug, Tess," Marcus said, leaning his hip against the stainless sink. "Digitalis purpurea. Foxglove. The English physicians used to brew it into tea for dropsy in the eighteenth century. If the dose was right, the edema cleared. If the dose was half a grain too heavy, the patient died in ventricular tachycardia."

"The therapeutic index is 0.8 to 1.5 nanograms per milliliter," Tess recited automatically. "Toxic threshold is two point zero."

"Narrow bridge," Marcus murmured. "Make sure Clara knows the early signs of toxicity. Inappetence, vomiting, depression. If Barnaby turns his nose up at peanut butter, stop the pill immediately."

"She already knows," Tess said, writing the dosing instructions on the white adhesive label. "Clara watches him closer than most veterinary interns."

Marcus looked at Tess for a long, quiet moment, his gray eyebrows knitting slightly. "She's a good person, Tess. Don't go making yourself sick with guilt over how things turned out."

Tess capped her black marker and stuck it into the breast pocket of her scrub top. "I just want to keep his heart beating, Marcus."

"I know you do," Marcus said gently. "Now go split those tablets before the afternoon appointments arrive."

The veterinary quality of life scale lay on the kitchen table in Clara's cottage on a Sunday morning in July.

It was the HHHHHMM scale, developed by Dr. Alice Villalobos for hospice veterinary oncology: Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility, and More good days than bad.

Each category was scored from zero to ten. A score above thirty-five was considered acceptable quality of life; a score below thirty-five meant the animal's suffering had begun to outweigh its comfort.

Tess sat at the table with a sharpened pencil. Clara sat beside her, holding a cold cup of coffee. Nate sat opposite them, his hands flat on the pine wood.

The house was completely still.

In the living room, Barnaby lay on his bed. He had refused his roasted sweet potato mash on Thursday. He had refused canned chicken broth on Friday. On Saturday, his back legs had given out completely when he tried to stand up to greet Nate at the door, his hips collapsing into the carpet.

He had not coughed—the diuretics were holding the pulmonary fluid back—but his resting respiratory rate had climbed to thirty-four breaths a minute while asleep, and his dark eyes were clouded with a deep, bone-weary exhaustion.

Tess looked at the paper.

"Hurt," Tess said, her voice flat, clinical, trembling at the edges. "Pain is controlled with gabapentin, but his chest pressure is constant. Five out of ten."

Clara nodded slowly. "Five."

"Hunger," Tess continued. "He has refused all food for forty-eight hours. Two out of ten."

"Hydration," Clara said quietly. "He drinks from a sponge if I hold it to his mouth, but his skin tent is four seconds. Three out of ten."

"Hygiene," Tess whispered. "He couldn't get up to urinate yesterday morning. He soiled his bed for the first time in his life. Four out of ten."

"Happiness," Nate said, his voice thick and rough. He looked through the doorway at the old dog. "He wagged his tail when I walked in, but he couldn't lift his head. Four out of ten."

"Mobility," Tess wrote. "Cannot stand without a towel sling under his abdomen. Two out of ten."

"More good days than bad," Clara whispered, tears spilling freely down her cheeks. "This is four bad days in a row."

Tess added the numbers.

Twenty-two out of seventy.

She set the pencil down. It rolled across the pine table, coming to rest against Nate's thumb.

"The scale has tilted," Tess said, looking up at Clara, then at Nate. "He is tired. He has fought this valve for ten months, and he has given us four hundred extra mornings. We cannot ask him to give us a bad afternoon."

Nate swallowed, his jaw working as he looked at the floorboards. He nodded once, reaching across the table to cover Tess's hand with his own.

"Tomorrow morning," Nate said softly. "In the living room. Where the sun comes through the window."

Clara reached out, taking both their hands in hers, sealing the choice in quiet, unbroken unity.

Tess looked at the score sheet on the kitchen table, the numbers stark in the morning sunlight: twenty-two out of seventy.

Beside her, Clara sat with her head bowed, her hand gripping the cold ceramic coffee mug. Nate sat motionless, his rough hands clasped between his knees, staring down at the pine floorboards.

"In veterinary medicine," Tess said, her voice shaking slightly despite her efforts at composure, "the hardest part isn't making the diagnosis. The hardest part is knowing when the battle has turned into cruelty. If we wait until he has another syncope episode, he could throw a saddle thrombus or rupture his atrium. It would be violent, terrifying, and painful."

"We won't let that happen," Nate said, lifting his head. His blue eyes were rimmed with red, but his voice was rock steady. "He deserves to go out peaceful, in his own house, with the people who love him."

"Tomorrow morning," Clara agreed, reaching over to squeeze Tess's trembling fingers. "We'll spend today loving him, and tomorrow morning we'll let him rest."

They sat together at the table in absolute, unbroken solidarity, united by their devotion to the golden retriever who had brought them all to grace.

Tess and Clara sat side by side at the kitchen table, the seven-point HHHHHMM quality of life assessment sheet lying flat between their coffee mugs.

The morning light poured through the window, cold and clear, illuminating the printed rows: *Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility, More Good Days Than Bad.*

Tess held her black clinical pen, but her fingers were steady. She had walked dozens of clients through this exact sheet over ten years of veterinary practice, but she had never filled it out for her own dog.

"Let's go through the categories," Clara said softly, her hand resting on Tess's knee under the table.

"Hurt," Tess began, her voice level. "Controlled with the tramadol and gabapentin. He's not in pain, but the dyspnea causes anxiety when he lies flat. Score: six out of ten."

"Hunger," Clara said. "He turned down his kibble yesterday. He only ate the roast chicken and sweet potato mash after I warmed it on the stove. Score: five."

"Hydration," Tess noted. "Normal. Skin turgor is fine, but the furosemide demands constant water intake. Score: seven."

"Hygiene," Clara said. "He needs help getting up after he urinates. His hind hocks are slipping on the linoleum. Score: five."

"Happiness," Tess murmured, looking toward the living room where Barnaby lay on his orthopedic bed, staring blankly at the wall. "He didn't greet us at the door this morning. He didn't pick up his tennis ball when I came in from the clinic. Score: four."

"Mobility," Clara whispered, her throat catching. "He can't climb the cedar ramp without support under his pelvis. Score: three."

"More good days than bad," Tess said, her voice dropping lower. "In the last seven days, he's had four bad days. Two syncopal episodes, four episodes of orthopnea where he had to sleep sitting up on his brisket to breathe."

Tess added the numbers with the tip of her pen.

"Thirty-two out of seventy," Tess said, looking up from the paper into Clara's eyes. "Below thirty-five, the veterinary guidelines consider quality of life compromised. We're keeping him alive for us, Clara. Not for him."

Clara bowed her head, two slow tears tracing down her cheeks. She reached across the table, taking Tess's hand in both of hers.

"He's tired, Tess," Clara whispered. "He's fought so hard for us all winter."

"I know," Tess said, a single tear escaping her eye and splashing onto the printed scale. "It's time to call Marcus and Nate."

After filling out the scale, Tess stood up and walked to the living room door.

Barnaby was lying on his side on his orthopedic bed by the sunny French doors. He had his front paws crossed, watching a magpie hop across the green grass outside.

When Tess walked into the room, he didn't lift his head from the bolster. He simply gave two slow, gentle thumps of his tail against the fleece, his dark eyes looking up at her with complete, unreserved trust.

Tess knelt beside him on the floor. She ran her hand down the length of his spine, feeling the prominent ridges of his vertebrae where the muscle had wasted over the winter months. Cardiac cachexia—the relentless metabolic drain of severe heart failure, consuming muscle protein to keep the failing pump supplied with fuel.

Barnaby nuzzled his gray muzzle into the palm of her hand, his breath warm and faint against her skin.

Clara came in from the kitchen, sitting down on the rug on Barnaby's other side. She placed her hand over Tess's on the dog's flank.

"He's ready, isn't he, Tess?" Clara asked quietly.

"He's ready," Tess said, her voice steady now, purged of all doubt. "He's given us everything he had. It's our turn to give him peace."

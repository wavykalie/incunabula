The ramp Nate had built was seven feet long, made of two-inch-thick western red cedar with an eighty-grit non-slip rubber tread glued along the center walking surface.

It sat in the bed of Nate's pickup truck outside the Pine Street bungalow on Saturday morning, glowing with a fresh coat of weather-sealant that smelled of linseed oil and warm amber wax.

Nate and Tess lifted the ramp down together, carrying it to the front porch steps.

"I built it with a four-to-one slope," Nate explained, fitting the top galvanized steel cleats over the edge of the second porch step. "A standard ramp is three-to-one, but with his left atrial enlargement, any climb steeper than fifteen degrees spikes his heart rate. This way, his back legs don't have to push. He can just walk flat."

Tess ran her hand over the edge of the cedar. The wood had been rounded with a router, smooth as velvet, every screw countersunk and plugged with matching cedar dowels. At the top of the ramp, carved cleanly into the header timber with a half-inch gouge, was a small, stylized golden retriever pawprint.

"It's beautiful, Nate," Tess said quietly.

Clara came out through the front door, wearing her wool cardigan and carrying a bowl of warm broth. She stopped on the top landing, looking down at the ramp and then at Nate.

"Try him on it, Clara," Nate said, stepping back onto the gravel path.

Clara walked down to the bottom of the ramp, knelt on the snow, and held out a spoonful of broth. "Come on, bear. Let's see your new road."

Barnaby looked at the cedar structure with retriever suspicion. He sniffed the rubber tread, sneezed once at the smell of fresh sealant, and looked at Tess.

"Go ahead, buddy," Tess encouraged softly. "Up you go."

Barnaby stepped onto the ramp. His claws gripped the rough rubber without slipping. He walked up the gentle incline with smooth, effortless ease, his hips level, his chest never hitching for air. When he reached the top landing, he swallowed the spoonful of broth from Clara's hand, turned around, and wagged his tail at Nate standing on the gravel below.

Nate smiled—a broad, genuine grin that lit up his weathered mountain face.

"Works like a charm," Nate said. He picked up his tool bucket from the truck bed and wiped his hands on his canvas pants. "I'm heading up to the pass to check the snowpack markers for the forestry crew. You two take care of him."

He turned and walked down Pine Street, his boots crunching in the dry snow, leaving the cedar ramp gleaming in the winter sun.

The cedar ramp stood against the porch steps of the Pine Street house, its fresh sealant glowing in the crisp mountain afternoon.

Tess and Clara stood side by side on the flagstone path, watching Barnaby walk up and down the ramp three times in succession, his tail wagging happily each time he reached the top landing to collect a roasted sweet potato cube from Clara's hand.

"Nate's joinery is incredible," Clara said quietly, admiring the neat wooden plugs covering the lag bolts.

"He's the best carpenter in the county," Tess agreed softly. "When he builds something, he builds it to outlast the weather."

Clara turned to look at Tess, her eyes soft in the winter sunlight.

"He built this for Barnaby," Clara murmured. "But he also built it to give us peace of mind. He wanted to make sure we knew he was standing with us, not against us."

Tess reached out, taking Clara's hand and squeezing her fingers.

"He did," Tess whispered. "And that's why we're going to make sure this dog has the best spring of his life."

Barnaby walked down the ramp one more time, trotted over to the truck, and leaned his golden flank against their legs in the warming afternoon air.

They tested the ramp three times before sunset.

Nate stood at the top on the porch deck, holding a piece of dried beef liver in his open palm. Tess walked at Barnaby's left shoulder, her hand resting lightly against his support sling to support his balance if his hocks gave way.

Barnaby placed his front paws onto the bottom rubber tread. The cedar planking didn't flex or creak beneath his seventy-pound weight; Nate had reinforced the subframe with two-by-four joists spaced twelve inches on center.

Barnaby took a step. Then another. His head stayed level, his gait steady and unhurried on the non-slip rubber. He walked up the twelve-foot incline without a single hitch in his breathing, reaching the porch landing where Nate handed him the liver treat.

Barnaby took the meat with a gentle snap of his jaws, his tail waving in wide, slow sweeps against the porch railing.

"Look at that," Nate said, a rare grin breaking across his rugged face. "Zero hesitation."

Clara came out the front door carrying three mugs of hot spiced cider on a wooden tray. She looked from Barnaby's wagging tail to the ramp, and then across to Tess.

"He didn't stumble once," Clara said, her eyes shining with quiet joy.

"His respiration didn't even jump two beats," Tess reported, checking Barnaby's flank. "Twenty-four at the top."

They stood on the porch together in the failing twilight, the three of them drinking hot cider while Barnaby curled up contentedly on the cedar landing, his gray nose tucked beneath his golden tail.

Before Nate left for the hardware store, he unbolted the wooden tool chest from the back of his truck.

"Take these extra rubber strips, Tess," Nate said, handing her a roll of adhesive tread. "If the ice freezes hard on the lower incline in January, cut four-inch squares and stick them between the cedar grooves. Gives his front pads extra bite."

Tess took the heavy black roll, its textured surface gritty like coarse sandpaper. "I'll keep them in the hall closet."

Nate looked at the front steps where the cedar ramp met the stone porch. The new wood looked golden and clean against the weathered gray siding of the old house.

"It's a good piece of work," Nate said softly, more to himself than to her. "Built to last three winters."

Barnaby walked over to the edge of the porch, rested his chin on Nate's knee, and let out a long, shuddering sigh of contentment.

Nate smiled, scratching the dog behind his silvering ears for two full minutes before turning back toward his truck.

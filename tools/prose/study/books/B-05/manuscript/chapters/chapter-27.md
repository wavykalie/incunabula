March in the high country was called mud season, a raw, unpredictable month where seventy-degree afternoons melted two feet of snow into chocolate-colored torrents, only for an overnight front to freeze the mud into concrete ruts that could snap a truck axle.

On the second weekend of March, Barnaby had what Marcus called a clinical rally.

It was a phenomenon every veterinary professional knew well and feared secretly: an unexplained, glorious resurgence of vitality in an end-stage patient, where the heart seemed to forget its failure for three miraculous days before the final decline began.

Barnaby woke up on Saturday morning with bright, clear eyes, whining at the front door before the alarm even sounded.

When Tess let him out into the backyard of the apartment, he didn't just walk; he trotted with his head high, his ears flopping, chasing a mountain chickadee across the thawing grass. His resting respiratory rate was fourteen breaths a minute. His appetite was ravenous; he finished his kibble, his sweet potato mash, and half of Tess's scrambled eggs with joyous, unshadowed greed.

"Look at him," Clara said, leaning against the wooden railing of the back landing with a mug of coffee. She had come over at seven to bring his morning pimobendan.

"He feels good," Tess said, a smile breaking across her face despite herself. "The pericardial effusion we saw on the last scan must have reabsorbed. His cardiac output is the best it's been since October."

"Let's take him to the creek trail," Clara suggested. "Just the flat part below the culvert."

They drove down to the valley floor where the Blue River wound through the willows.

The trail was a soup of red mud, melting snowbanks, and running meltwater that rushed over the river rocks with a loud, joyful roar. Barnaby trotted ahead on his loose leash, his tail helicoptering in circles as he splashed straight through the puddles, sending brown water flying up onto his golden flanks.

He smelled the wet earth, the rotting willow bark, the fresh scent of emerging snow-buttercups pushing through the slush.

At the river bend, where a gravel bar formed a shallow eddy of crystal-clear snowmelt, Barnaby stopped, looked back at them with his tongue lolling in pure retriever ecstasy, and waded out until the freezing water touched his chest. He lowered his snout, took three long drinks of river water, and let out a triumphant, ringing bark that echoed off the granite cliffs.

Clara laughed—a bright, ringing sound that carried across the water.

Tess stood beside her on the gravel bar, laughing too, her arm slipping around Clara's waist. Clara leaned into her, resting her head on Tess's shoulder, their coats brushing as they watched their old dog splash in the mountain spring.

For three days, the shadow lifted completely, and the world was made of sunlight, cold water, and golden fur.

The Blue River was running high with chocolate meltwater, carving through the gravel bars and roaring past the willow flats in a surge of mountain energy.

Barnaby splashed in the shallows for twenty glorious minutes, his golden coat plastered to his ribs with wet sand and cold river water. He chased floating pinecones, dug violently in the thawing gravel, and barked at a pair of mallards that exploded from the reeds with a clatter of wings.

Tess and Clara stood on the sandbar, laughing until their sides ached as they watched the eleven-year-old dog act like an eight-month-old pup.

"Look at that gait!" Tess exclaimed, her eyes tracking his movement. "No hitch. No shortness of breath. His cardiac reserve is completely compensating today."

"It's a miracle," Clara said, leaning her head on Tess's shoulder.

"It's not a miracle," Tess murmured softly, her arm tightening around Clara's waist. "It's a rally. Dogs do this sometimes before the final phase. Their bodies marshal every remaining ounce of energy for one last run in the sun."

Clara went quiet for a moment, watching Barnaby shake himself with a joyous, spray-filled explosion of cold river droplets.

"Then let's make sure he remembers every second of it," Clara whispered, stepping off the sandbar to run through the shallows after him, her boots splashing water into the bright spring air.

They walked the lower meadow loop for forty minutes under a warm March sun.

The mountain snow was melting rapidly, turning the cow pastures along Willow Creek into a mosaic of dark standing water, pale brown fescue grass, and patches of porous white ice. The air smelled of wet earth, rotting willow roots, and the first green shoots of spring.

Barnaby trotted ahead of them on the raised gravel berm of the irrigation ditch, his tail held high and curved like a golden sickle. He paused every fifty yards to plunge his nose into the cold runoff, blowing bubbles through his nostrils before looking back at them with wild, puppy-like enthusiasm.

"Look at him," Clara laughed, taking Tess's hand as they stepped over a deep rut of red mud. "You'd never guess he had a grade four murmur."

"Mitral disease has these strange plateau phases," Tess said, squeezing Clara's fingers. "The heart hypertrophies to compensate, the enalapril keeps the resistance low, and for a week or two the dog feels almost normal. We call it the cardiac honeymoon."

"How long does the honeymoon last?" Clara asked, her voice softening.

Tess looked out across the mud-soaked meadow to the granite peaks glistening in the spring thaw. "A week. A month. Maybe two. We take every single good day as a gift."

Barnaby gave a sharp, joyous bark at a red-winged blackbird perched on a cattail stalk, his golden coat gleaming in the March light, and bounded through a shallow puddle toward the car.

They paused at the old wooden bridge crossing Willow Creek, leaning their elbows on the sun-warmed fir railing.

The creek ran high and brown with snowmelt, swirling in frothy eddies around the granite bridge footings. A pair of belted kingfishers darted between the budding willows, their harsh rattling calls echoing above the rush of the water.

Clara reached into her pocket and pulled out a small sketchpad bound in brown kraft paper. With a stub of graphite, she began to capture the curve of Barnaby's jaw as the dog watched the foam bubbles drift downstream.

"Look at that line," Clara murmured, sketching with quick, sure strokes. "The way his neck muscles taper into the shoulder blade. A retriever's neck is built like a suspension cable."

Tess watched Clara's fingers work: disciplined, confident, creating form from empty paper in sixty seconds.

"You should draw him more often," Tess said.

Clara looked up, her gray eyes warm in the spring glare. "I draw him every morning, Tess. My desk is covered with him."

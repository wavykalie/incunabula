The shared spreadsheet arrived in Tess's inbox at 5:12 AM on a Monday, with the subject line: *Barnaby Care Matrix (Nov-Apr)*.

Tess sat at her small kitchen table in her gray thermal henley, nursing a mug of dark roast coffee while the mountain wind rattled the storm window. She clicked the link.

The Google Sheet opened into a clean, ten-column grid formatted with muted slate headers and thin gray cell borders. It wasn't the crude, hasty checklist Tess had expected from someone outside clinical medicine. It was an operational hospice dashboard, structured with the meticulous visual hierarchy of an botanical field guide.

Column A: Date & Day of Week.
Column B: AM Pimobendan (5 mg, empty stomach @ 07:00).
Column C: AM Enalapril (10 mg with meal @ 08:00).
Column D: AM Resting Respiratory Rate (counted asleep, target < 25).
Column E: Midday Stool Consistency & Urination Frequency (furosemide tracking).
Column F: PM Pimobendan (5 mg, empty stomach @ 19:00).
Column G: PM Furosemide (40 mg with snack @ 20:00).
Column H: PM Resting Respiratory Rate.
Column I: Appetite & Joint Mobility Score (1-5 scale).
Column J: Clinical & Behavioral Observations.

Tess scrolled down through the first two weeks of November, where Clara had already backfilled the entries from her Pine Street notes.

*Nov 3 (PM): Sleeping rate 18. Drank 600ml water after evening walk. Stood on back legs to smell dried lavender on drying rack.*
*Nov 5 (AM): Hesitated on the three porch steps. Left hip stiff in the morning frost. Ate sweet potato mash eagerly; swallowed both pills in one bite.*
*Nov 8 (PM): Sleeping rate 22. Thunder from the pass woke him at 2 AM; rested his chin on my wool slipper until the wind dropped.*

Tess leaned closer to the laptop screen. In cell J14, under the notes for the night after Barnaby's emergency hospital stay, Clara had written:

*Left ear canal slightly dry from the oxygen cage airflow. Applied two drops of organic almond oil to the pinna margin. Slept six uninterrupted hours with his back pressed against the woodstove hearth.*

Tess rubbed her eyes.

In seven years at the Silvercreek Animal Clinic, Tess had reviewed hundreds of owner monitoring sheets. Ranchers brought in greasy envelopes with pencil scratches: *dog seemed slow wednesday.* Vacationers brought in crumpled sticky notes with half-remembered pill counts.

Clara had not only captured the exact hemodynamic monitoring parameters Marcus had scribbled on the prescription receipt; she had woven them into a tender, continuous portrait of the dog's inner state. She was tracking fluid balance through water consumption, cardiac workload through respiratory frequency, and emotional comfort through where he rested his skull.

Tess tapped her fingers against the trackpad. She opened the permissions tab. Clara had set the document to *Can Edit*, adding a brief note in the sharing dialogue:

*Tess — add any clinical metrics Marcus wants us to track. I can adjust the mobile layout so we can both update from our phones at handover. Let me know if the color coding works for your eyes. —C.*

Tess typed into cell J16, her fingers moving swiftly across the keys:

*Added column for femoral pulse quality (snap vs weak). If resting respiration crosses 28 on two consecutive checks, double the PM furosemide to 80mg and notify me immediately. The color coding is good. Thank you.*

She hit enter. Almost instantly, a small purple avatar with the initial *C* appeared in cell J16.

A comment bubble expanded in real time:

*Got it. Updating the alert rule now. Stay warm on your drive to the clinic today.*

Tess stared at the little purple cursor pulsing quietly in the digital cell. She lifted her mug, took a sip of hot coffee, and watched the snow begin to fall across the pine tops outside.

Tess opened the second tab of the spreadsheet, titled *Medication Inventory & Refill Cadence*.

Clara had mapped the remaining inventory down to the exact unit count: seventy-eight tablets of pimobendan, forty-four tablets of enalapril, thirty-six tablets of furosemide. Below the counts, she had calculated the reorder thresholds based on the delivery schedules of the veterinary distributor in Denver.

*Note: Pass closures in December usually add forty-eight hours to FedEx ground deliveries. Order next ninety-day Vetmedin batch when stock reaches thirty tablets (approx Dec 1).*

Tess stared at the entry.

Most clients waited until the last pill rattled in the plastic vial on a Saturday afternoon before calling the emergency pager in a panic. Clara had built winter freight contingencies into a veterinary inventory schedule four weeks before the first blizzard was even forecasted.

Tess clicked into the cell, typing:

*Distributor order placed this morning. We will keep a sixty-day buffer in the clinic safe under Barnaby's name so mountain highway closures cannot interrupt therapy. Your calculations are exact.*

Across the room, Barnaby gave a deep, contented sigh, his front paws stretching out until they touched the base of the woodstove. Tess closed her laptop, walked to the stove to add a split pine round to the red embers, and listened to the steady, quiet rhythm of the dog breathing in the morning light.

Later that evening, after the clinic closed, Tess logged into the spreadsheet from her laptop at the kitchen table.

A new row had appeared under the morning medications column, logged at 8:15 AM in Clara's clean notation:

*Pimobendan 5mg administered with three tablespoons pumpkin puree. Chewed readily. Resting respiration while sleeping on wool rug: 22 breaths per minute. Left ear scratched for ten minutes after meal.*

Tess leaned back in her wooden chair. She tapped her pen against her tea mug.

Most owners recorded whether the pill went down. Clara recorded the pumpkin puree, the respiration count, and the ten minutes of scratching behind the left ear. It was the kind of observational precision that usually took veterinary students three years of clinical rotations to master.

Tess opened the comment bubble on the cell. She typed:

*Good catch on the resting rate. If it stays under 25 at rest, we hold the furosemide dose steady.*

She paused, her fingers hovering over the plastic keys. Then she added:

*He loves the pumpkin.*

She hit save, watching the green checkmark appear in the cloud sync icon, and closed the laptop screen with a quiet click.

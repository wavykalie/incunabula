The ultrasound room at the Silvercreek Animal Clinic had no windows. It occupied a former storage vault at the back of the masonry foundation, cool in summer and cold in winter, smelling perpetually of acoustic coupling gel, isopropyl alcohol, and dry concrete dust. Marcus had bought the refurbished Sonosite machine three years ago from an equine surgery center in Fort Collins. Its gray plastic casing was yellowed around the power toggle, but the cardiac phased-array probe was clean, its piezoelectric crystal array intact.

Tess adjusted the dimmer switch on the wall until the room sank into an underwater gloom. The only light came from the luminescent phosphor of the monitor screen, painting the cinderblocks in faint blue-green lines.

Barnaby lay on his right side atop the specialized foam positioning pad, his head cradled in Clara's lap. Clara sat cross-legged on the rubber floor mat, wearing dark denim and a gray fleece pullover with the sleeves pushed up. Her fingers moved through the fur of Barnaby's ears, working the base of his skull with slow, circular friction. The dog's eyes were half-closed, his breathing steady at eighteen breaths per minute in the dim quiet.

Marcus sat on an adjustable rolling stool, holding the five-megahertz probe in his right hand. He squeezed a thick, clear ribbon of acoustic gel onto the silicone footprint.

"Alright, old friend," Marcus muttered. "Let's see what you're hiding in there."

He parted the golden fur on the shaved patch over the fourth intercostal space, pressing the transducer head into the cardiac notch behind the elbow. The monitor flared into life. A fan of gray-scale ultrasound beams sliced through the chest wall, resolving the dark, fluid-filled chambers of the heart into flickering motion.

Tess leaned over the control panel, her fingers hovering on the freeze button and the caliper wheel. Her jaw was clamped so tight her molars ached.

"Right parasternal long-axis view," Marcus said, his voice dropping into the clinical drone of thirty years in practice. "Four-chamber alignment. Look at that mitral valve."

The image was unmistakable. The thin, delicate fibrous leaflets that should have snapped together like crisp sailcloth were thickened, rolled, clubbed into bulbous nodules of mucopolysaccharide matrix. Instead of meeting in a clean, tight line of apposition during systole, the septal leaflet billowed backward into the left atrium like a blown umbrella caught in a mountain downdraft.

Marcus rolled the trackball, engaging the color Doppler.

The screen exploded into a swirling mosaic of blue, turquoise, and flame-yellow turbulence. A broad regurgitant jet flooded the entire left atrial cavity, swirling in violent eddies that bounced off the dorsal wall.

"Severe regurgitation," Tess said, her voice barely louder than the cooling fan of the console. "It fills more than seventy percent of the atrial area."

"Eighty," Marcus corrected gently. "Rotate to the short-axis at the heart base. Let's get the ratio."

Tess turned the transducer angle slightly, angling the beam dorsal and cranial. The image shifted into the classic Mercedes-Benz sign of the aortic valve surrounded by three symmetrical cusps. Beside it, the left atrium loomed like an over-inflated black balloon, dwarfing the aortic root.

Marcus tapped the caliper toggle. "Measure it, Tess."

Tess took the trackball. Her fingers were cold, but her movements were practiced and precise. She placed the first crosshair on the internal margin of the aortic root, scrolled the dotted line across, and dropped the second point. One point eight centimeters. Then she moved the calipers to the left atrium, drawing the maximal diameter parallel to the commissure between the non-coronary and left coronary aortic cusps. Three point four centimeters.

The digital readout in the corner of the monitor calculated the ratio instantly: LA:Ao 1.89.

Normal was less than 1.6. Anything over 1.7 was severe left atrial enlargement. Stage C degenerative mitral valve disease. The heart was remodeling, stretching its thin-walled chamber under back-pressure, straining against the pulmonary return.

"One point eight nine," Tess whispered.

From the floor, Clara looked up from Barnaby's head. Her gray eyes reflected the blue phosphor of the screen. "Tess? What does the number mean?"

Tess swallowed the dry knot in her throat. She had given this speech two hundred times to crying clients in exam room one: to tourists whose dogs collapsed on hiking trails, to ranchers whose cowdogs started hacking behind the barn. She knew the clinical script by heart. She knew how to modulate her pitch, how to emphasize quality of life over duration, how to offer hope without lying about the biology.

Looking down at Clara's upturned face, the professional armor cracked.

"It means his heart has remodeled," Tess said, the words rough and unvarnished. "The left atrium is nearly double the diameter of his aorta. The mitral valve isn't closing. Every time his left ventricle squeezes to send blood to his brain and his legs, half that volume gets blasted backward into his lungs."

"How long?" Clara asked. She didn't flinch. Her hand never stopped moving over Barnaby's neck.

Marcus reached over and hit the store button, archiving the loop into the hard drive. The screen froze on a frame of turbulent turquoise jet.

"With medication," Marcus said, his gravelly voice filling the small room with calm gravity, "we can offload the pressure. Pimobendan to help the muscle squeeze more efficiently and open up the peripheral vessels. Enalapril to block the angiotensin cascade and stop the kidneys from retaining sodium. If he develops pulmonary edema, we add furosemide to pull the water out."

He took a cotton towel, wiped the gel off the probe face, and set it into the silicone cradle.

"Median survival from the onset of Stage C is twelve months," Marcus continued, looking straight at Clara, then at Tess. "Some dogs give us fourteen. Some give us eight. An arrhythmia can take them on a good afternoon, or the valve chordae can rupture during a brisk run. But with careful management, the next year can be comfortable. He can still eat his dinner, he can still walk down to the mailbox, he can still enjoy the snow."

A heavy silence settled over the room, broken only by the steady, rhythmic rasp of Barnaby's breath against Clara's knee.

Nate was waiting in the hallway outside. He hadn't come into the dark room; he had claimed he was claustrophobic, but Tess knew the truth. Nate couldn't bear to see medical machines connected to something he loved. He had stood by the clinic front desk, staring at a cardboard display of flea collars, turning a brass key ring around and around his thumb.

Clara leaned forward, resting her forehead against Barnaby's golden ear. Her shoulders rose and fell once, a deep, silent intake of mountain air. When she looked up, her expression was clear, resolute, stripped of panic.

"We need a schedule," Clara said. "Exact times for the pills. Who gives what, when he eats, what symptoms we watch for."

Tess looked down at her. In the blue glow of the monitor, Clara's features were sharp, honest, entirely present. There was no territorial posturing, no awkward tiptoeing around the ghost of Tess and Nate's marriage. There was only the dog on the floor and the twelve months ticking down on the clock.

"Pimobendan must be given on an empty stomach," Tess said, her voice finding its clinical footing again. "An hour before food. Five milligrams twice a day. Seven in the morning, seven at night. Enalapril with his breakfast, ten milligrams once daily."

"I'll buy pill organizers," Clara said. "One for Pine Street, one for your apartment."

Marcus nodded slowly, watching both women from his rolling stool. He reached into his coat pocket, pulled out a clean white prescription pad, and began scribbling with his black fountain pen. The nib scratched across the paper with dry, steady purpose.

"I'm writing ninety days of Vetmedin," Marcus said. "Five milligram chewables. Don't let him chew them if he starts refusing food; crush them in broth. And keep a bottle of injectable furosemide in your glove box, Tess. If the lungs flood at three in the morning, you don't want to be fumbling with keys to the pharmacy cabinet."

Tess took the slips of paper from his hand. The ink was still wet, glinting under the dim desk lamp. She tucked them into her breast pocket, right behind her nametag.

"Thanks, Marcus," she said.

"Don't thank me, kiddo," Marcus replied, standing up and stretching his stiff lumbar spine. "Just keep his resting rate under thirty. That's the only metric that matters now."

Clara gathered Barnaby's paws, helping the big dog roll onto his chest. He stood up slowly, shaking himself from nose to tail. A few specks of dried acoustic gel flew across the rubber floor, and he gave Clara's chin an affectionate, wet swipe with his tongue.

Clara smiled—a small, tired, genuine curve of her mouth that transformed her whole face. She reached up and wiped the dog saliva from her jaw with the back of her wrist, looking directly at Tess.

"Let's get him into the truck," Clara said.

Tess unhitched the canvas leash from the door hook, clipped it to Barnaby's flat leather collar, and led the way into the bright morning corridor. Nate was leaning against the reception desk, his jaw set in rigid apprehension.

"Well?" Nate asked, his voice cracking slightly.

"Twelve months, Nate," Tess said, meeting his gaze directly. "If we do the work."

Nate looked at the dog, swallowed hard, and pushed open the front glass door for them without another word.

Marcus pulled the acoustic gel bottle from the warmer on the shelf and set it in the plastic holder.

He wiped the curved transducer with an alcohol wipe, then sat back down on his swivel stool. The blue glow of the ultrasound monitor bathed the small darkroom in aquatic light, casting long shadows of their silhouettes against the white acoustic ceiling tiles.

"Look at that color Doppler map, Tess," Marcus said, pointing to the turbulent mosaic of teal, crimson, and gold flaring across the mitral orifice. "The regurgitant jet is hitting the caudal roof of the left atrium. It's eccentric, blowing right along the lateral wall."

"That explains the lateralization of the murmur on physical exam," Tess said, watching the frame rate counter tick in the corner of the display. "The jet velocity is 5.4 meters per second. That gives a pressure gradient across the valve of over one hundred and ten millimeters of mercury."

Marcus nodded slowly, his fingers adjusting the baseline gain. "The left ventricle is dilating to accept the regurgitant volume. End-diastolic diameter is fifty-four millimeters. For a golden retriever his size, normal is forty-two."

Barnaby let out a soft snuffle from the padded foam trough on the table. His front paws rested against Tess's forearm, his fur warm and fragrant against her skin.

"How does Nate feel about this?" Marcus asked quietly, his eyes still fixed on the echocardiogram screen.

"Nate's quiet," Tess said. "He looked at the floor when I told him the murmur had progressed. He asked if there was a surgical fix."

"You told him no?"

"I told him open-heart mitral valve repair requires cardiopulmonary bypass in London or Tokyo," Tess murmured. "Fifty thousand dollars and a forty percent perioperative mortality rate. It's not realistic, Marcus."

"No," Marcus agreed gently. "It isn't. The realistic road is medical management. Pimobendan to keep the pump working, ACE inhibitors to lower the afterload, and furosemide when the lungs get wet."

He clicked the freeze button on the console, the ultrasound image freezing in mid-systole. Marcus pulled off his rubber gloves with a sharp snap and reached for the paper towel roll.

"Let's get him cleaned up and back to his supper," Marcus said.

Marcus pulled the darkroom door open, the corridor light illuminating their scrubs.

Barnaby sat up slowly on the padded table, shaking his head until his ears flapped. He sniffed the plastic bottle of acoustic gel, sneezed once, and pressed his forehead against Tess's collarbone.

"He's a brave boy, Marcus," Tess whispered, unhooking the table leash.

"The bravest," Marcus agreed, patting the dog's golden flank with his heavy hand.

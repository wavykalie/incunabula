The package sat on Tess's kitchen table on a cold Tuesday in February, wrapped in brown kraft paper and tied with a thick loop of natural jute twine.

Beside it lay a small glass jar of fresh honeysuckle tea and Barnaby's empty canvas tote. Clara had dropped the package off while Tess was at the clinic running bloodwork on a diabetic cat.

Tess untied the twine. She peeled back the heavy paper, revealing a piece of heavy, deckle-edged watercolor paper mounted inside a simple frame made of weathered gray barn wood.

It was an original watercolor study.

In the center of the paper, painted with astonishing botanical delicacy, was a single stalk of *Lupinus argenteus*—silvery mountain lupine. The palmate leaves were rendered in shades of celadon and sage, the tiny silvery hairs along the margins captured with single-bristle brushstrokes. The purple and blue blossoms spiraled up the stem, luminous and alive against the creamy grain of the paper.

In the lower right corner, pressed into the paper with dried raw umber pigment, was the distinct, five-padded impression of Barnaby's front left paw.

Beside the pawprint, Clara's handwriting was small, precise, and permanent:

*Lupinus argenteus. Silvercreek, Colorado. Elev. 8,800 ft.*

Tess turned the frame over.

On the back of the archival backing board, written in sepia ink beneath Clara's signature, was a short inscription:

*Wild lupine only establishes its roots where the earth has been disturbed by landslides, fire, or heavy machines. It needs the broken ground to take hold. You and I did not choose the storm that broke our soil, Tess, but I know what I want to grow in the clearing. I love you. —Clara.*

Tess stood motionless in the middle of her kitchen.

The words seemed to vibrate off the cardboard, echoing through the empty apartment with a quiet, undeniable clarity.

She looked down at the watercolor. She saw the hours Clara had spent hunched over the drafting table on Pine Street, mixing pigments, dipping Barnaby's paw into non-toxic umber wash, guiding the heavy foot onto the paper with infinite patience.

She heard Barnaby's breath from his bed in the living room—slow, rhythmic, steady at sixteen breaths per minute.

Tess picked up the frame with both hands. She carried it into her living room and hung it on the wall beside the front window, where the morning sun would catch the purple petals first thing at dawn.

Tess hung the framed watercolor of the mountain lupine on the living room wall beside the front window of her apartment.

The afternoon sunlight hit the glass, making the cobalt and purple pigments of the blossoms glow with luminous, vibrant life. At the bottom right corner, Barnaby's raw umber pawprint stood out sharp and clear against the creamy deckle edge of the heavy paper.

Tess stepped back, folding her arms across her chest as she read Clara's inscription on the backing board in her mind:

*Wild lupine only establishes its roots where the earth has been disturbed by landslides, fire, or heavy machines. It needs the broken ground to take hold. I love you.*

Barnaby walked over from his bed, looked up at the new frame on the wall, and gave one soft, inquiring huff through his nostrils.

Tess knelt down, wrapping her arms around his golden neck and pressing her face into his thick fur.

"She loves us, bear," Tess whispered into his ear, feeling the steady, rhythmic beat of his failing heart against her ribs. "She really loves us."

Barnaby licked her cheek, his tail tapping the baseboard in calm, unhurried rhythm, while the winter sun cast long golden rays across the floorboards.

Tess held the framed painting in both hands, her thumbs resting on the clean mitered corners of the walnut frame.

The watercolor paper was Arches three-hundred-pound cold press, heavy and textured like antique linen. In the center of the sheet, Clara had painted a clump of *Lupinus sericeus*—the silky lupine that bloomed in June along the dry mountain ridges above Silvercreek.

The painting was alive with light. The palmate leaves were rendered in subtle shades of sage and dusty olive, coated in fine, silvery hairs that seemed to catch the morning sun. The flowering spikes rose in tall spires of intense violet-blue, with individual blossoms opening from tight green buds at the top to deep indigo keels at the bottom.

Beside the lowest stem, pressed into the white paper with sepia watercolor ink, was Barnaby's actual right front paw print: the wide triangular central pad, the four distinct toe beans, and the blunt impressions of his trimmed nails.

"You took his print," Tess whispered, her eyes stinging with sudden, unexpected tears.

"Last Tuesday, while he was napping on the studio rug," Clara said softly, stepping closer to Tess's chair. "I mixed raw umber with gum arabic so it would wash off his paw with warm water. He opened one eye, sniffed the brush, and went back to sleep."

Tess traced the curve of the paw print with her fingertip. "It's perfect, Clara."

"Turn it over," Clara murmured.

Tess turned the wooden frame in her hands.

On the brown kraft paper backing, Clara had written three lines in dark walnut ink, in her delicate, disciplined botanical hand:

*For Tess.*
*For the woman whose hands keep our gentle boy alive.*
*For everything that begins in the mountain thaw.*
*With all my love, Clara.*

Tess stared at the handwritten words. The air in her small kitchen seemed to stop moving. The words weren't a tentative hint or a polite friendly compliment; they were a direct, open declaration of love, written without apology or hesitation.

Tess looked up. Clara was standing two feet away, her hands tucked into the pockets of her wool trousers, her gray eyes watching Tess with quiet vulnerability.

"Clara," Tess whispered.

"I meant every syllable, Tess," Clara said, her voice steady and clear. "I know this is complicated. I know where we are, and who Nate is, and what this town looks like from the outside. But I love you. I've loved you since the night you stood over Barnaby in the clinic with tears in your eyes and refused to let him die."

Tess set the painting carefully on the kitchen table. She took two steps forward, reached out, and pulled Clara into her arms.

Clara buried her face in the curve of Tess's neck with a soft, trembling sigh. Tess held her tightly, feeling the warmth of Clara's body pressed against hers, the beating of Clara's heart answering her own, while Barnaby slept peacefully on the rug by their feet.

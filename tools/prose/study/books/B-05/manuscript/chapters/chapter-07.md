By the third week of October, the aspens along the gulch had dropped their gold. The leaves choked the road culverts and lay plastered against the asphalt like wet copper coins. Above eight thousand feet, the wind smelled of granite and approaching blizzards, stripped of the sweet pine warmth that carried through July.

Tess took the reservoir trail at two in the afternoon, during her midweek half-day off from the clinic. The trail was flat, a gravel service road running along the south rim of the municipal reservoir where the water was drawn down for winter, leaving a wide shelf of pale mud and dried willow scrub.

Barnaby walked on a loose six-foot lead, his pace steady but subdued. Two months ago, he would have ranged thirty yards ahead, quartering through the rabbitbrush with his nose buried in the vole tunnels. Today, he stayed three paces in front of Tess's left knee, his tail held low in a neutral curve.

At the half-mile marker, where the service road curved gently uphill toward the pump house, the grade steepened by five percent.

It wasn't a mountain climb. It was a twenty-yard rise that a healthy retriever wouldn't even notice.

Halfway up the incline, Barnaby stopped.

His front feet remained planted on the frozen gravel, but his head lowered until his chin was level with his carpal joints. His ribs expanded with a sudden, exaggerated effort, the muscles between his intercostal spaces sucking inward as he pulled for air. His tongue lolled out, pale lavender at the edges instead of healthy salmon pink.

"Whoa," Tess said, stepping up beside him immediately. "Hold up, bear. Stop."

She dropped to one knee on the cold dirt. She didn't have her stethoscope, but she didn't need it. She pressed the palm of her bare hand against his left thorax, directly behind the elbow.

The apex beat was thumping against his ribs like a fist hitting a leather cushion: rapid, bounding, irregular. A gallop rhythm. The heart was trying to overcome the sudden increase in afterload caused by the gentle uphill grade, and the failing valve was blowing wide open under the strain.

Barnaby turned his head and leaned his seventy-pound weight directly into Tess's shins. He rested his chin on her fleece knee, his dark eyes wide and clouded with mild confusion. He wasn't in pain; he was simply running out of oxygen.

"I know," Tess whispered, wrapping one arm around his neck and pulling his chest tight against her ribs. "I've got you. Catch your breath."

She counted his respirations. Forty-two breaths a minute. Too fast. The pulmonary capillaries were congesting under the back-pressure, fluid threatening to spill across the alveolar membranes into the air sacs.

They sat together on the shoulder of the gravel road for seven minutes while the mountain wind whipped through the bare aspen limbs above them. Slowly, as the demand for cardiac output dropped, his breathing settled. Thirty-four. Twenty-eight. Twenty-two. The pale tinge on his tongue faded back to rose.

Barnaby gave a small, quiet sneeze, shook his head until his leather tags jingled, and looked back toward the parked Subaru.

"Yeah," Tess said, standing up and dusting the dry mud from her knees. "We're done for today. Let's go home."

She turned him around. As they walked the downhill half-mile back to the car, Barnaby pressed his shoulder against the side of her leg with every third step, checking to make sure she was still there.

Tess lifted Barnaby into the rear cargo bed of her Subaru, taking his weight under his chest and pelvis so his arthritic hocks didn't have to push against the bumper. She smoothed his blue hospital fleece over his hindquarters, tucking the edges around his paws to keep the wind out.

Barnaby circled twice, curled into a tight golden ball, and rested his gray muzzle on his front paws with a long, tremulous sigh.

Tess closed the rear hatch, the latch engaging with a solid, mechanical thud that echoed across the empty gravel lot.

She stood on the shoulder of the reservoir road for a moment, looking out across the gray expanse of water. The wind was picking up from the north, carrying the scent of granite dust and frozen mud off the Continental Divide. Along the shoreline, the yellow aspen leaves had formed a thick, sodden carpet in the drainage ditches, rotting into the dark soil before the snow arrived.

She knew what that five percent incline meant.

In canine cardiology, exercise intolerance was the primary clinical hallmark of stage C progression. When the left atrium reaches its volumetric capacity, any increase in venous return from skeletal muscle activity immediately spikes pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure. The dog doesn't get winded from muscle fatigue; he gets winded because his alveoli begin to weep fluid.

She pulled her cell phone from her parka pocket, unlocked the screen, and opened the shared spreadsheet.

In the midday note section for October 24, she typed:

*Reservoir service road walk: stopped at the pump house rise (approx 5% grade). Dyspneic, respiratory rate 42, tongue pale rose. Reversible within seven minutes of rest. No coughing. Restrict all walks to zero-grade terrain. No uphill trails.*

She hit save.

She put the phone away, climbed into the driver's seat, and turned the ignition. The Subaru's heater blew cold air across her boots for a mile before the engine warmed, humming steadily as she drove down through the bare aspens toward the quiet streets of Silvercreek.

When Tess reached the clinic driveway, the dusk had gathered under the ponderosa pines. Marcus's blue truck was still parked near the equipment bay. Through the lighted square of the pharmacy window, she could see him pouring filtered water into an amber glass beaker.

She cut the Subaru's engine. Barnaby stirred in the cargo bed, his front claws clicking against the plastic liner as he stood up and stretched.

"One second, bear," Tess said softly.

She walked around to the tailgate, opened the hatch, and lifted him down. His front paws landed firmly on the gravel, but his hindquarters dipped before catching his weight.

Marcus came out the back door carrying an empty recycling bin. He set it beside the wooden ramp, his eyes resting on Barnaby's gait.

"How was the reservoir?" Marcus asked, leaning his hands on his hips.

"Flat terrain only from now on," Tess said, clipping the lead to Barnaby's collar. "He hit the five percent rise by the pump house and blew his rhythm. Respiration spiked to forty-two."

Marcus nodded once, his weathered face solemn under the brim of his wool cap. "Stage C doesn't negotiate with geography, Tess. Keep him on the bottom flats."

"I updated the spreadsheet," Tess said.

"Good girl," Marcus murmured. "Go home and feed him his supper."

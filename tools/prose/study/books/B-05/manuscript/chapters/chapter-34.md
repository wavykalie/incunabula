The backyard barbecue behind Pete Henderson's ranch house on Saturday evening was a high-country tradition celebrating the end of spring branding.

Two cords of apple wood burned down to white coals in a split-barrel steel pit, roasting fifty pounds of beef brisket that perfumed the cool evening air with sweet, caramelized smoke. The local bluegrass band—the Silvercreek String Band—was set up on the tailgate of a flatbed trailer, the fiddle and banjo tunes drifting across the hayfields toward the mountains.

Tess and Clara arrived together at six o'clock, walking through the cedar gate with Barnaby between them.

The dog walked slowly on his leash, his nose twitching at the scent of roast beef. He wore his red fleece jacket to protect his chest from the evening mountain chill.

For five seconds, as they stepped onto the lawn where sixty townspeople stood with beer bottles and paper plates, the conversation near the horseshoe pit died down to a low murmur.

Martha Gable stood by the potato salad table. Joe Miller stood with his arms crossed by the beer keg. Pete Henderson leaned against a cedar fence post, wearing a clean silver-belly Stetson.

Tess felt Clara's hand brush against her lower back—a light, steady, grounding touch that anchored her against the stares.

Pete Henderson pushed off from the fence post. He walked across the lawn in his scarred cowboy boots, stopping right in front of them.

He looked down at Barnaby, then looked up at Tess and Clara standing side by side.

"Evening, girls," Pete said, his gravelly voice carrying across the quiet grass.

"Evening, Pete," Tess said, keeping her chin up.

Pete reached into his pocket, pulled out a thick, warm slice of burnt brisket edge wrapped in a paper napkin, and held it down to Barnaby. The old retriever inhaled the meat in one ecstatic gulp, licking Pete's scarred knuckles with enthusiastic gratitude.

Pete chuckled, rubbing the dog's graying ears. Then he looked up at Clara.

"Hear your botanical prints won first prize down at the Denver Western Art show last week, Clara," Pete said.

Clara smiled warmly. "They did, Pete. Thank you."

"Good," Pete grunted, nodding firmly. "We like having artists in this valley who know what real mountain scrub looks like."

He turned toward the beer keg and raised his voice so the whole yard could hear: "Hey, Joe! Bring two cold Colorado native ales over here for Tess and Clara before you drink the whole damn keg yourself!"

The yard laughed, the fiddle kicked into a lively reel, and the small mountain town folded them into its warmth without another word.

The bluegrass band launched into an upbeat mountain tune, the fiddle notes soaring over the crackle of the barbecue pit.

Tess and Clara sat together at a picnic table near the hayfields, their paper plates balanced on the weathered wood. Barnaby lay under the table, his head resting comfortably across Clara's boot, chewing happily on a piece of grilled beef fat Pete Henderson had brought over on a paper napkin.

Martha Gable walked past, carrying a bowl of coleslaw. She stopped, smiled down at Barnaby, and patted Tess's arm.

"He looks real sweet tonight, Tessie," Martha said, her tone warm and neighborly. "You and Clara did a wonderful job bringing him through the winter."

"Thank you, Martha," Tess said, smiling back.

When Martha walked away, Clara reached under the table, finding Tess's hand and interlacing their fingers in the cool evening shade.

"You see that?" Clara whispered, her gray eyes dancing with quiet joy. "No pitchforks. No angry town council."

"Just potato salad and brisket," Tess laughed softly, leaning in to kiss Clara's cheek under the golden mountain sunset.

They sat together at the long redwood picnic table in Dr. Bell's backyard, under the shade of three towering ponderosa pines.

The evening air was warm and still, carrying the scent of sweet barbecue smoke, grilled sweet corn, and pine needles baking in the sun. Around the yard, thirty people from Silvercreek—clinic clients, town merchants, ranchers from the valley bottom, and high school teachers—mingled with paper plates piled high with smoked brisket and potato salad.

Barnaby lay on a thick wool blanket under the table, his head resting comfortably across Clara's canvas sneakers, his tail thumping softly whenever someone dropped a scrap of grilled beef near his paws.

Marcus walked over carrying a pitcher of fresh lemonade and three glasses. He set them on the wooden table with a cheerful clatter.

"How's the patient holding up?" Marcus asked, nodding toward Barnaby.

"Eighty-four beats a minute on the mexiletine," Tess said, pouring lemonade into a glass for Clara. "No syncopal episodes in six days."

"Good," Marcus said. He pulled up a folding chair, turning it backward so he could rest his forearms on the metal backrest. He looked across the table at Tess and Clara, his weathered face creased with genuine affection.

Betty from the diner walked past, setting a bowl of warm peach cobbler between them.

"You two save room for cobbler," Betty said with a wink. "I used the Palisade peaches from the freezer. Best batch of the year."

"Thank you, Betty," Clara smiled, her face radiant in the evening light.

Around them, the conversation hummed with easy mountain familiarity: the price of alfalfa hay, the late snowpack in the high passes, the upcoming town election for school board. No one stared. No one whispered behind their napkins.

Clara reached under the table, finding Tess's hand beneath the wooden bench. Their fingers laced together naturally, resting on Tess's thigh.

Tess looked at Clara's profile against the golden mountain twilight: the quiet confidence in her posture, the soft laugh lines at the corners of her eyes, the peaceful belonging in her expression.

Barnaby let out a long, contented sigh from his blanket beneath the table, his paws twitching in a dream of summer meadows.

Later in the evening, after the cobbler plates were cleared, Dr. Bell took out his old acoustic guitar and sat on the wooden bench beside the outdoor fire pit.

He tuned the low E-string with slow, deliberate turns of the brass peg, his calloused thumb plucking a mellow chord that drifted through the pines. A few of the older ranchers pulled their lawn chairs closer to the embers, holding plastic cups of cider and talking in low, respectful murmurs.

Barnaby lifted his head from under the table, his ears cocked toward the music.

"He likes Marcus's playing," Clara said, resting her chin on her hand as she watched the dog.

"Marcus used to play guitar in the clinic recovery ward during quiet Friday night watches," Tess remembered. "He told me animals respond to low vibrational frequencies. Said it dropped canine heart rates faster than acepromazine."

Clara laughed softly, her eyes reflecting the orange glow of the birch fire.

Across the lawn, Marcus started into a slow, fingerpicked arrangement of an old mountain fiddle tune, the notes hanging clear and pure in the warm summer dark.

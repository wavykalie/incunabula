Clara's botanical illustration studio was located on the south side of the Pine Street bungalow, in what had originally been a glass-enclosed sleeping porch when the house was built in 1924.

Tess walked up the wooden ramp Nate had installed beside the front porch stairs. The smell of boiling linseed oil and dried elderberry drifted through the storm door. When Tess knocked, Clara's voice called out from inside:

"It's open, Tess! Come on through."

Tess stepped into the mudroom, kicked off her slush-coated work boots, and walked into the studio.

The room was bathed in northern mountain light that poured through six tall casement windows. Long birch worktables ran the length of the exterior wall, covered in sheets of heavy, rough-edged 300-pound Arches cold-press watercolor paper, jars of squirrel-hair brushes, ceramic mixing palettes with dried dabs of burnt umber and sap green, and rows of glass specimen jars holding dried alpine seed pods.

Barnaby was lying on a sheepskin pelt beneath the widest table, right beside an electric radiator that clicked softly as its thermostat cycled. His head was propped on a thick stack of outdated Denver telephone books that Clara had wrapped in green linen to create an elevated pillow.

"Look who's here," Clara said.

Clara was perched on a tall wooden drafting stool, holding a tiny number two sable brush between paint-stained fingers. She was working on a large-format botanical study of *Silene acaulis*—moss campion—capturing the microscopic pink petals and dense cushion foliage that clung to high-altitude scree fields above twelve thousand feet.

She set the brush down across a ceramic groove and turned on the stool. She was wearing an indigo linen apron over a gray wool sweater, her dark hair gathered at the crown of her head with a carved cherrywood hair stick.

"The kettle just clicked," Clara said, gesturing toward a cast-iron electric kettle on a side table. "I have mountain mint and dried chamomile from the garden. You look like you spent four hours wrestling calves in the cold."

"Colic surgery on an Arabian mare," Tess said, leaning back against the doorjamb and unzipping her canvas parka. "Three hours of abdominal lavage in an unheated barn up on Willow Creek. My fingers still feel like frozen cedar roots."

"Sit," Clara said. It was not a polite request; it was a firm, quiet instruction. She pointed to a deep wicker armchair cushioned with faded calico beside the radiator.

Tess sat down. The heat radiating from the iron fins hit her lower back like a hot compress. Barnaby opened his eyes, gave a slow, contented grunt, and slid his front paws across the floorboards until his claws touched the toe of Tess's wool socks.

Clara poured hot water into two heavy, unglazed earthenware mugs. The steam rose into the studio air, carrying the crisp, sharp scent of dried mint and sweet dried blossoms. She handed one mug to Tess, her fingers brushing Tess's knuckles as the ceramic changed hands.

Tess felt that small, electric point of contact register straight up her arm. She wrapped both hands around the hot mug, staring down into the dark amber liquid.

"This is the handover tea," Tess said, trying to keep her tone dry.

"It's just tea," Clara replied softly, leaning her hip against the edge of the birch drafting table. "You don't have to turn everything into a formal clinical transaction, Tess. You're allowed to sit down for ten minutes without holding a clipboard."

Tess looked around the studio. On the wall above Clara's table hung framed watercolor studies of Colorado alpine flora: dwarf clover, fairy primrose, bristlecone pine needles, and alpine forget-me-nots. Every line was drawn with forensic precision, capturing the cellular veining of the petals and the weathered cracks in the bark.

"You draw them like an anatomist," Tess observed.

"Plants have anatomy," Clara said. "They have vascular bundles, xylem tubes, and pressure gradients that lift water against gravity. If you don't understand how the sap moves through the stem, the flower you paint is just a pretty lie. It has no structure."

She looked down at Barnaby, whose chest rose and fell in sixteen calm, unhurried cycles.

"Like his heart," Clara added quietly. "You look at the ultrasound screen and see fluid dynamics and valve leaflets. I look at him and see how the cold air changes the way he holds his neck. We're both just trying to read the structure before it breaks."

Tess took a sip of the hot tea. The mint cut through the lingering taste of barn dust and antiseptic scrub.

"He's lucky he has you here," Tess murmured, looking at Clara's hands.

Clara paused, her gray eyes locking onto Tess's with an intensity that made the quiet room feel suddenly smaller.

"I think we're both lucky he brought us to the same table," Clara said.

Barnaby sighed, his chin sinking deeper into the green linen pillow, while the winter dusk gathered blue and cold against the studio glass.

Tess set her empty mug on the side table, stepping closer to Clara's birch drafting table.

On the paper before Clara lay an unfinished watercolor of *Gentiana parryi*—alpine parry gentian. The deep, intense cobalt of the bell-shaped petals had been laid down in wet-on-wet glazes, bleeding into a soft, velvety wash that captured the cool shadows of high-altitude cirques. Beside the palette lay a small magnifying loupe with a brass handle and a notebook filled with pencil sketches of dissected floral ovaries and stamens.

"The blue is incredible," Tess said quietly, leaning in. "Where do you find that pigment?"

"French ultramarine mixed with a touch of cobalt violet and bone black," Clara explained, her voice animated with quiet craftsman passion. "Gentians are difficult. If you make them too purple, they look like plastic garden flowers. If you make them too gray, they look dead. You have to capture that cold, electric sharpness that only happens at eleven thousand feet."

She picked up the brass loupe, handing it to Tess.

"Look at the margin of the petals," Clara said. "The tiny fringed teeth. In a storm, they curl inward to protect the pollen grains from freezing rain."

Tess looked through the lens. Under the magnification, Clara's brushstrokes resolved into astonishing microscopic detail—individual paper fibers holding pools of cobalt wash, tiny veins rendered in hair-thin strokes of sap green.

"You have more patience than a surgeon," Tess remarked, lowering the loupe.

Clara smiled, her gray eyes warm and luminous. "Surgeons stop bleeding, Tess. I just record what grows in the cold. It's easy to be patient when you love what you're looking at."

She looked from the painting down to Barnaby, who was watching them with calm, intelligent brown eyes.

"We should get him his evening broth," Clara said softly, turning toward the kitchen. "The sun's going behind the ridge."

Clara walked over to the wooden flat-file cabinets lining the north wall. She pulled open a wide, shallow drawer and lifted out a heavy sheet of Arches cold-press watercolor paper.

"This is what I was working on this morning before you came," Clara said, turning the sheet so Tess could see.

It was an anatomical study of *Aquilegia coerulea*—the Colorado blue columbine. The sepals were rendered in pale, luminous periwinkle, the petals flared in cream, and the slender nectar spurs curved backward with exquisite, almost mathematical fidelity. Beside the main blossom, Clara had dissected the ovary and stamen, painting the reproductive organs in microscopic cross-section with tiny, calligraphic notes in dark walnut ink.

"Look at the spur curve," Tess said, stepping closer. The faint scent of clove and linseed oil rose from the paper. "You captured the exact angle of the nectar gland."

"I spent four hours under the magnifying lamp with a dissection needle," Clara said, looking up at Tess with a modest smile. "Plants don't forgive approximations. If you get the leaf venation wrong, a botanist from Fort Collins will write you a three-page letter about your careless morphology."

"Sounds like veterinary histology," Tess said, a quiet laugh escaping her throat. "Marcus once failed a fourth-year student for confusing a cardiac myocyte with skeletal muscle under the microscope."

"Did he?" Clara asked, her eyes lighting up with interest.

"He told the student that if he couldn't recognize an intercalated disc, he had no business listening to a beating heart," Tess said.

Barnaby gave a low, content sigh from his place by the potbelly stove, stretching his paws toward the warmth.

Clara set the watercolor sheet back in the drawer and closed it with a soft glide of oiled oak. "I'm glad Marcus has you at the clinic, Tess."

"I'm glad you're in Silvercreek, Clara," Tess replied, setting her empty tea mug on the counter.

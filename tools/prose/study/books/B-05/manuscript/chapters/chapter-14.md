The Mountain View Diner sat on the corner of Granite Street and Highway 9, its aluminum siding tarnished by thirty winters of magnesium chloride spray from the state snowplows. Inside, the booth cushions were split red vinyl repaired with silver duct tape, and the air smelled of burnt bacon fat, hash browns, and stale filter coffee.

Nate Gallagher sat in the corner booth at 6:30 AM on a Friday, wearing his brown canvas chore coat and a fleece watch cap. Across the laminate table sat Joe Miller, who ran the county road grader crew and had played high school football with Nate twelve years ago.

"Pass the pepper, Nate," Joe grunted, shoveling a forkful of scrambled eggs into his mouth.

Nate slid the glass shaker across the table. He hadn't touched his plate of corned beef hash. He held a thick white ceramic coffee mug in both hands, his thumbs tracing the chipped glaze on the rim.

"Saw Tess's Subaru parked outside your place on Sunday evening," Joe said casually, his eyes fixed on his plate. "Third weekend in a row. My wife said she saw Clara carrying an old dog bed out to Tess's tailgate while you were stacking firewood out back."

Nate looked out the greasy window. The morning plow had already pushed a three-foot berm of dirty brown slush along the sidewalk.

"We share custody of Barnaby," Nate said, his voice level. "The dog's sick. He has that heart condition. Tess handles the clinic side, Clara handles the diet."

Joe chewed his toast slowly. He wiped his mustache with a paper napkin, then leaned back against the red vinyl.

"People in town find it pretty damn strange, Nate," Joe said. "You and Tess split up in April after four years of keeping your heads down and barely saying twenty words at the grocery store. Then Clara moves in, and now the two women are texting each other six times a day about dog pills and having tea on the porch like they were college roommates."

Nate set his coffee mug down on the saucer with a hard clatter.

"What people think in this town has never built a bridge or plowed a road, Joe," Nate said, his jaw tightening.

Joe held up both hands in a defensive gesture. "Hey, I'm not giving you grief, buddy. I'm just telling you what folks are saying down at the hardware store. It's an unusual setup, that's all. Clara's a pretty girl. Tess is sharp as a tack. A man could get lost in his own house with two women like that running the schedule."

Nate stared at his untouched food.

"I'm not lost in my house," Nate said quietly.

He looked down at his rough, sawdust-stained palms. He thought about coming in from the woodshop two nights ago at nine o'clock, freezing cold, with cedar dust caked in his eyebrows. He had walked into the warm kitchen expecting to find Clara working on her watercolors at the table.

Instead, Clara had been sitting on the floor with her phone against her ear, laughing softly at something Tess had said from her apartment across town. Clara had been holding Barnaby's paw, describing the way he had barked at a mule deer in the yard, her face lit with an easy, unshadowed warmth that Nate had never once seen her wear during their eight months together.

When Clara had noticed him standing in the mudroom doorway, the laughter had faded instantly into polite, careful concern: *Hey, Nate. You want me to heat up the stew?*

"She talks about Tess more than she talks about the weather, Joe," Nate said, the admission slipping out before he could stop it.

Joe looked at him, his easy humor dropping away into the sober, watchful silence of small-town men who knew when a friend's shoe had hit a nail.

"You want me to tell folks to shut their mouths?" Joe asked.

"No," Nate said, standing up and pulling a crumpled ten-dollar bill from his pocket, tossing it onto the table. "Folks can say whatever they want. It won't change the dog's heart, and it won't change what's happening on Pine Street."

He pulled his watch cap down over his ears, zipped his canvas coat to the chin, and pushed through the glass door into the biting mountain dawn.

Nate walked out of the Mountain View Cafe into the bitter morning wind, his canvas jacket zipped tight against the chill. He climbed into his Ford F-250, turning the ignition and listening to the diesel block heater cycle off as the engine rumbled to life with a plume of white exhaust.

He didn't drive straight to the high school.

Instead, he drove slowly down Pine Street, slowing as he passed the front yard of the bungalow. The snow had piled high against the porch steps where he had shoveled the path at dawn. In the front window of the sleeping porch, the warm yellow light of Clara's studio lamp was already glowing through the frosty glass.

He could see Clara's silhouette at the drafting table, her head bent over her watercolors, her dark hair pinned up with the wooden hair stick.

On the sheepskin rug beside her table, Barnaby lay sleeping in the glow of the electric radiator.

Nate sat in the idling truck, his gloved hands resting on the steering wheel.

He remembered when Tess had lived in that house. He remembered coming home from a long day at the lumber yard to find her asleep on the sofa with her veterinary pathology textbooks spread across the rug, exhausted from thirty-hour clinic rotations. He had tried to be what she needed, but their lives had run on different frequencies—his grounded in the physical reality of timber and tools, hers consumed by the fragile, high-stakes biology of healing and loss.

And now Clara was in that house, speaking Tess's language, holding the thread of Barnaby's care with an effortless, tender grace that Nate could admire but could never duplicate.

He didn't hate them for it. He couldn't.

He put the truck in gear, turned onto Granite Street, and drove toward the high school woodshop to start the morning saws.

Nate paid his check with a crisp twenty-dollar bill and walked out the glass front door of the Mountain View Cafe into the crisp morning air.

The town of Silvercreek was waking up under a pale November sun. Across the street, the hardware store had set out racks of snow shovels and bags of coarse rock salt along the wooden boardwalk. Above the town roofs, the red rock cliffs of the valley wall rose sheer and dry into the blue sky.

Nate climbed into the cab of his Ford F-250. On the bench seat beside him lay a manila folder holding the architectural sketches for the town library reading table he had agreed to build over the winter break.

He sat behind the steering wheel without turning the key.

Through his side mirror, he watched the blue clinic Subaru pull up to the post office drop box. Tess stepped out in her navy canvas jacket and fleece vest, holding a stack of laboratory specimen mailers. She moved with her usual quick, economical stride, dropping the envelopes into the metal chute with a sharp clank.

Barnaby sat in the front passenger seat of the Subaru, his gray muzzle resting against the glass, watching Tess every second she was out of the car.

Nate watched his former dog through the clear morning light. He noticed the slight gray hollow above Barnaby's eyes, the stiffness in his shoulders when the dog shifted his weight to track Tess's return.

Nate turned the ignition key. The diesel engine rumbled to life with a deep, familiar shudder, filling the cab with warm air.

He shifted into reverse, waited for an oil delivery truck to pass, and pulled out onto Main Street, heading toward the high school woodshop where eight students were waiting for their morning joinery lesson.

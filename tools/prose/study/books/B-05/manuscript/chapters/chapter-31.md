May brought the high runoff to the Blue River. The mountain snowpack, four hundred percent of normal after the record winter, was melting under a relentless spring sun, turning the river into a roaring, frothing green torrent that vibrated through the riverbed gravel.

On a warm Saturday afternoon, Tess, Clara, and Barnaby walked down to the willow meadows below the old reservoir bridge.

The meadow was brilliant with early mountain dandelion and wild blue flax. Barnaby walked slowly, his hips swaying with the stiff, careful dignity of an old retriever, his breathing steady at eighteen breaths per minute in the cool mountain breeze. He wore no leash; here, in the wide flat bend where the current slowed into a wide, sandy eddy, there was nowhere to fall.

Clara carried an old wool army blanket and a basket containing two sandwiches and a plastic container of cold roast chicken for Barnaby.

"Right here," Clara said, spreading the blanket over a dry patch of buffalo grass near the bank.

Barnaby didn't sit on the blanket. He walked straight down the gravel slope to the water's edge, his tail giving three slow, majestic sweeps. He paused, testing the temperature of the snowmelt with his front paws, then stepped out into the clear, sunlit shallows until the green water swirled around his hocks.

He stood there for five minutes, motionless, staring across the river at the aspens putting out their pale, electric-green leaves.

Tess sat on the blanket beside Clara, leaning her shoulder against Clara's arm.

"Look at him," Tess whispered. "He's remembering every duck he ever chased across that bend."

"He's beautiful," Clara said softly, resting her hand on Tess's thigh.

Barnaby turned around in the shallows, took two deliberate mouthfuls of river water, and splashed his way back up the bank. He didn't shake—his balance was too delicate now for the violent rotary torque of a retriever shake—he simply walked over to the blanket, lowered his front end with a soft grunt, and flopped onto his side in the warm grass between them.

Clara opened the container, offering him a tender strip of white chicken breast. Barnaby took it from her fingertips with exquisite, gentle politeness, swallowed it whole, and rested his chin across Tess's ankles.

Tess closed her eyes, feeling the warm May sun soak through her denim shirt, the cool breeze coming off the snowfields on the Continental Divide, and the solid, rhythmic breathing of the dog against her bones.

Tess packed the empty chicken container into the wicker basket, watching the sun glint off the ripples of the Blue River.

Beside her on the army blanket, Clara was sketching in a small leather-bound field notebook, her pencil moving across the paper in rapid, delicate strokes, capturing the curve of Barnaby's paws as he slept in the warm clover.

"Look at the way his shoulder muscles have relaxed," Clara murmured, not looking up from her drawing. "When we were on Pine Street in November, his back was always hunched against the cold. Today he looks like he's made of butter."

"The butorphanol helps his chest discomfort," Tess explained, leaning her head on Clara's shoulder. "And the warm weather dilates his cutaneous vessels, taking afterload off the left ventricle. He feels good because his heart doesn't have to fight the vasoconstriction."

Clara stopped her pencil, looking down at the sketch of the sleeping golden retriever.

"It's a gift, Tess," Clara whispered softly, laying her palm against Tess's cheek. "Every single afternoon like this is a gift."

Tess turned her face into Clara's palm, kissing the warm center of her hand, while Barnaby exhaled a long, peaceful breath into the mountain air.

They sat on a flat granite boulder on the gravel bar while Barnaby explored the shallow water.

The sun beat down warm and clean through the budding cottonwood branches. Across the creek, the south-facing bank was carpeted in bright yellow buttercups and wild iris shoots pushing through the winter mulch.

Barnaby waded out until the cold mountain runoff lapped against his golden brisket. He dipped his muzzle into the current, biting playfully at the silver ripples before lifting his head to let cold water cascade from his whiskers.

"Look at his tail," Clara said, leaning her head against Tess's shoulder. "It hasn't stopped wagging since we turned off the highway."

"Cold water stimulates the vagus nerve," Tess noted, a soft smile curving her lips. "It slows the heart rate naturally. He feels the relief in his chest."

Barnaby splashed back toward the gravel bar, holding a waterlogged pine branch between his jaws like a trophy. He deposited the wet stick directly across Clara's boots, gave a mighty shake that sent a spray of cold creek water over both of their faces, and rested his wet chin on Tess's knee.

"You scoundrel," Tess laughed, wiping her wet cheek with the sleeve of her flannel shirt.

Barnaby blinked his dark brown eyes, his tongue lolling happily in a healthy, rose-pink grin.

Clara reached down and scratched the thick fur along his neck, her fingers sinking into the damp ruff. "Good boy, Barnaby. The best boy in the valley."

They stayed by the water until the shadows of the cottonwoods stretched across the gravel, drinking cold tea from the thermos while Barnaby dozed in the warm sand beside them.

When the sun began to dip behind the western ridge, the mountain air cooled by ten degrees in fifteen minutes.

Tess unrolled the spare wool blanket from the back of the Subaru, wrapping it around Barnaby's hindquarters as he lay on the gravel bar. He didn't protest; he simply rested his muzzle on the soft plaid wool and closed his eyes.

Clara poured the remaining tea from the thermos into a single mug, handing it to Tess before sitting down beside her on the granite stone.

"Marcus told me this morning that the clinic had four newborn calves over the weekend," Clara said, leaning her head against Tess's shoulder.

"Three heifers and a bull," Tess said, taking a sip of the warm mint tea. "The Miller ranch up on Cottonwood Creek had a dystocia at three in the morning. Old man Miller was so tired he could barely hold the flashlight while I manipulated the front legs."

"Did the calf make it?"

"Eighty-pound heifer," Tess said, a quiet satisfaction warming her voice. "Stood up twenty minutes after delivery and nursed."

Clara smiled into the mountain dusk. "You have such good hands, Tess."

Tess turned her head, pressing her lips against Clara's temple, breathing in the scent of rain, wool, and fresh cedar in Clara's hair.

The pill splitter on Tess's kitchen counter was made of blue transparent acrylic, with a triangular stainless steel blade set into the lid that had dulled over three years of cutting thyroid tablets. Tess pressed down on a yellow oblong of pimobendan. The tablet crumbled into uneven fragments—one chunk roughly three milligrams, the other two splintering into fine powder against the plastic groove.

"Damn it," Tess muttered.

She reached for a razor blade from the magnetic strip beside the sink, carefully scraping the yellow powder into a spoon. Pimobendan had a narrow therapeutic window. Too little, and the positive inotropic effect failed to support systolic contraction; too much, and the dog risked ventricular premature contractions.

She checked the digital clock on the microwave: 6:48 AM. Twelve minutes before the seven o'clock window.

The handover had occurred forty-eight hours ago. Barnaby lay on his memory foam orthopedic mattress in the corner of her living room, his front paws crossed, his tail giving a single, languid wag when she looked his way. He knew the kitchen routine. He knew the click of the cabinet door meant food was coming, but he also knew the sharp, bitter smell of crushed pharmaceuticals.

Tess opened the refrigerator. On the top shelf, wedged between a carton of almond milk and a jar of pickled jalapeños, sat a sixteen-ounce glass Mason jar.

A square of unbleached kraft paper was tied around the lid with kitchen twine. On the paper, written in sepia ink with fine botanical cross-hatching, was a small drawing of an alpine sweet potato vine, complete with delicate root nodules and heart-shaped leaves. Beneath the illustration, in Clara's handwriting:

*Roasted garnet yams mashed with unsalted bone broth and a pinch of ground cinnamon. No sodium. Blends smoothly around the pills so he won't spit out the bitter core. Keep refrigerated. —C.*

Tess stared at the jar.

She unscrewed the metal band. The sweet potato mash smelled warm, earthy, slightly sweet from the oven caramelization. She dipped a teaspoon into the orange puree. It was silky, whipped to an exact consistency that would coat the crumbled pimobendan without requiring the dog to chew.

Tess had worked in veterinary medicine for seven years. She had watched hundreds of owners struggle with medication compliance. They shoved pills down dogs' throats until their knuckles were bruised and their pets ran to hide under the bed. They bought commercial pill pockets that cost twelve dollars a bag and were loaded with potassium sorbate and sodium chloride—the exact compounds guaranteed to worsen cardiac fluid retention.

Nobody had ever roasted garnet yams, whipped them with homemade broth, and delivered them in a labeled glass jar with an ink sketch on the lid.

Tess placed the three-milligram chunk and the yellow powder into the center of the spoonful, folding the sweet potato puree over the medicine like an envelope. She walked over to Barnaby's bed.

"Breakfast prep, bear," she said.

Barnaby sat up, his ears pricking forward. He extended his pink tongue, engulfing the entire spoon in one enthusiastic, sweeping motion. He swallowed without a single cough. His tongue flicked out twice, cleaning his black flews, and he looked up at her, waiting for more.

"Good boy," Tess whispered.

She set the spoon in the sink, then picked up the kraft paper tag from the counter. Her thumb traced the edge of the paper where Clara's scissors had cut a clean bevel. The sepia ink was waterproof, an artist's pigment that wouldn't run even if condensation dripped from the glass.

Tess opened the third drawer of her desk—the drawer where she kept her divorce decree, her clinic certification cards, and the spare keys to the Subaru. She laid the small paper tag flat in the wooden tray beside her brass calipers, closed the drawer, and turned the kettle on to boil.

The morning sun hit the Continental Divide, turning the snowfields on the high peaks a blinding, icy rose. Tess stood by the window with her coffee mug, watching the mountain chickadees flit between the bare aspen branches.

She thought about Clara sitting at the kitchen table on Pine Street, roasting yams in the dark of early morning, humming under her breath while the winter wind whistled through the cedar siding. It was a picture of domestic care that Tess had never known how to construct. In her four years with Nate, food had been fuel: quick bowls of chili before a night shift, frozen breakfast burritos eaten in the truck.

Caring for Barnaby had stripped away that clinical detachment. It forced her to touch the food, to measure the warmth, to acknowledge that survival was not just milligrams and milliequivalents, but patience and sweet broth.

Barnaby let out a long, shuddering sigh from his bed, curled his paws under his brisket, and closed his eyes in the warm sunlight.

Tess stood at her kitchen sink, washing Barnaby's empty stainless steel bowl with a yellow sponge. The water ran hot, steaming against the cold porcelain, carrying away the sweet, earthy smell of Clara's roasted yam puree.

Outside the kitchen window, the morning sun was slowly creeping down the face of Mount Silverheels, turning the granite avalanche chutes from blue shadow into blazing gold.

Barnaby had walked back to his orthopedic mattress by the woodstove. He lay on his side, his four legs stretched out, his breath moving with a calm, regular rhythm that made Tess's chest feel lighter than it had in days.

She thought about the morning Nate had brought Barnaby home in November of 2015.

It had been four degrees outside, the snow piled three feet deep along the fences of their first rental cabin on Willow Creek. Nate had walked in through the front door carrying a cardboard apple box from the Leadville grocery store, his coat covered in frost. Inside the box, wrapped in an old flannel work shirt, was an eight-week-old golden retriever pup with ribs sticking out like birdcages and eyes clouded with dehydration.

*Found him at the county shelter,* Nate had said, his voice hesitant, worried that she would be angry about the unexpected expense. *They said he came down with parvovirus yesterday. They were going to put him down at noon if nobody took him.*

Tess hadn't said a word. She had stripped off her coat, carried the box straight to the kitchen table, and run an intravenous catheter into the puppy's tiny cephalic vein while Nate held the flashlight. She had stayed awake for seventy-two hours, sleeping on the floor with her hand on the puppy's neck, administering metoclopramide, ampicillin, and warm lactated Ringer's until the vomiting stopped and the fever broke.

When the pup had finally stood up on wobbling paws and licked Nate's thumb, Nate had wept openly at the kitchen table.

They had named him Barnaby that morning.

Tess dried the stainless steel bowl with a clean dish towel and set it on the rubber feeding mat.

She looked at Barnaby sleeping in the corner. Eleven years of shared life. Every trail in this valley carried his pawprints; every mountain pass held the memory of his golden coat running through the willow brush.

The marriage had unraveled, dying not in a sudden fire, but in a slow, quiet freezing of emotional warmth. But the dog remained, an enduring anchor linking past and present.

She walked into the living room, knelt beside the bed, and ran her fingers through the long, feathery fur behind his ears.

Barnaby opened his eyes, gave a soft grunt of canine satisfaction, and rested his heavy chin across her knee. Tess sat with him in the morning light, listening to the crackle of the woodstove, until the town church bells chimed eight o'clock across the valley.

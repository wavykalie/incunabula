The diaphragm of the Littmann Classic III was cold against the flat of Tess's thumb. She warmed the chill steel in her palm, breathing through her nose to catch the familiar baseline of the back room: chlorhexidine scrub, autoclaved surgical linen, dried alfalfa from the guinea pig hutch in kennel four. Under her knuckles, Barnaby's ribs rose and fell in a steady, heavy cadence. Fourteen breaths a minute. Good resting rhythm. He sat on the ribbed rubber mat of exam table two with his graying muzzle resting on the toe of her left work boot, heavy as a sack of winter potatoes.

"Hold still, bear," Tess said softly.

He gave one solid thump of his tail against the stainless steel skirt of the table.

She set the diaphragm just behind his left elbow, beneath the triceps muscle where the apex beat met the fifth intercostal space. She expected the slow, familiar click of an old retriever's valve, the dry friction of eleven years running through lodgepole scrub and retrieving dead mallards from the marsh below Turquoise Lake. Instead, the binaurals drove a wet, rushing rasp straight into her ears.

Whoosh-dup. Whoosh-dup. Whoosh-dup.

The sound was harsh, loud, occupying the entire systolic interval. It didn't taper. It didn't wait for exertion. It masked the first heart sound completely, a turbulent jet of high-pressure blood regurgitating backward through an incompetent mitral valve into the left atrium.

Tess froze. Her fingers pressed into the golden coat.

"Come on," she whispered. "No."

She slid the chestpiece two centimeters dorsal, listening over the left atrium. The murmur flared louder, radiating toward the spine. A grade five out of six. A faint thrill vibrated against her fingertips. She closed her eyes and counted. Pulse rate ninety-four. Femoral pulse slightly snappy, bounding with the sudden volume shift.

Marcus walked in from the pharmacy alcove with a brown plastic bottle of cephalexin and a half-empty mug of diner coffee. The soles of his worn Dansko clogs clicked against the linoleum. He didn't look up from the label until he noticed how still she was standing.

"Ear infection on the Spaniel cleared up," he said. Then he stopped. He looked at her hand holding the stethoscope. "Tess?"

"Take a listen," she said. Her voice came out flat, clipped into clinic efficiency. "Left apex."

Marcus set his coffee on the counter beside the centrifuge. He unhooked his own Littmann from around his neck. The tubing was stiff with age, wrapped in black tape near the binaural spring. He stepped up to the table. Barnaby lifted his head to lick the doctor's wrist. Marcus rested one broad palm over the dog's forehead, murmured something low and indistinct, and placed the bell against the chest.

The wall clock ticked. The suction unit in dental hummed through the wall.

For forty seconds, Marcus didn't move. His gray eyebrows drew down until they touched the rims of his wire bifocals. He moved the bell half an inch cranial, then back to the mitral area. Finally, he straightened up and pulled the earpieces down to rest on his collarbone.

"When did he last have a chest film?" Marcus asked.

"Six months ago," Tess said. "Before the divorce finalization. He was clean. No cardiomegaly. Normal vertebral heart score. Nine point five."

"Well," Marcus said gently. He tapped his prescription pad against his forearm in three measured beats. "That valve isn't closing, kiddo."

"It's a five," Tess said. "The jet is wide. Listen to that second sound split."

"It's a five," Marcus agreed. "The jet is wide. The leaflets are thickening. In an eleven-year-old golden, it moves when it wants to."

Tess felt the sudden, metallic dryness at the back of her mouth that always came before a crash cart call. She slipped two fingers down to Barnaby's groin, checking the pulse quality again. Strong, but too quick to drop off. The ventricle was compensating by pumping harder against less resistance, throwing volume backward into a chamber never built to take systemic pressure.

"He hasn't been coughing," she said. The defense sounded weak even to her own ears. "He ran the ditch trail on Thursday. He ate his kibble. He finished a whole bowl of broth this morning."

"Coughing comes when the atrium pushes the mainstem bronchus up," Marcus said. He reached over and stroked Barnaby's flank, feeling the coarse hair over the spine. "You know the timeline as well as I do, Tess. We need the probe."

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "First appointment before surgeries. We'll shave his chest, get the four-chamber view, measure the left atrium to aorta ratio. If it's over one point six, we start pimobendan."

"He's supposed to go to Pine Street tonight," Tess added. The words tasted like cold tin. "It's Nate's week."

Marcus picked up his coffee mug. He took a sip, staring down at the retriever. "You want me to call Nate?"

"No," Tess said. "I'll do it. Nate doesn't understand auscultation anyway. He'll think it's a cold."

"Clara's there, isn't she?"

Tess didn't answer right away. She wound the rubber tubing of her stethoscope around her palm, tighter than necessary, until the black loops dug into her skin. Clara Evans had lived in Nate's cedar-shingled house on Pine Street for four months now. Clara kept pressed lupine in glass frames and brewed tea that smelled of peppermint and dried nettle. Clara didn't own a stethoscope, but she was the one who remembered to brush Barnaby's undercoat when the autumn shedding began.

"Yeah," Tess said. "Clara's there."

Marcus leaned his hip against the counter, his eyes compassionate behind his wire rims. "Tess, you've been working seventy-hour weeks since the separation. You don't have to carry this case as an island."

"He's my dog, Marcus," Tess said, her voice tight. "Nate and I raised him from eight weeks. When Nate brought him home from the Leadville rescue in that cardboard crate, he had parvo. I sat up three straight nights on the kitchen floor with a subcutaneous catheter and lactated Ringer's. I know his baseline better than anyone."

"Knowing his baseline doesn't make you bulletproof," Marcus remarked quietly. "Degenerative mitral disease is an unforgiving train. It runs on biology, not sentiment. If you treat every regurgitant murmur like a personal failure, this winter will break you in two."

Tess looked down at the table. Barnaby had rested his chin flat against the rubber mat, his amber eyes gazing up at her with unreserved devotion. He didn't know his mitral leaflets were failing. He didn't know his left atrium was expanding under pressure. He only knew that her hands were familiar, that the rubber mat was warm, and that she was near.

"I'll pack his joint chews," Tess said, stepping away from the table to gather her clipboard. "And his prescription diet. I'll make sure Nate knows not to let him run the logging roads."

Marcus watched her for a beat, then sighed and reached for his cephalexin bottle. "Seven-thirty tomorrow, Tess. Don't be late. We need that acoustic window before the day gets crazy."

Barnaby gave another soft grunt, lifted his heavy skull, and licked the tip of Tess's index finger where she held the pen.

Tess wiped her thumb against her scrub pants, snapped off her exam gloves, and walked to the pharmacy alcove to fill the transport bag.

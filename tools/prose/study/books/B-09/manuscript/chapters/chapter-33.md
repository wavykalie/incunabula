# Chapter 33: The Flight of Moretus

The circle of dockers closed with the slow, crushing momentum of an advancing river dike.

Moretus backed away across the ice, his fine leather shoes slipping on the crusted snow, his crimson marten-lined cloak trailing in the dirty slush. The ivory walking cane had dropped from his hand; his kidskin gloves were stained with mud where he had stumbled against an ice ridge.

"Dirck," the proctor gasped, his hands outstretched in desperate, frantic entreaty. "Listen to reason. I have fifty guilders in my purse. I can give you the safe-conduct for the Zeeland boats. I can speak to the burgomaster—"

"The burgomaster is a Spanish pensioner like yourself," Master Dirck said, his iron cargo hook held steady before him. "And you have no safe-conduct for the ice, Master Proctor."

Behind Moretus, two young boatmen were already cutting through the ice with their heavy iron ice-chisels.

*Chink. Chink. Chunk.*

The sharp steel drove into the gray surface thirty paces behind the proctor, opening a jagged, black hole four feet wide where the deep, freezing current of the Scheldt boiled beneath the ice shelf, dark and murderous with river mud.

"The ice hole," a caulker shouted, his face contorted with eight months of starvation and rage. "Let the guild proctor test the water he sold to Parma! Throw him in! Let him swim down to Calloo with his Spanish ducats in his pockets!"

A roar of furious agreement went up from fifty throats.

The four guild beadles dropped their ash partisans onto the ice with a frantic clatter of iron, turned on their heels, and ran like terrified sheep toward the stone stairs of the Crane Wharf, scrambling over the frozen masonry without looking back at their master.

Moretus was left alone on the ice.

He stood twenty feet from the open ice hole, his breath coming in ragged, hysterical gasps, his eyes wide and white with the absolute, unvarnished terror of a man who realized that his gold, his titles, and his imperial warrants were meaningless against the raw, muscular fury of the men he had starved.

He looked at Katrijn.

She stood beside the sledge, her hands folded in her apron, her face motionless as carved oak.

"Katrijn," Moretus choked out, his voice cracking into a high, pathetic squeak. "Tell them. Tell them you stole the text. Tell them it was a forgery."

Katrijn looked at him.

Her eyes were cold, clear, devoid of mercy and devoid of malice.

"Hendrik forgave his executioner upon the scaffold, Master Moretus," she said, her voice carrying over the mutter of the crowd. "He forgave the man who swung the broadsword. But he did not forgive the man who sold the truth for forty pieces of foreign gold."

She turned to Master Dirck.

"Do not drown him in the river," Katrijn said. "The Scheldt is foul enough without a traitor's carcass. Let him run to the citadel. Let him run to Alexander Farnese and tell his Spanish master that Antwerp has read the book."

Master Dirck stared at Katrijn for three seconds. Then he lowered his iron cargo hook, spat into the snow, and looked at Moretus.

"You have two minutes, proctor," the porter said, his voice flat as ditch water. "Run toward the redoubt before my boys decide your cloak is worth more than your skin."

Moretus did not wait for a second warning.

He gathered the skirts of his crimson cloak in both hands, turned, and fled across the frozen Scheldt toward the distant palisade of the Spanish pontoon bridge. His boots slipped and skittered across the crusted snow; he fell once onto his hands and knees, scrambled up with a whimper of terror, and ran headlong into the morning fog like a hunted hare, his marten fur flapping behind him in the river wind.

A thunderous roar of laughter and jeers went up from the dockers.

Master Dirck turned back to the sledge. He reached into the severed wicker hamper, lifted two bundles of the printed octavos, and held them high above his head.

"Jan," he shouted to the carpenter on the crane. "Take twenty copies to the Saint Nicholas guildhall. Tell the carters to hitch their horses. Pieter, take two bundles to the meat market. Tell the weavers on the Predikherenrei that Hendrik Vane's press has answered the Spanish decree."

The dockers surged around the sledge, their rough, calloused hands reaching into the wicker hampers, taking the white pamphlets with an eager, reverent hunger that exceeded their desire for bread.

Within ten minutes, the two wicker hampers were bare to the willow bottom.

Four hundred copies of *A True Record* were moving through the fog, carried beneath woolen jerkins and tucked inside coarse leather aprons, fanning out through the gates, alleys, and guildhalls of the besieged city.

Katrijn stood on the empty sledge runners, looking out across the gray ice where Moretus's tracks vanished into the northern mist.

Giles stood beside her, his shoulders straight, his hand resting on the ash crossbar, his scarred ear pale in the rising sun.

"We must go to the cellar," Giles said softly.

Moretus reached the first pressure ridge of the frozen Scheldt, his boots slipping on the glazed ice, his hands clawing at the rough hummocks of frozen slush. Behind him, the roar of the crowd on the Crane Wharf rose in a wave of mocking derision that echoed off the brick gables of Sint-Jansvliet.

He looked toward the Spanish pontoon bridge half a mile down the current. The thirty-two armed ketches were silent in the morning mist, their guns pointed toward the sea, powerless to protect a fleeing guild proctor from the rage of his own citizens. His marten-lined cloak tore on an iron mooring ring as he scrambled past a frozen barge, but he did not pause to look back.

The ice shelf groaned beneath Moretus's desperate feet as he scrambled across the pressure ridges toward the palisade of Calloo. The fine marten fur of his cloak was torn, dragged through freezing mud and salt slush; his ivory cane lay broken in the snow behind him, kicked into the open ice hole by an angry caulker.

From the Crane Wharf, seventy voices roared with derisive laughter that echoed off the stone walls of the river warehouses, watching the grand inquisitor of the Stationers' Guild flee like a cornered rat into the gray mist of the Spanish lines.

The ink-pelts were wearing thin at the crown. Giles held the leather up to the candle flame, checking for pinholes where the stuffing might burst through under pressure. With a bone needle and waxed shoemaker's thread, he took four quick stitches through the seam, securing the wool fleece tight against the wooden core. The smell of hot pitch rose from the brazier where he kept a tin cup of resin warm to seal the thread ends.

Katrijn watched him work with steady admiration. For twenty years Giles had lived in the roar of the press, his ears deafened by the thunder of the oak bed and the clanking of the iron rounce. He had lost his hearing, but his hands had gained a miraculous sensitivity to weight and balance. He could tell within half an ounce whether a sheet of paper had received the correct volume of ink, merely by the drag of the pelt against the lead face.

"Look at the seventh line of the second page," Katrijn said, leaning over the stone and pointing with her bodkin. "The ligature for *ct* has a cracked loop. If the ink clogs the break, the word *fact* will read as *fart* to any drunken docker who buys the sheet at the tavern door."

Giles leaned down, his squinting gray eyes an inch from the metal. He picked up an iron pincers, plucked the defective sort from the galley with a surgeon's deftness, and dropped a fresh sort from the case into the vacant space. With his thumb, he drove the lead home against the brass rule until it seated flush against the shoulder of the adjacent letter. He looked up at Katrijn and nodded once, his scarred lips tightening in a grim, silent grin.

Giles leaned his massive bulk over the composing stone, checking the alignment of the columns with a boxwood rule. His fingers were covered with black ink up to the knuckles, the varnish worked deep into the grain of his scarred palms. He had worked twenty years at the press without a holiday, through the Spanish Fury, through the French Fury, through the great frosts that froze the Scheldt from bank to bank.

Katrijn watched his concentration with profound respect. In the silent world where Giles lived, every movement had its own precise, physical language. He knew the weight of a sheet of paper by the air it displaced as it settled upon the tympan; he knew the thickness of the ink by the drag of the sheepskin against the metal sorts; he knew the balance of the timber press by the vibration that traveled up through his boots into his knees.

"We have enough paper for four hundred sheets, Giles," she whispered, touching his broad shoulder to gain his attention. She held up four fingers in the candlelight. Giles looked at her hand, nodded with a curt, resolute jerk of his chin, and reached for the wooden lever with a look of fierce, unyielding pride.

Giles picked up an iron pincers and extracted a damaged sort of lowercase *m* from the fourth line of the page. The lead letter was bruised at the shoulder, worn down by ten thousand impressions on Hendrik's old press. He dropped the damaged type into the waste-box and selected a crisp replacement from the upper case, pressing it into place between the setting rule and the wooden reglet with his thumb.

Katrijn watched the compositors' fingers dance across the partitioned compartments of the upper case, plucking capital letters from the small wooden boxes with instinctive speed. Each lead sort was a weapon in the silent war against the Spanish council, cast in the secret furnace and sharpened by the cold truth of the printed word.

"Yes," Katrijn said. "We have the press to dismantle before the garrison arrives."

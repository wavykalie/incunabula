# Chapter 25: The Rejection of Gold

Van Hout pulled the knife from his leather belt.

For half a second, Giles lifted the iron crowbar to strike him down, but the alderman did not turn the blade toward them. He slashed the heavy silk lining of his broadcloth cloak with three violent jerks of the steel.

From the opened seam, a stream of gold coins spilled across the marble composing stone.

Instead of silver carolus guilders, Spanish gold ducats poured from the severed hem—heavy, lustrous, stamped with the profile of Philip and the coat of arms of Castile and Aragon. They rolled across the black ink stains, chinking against the lead chases, gleaming with an obscene, blinding brilliance in the yellow tallow light.

"Fifty ducats," Van Hout gasped, his hands clawing the coins together into a glittering heap. "Take them. Every coin is full weight, struck at the royal mint in Brussels. It will buy passage for you, the boy, and the children to England on an armed ketch from Middelburg. You can buy a stone house in London. You can open a new shop on Fleet Street with three brass presses."

He pushed the pile of gold toward Katrijn's chest.

Katrijn looked at the gold.

In fifty ducats lay freedom: an end to the coal dust, an end to the freezing cellar drafts, an end to the terror of boots on the cobblestones. In England, Queen Elizabeth's printers operated under the royal charter; there were no Spanish pikemen at the bridge heads, no Inquisitorial censors searching kitchens for lampblack stains. Clara and Pieter would have buttered wheaten bread every morning, wool stockings without holes, and a warm room with glass windows overlooking the Thames.

She reached out her hand.

Her soot-blackened fingers touched the cold, smooth metal of the top ducat.

She picked it up, turned it in the candlelight, and looked at the date stamped beneath the Habsburg crest: *1584.*

The year Hendrik died.

This was the Spanish gold that Parma had sent to the municipal council. The money paid to the aldermen to starve the river redoubts. The silver and gold that had bought the executioner's broadsword, the wood for the gallows, and the iron chains that locked the Scheldt.

She looked up at Van Hout.

"Where did this gold come from, Alderman?"

Van Hout swallowed hard, his eyes shifting away from hers. "It is my private fortune. From my spice warehouses at the New Bourse."

"You lie," Katrijn said, her voice dropping to a flat, deadly whisper. "This is the treasury of the garrison. The bribe paid to the council committee on the fourteenth of May. You kept your share in the hem of your coat while you watched my husband bleed into the sawdust."

She threw the gold ducat directly into Van Hout's face.

The heavy coin struck him across the cheekbone, cutting the skin, leaving a thin bead of crimson blood that welled on his pale cheek. The ducat fell to the flagstones with a dull, hollow ring.

"Pick up your foreign blood-money," Katrijn commanded.

"Katrijn—"

"Pick it up!" she snarled, her eyes burning with an incandescent rage that made the alderman cower against the brick vault. "If you leave one piece of that Spanish filth on my stone, I will call the weavers from the Predikherenrei and tell them the Warden of the Dikes is hiding his gold in my coal hole! They will tear you into pieces before the watch can reach the corner!"

Van Hout knelt on the stone floor, his hands trembling as he scrambled to scoop the ducats back into his torn cloak lining, sobbing in humiliated terror.

Katrijn turned her back on him.

The gold coins lay scattered across the black marble composing stone, gleaming like spilled grease under the yellow tallow flame. Each ducat was stamped with the high-nosed profile of King Philip and the imperial motto: *Dominus Mihi Adiutor.* Katrijn looked at the gold with the cold, unyielding detachment of an artisan who knew the price of blood in Brabant.

Hendrik had earned twelve copper stuivers a day when he was an apprentice, sleeping under the paper racks and eating salted cabbage. In fifty gold ducats lay the wealth of a merchant's lifetime. Yet the metal felt greasy, cold, carrying the faint, metallic scent of the Spanish garrison's pay-chests that had starved the river redoubts.

Katrijn stood before the composing stone, her hands folded over her coarse linen apron. The pile of Spanish gold ducats lay untouched between them, gleaming with an obscene brilliance in the dim tallow flame. Each coin was stamped with the arms of Castile, Leon, and Aragon: the symbols of an empire that had brought war, famine, and terror to the cities of the Low Countries.

"Take the gold, Alderman," she repeated, her voice cutting through the stillness like a cold chisel. "Take it to the river boatmen. You may need it to buy bread when the Spanish tercios are driven into the polders."

Katrijn ran her finger along the edge of the copper galley. The brass corners had been riveted with tiny bronze pins, ground flat with a file so that no burr would snag the damp rag paper as it slid from the stone.

"Giles," she said, picking up the two wicker hampers. "Take the second load into the sewer pipe. The frost is lifting on the river."

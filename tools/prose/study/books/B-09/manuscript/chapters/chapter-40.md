# Chapter 40: The First Clean Page

The spring thaw arrived in the second week of April.

The ice melted from the canals with a soft, musical gurgling, carrying the gray winter slush out into the deep green current of the Scheldt. Sunlight—warm, golden, smelling of wet earth and budding willow trees—poured through the high arched windows of the upper workshop on the Sint-Jansvliet quayside.

The shop had once belonged to an old sailmaker who had retired to Zeeland.

Now, the clean pine floorboards were scrubbed white with lye; the brick walls were freshly lime-washed; and in the center of the room, standing proud and solid in the morning light, stood the reassembled common press.

Giles had planed the split oak cheek with an adze, reinforcing the joint with two forged iron straps bolted clean through the heartwood.

The iron screw spindle, freshly cleaned of coal dust and greased with sweet olive oil, turned in its brass nut with a smooth, silent glide that required no more force than the push of two fingers. The twin sheepskin ink-balls hung from their hooks beside the stone, their leather newly re-stuffed with washed fleece, soft and bouncy as young lambs.

Katrijn stood at the composing stone.

She held the walnut composing stick in her left hand, her fingers moving across the compartments of a brand-new upper and lower case of English Pica type, bought with the remaining silver guilders from the merchant fleet at the water gate.

The metal was clean, bright as newly minted silver, casting tiny sparkles of light across the white paper pile.

She set the letters one by one, upside down and left to right, her thumb guiding each sort against the brass rule with rhythmic precision:

*P-R-I-N-T-E-D.*

She did not set an anonymous text. She did not set a Latin indictment or a municipal receipt.

She set her own title page:

*THE COVENANT OF CRAFTSMEN.*
*Being an Account of the Free Liberties and Guild Rights of the City of Antwerp.*
*Printed by Katrijn Vane, Widow and Master Printer.*
*At Her Shop upon the Sint-Jansvliet.*
*Anno Domini 1585.*

She transferred the completed lines to the iron chase, locked the oak quoins with three firm taps of the wooden mallet, and slid the chase onto the bed of the carriage.

Giles stepped to the press, his scarred face glowing in the spring sunlight.

He took the twin ink-balls, worked the fresh walnut ink across the leather domes, and rocked them over the forme. The clean Pica letters took the black oil with a sharp, glistening sheen.

Katrijn laid a fresh sheet of white Dutch rag paper onto the tympan points.

She folded down the frisket, turned the wooden rounce handle two turns forward, and nodded to Giles.

"Pull," she said.

Giles caught the ash lever with both hands, bent his knees, and threw his weight against the wood.

The platen descended. The timber struck the bed with a firm, solid, magnificent blow that echoed through the sunlit workshop.

"Up," Katrijn commanded.

Giles eased the bar back. Katrijn rolled the carriage out, lifted the tympan, and peeled the paper from the points.

The impression was perfection.

The black letters stood out against the snowy linen sheet with razor-sharp authority, clean, beautiful, permanent, bearing her own name beneath the mark of the hammer and the anvil.

The upper workshop smelled of fresh pine shavings, clean linseed oil, and spring rain. Through the open leaded windows, the sound of the river came up from the quayside: the clinking of anchor chains, the shouts of the boatmen, the song of the larks rising over the green dikes of the polder.

Katrijn ran her hand over the oak carriage of the press. The timber was smooth, seasoned, solid as stone. Beside her, Giles was beating the new sheepskin ink-balls against the block, his face bright with the pride of a master pressman who had seen the battle through to the end.

The sunlight poured through the leaded glass windows of the upper shop on Sint-Jansvliet, warm and golden, smelling of fresh river water and blooming willow dikes. The reassembled common press stood in the center of the clean pine floorboards, its iron screw gleaming with fresh oil, its oak cheeks straight and true.

Katrijn held the printed sheet up to the morning breeze. The black Pica letters stood out against the snowy Dutch rag paper with bold, razor-sharp authority: *Printed by Katrijn Vane, Widow and Master Printer, 1585.* The press was free, the city was alive, and her name was set in lead for the world to see.

The ketch dipped into the cold gray swell of the Western Scheldt, the salt spray whipping across the deck like handfuls of gravel. Katrijn stood by the tiller beside the Dutch master, her wool cloak pinned tight against the biting gale. Behind them, the great spire of Antwerp Cathedral rose against the smoky horizon, a pale finger of stone pointing into the winter clouds, surrounded by the smudge of a hundred Spanish campfires.

Down in the dark cargo hold, Giles sat on an upturned cask of salt herring, his deaf head resting against the rough oak planking of the hull. Between his knees, he held the pine chest containing Hendrik's matrices and the two copper composing sticks. The timber hull creaked and strained against the surge of the North Sea tide, but the metal inside was dry and safe.

Katrijn reached into the pocket of her cloak and took out the final proof sheet. The ink had dried hard and black upon the rag paper, the sharp Garamont types biting deep into the linen. She folded the sheet into four and tucked it inside the breast of her bodice, right against her skin. The Spanish army had taken the walls, the Duke of Parma had proclaimed the King's peace, and the priests were chanting Te Deum in the looted cathedral. But the words had flown across the provinces. In Middelburg, in Leiden, and in Amsterdam, the presses were already turning.

The wind shifted into the north-east, bringing the smell of salt marsh and open sea across the deck. Katrijn stood by the rail, watching the distant low dunes of Zeeland rise from the gray water like sleeping whales. Beside her, Giles pulled his rough wool cap down over his ears, his squinting gray eyes following the white gulls that wheeled above the wake.

In her pocket, the proof sheet rustled softly against her wool kirtle. The ink was dry now, hard as stone, permanent as the North Sea tide. The Duke of Parma had won the gates of Antwerp, but the words that had broken his victory were already flying north on every Dutch ketch that ran the river. In Middelburg, the presses would begin printing before nightfall, and the story of the unlicensed press would be read in every free town from Flushing to the Zuiderzee.

Katrijn looked back at the distant spire of Antwerp, fading into the sea mist behind the low islands of Zeeland. The salt wind tasted clean and cold upon her lips, sweeping away the soot and sulfur of the siege.

Katrijn carried the fresh print to the open window, holding it up to the sun to dry in the warm spring breeze that blew off the free river.

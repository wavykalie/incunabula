# Chapter 11: The Proctor's Visit

The rap at the kitchen street door came at noon, while Katrijn was paring cold turnip roots into an earthen pot.

She did not startle. In the months since the Vrijdagmarkt raid, she had trained her nerves to obey the silence of the workshop. She wiped the iron paring blade on her linen apron, laid it on the cutting board beside three parsnip tops, and smoothed her black woolen skirt before walking to the threshold.

Through the heavy oak door, she heard the scrape of fine Spanish leather shoes on the damp stoop.

She drew the bolt.

Inspector Balthasar Moretus stood on the doorstep, framed against the gray light of the Sint-Jansvliet quayside. Behind him, standing in the muddy gutter, waited two uniformed guild beadles in brown woolen tabards, each holding a five-foot ash staff tipped with the silver shield of Saint Luke.

Moretus smiled. It was a cold, practiced grimace that did not disturb the smooth skin around his hazel eyes. He wore a heavy traveling cloak of dark green broadcloth, lined with marten fur, and carried a pair of perfumed kidskin gloves tucked beneath his left arm.

"Mistress Vane," he said, touching the brim of his black velvet bonnet. His voice was soft, purring with the educated cadence of the Plantin counting house. "A chilly midday for the quays. May an officer of the guild warm his fingers at your hearth for three minutes?"

Katrijn stepped aside, bowing her head just enough to observe guild custom. "The hearth has only peat ashes, Master Proctor. But the threshold is open to the council."

Moretus stepped into the narrow kitchen, his marten fur brushing against the low doorframe. He glanced once around the room, taking in every object: the three earthenware plates on the oak dresser, the iron pot over the cold hearth, the scrubbed pine table where eight-year-old Clara sat winding gray wool yarn into a wooden ball. Five-year-old Pieter sat on the floor by the chimney corner, his small knees drawn to his chest, clutching a piece of charred firewood.

Moretus walked to the table. He stood looking down at Clara, who did not lift her eyes from the yarn.

"A dutiful daughter," Moretus remarked, his fingers lightly tapping the tabletop. "Hendrik's brow, I think. Let us pray she inherits her mother's prudence rather than her father's stubborn errors."

"Hendrik answered to the law on the Grote Markt, Master Proctor," Katrijn said, standing beside the dresser with her hands folded over her apron. "The guild took his presses, his paper stock, and his metal. There is nothing left in this house for the bailiffs to inventory."

"The guild took what was listed on the sheriff's register, mistress," Moretus said softly, turning to face her. He pulled a small silver vinaigrette box from his velvet pocket, unlatched the pierced lid, and inhaled the sharp scent of cloves and vinegar. "Yet there are rumors that drift across the Meir like river fog. Printers' devils in the taverns speaking of cheap Dutch tracts. Scraps of seditious doggerel found beneath the arch of the water gate. One hears that certain broken presses have grown new wooden limbs in the cellars of Sint-Jansvliet."

"We live on parish soup and what sewing I can find from the sailmakers," Katrijn replied. Her heart beat with a steady, dull rhythm against her ribs. "If there are presses in the canal vaults, they belong to the rats."

Moretus took two slow paces toward her. He reached out with his right hand, his perfumed fingers surprisingly strong, and took Katrijn's right wrist.

He lifted her hand into the gray light of the window.

His thumb moved across her knuckles, pressing into the rough, cracked skin where the lye water had eaten away the flesh around the joints. He turned her hand over, examining her fingertips and the margins of her nails.

Beneath the nail of her forefinger, deep in the groove where the cuticle met the bed, lay a microscopic crescent of lampblack: a stain so dark and permanent that three days of scrubbing with pumice stone had failed to lift it.

Moretus held her wrist suspended between them. His thumb rubbed the dark crescent with slow, deliberate pressure.

"Lampblack," the proctor murmured, his eyes locking onto hers. "Not chimney soot, Mistress Vane. Chimney soot is gray, gritty with coal ash. This has the silky bite of boiled oil and fine pine soot. The mark of the composing stick. The black brand of the devil's wooden box."

Katrijn did not pull her hand away. If she flinched, the two beadles on the stoop would have their crowbars into her floorboards within twenty seconds.

"The dyer's widow on the corner paid me three stuivers yesterday to clean her iron ink kettles," Katrijn said, her voice flat and cold as river ice. "She boils walnut skins and copperas to blacken mourning wool for the burgomaster's household. If the guild forbids a starving widow from scouring pots for bread, you had better write the prohibition into your register and nail it to the church door."

Moretus stared at her for five long seconds. The silence in the kitchen was broken only by the click of Clara's wooden yarn bobbin.

Slowly, the proctor released her wrist. He drew a fine cambric handkerchief from his cuff, wiped his thumb where it had touched her skin, and tucked the cloth back into his sleeve.

"The Duke of Parma has thirty thousand men across the Scheldt, Mistress Vane," Moretus said, his purr dropping into a dry, venomous whisper. "When the river stockade is joined, this city will starve until the burgers eat their own boots. The men who pay you thirty silver guilders for tracts will not be there to pull your neck from the hemp cord. Remember that when you hear the night carts on the stones."

He turned on his heel, walked through the doorway without looking back at the children, and stepped out onto the wet quayside. The two beadles fell in behind him, their heavy ash staves clattering against the cobblestones as they marched toward the canal bridge.

Moretus turned his gaze to the wooden dresser where three earthenware bowls and an iron candlestick sat in a row. Behind the plates leaned a small leather-bound book, its calfskin cover stained with water and rubbed thin at the corners. The proctor walked over, reached out with two gloved fingers, and lifted the volume by its spine.

"A Latin grammar, Master Proctor," Katrijn said, her voice completely flat. "Printed in Cologne twelve years ago. Hendrik bought it for three stuivers at the churchyard book fair to teach Clara her conjugations."

Moretus flipped the leaves with his thumb. The dry rag paper crackled in the cold room. He paused at the colophon, examining the printer's mark: a simple woodcut of an olive tree encircled by a serpent. For half a heartbeat, his eyes narrowed as if searching for a hidden watermark in the margins. Then, with a faint sniff of distaste, he tossed the book back onto the dresser.

Moretus stood in the center of the kitchen, his marten fur cloak pulled tight across his chest, his eyes sweeping the bare whitewashed walls. He pointed with his kidskin glove toward the ceiling beam where two dried turnip bunches hung from a wooden peg.

"The guild of Saint Luke has seventy sworn masters in this city, Mistress Vane," the proctor said, his voice dropping into a smooth, didactic cadence. "Master Plantin operates twenty-two presses on the Vrijdagmarkt. He employs eighty journeymen, fifty compositors, and ten punch-cutters from Paris. He has printed the Royal Polyglot Bible in eight folio volumes for His Catholic Majesty. That is printing: an ordered, sacred hierarchy protected by the imperial privilege. What you and Hendrik practiced was not a craft; it was a contagion. A reckless, heretical fever that brings the executioner's broadsword into the market square."

Katrijn examined the tympan cloth on the outer frame. The linen had slackened in the river damp, sagging like an old sail. She took an iron pin and tightened the corner cord, drawing the coarse weave taut until it twanged under her thumb like a lute string. If the tympan lacked tension, the paper would drag across the inked face before the platen made contact, producing a fuzzy, smeared impression known in the craft as a monk's ghost.

Katrijn rubbed a pinch of powdered chalk over the surface of the leather tympan to absorb any stray grease. If oil touched the clean linen paper, the printed letters would smudge into illegible grey smears before the sheet could be hung to dry.

Katrijn smoothed the sheepskin covering of the outer tympan with her palm. The leather was supple, rubbed with almond oil each Saturday to prevent the cellar frost from stiffening the hide into brittle cardboard.

Katrijn walked to the door, slid the heavy iron bolt into place, and leaned her forehead against the cold oak timber.

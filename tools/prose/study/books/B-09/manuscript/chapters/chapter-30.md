# Chapter 30: The Ambush on the Ice

Inspector Balthasar Moretus stood at the mouth of the ice channel, his crimson marten-lined cloak wrapped tight against the freezing river wind.

Beside him stood four armed beadles of the Stationers' Guild, their woolen tabards belted over rusted iron breastplates, each holding a six-foot ash partisan with a curved billhook blade. A fifth man—a burly sergeant of the Spanish garrison with an iron morion helmet—held the chain of the brindled mastiff, which strained toward the sledge, its black lips drawn back over yellow fangs, barking with a deep, echoing roar that bounced off the granite walls of the Crane Wharf.

The sledge came to a halt ten paces from the proctor.

Giles stood in the hauling traces, his head lowered, his hands resting on the ash crossbar, his breathing slow and heavy.

Moretus did not move. He carried his silver pomander ball in his left hand, holding it to his nostrils against the stench of the river silt. His hazel eyes swept over the sledge, taking in the dirty horse blanket, the two wicker hampers, and the two shivering figures standing in the gray dawn mist.

"A diligent widow," Moretus said, his purring voice carrying clearly across the silent ice. "To haul fish across the frozen Scheldt before the morning bell has finished tolling. I did not know the Saint Elizabeth hospital bought its pickled herrings from the river mud, Mistress Vane."

Katrijn stepped forward from the rear of the sledge, curtsying on the rough ice.

"The hospital has forty sick carters in the wards, Master Proctor," she said, her voice steady and deferential. "The fish stalls on the Meir were cleared by the soldiers yesterday. We bought two hampers from the Zeeland hoy that sits locked in the ice off the salt meadow."

"From a Zeeland hoy?" Moretus smiled, stepping slowly forward, his fine leather shoes crunching in the crusted snow. "A Zeeland hoy that slipped through the Duke of Parma's blockade in the dark? How remarkable that a starving printer's widow should find an enemy barge when thirty-two Spanish gunboats could see nothing."

He stopped beside the first wicker hamper.

He lifted his ivory walking cane, using the silver tip to push the filthy horse blanket aside, revealing the willow weave of the basket beneath.

"The dyer's cellar was empty when my sergeants kicked the door down an hour ago," Moretus remarked, looking at her with cold, mocking amusement. "The press was gone. The cases were overturned; the lead sorts were scattered into the coal bin. But my men found two things of great interest upon the stone, Katrijn."

He reached into his pocket and drew out two objects.

The first was a scrap of proof paper, torn from the margin of the eighth page, stained with a single fingerprint of black lampblack ink.

The second was a small iron-bound tin box.

Katrijn's breath caught in her throat. Her hand involuntarily touched her apron pocket, finding it empty. In the panic of the sewer escape, she had left the box on the stone behind the ink-block.

"Your husband's watch," Moretus said softly, dangling the silver case by its broken brass chain. "And the copy of his execution warrant. Signed by our good friend, Alderman Lucas Van Hout."

He dropped the watch into his velvet pocket with a contemptuous click.

"The alderman is already in the irons at the stadhuis," Moretus continued, his voice dropping into a harsh, triumphant rasp. "He confessed everything when the provost showed him the thumbscrews in the council cellar. He named the press. He named the widow. He named the boy who dragged the lead through the sewers."

The proctor turned to the four beadles.

"Cut the ropes," he commanded. "Let us see what kind of fish Master Hendrik Vane's widow carries to the hospital."

One of the beadles stepped forward with a broad butcher's knife, slitting the hemp lashings that bound the first hamper to the sledge crossbars. He seized the willow lid and ripped it open.

The gray, salt-crusted herrings spilled across the crusted snow, slumping onto the ice in a foul, greasy heap.

The beadle plunged his hand into the basket, digging through the remaining fish until his fingers struck the hard, flat surface of the tarred sailcloth beneath. He drew his knife across the canvas with a violent, tearing slash.

The white edges of four hundred folded octavo pamphlets sprang out through the severed cloth like white teeth.

Moretus reached into the opening with his gloved fingers.

He pulled out a single sixteen-page pamphlet, turned it to the gray dawn light, and read the bold Great Primer letters on the title page:

*A TRUE RECORD OF THE SMASHED PRESSES.*
*Wherein the Betrayal of Antwerp is Laid Open to the Commons.*

The proctor's face went white.

The smooth, purring arrogance vanished from his features, replaced by a sudden, violent rage that distorted his thin lips into a snarl. His hazel eyes moved down the first page, scanning the numbered articles until they stopped at Article III: his own name, set down in razor-sharp Brevier lead, detailing the four hundred gold ducats he had drawn from the Spanish paymaster at the Italian Bourse.

"You printed this," Moretus whispered, his voice trembling with fury.

He crumpled the pamphlet in his fist, crushing the linen paper into a tight ball, and tossed it into the snow at Katrijn's feet.

"You miserable, heretic gutter-bitch," he snarled, stepping toward her with his walking cane raised to strike. "You dare set my name in lead? You dare accuse the sworn officers of the King's guild before the commons?"

Katrijn did not move. She did not flinch from the raised cane.

She stood upright on the frozen Scheldt, her hands resting flat against her woolen apron, looking directly into the face of the man who had ordered her husband's death.

"The letters are straight, Master Proctor," she said, her voice ringing clear and cold as an iron bell across the river ice. "There is not a turned sort in the entire fount."

Moretus lowered his cane, his chest heaving, his fingers twitching with venomous rage.

"Seize them," he shouted to the Spanish sergeant and the beadles. "Tie their hands with the hauling ropes! Drag them to the Red Gate! I will have the executioner build the fire on the Grote Markt before the noon bell rings!"

Inspector Moretus stood motionless on the ice, his hand resting on the hilt of his ivory cane, his hazel eyes fixed upon Katrijn's face. The freezing river wind whipped the hem of his crimson cloak around his boots, but he seemed not to feel the cold. His lips were drawn back in a thin, pale sneer that showed his small, sharp teeth.

"You thought yourself clever, Mistress Vane," the proctor murmured, his voice silky with malice. "To hide your dead husband's press in a dyer's coal hole. Did you think the Stationers' Guild would forget the man who printed the psalms? Did you think thirty pieces of Van Hout's silver would buy you a free passage across the river?"

The four guild beadles stood in the snow with their ash partisans leveled at Giles's chest, their faces pale beneath their iron morions. They had recognized Katrijn; they had seen the dead printer's widow standing on the ice with four hundred copies of the truth at her feet, and they knew that every word on those printed pages was an accusation that carried the penalty of the rope.

Moretus took a step closer, his kidskin gloves trembling with uncontrollable fury. His gaze dropped to the open pamphlet lying in the snow, where his own name stood out in bold, sharp Brevier lead: *Master Balthasar Moretus, Proctor of the Stationers' Guild, four hundred gold ducats drawn from the Spanish paymaster.*

The cellar beneath the dyer's warehouse was colder than the street. Frost had formed on the stone vaulting, sparkling like salt in the candlelight. Giles set the heavy oak base of the press onto two square balks of seasoned pine, leveling the frame with small wooden wedges driven home by taps from an iron hammer. The timber stood true and plumb, its massive uprights capable of resisting the immense torque of the hand lever.

Katrijn unpacked the paper reams from the bottom of the fish hampers. The sheets were cold to the touch, the linen fibers crisp and dry. She laid twelve sheets at a time onto a clean wool blanket, spraying them with a fine mist of clean rainwater from a brass syringe to soften the sizing before printing. If the paper was printed dry, the ink would sit upon the surface like soot on glass, rubbing off on the reader's thumb; if it was properly dampened, the lead face would press deep into the linen fibers, embedding the word into the very heart of the rag.

"The proclamation is set in twenty-four-point capitals, mistress," Giles whispered, pointing to the copper galley on the bench. "The letters are tall enough to be read from across a tavern table. When the weavers gather at the Meat Shambles tomorrow morning, they will see the signatures of the traitors before the guild wardens can pull the placards down."

"Let them read it," Katrijn said, her fingers smoothing the damp linen against the sheepskin tympan. "Let every mother who buried a child of the hunger fever see how the aldermen sold our bread to the Spanish commander for gold florins. When the morning comes, Antwerp will know who opened the gates."

The cellar floor was paved with blue Tournai flagstones, cold and slippery with condensation. Giles adjusted the wooden stays of the press, driving two iron spikes into the joints between the paving stones to hold the frame rigid against the torque of the lever. The oak timbers groaned under the tension, settling into place with a sharp, dry snap.

Katrijn opened a dry pine chest and lifted out the ink-pelts. She had greased the sheepskin with neat's-foot oil before leaving the Sint-Jansvliet, wrapping the balls in damp linen cloths to keep the leather soft. She pressed her thumb into the fleece. The wool yielded with an elastic resilience that showed the stuffing had remained warm inside the packing. She picked up the wooden ball-stock and handed it to Giles, who wiped his palms on his leather apron and took his place before the inking stone.

"The proclamation will be printed by dawn," Katrijn said, her voice quiet in the hollow vault. "Three hundred copies. One for every guildhall, one for every church porch, and one for every tavern on the quays. When the morning tide turns, the town council will have to choose between the Spanish gold and the vengeance of the weavers."

Giles drove two more oak wedges beneath the foot of the press frame, testing the stability of the timber with his heavy fist. The massive uprights stood firm as the trunk of an old churchyard oak. Katrijn arranged the composing rules upon the stone shelf, laying each brass strip in order of length. The cellar was cold and damp, but the iron stove was beginning to radiate a faint warmth from the burning peat turf.

Giles picked up an iron oil-can and lubricated the bronze bushings of the rounce spindle with three drops of refined lard oil. The gears engaged without a sound, sliding on the seasoned beechwood track with the smooth, silent glide of a boat entering calm water. Katrijn laid out the fresh sheets on the dampening cloth, ready for the platen.

The four beadles stepped forward, their halberd points leveled at Giles's chest, as the Spanish sergeant unlocked the chain of the mastiff.

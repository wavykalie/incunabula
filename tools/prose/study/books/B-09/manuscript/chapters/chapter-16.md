# Chapter 16: The Metallurgy of Type

The iron ladle rested on the glowing peat embers, its bowl filled with molten metal that shivered like mercury in the heat.

Katrijn bent over the crucible, holding a small horn spoon in her left hand to skim the dross from the surface. A crust of yellowish-gray oxide gathered at the rim—the slag of cheap English lead and impurities from old pewter—which she drew off and dropped into an earthen saucer, where it hissed and hardened into dull gray powder.

"More antimony," Giles said, holding out an iron pair of pincers gripping a jagged lump of silvery, crystalline ore the size of a pigeon's egg.

"Only half," Katrijn warned. "If you put too much antimony into the melt, the metal becomes brittle as glass. The fine serifs of the lowercase *f* and *t* will snap under the pressure of the platen on the fortieth pull."

Giles tapped the lump with a small smith's hammer, breaking off a fragment that fell into the molten lead with a soft *plip*.

The alloy of the printer was a delicate, dangerous secret. Lead alone was too soft; it would mash flat like tallow beneath the screw after a single day's run. Tin alone was too malleable, running too slowly into the fine channels of the matrix. It was the antimony—that toxic, brittle, crystalline semi-metal mined in the mountains of Germany—that gave the alloy its miraculous property: it expanded slightly as it cooled in the mold, forcing the liquid metal into the deepest crevices of the punched copper matrix, ensuring that every hairline serif and delicate curve emerged with razor-sharp fidelity.

Katrijn took her casting mold from the wooden bench.

It was Hendrik's hand mold, crafted by a precision punch-cutter in Lyon: two interlocking halves of seasoned boxwood lined with polished iron plates, held together by a steel bow spring. In the throat of the mold sat the tiny copper matrix, stamped with the reversed face of a Brevier lowercase *e*.

They had run out of *e* sorts while setting the twelfth page of the indictment.

Without sixty more *e*s, the press was dead. You could not write a Dutch sentence without that vowel; the tongue was built upon it, from the definite articles to the plural verbs.

Katrijn took the iron casting ladle by its turned wooden handle.

Her movement was swift, practiced, repeated ten thousand times in her youth. She dipped the lip of the ladle into the silvery pool of molten alloy, lifted two drams of liquid metal, and brought it to the funnel-shaped orifice of the hand mold.

With a sudden, coordinated jerk of her left wrist, she poured the metal and tossed the mold upward in a quick, lifting snap.

That upward jerk was the founder's art: it drove the heavy molten lead down into the tiny chamber before the chilled iron could freeze the metal, forcing the liquid into the face of the copper matrix.

*Clack.*

She released the steel bow spring. The mold fell open in two halves.

A tiny, gleaming sliver of lead dropped onto the wooden tray: a perfect Brevier sort, complete with its cast face, its body, its nick, and a long, tapering jet of waste metal at the foot.

Katrijn picked up the sort with a pair of brass tweezers.

The metal was hot enough to scorch the skin, but she held it close to the candle flame, examining the face with her magnifying lens. The tiny oval loop of the *e* was clear and open; the crossbar was straight as a drawn line; the serif at the foot had no air bubble or cold-shut flaw.

"Break the jet," she told Giles.

Giles took the hot sort in his gloved fingers, snapped the waste jet off with a sharp twist, and ran the base of the lead across a fine flat file to bring the sort to the exact height-to-paper of the rest of the fount.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

Katrijn poured, jerked, opened, and released. One sort every eight seconds. The cellar filled with the sweet, sickening metallic fumes of vaporized lead and antimony, making her throat dry and her tongue taste of cold copper coins. But she did not pause.

Katrijn dipped the iron ladle back into the crucible. The molten alloy was gleaming like liquid quicksilver beneath the thin skin of yellowish dross. She reached for the small stoneware box of powdered sal-ammoniac that Hendrik had kept for purifying the melt, taking a pinch between her fingers and scattering the white crystals across the liquid surface.

The powder crackled with a sharp, pungent puff of white smoke that smelled of burned horn and salt. The slag pulled away to the edges of the bowl, leaving the pool of lead, antimony, and tin pure, bright, and mirror-smooth. She brought the hand mold to the ladle lip, her muscles braced for the rhythmic snap of the casting jerk.

Katrijn took the newly cast *e* sort from the brass tweezers, laid it upon the smooth marble test stone, and ran a steel height-to-paper gauge across the face. The gauge slid over the letter with a faint, musical hiss, neither catching on the serif nor rocking on the shank. The height was true to the thousandth part of an inch: the exact French standard of the Plantin workshops.

The balance of the metal was everything. A fount that had too much soft lead would crush flat under the platen after forty pulls, turning the clean letters into black, illegible blots; a fount that had too much brittle antimony would shatter like dried clay when the screw descended. She looked at the eighty-two newly cast characters resting on the pine board, bright and sharp as newly struck silver groats.

The smell of fresh printer's ink was as distinct as newly baked bread. It was the smell of boiled walnut oil, crushed soot, and pine rosin, sharp enough to cut through the reek of rotting vegetables in the cellar drains.

Katrijn rubbed her knuckles over the rough surface of the work-table. A film of white lime dust had settled across the boards from the cellar ceiling. Through the floor, she could hear the ice cracking against the canal wall like gunshots in the freezing twilight.

By the time the iron ladle was empty, eighty-two clean, newly minted *e* sorts lay cooling on the pine board, ready to spell the treason of the Stationers' Guild.

# Chapter 22: The Cracked Spindle

The sound was not a crack; it was a screech of dry iron grinding against brass.

Halfway through the two hundred and fortieth impression, as Giles threw his weight onto the ash lever, the heavy wooden screw slipped its thread in the overhead gallows.

The bar kicked violently backward.

The ash handle struck Giles across the ribs with the force of an iron club, tossing him backward into the coal bin. He hit the stone floor with a muffled cry, rolling onto his side in the black slack, gasping for air as his fingers clawed at his bruised chest.

The press shuddered. The platen tilted four inches to the left, hanging crooked on its suspension cords, jammed against the iron cheek of the frame.

Katrijn dropped the paper peel.

"Giles!"

She scrambled over the flagstones, kneeling beside the boy in the coal dust. His face was gray, his lips drawn back from his teeth, his breath whistling in his throat where the wood had bruised his ribs.

"Can you breathe?" she demanded, pressing her hand against his side.

Giles nodded weakly, coughing once into his sleeve. A fleck of dark blood stained his lip, but he pushed her hands away and struggled onto his knees.

"The screw," he croaked, pointing toward the press. "The brass nut stripped."

Katrijn rose and stepped to the press bed.

The massive iron screw spindle, three inches thick, hung tilted in the throat of the upper crossbeam. The boxwood cheek on the right side of the gallows had split along the mortise joint: an old crack from the Vrijdagmarkt raid, reopened by the pressure of two hundred heavy pulls. Under the strain, the iron collar had wrenched sideways, jamming the screw threads against the brass box.

If the gallows split completely, the press was dead.

There was no carpenter in Antwerp who could replace a master printer's oak gallows in the dead of curfew without alerting every guild proctor on the Scheldt.

"Bring the iron wedges," Katrijn said, her voice tight and cold. "And the heavy maul."

Giles dragged himself to the tool chest, his hand pressed against his aching ribs. He brought back two triangular wedges of seasoned heart-of-oak and the twelve-pound iron-banded mallet that Hendrik had used for driving chase quoins.

Katrijn climbed onto the press carriage.

She examined the split in the upright cheek. The gap was the thickness of her thumb, revealing the pale, splintered grain of the interior timber. She took the tallow pot, smeared grease deep into the fracture to lubricate the wood, and set the first oak wedge into the mouth of the split.

"Hold the beam," she told Giles.

Giles braced his good shoulder against the iron screw, heaving upward with all his remaining strength to relieve the pressure on the brass nut.

Katrijn lifted the heavy maul with both hands.

She swung the mallet in the cramped cellar vault, striking the oak wedge with a solid, thunderous blow: *CLANG.*

The wedge sank an inch into the split. The timber groaned, resisting the iron band, but the fracture held. She struck again, driving the wedge home until the split cheek compressed back into the mortise joint, forcing the iron screw back into true vertical alignment.

She reached down, seized the ash lever, and pulled.

The screw turned. The iron threads bit into the brass box with a stiff, heavy groan, but they held. The platen leveled itself above the bed, hanging square and true once more.

Katrijn climbed down from the carriage. Her hands were shaking so hard she had to grip the frame to steady herself.

"It will hold for two hundred more pulls," she said, looking at Giles. "Not one more. If we pull too hard, the entire cheek will blow out like a burst cannon."

Giles wiped the coal soot from his mouth with the back of his hand. He stepped to the bed, took the twin ink-balls, and worked them over the charcoal embers.

Giles knelt beside the timber bed, his hand pressed against his bruised ribs, holding an iron plumb-bob suspended from the center of the gallows beam. The lead weight swung gently over the carriage, settling two inches to the right of the scribe line that Hendrik had carved into the oak sill ten years before.

"The mortise has widened half an inch," Giles wheezed, his breath smelling of blood and bile. "When the screw turns, the lateral torque forces the right cheek outward. If we do not bind the uprights with hemp rope, the tenon will shear off under the next pull."

Katrijn ran her fingers along the iron collar where the screw entered the brass box. The metal was burning hot to the touch, friction and strain having expanded the threads until the screw bound against the nut. She scooped a generous handful of cold mutton tallow from the earthen pot, working the white grease deep into the spiral channels with her thumbs.

"The timber has taken the wedge," Giles grunted, leaning his shoulder against the upright gallows. "The grain is tight. It will hold for the final signature if we do not throw our weight too hard against the bar."

She checked the catch on the sliding timber tray beneath the stone bed. The iron bolt was greased with mutton suet, sliding into its notched housing without a click.

Katrijn slid the brass bodkin into her hair to keep the curls from her eyes. The metal was cold against her scalp. She checked the alignment of the wooden quoins with a light tap of her knuckles.

"Sheet two hundred and forty-one," he said.

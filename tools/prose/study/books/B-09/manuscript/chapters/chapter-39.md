# Chapter 39: The Sinking of the Pontoon

The explosion shook the bed of the Scheldt at the turn of the February tide.

It was not the thunder of siege artillery; it was a catastrophic, earth-shattering detonation that rattled the roof slates of Antwerp three miles away and sent a column of fire five hundred feet into the midnight sky.

The Dutch Sea Beggars had struck the Spanish bridge.

From the quayside walls of Sint-Jansvliet, Katrijn and Giles stood among three thousand cheering citizens, watching the southern horizon burn with orange and crimson flame.

Two weeks after the circulation of *A True Record*, the political balance of the siege had collapsed. The municipal council, confronted by the exposed evidence of Moretus's bribery and the open insurrection of the water guilds, had refused to ratify the terms of surrender. The town gates remained locked against the Spanish tercios; the bread stores in the municipal magazines had been seized and distributed to the common bakers; and an armed delegation of river boatmen had slipped across the flooded polders to contact the Dutch fleet at Flushing.

The plan had been conceived by an Italian engineer in the service of the Prince of Orange: the *Finis Belli*, a floating mine-ship loaded with seven thousand pounds of fine gunpowder, brick masonry, and iron scrap, disguised as an ordinary grain hoy and drifted down the Scheldt on the ebb tide.

The ship had struck the center of Alexander Farnese's pontoon bridge off Calloo.

Through the winter darkness, the river was an inferno.

Thirty-two Spanish ketches were blown to splinters; the great floating fort of Saint Maria was torn in half, its brass cannons hurled into the river mud; and a breach three hundred yards wide was torn through the center of the stockade, opening the channel to the sea.

A roar went up from the walls of Antwerp:

"The Beggars! The Beggars are through!"

Dawn broke over a river that was no longer dead.

Through the drifting smoke and the floating wreckage of the Spanish palisade, the white sails of the Dutch relief fleet appeared in the river mouth: forty armed hoys from Zeeland, their decks piled high with sacks of Baltic rye, barrels of salt pork, and kegs of gunpowder, flying the orange, white, and blue colors of freedom.

The siege was broken.

The Spanish army remained entrenched in the flooded polders of Calloo, but their iron grip on the Scheldt was shattered. Antwerp was linked once more to the ocean, to the free ports of Holland, and to the printing houses of London and Amsterdam.

In the market square, the citizens dragged the iron pillory from the center of the Grote Markt, smashing the timber posts with sledgehammers, tossing the fragments into a victory bonfire that roared before the steps of the town hall.

Master Dirck, the head porter of the Crane Wharf, stood atop an empty beer barrel, holding a frayed, soot-stained copy of *A True Record* in his right hand.

"This was the hammer!" the porter shouted to the thousands of artisans and dockers who crowded the square. "This was the spark that woke the river! Honour to the free word! Honour to Master Hendrik Vane!"

The crowd roared with a thunderous, unified voice that shook the bronze bells of the Cathedral.

Katrijn stood on the lower steps of the cloth hall, holding Clara and Pieter by their hands.

She did not join in the shouting. She watched the bonfire smoke curl upward into the clear blue winter sky, her heart filled with a deep, serene, unshakeable peace.

The dead man had won his battle.

The Dutch relief fleet dropped anchor in the middle of the Scheldt before the Crane Wharf, their decks crowded with armed sailors and sacks of Baltic wheat. Forty broad-beamed hoys from Flushing and Middelburg, their masts hung with pine boughs and orange pennants, their crews singing the psalms of Clement Marot across the open water.

The Spanish bridge was broken. The thirty-two armed ketches had been burned to the waterline, their blackened timbers drifting down the current to the North Sea, while the citizens of Antwerp lined the quays three deep to haul the sacks of grain onto the city wagons.

The Dutch relief fleet sailed through the shattered center of the Spanish palisade on the morning flood tide, their decks loaded with four thousand sacks of Baltic rye and two hundred barrels of salt pork from Zeeland.

From the Crane Wharf to the water gate, three thousand citizens lined the river walls, cheering with a thunderous roar that shook the Cathedral bells as the first grain hoys dropped anchor before the city quays.

Giles cleaned the lead sorts with a stiff bristle brush dipped in a trough of boiling lye. The caustic solution hissed against the greasy metal, dissolving the black varnish and lifting the ink from the deep counters of the letters. The fumes of the potash rose into the cold air, stinging his nostrils with a sharp, alkaline bite. When the metal was clean and shining like freshly cast pewter, he rinsed the forme in three changes of clear canal water to prevent the lye from corroding the copper matrices.

Katrijn watched the water drain from the stones. The press was disassembled piece by piece: the heavy oak platen, the iron screw, the polished granite bed, and the two massive upright cheeks. Each timber was wrapped in oiled sacking and tied with hemp rope, ready to be lowered into the bilge of the smuggling ketch moored behind the timber wharf. Before the Spanish heralds could post their rewards on the cathedral doors, Hendrik's press would be floating toward the open waters of the North Sea, bound for the free printshops of Middelburg.

The canal boat rocked beneath their weight as they lowered the timber frame into the hold. Giles stood knee-deep in the bilge water, his strong arms guiding the oak platen into its cradle between two barrels of pickled herring. The smell of salt brine, tarred rope, and damp river silt filled the narrow space beneath the hatches.

Katrijn handed down the two pine cases containing the matrices. Her fingers were raw from the cold, but her pulse was steady and calm. Hendrik's type was safe beneath the cargo of fish. In three days, the ketch would be sailing past the Spanish forts at Lillo into the wide waters of the Zeeland estuary, where no King's officer could search a Dutch vessel without a broadside from an Orange privateer.

Giles tightened the hemp ropes around the dismantled wooden cheeks of the press, tying each knot with a double half-hitch. The seasoned oak timbers were secure, ready to withstand the swell of the estuary.

The press had spoken; the lie had broken; and her children were standing in the sunlight of a free city.

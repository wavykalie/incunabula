# Chapter 26: The Sound of Iron Boots

The shouting began at the five o'clock bell at the head of the Sint-Jansvliet lane.

It was not the panic of an individual arrest, but the loud, drunken, bellicose roar of twenty Flemish weavers spilling out from the cellar tavern of the Blue Anchor into the freezing mist.

"Down with the grain tax!" a voice bellowed through the alley. "Down with the Spanish flour excise! Give us our rye, you thieving Walloon swine!"

The crash of an earthenware beer jug against an iron helmet echoed through the alley, followed immediately by the sharp, metallic scrape of halberd blades being drawn from leather sheaths.

Katrijn, crouching inside the dark mouth of the sewer conduit, paused with her hand on the wicker hamper.

Beside her, Giles was half-submerged in the frozen mud, his face turned toward the iron ventilation grating above. Through the bars, the orange glare of pitch torches flickered wildly across the brickwork, accompanied by the thundering clatter of thirty pairs of iron-shod boots running across the canal bridge.

"The weavers," Giles whispered, a faint, fierce grin touching his soot-stained lips. "Old Master Baetens from the Predikherenrei. He swore yesterday he would break the heads of the excise collectors before the week was out."

"It is a diversion," Katrijn murmured.

Above them, the riot was escalating with the swift, violent momentum of a besieged city that had reached the end of its endurance.

Antwerp had been starved for eight months; its trade was dead, its wealth stolen by foreign garrisons, its pride crushed beneath the Spanish tercios. The sight of twenty drunken weavers armed with iron weaving shuttles, heavy wooden beaters, and butcher knives was all the excuse the neighborhood needed.

Windows flew open along the gables of the Sint-Jansvliet.

Earthenware chamber pots, broken brickbats, and heavy iron cooking pans came crashing down from the third-story sashes into the alley, pelting the Spanish pikemen who were trying to form a line across the roadway.

"Rebels!" a Spanish officer screamed in his native tongue, his voice shrill with fury. "Clear the street! Level your pikes! Fire upon them!"

A matchlock musket roared with a deafening, thunderous blast that shook the foundation walls of the dyer's cellar. The heavy lead ball struck the brick lintel of the house opposite, showering the gutter with sharp shards of stone and hot sulfur smoke.

A woman shrieked from an upper window; then a chorus of enraged Dutch curses rose from the weavers:

"Murderers! Spanish dogs! Bread! Give us our bread!"

The sound of the struggle rolled away down the lane toward the quayside, as the weavers retreated in a fighting mass, drawing the thirty pikemen and bailiffs away from the dyer's stoop toward the open market of the fish stalls.

Katrijn turned to Giles in the blackness of the conduit.

"Now," she said.

The sewer pipe was three feet wide, built of rough red brick that was coated in a freezing, gelatinous layer of old dyer's slime and canal mud. The air smelled foul, thick with the stench of rotting offal, human waste, and stale lye, but it was cold enough to keep the mud partially frozen under their knees.

Katrijn pushed the first wicker hamper ahead of her through the pipe.

The basket scraped against the brick vault with a dry, rhythmic rustle. Behind her, Giles crawled on his elbows and knees, dragging the second hamper by its hemp rope, his breath whistling in his throat as the mud soaked through his wool breeches to the bare skin.

Ahead of them, forty feet down the pipe, a faint gray circle of light gleamed in the darkness.

It was the sewer mouth.

Katrijn crawled toward the light, her hands plunging into icy mud that numbed her fingers to the bone. When she reached the end of the brick barrel, she pushed the wicker basket through the opening and peered out between the wooden piles.

The conduit opened into the shadow beneath the timber substructure of the brewery wharf.

Above her head, massive oak pilings, green with river slime and encrusted with dried mussels, rose twelve feet to the quayside deck. Beneath the wharf, where the tide usually ran three feet deep against the shore, the Scheldt canal had frozen solid into a flat, gray plain of thick river ice that stretched unbroken from the wharf piles across the channel to the salt warehouses on the opposite bank.

The ice was gray, rough, dusted with a light powder of freezing snow that muffled every sound.

Katrijn climbed out of the sewer mouth, her boots sinking two inches into the crusted snow on the ice. She turned, caught the handles of the second hamper from Giles's mud-caked hands, and pulled the boy out onto the frozen surface.

They crouched together in the deep, freezing shadow of the oak pilings.

Across the canal, half a mile away through the drifting morning fog, the great cranes of the Crane Wharf stood like silent timber skeletons against the gray dawn sky.

Katrijn looked at Giles. His face was gray with exhaustion, his smock torn at the elbows, his hands covered in black mud and dried blood from his knuckles.

"The children?" he whispered.

"Clara has Pieter at Huybrechts's bakehouse," Katrijn said, her voice steady. "They are safe in the flour bins. We have four hundred pamphlets in the wicker, Giles. Pull the sledge."

Above the sewer vault, the battle on the Sint-Jansvliet quayside was turning into a general riot. The weavers from the Predikherenrei had been joined by twenty coopers and barrel-makers armed with heavy oak staves and iron hoop-drivers. A wagon loaded with empty beer casks had been overturned across the bridge approach, creating a barricade that blocked the Spanish cavalry patrol from advancing into the lane.

Through the brick ventilation grating, Katrijn could hear the sharp, crackling roar of a pitch barrel burning in the street, followed by the hoarse, collective shout of three hundred Dutch artisans driving the Walloon pikemen back toward the market colonnade.

Through the brickwork of the sewer pipe came the muffled roar of the crowd on the Sint-Jansvliet quayside: the clash of iron pikes, the shattering of window glass, and the hoarse battle cry of the Dutch guildsmen: *Oranje boven!* The weavers were holding the bridge with their heavy wooden beaters, forcing the Walloon watch back toward the Meir colonnade.

Giles crawled through the frozen mud with his head down, dragging the second wicker hamper behind him, his boots scraping the brick invert with a steady, rhythmic rasp. Katrijn followed close behind, her fingers buried in the willow handles of the first basket, her eyes fixed on the gray circle of light that marked the sewer mouth ahead.

The ink-maker's pestle was made of green porphyry stone, smooth and heavy, worn down by generations of apprentice hands. Giles gripped the stone stem, grinding the dry Frankfurt vine-black into the cold varnish with small, deliberate circles. Every turn of the stone reduced the soot particles, turning the powdery pigment into a slick, glossy butter that smelled of roasted walnut kernels and bitter pine gum.

Katrijn watched the doorway at the head of the cellar stairs. A thin seam of daylight showed beneath the heavy oak door, pale and gray with winter fog. She could hear the voices of the watermen down at the quay, arguing over the price of salt herring and peat turf. In a normal winter, fifty merchant vessels would have been unloading Baltic rye at the Crane, and the porters would have been singing guild ballads over their beer. Now three sunken hulks blocked the main channel, their blackened masts sticking out of the gray Scheldt like broken bones.

Katrijn picked up a small horsehair brush and swept the dust from the galleys. The lead types gleamed like fresh silver under the candle flame. Through the iron grating, the distant tolling of the cathedral bells announced the sixth hour of the evening, and the guards closed the city gates against the frozen polders.

Katrijn ran her finger along the edge of the printed galley. The ink had taken evenly across the column, the black letters standing sharp and clear against the cream-white rag paper without a single blur.

Giles reached into the shadow of the wharf piles, pulled out the low, two-runner timber sledge they had hidden there two nights before, and lashed the two wicker hampers to the pine crossbars with hemp cord.

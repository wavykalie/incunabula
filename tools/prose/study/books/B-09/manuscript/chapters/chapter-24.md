# Chapter 24: The Alderman's Panic

Lucas Van Hout did not knock.

He threw the cellar hatch open from the alley with a violent crash of wood against stone, tumbled down the ladder head-first, and crashed into the coal heap, groaning as the sharp anthracite lumps tore the fine Flemish wool of his breeches.

He scrambled to his feet like a madman, his velvet bonnet gone, his gray hair wild and soaked with freezing sleet.

"Douse the fire!" he shrieked, his voice cracking into a high, hysterical sob that bounced off the vaulted ceiling. "Douse it! Burn the paper! Burn everything!"

Giles stepped between the alderman and the press, raising his iron crowbar.

Katrijn caught Giles's arm, forcing the bar down. She stood before the composing stone, where the final stacks of four hundred complete pamphlets lay gathered into neat octavo quires.

"Quiet, Alderman," she said, her voice low and sharp as an awl. "The watch is on the bridge."

"The watch is at my door!" Van Hout gasped, clutching Katrijn's woolen sleeve with both hands, shaking her with frantic, desperate strength. His eyes were wide, white-rimmed, staring with the madness of a cornered beast. "Moretus has my house on the Keizerstraat! Six Spanish sergeants and two provost bailiffs! They broke my library cabinet with axes two hours ago! They found my copies! They found the drafts of the ninth article!"

Katrijn felt the blood drain from her face. "The drafts?"

"The preliminary lists!" Van Hout sobbed, saliva spraying from his trembling lips. "The notes I took during the secret committee! My own clerk betrayed me! He went to Moretus at midnight and sold my desk keys for fifty florins!"

He lunged toward the iron brazier, seized the iron shovel from Giles's hand, and scooped a glowing mound of red peat coals.

"We must burn them!" the alderman screamed, turning toward the drying lines where four hundred damp printed sheets hung in the draft. "If Moretus finds these sheets in the city, he will have me on the rack before noon! I will be quartered on the Grote Markt! My sons will be beggars in the street!"

Katrijn stepped directly into his path.

She did not flinch from the glowing shovel. She stood with her arms outstretched, her black apron spattered with lye and lampblack, her face dark with the coal soot of three days' labor.

"Put the shovel down, Alderman."

"Get out of my way, woman!" Van Hout snarled, his patrician dignity entirely dissolved into squalid animal terror. "I paid for the paper! I paid for the ink! I own this work! Burn it, or by God, I will call the Spanish watch myself and tell them you stole the text from my study!"

Katrijn took one step forward.

She looked straight into Van Hout's wild, bloodshot eyes. Her voice was not loud, but it had the crushing, immovable weight of the oak platen descending upon the forme.

"You paid thirty guilders for a dead man's labor," she said. "You gave me silver to buy your way out of the blood on Hendrik's shirt. And now, because the bailiffs have knocked at your carved oak door, you want to throw four hundred copies of the truth into the ash heap?"

"It is my life!" Van Hout wailed, falling to his knees on the flagstones, the iron shovel dropping from his hands and scattering glowing coals across the wet stones. "Do you not understand, woman? It is my life! They will hang me from the town hall balcony!"

"Then hang!" Katrijn said, her eyes flashing with cold, ruthless fury. "Hendrik hanged! Jan De Smet hanged! Master Visscher is in the Steen with his fingers crushed in the screws! Did you think your alderman's gold bought you a separate justice from God?"

Van Hout knelt in the coal dust, weeping like a beaten child, his face pressed against her muddy clogs.

"Fifty guilders," he whimpered into her skirts. "I have sixty gold florins sewn into the lining of my cloak. Take it, Katrijn. Take all of it. Take the children and hire a boat to Zeeland through the dikes. Only burn the sheets. Burn the names. Let the dead stay in their graves."

Katrijn looked down at the kneeling magistrate.

This was the man who had sat on the velvet bench beneath the imperial arms while the executioner unbuttoned Hendrik's collar on the scaffold. This was the authority that ruled Antwerp: frightened, corrupt, ready to barter every life in the city to save its own velvet robes and merchant ledgers.

"The ink is dry, Alderman," Katrijn said.

She reached down, took Van Hout by the collar of his broadcloth cloak, and hauled him to his feet with the raw, muscular strength of a woman who had pulled a press lever for ten years.

She turned him toward the composing stone.

There, neatly gathered into four wicker hampers covered with sailcloth, sat four hundred copies of *A True Record*.

"The text is printed," she told him. "The truth belongs to Antwerp now. It does not belong to you, and it does not belong to me. In one hour, the morning bells will ring. If you want to save your neck from Moretus, go to the docks. Stand with the boatmen when they read your pamphlet. Tell them you signed the warrants under terror, and ask them for their pikes."

Van Hout stared at the hampers.

His breath slowed, his tears drying into dirty mud tracks across his jowls. He looked from Katrijn's hard, unyielding face to the dark mouth of the sewer pipe leading out toward the canal.

"Moretus has the bridges," the alderman whispered, his voice trembling.

Van Hout leaned his heavy back against the damp brick vault, his breath coming in shallow, shuddering gasps. The beaver fur of his collar was matted with dirty slush; his linen ruff was torn and stained with soot from his tumble down the coal chute. He looked at the drying lines where four hundred copies of his own indictment hung like banners in the candlelight.

"My brother's sons are seventeen and fourteen, Katrijn," the alderman groaned, his voice cracking into a ragged whisper. "They are in the water cells beneath the citadel. Parma keeps them in two feet of river water, with bread once every two days. When the inquisitors showed me their signet rings, I would have signed the execution of my own mother to see them out of those irons."

Van Hout looked down at his trembling, soot-stained hands. His broadcloth cloak was torn at the shoulder, revealing the gray wool lining beneath; his gold signet ring was smeared with coal dust. For thirty years he had lived in the great stone house on the Keizerstraat, surrounded by tapestries from Brussels and silver plate from Augsburg, commanding the respect of the merchant guilds.

Now he was a hunted fugitive in a dyer's coal hole, his name branded as a traitor by the Spanish commander and his life hanging by the thread of an unlicensed widow's silence. He looked at Katrijn with a mixture of terror and desperate awe, realizing that the woman with the ink-stained knuckles possessed an authority that no municipal seal could ever grant.

Lucas Van Hout leaned his heavy back against the stack of dry firewood, his breath rattling in his throat like gravel in a dry sieve. He untied the fur collar of his cloak with trembling fingers, revealing the gold chain of the burgomaster's office tucked inside his doublet. The heavy gold links clinked against the brass buttons of his vest.

"Do you know what Parma told the deputies at Beveren, mistress?" the alderman asked, his voice shaking with a ragged tremor. "He said that the Prince of Orange was dead in Delft, shot through the heart with three poisoned balls by a Spanish assassin. He said that the provinces were a headless snake thrashing in the dust. He laid three letters on the table before us, signed by our own magistrates, offering to open the Red Gate for fifty thousand ducats. When I saw my brother's seal upon the third letter, the marrow turned to ice inside my bones."

He wiped a film of cold sweat from his forehead with the cuff of his velvet sleeve. "I took the Spanish gold, Katrijn. I took two thousand silver carolus florins in a canvas bag behind the church of Saint Walburga. I told myself the silver was for flour, for dried cod, for powder to supply the city garrison. But the flour never came. The powder was mixed with river sand. The money went into Spanish pockets, and now Moretus holds the receipt with my own seal stamped in green wax."

Giles paused with the wooden rounce half-turned in his fist. His deafened ears caught none of the alderman's confession, but his eyes followed the twitching mouth and the shaking fingers. He spat onto the cold floorstones, wiped his soot-stained forearm across his brow, and turned the oak handle with a ferocious, vengeful heave that set the timber joints groaning.

Van Hout sank onto a wooden stool, his heavy hands trembling between his knees. The velvet of his cloak was soaked through with melted snow. He pulled a silver-gilt seal from his pocket and stared at the engraved coat of arms: three silver lozenges on a blue field, the ancient emblem of his house in Antwerp.

"My grandfather built the warehouse at the English Bourse with his own ships," the alderman whispered, his chin sinking toward his breast. "He traded with Danzig, with Lisbon, and with London. He believed that an Antwerp merchant was the peer of any count in Spain. Now I sit in a cellar with a printer's widow, hiding from my own council clerks like a common thief. If Moretus arrests me tomorrow, my sons will be disinherited and my daughter will be forced into a convent in Louvain."

Katrijn stood over him, holding a grease lamp in her soot-stained hand. "Your sons will have bread, Alderman, if the city holds. But if Parma enters the gates, there will be no inheritance for any Protestant in Brabant. The Spanish inquisitors will seize your warehouses, your bills of exchange, and your stone house on the Meir. The only defense left to us is to publish your confession before the guild wardens vote to surrender the dikes."

Van Hout looked down at his boots, where the yellow trench mud had dried into a stiff crust. "When Parma took Dendermonde in August, he hung thirty reformed ministers from the ramparts," the alderman whispered, his throat catching on the words. "He had their bodies stripped and thrown into the river ditches for the swine to eat. I saw the corpses floating past the water gate when I rode out with the peace delegation. That was the day I agreed to meet his secretary behind the church of Saint Walburga."

Van Hout rubbed his throbbing temples with the heels of his hands, his eyes bloodshot in the candlelight. "I have sat in the council chamber for twenty years, Katrijn," he muttered, his voice breaking with shame. "I thought the peace of Antwerp could be bought with silver florins and diplomatic delays. But Parma does not sell peace. He sells chains, and he demands our souls as the first installment of the purchase price."

"We are not taking the bridges," Katrijn said. "We are taking the ice."

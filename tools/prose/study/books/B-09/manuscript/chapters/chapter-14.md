# Chapter 14: The Tavern on the Cow Gate

The taproom of the Red Lion on the Cow Gate smelled of stale rye beer, wet wool, and the greasy smoke of tallow candles stuck into empty stoneware jugs.

Giles sat in the darkest corner beneath the timber stair, his back pressed against the lime-washed brickwork, an earthen mug of sour small beer resting between his hands. His rough wool cap was pulled low over his damaged left ear, and he kept his ink-blackened fingers wrapped around the earthenware clay so the guild spies could not see the marks on his knuckles.

Around him, thirty harbor dockers, grain carters, and river bargemen sat crowded on the rough pine benches, their voices rising in a sullen, angry roar over the clatter of pewter tankards.

"Parma has driven thirty piles into the channel at Calloo," an old shipwright with a salt-gray beard shouted, slamming his fist onto the trestle. "My brother was on the scouting hoy from Zeeland yesterday. He saw the Spanish palisade stretching from the Brabant shore three hundred paces into the current. They have thirty-two armed ketches anchored in line, lashed together with iron ship-chains thick as my thigh. No grain fleet from Holland will break that timber wall."

"The burgomaster says the grain stores in the citadel will last until Easter," a young carter muttered, staring into his beer.

"The burgomaster lies through his gold teeth!" a dyer rasped from the bench beside the hearth. His apron was stained crimson with madder root. "There is no grain in the citadel! The Spanish garrison took four hundred sacks of rye out of the town magazines on Tuesday night and loaded them onto wagons for the camp at Beveren. The aldermen are filling their own cellars while our children eat turnip skins and chaff. My sister buried her youngest boy this morning. Starved to the bone, he was."

A heavy silence fell over the table, broken only by the crackle of green willow logs in the fireplace.

A stranger in a carter's frieze coat leaned forward from the end of the bench. He had a scarred lip and the squinting eyes of a man who worked with river ropes.

"They say there is a paper coming," the carter whispered, glancing over his shoulder toward the taproom door where the tavern keeper stood wiping mugs.

Every man at the table went still.

"What paper?" the shipwright asked.

"A printed tract," the carter murmured, lowering his voice until the dockers had to lean across the beer puddles to hear. "A brother of the Saint Nicholas guild saw a proof sheet in a warehouse near the brewery wharf. It gives the names. It tells which magistrates took the Spanish ducats to close the river redoubts, and which guild deacons sold their master printers to Moretus's bailiffs."

"Moretus," the dyer spat, crossing his fingers against the evil eye. "That dog has twenty thousand guilders in the Spanish bank at Genoa. He walks the Meir with his marten fur like a prince, while Hendrik Vane rots in a ditch outside the walls."

"Hendrik Vane was an honest craftsman," the old shipwright said, his voice deep with remembered respect. "He printed the maritime charters for the water guilds when the Spanish governors tried to steal our river rights ten years ago. He never charged the boatmen a brass mite for the seal. When Moretus broke his press, the heart went out of the Vrijdagmarkt."

Giles sat motionless in his corner, his thumbs digging into the rough clay of his mug.

He felt the heavy surge of pride and grief tightening his throat. In this foul, smoke-blinded tavern, among men who smelled of river mud and sweat, Hendrik's name was not the name of a condemned heretic; it was a rallying cry, an open wound that Antwerp had not forgotten.

"If the paper comes," the shipwright continued, leaning both elbows onto the timber, "we will not let Moretus burn it. If the paper names the traitors on the council, we take our boat-hooks and iron rammers and we march on the town hall. We open the city magazines ourselves, and we hang the Spanish pensioners from their own window sashes."

"Aye!" two carters growled, their hands dropping to the wood knives in their belts.

The tavern door swung open with a bang of cold wind and sleet.

Two uniformed sergeants of the civic night watch stepped into the taproom, their iron helmets dripping rain, their halberds held upright. The tavern fell dead silent. The keeper immediately picked up an earthen jug and rushed forward with a servile bow, offering two mugs of double beer on the house.

The sergeants did not take the beer.

Their eyes swept the crowded room, cold, suspicious, searching the shadows beneath the stairs. Giles kept his head down, staring into the dark dregs of his cup, his breath steady and slow.

"Curfew bell rings in twenty minutes," the lead sergeant announced, his voice ringing harsh against the low beams. "Clear the benches and douse your fires. The provost has doubled the guard at the Cow Gate. Any man found on the quays after the bell will spend the night in the Steen irons."

The dockers rose slowly, grumbling under their breath, shuffling toward the door with their heads down.

At the long trestle table near the beer kegs, three bargees from the Rupel were arguing over the depth of water on the drowned meadows. One of them had drawn a rough map in spilled beer on the oak boards, dipping his forefinger into the foam to trace the line of the Spanish dikes from Calloo to Beveren.

"The Spanish have thirty-two gunboats anchored in the river," the bargee growled, tapping the wet table. "Each hoy has four twenty-four-pound brass culverins mounted in the bows. If a Dutch ketch tries to pass the boom, the gunners will blow her timbers into kindling before she can come about. The only way through that timber wall is fire. If the Zeelanders do not send burning ships down on the ebb tide, Antwerp will eat horse meat before the snow melts."

A sailor in a salt-stained frieze jacket slammed his pewter tankard onto the table, splashing foam across the wet boards. He had a gold hoop in his left ear and the weathered, squinting eyes of a man who had sailed the North Sea fisheries from Dunkirk to the Shetlands.

"The Spanish fleet at Dunkirk has thirty-six armed pinnaces ready to put to sea," the sailor growled, wiping his mouth with his sleeve. "They have two thousand Walloon pikemen embarked on the transports. If the Sea Beggars cannot break the river palisade at Calloo before the deep freeze sets in, Antwerp will be invested from the land and the sea together. The only bread you will eat this winter will come from Parma's grain wagons, and you will pay for it on your knees before the inquisitor's crucifix."

The ink-balls hung from iron pegs driven into the cellar ceiling timbers. Giles took down the pair, running his palms over the stretched sheepskin pelts. The leather was tanned with alum and dog-dung, supple as kidskin, padded inside with carded wool that had been boiled twice to remove the lanolin grease. If the pelt was too dry, it would shed flakes into the galley; if the wool was too damp, the ink would curdle upon the type like sour cream.

"The wool is holding the frost, mistress," Giles whispered, his rough voice muffled by his sleeve. "I will warm the balls over the charcoal brazier before we ink the forms. Cold leather makes a patchy sheet, and this text must be read by men who have only rush-lights to see by."

Giles dipped the tip of his finger into the tallow pot and greased the wooden tracks of the press bed. The seasoned beechwood rails slid smoothly under the frame, without the telltale iron shriek that might carry through the floorboards to the street above.

Giles placed the iron chase upon the imposing stone, testing the squareness of the corners with a bronze rule. If the chase bowed by even half a millimeter under the pressure of the wooden quoins, the forme would spring in the middle during printing.

Giles waited until the taproom was half empty. Then he slid from the bench, pulled his wool cap over his scarred ear, and slipped out through the side scullery into the freezing darkness of the alley.

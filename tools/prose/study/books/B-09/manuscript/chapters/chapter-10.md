# Chapter 10: The Search on the Canal

The handcart had a loose iron tire on the left wheel that clattered over the cobblestones with every rotation.

Giles had greased the axle with mutton fat and wrapped the iron rim in strips of old linen sackcloth, but in the dead silence of the curfew hours, every bump against the granite kerb sounded to Katrijn like a musket shot echoing off the canal walls.

The November fog had rolled in thick from the Scheldt, smothering the city in a wet, freezing shroud that smelled of dead shellfish and coal smoke. Across the Sint-Jansvliet basin, the hulls of the moored peat barges were dark, hulking shapes looming through the mist, their rigging dripping steady beads of water into the black canal below.

Katrijn walked two paces ahead of the cart, her woolen shawl pulled over her nose and mouth. Under her cloak, her right hand gripped the municipal pass that Van Hout had given her in the churchyard.

In the belly of the two-wheeled barrow lay two reams of fine Troyes rag paper—one thousand sheets of heavy, white linen stock, packed into four bundles and wrapped in oilcloth to keep the river dampness from swelling the margins. On top of the paper, covering the bundles completely, lay six wicker laundry hampers filled with foul, blood-stained bandages, soiled sheets, and gray woolen shrouds discarded from the fever ward of the Saint Elizabeth hospital.

The stench of vinegar, dried blood, and carbolic soap was sharp enough to make Katrijn's eyes burn in the cold.

"Hold the cart," she whispered, raising her hand.

They had reached the foot of the Sint-Jansvliet bridge.

Through the drifting fog, the yellow light of an iron brazier gleamed from the stone arch. Two Spanish soldiers sat on empty salt casks beside the parapet, their twelve-foot pikes propped against the bridge rail, warming their gloved hands over the glowing coals. A third man—a sergeant in a buff leather coat and an iron breastplate—leaned against the bridge pillar, smoking a short clay pipe that glowed red in the mist.

"Walloon," Giles muttered behind her, his voice barely audible above the rattle of the wheel. "Not Spanish. He understands Dutch."

"Keep your mouth shut," Katrijn said. "Look at the cobblestones. If he speaks to you, point to your ear."

She stepped onto the bridge incline, her wooden pattens clicking sharply on the wet stones. Giles followed, his head down, his shoulders hunched as he pushed the heavy cart up the slope.

The sergeant lifted his head. He took the clay pipe from between his yellow teeth, spat a stream of brown juice into the gutter, and stepped into the center of the roadway, his hand resting on the hilt of his heavy broadsword.

"Halt the barrow," he barked in rough, French-accented Dutch.

Giles dropped the cart shafts. The barrow settled with a dull thump.

The sergeant walked slowly around the front of the cart, his iron-shod boots scraping the stones. When he caught the rank, sweetish stench of the hospital linens, his face twisted in disgust. He pulled his leather collar up over his nose and stepped back two paces.

"What is this filth?" he demanded, pointing with the stem of his pipe.

Katrijn stepped forward, curtseying low as a widow of the artisan class was expected to do before a crown officer. She drew the folded paper from her bodice and held it out with both hands.

"Linens for the pest-wash, Captain," she said, her voice trembling with just enough fear to sound natural. "From the fever ward at Saint Elizabeth. The council has ordered all bedding from the plague cellar burned or boiled in lye at the salt meadows outside the Red Gate."

The sergeant took the paper gingerly by its dry corner, holding it toward the light of the brazier.

He could not read the Latin script—few mercenaries in Parma's regiments could read more than their pay chits—but he knew the heavy red wax seal of the municipal admiralty and the bold signature of the city magistrate. His thumb rubbed the edge of the seal, testing its hardness.

"The Red Gate is closed until dawn," the sergeant said, glaring at her through the fog. "No carts pass the walls after the nine o'clock bell."

"The hospital warden swore the pass would clear us to the laundry wharf on the canal, my lord," Katrijn pleaded, keeping her eyes fixed on the muddy buckles of his boots. "The dead wagons are bringing three more bodies from the hospital cellar tonight. If the bedding is not washed in the lye vats before tomorrow's noon bell, the sisters cannot admit the wounded soldiers from the polder forts."

The mention of wounded soldiers seemed to carry weight. The sergeant grunted, handed the paper back to her, and turned toward the two men by the brazier.

"Pedro," he shouted in Spanish. "Bring the pike."

One of the soldiers rose from his salt cask, picked up his heavy ash pike, and walked over to the barrow. He reversed the weapon, holding the fifteen-pound ash shaft like an iron crowbar.

Katrijn's breath caught in her throat.

If the iron point penetrated the wicker hampers and pierced the oilcloth beneath, it would strike the hard, dense edges of two reams of Dutch-milled rag paper—paper that did not yield like soft linen, but rang solid as seasoned oak.

The soldier jabbed the blunt iron butt of the pike into the top hamper.

A squelch of wet, vinegar-soaked rags answered the thrust. The stench of hospital sickness billowed from the disturbed basket, so foul that the Spanish soldier cursed in his native tongue, pulled the weapon back, and spat into the canal.

"Basta," the soldier growled, waving his hand toward the opposite bank. "Es mierda del hospital. Let them go, Sargento."

The sergeant took a long pull on his clay pipe, blowing a stream of blue smoke into the cold air.

"Take your pestilence across the bridge, woman," he snarled. "And if I catch you in the lanes after the morning bell, I will throw you and your idiot boy into the moat."

"God preserve your lordship," Katrijn murmured, bowing her head.

She tapped Giles on the shoulder. The boy gripped the cart shafts, lifted the barrow with a grunt of strain, and leaned into the leather strap. The cart creaked forward over the bridge crest, past the glowing brazier, and down the western slope into the dark, narrow alleys of the Sint-Jansvliet quarter.

They did not speak. They did not look back.

They pushed the cart through three twisting alleys, their footsteps drowned by the steady dripping of the fog from the gables, until they reached the low wooden door of the abandoned dyer's house.

Giles dropped the shafts. He collapsed against the damp brick wall, his forehead pressed against his forearm, his breath coming in dry, painful sobs. His hands were shaking so violently that he could not unhook the barrow traces.

Katrijn reached down, took his soot-stained wrists in her hands, and squeezed them until the bone gave under her fingers.

"We have the paper, Giles," she whispered in his good ear.

The boy looked up. His eyes were wide, dark with the remembered terror of the bridge, but in the dim light of the alley, his jaw set hard as iron.

The handcart creaked as Giles guided the iron-shod wheels over the slick granite incline of the bridge. The fog was so dense that the water of the Sint-Jansvliet canal was invisible beneath them, marked only by the hollow sloshing of the tide against the stone piers and the smell of dead eels in the mud.

The Walloon sergeant took a deep breath of his clay pipe, blowing a cloud of gray smoke into the drizzle. He looked at Katrijn with the dull, contemptuous indifference of a foreign mercenary who had seen fifty Flemish towns starve and burned two dozen villages along the Meuse. To him, she was only another gray, shivering woman in a black hood, dragging the rotting bedding of plague victims to the ditch.

Giles pushed the two-wheeled barrow down the narrow descent of the Sint-Jansvliet bridge, his wooden clogs sliding on the wet river silt that coated the cobblestones. The fog was thickening, swirling in white, ghostly eddies around the dark timber gables of the dyer's house, muffling the distant chime of the Cathedral clock as it struck the second hour of the morning.

Katrijn walked with her hand resting on the canvas cover of the laundry hampers, feeling the solid, dense weight of the paper reams beneath the soiled linen bedding. One thousand sheets of French rag paper. Enough to print four hundred copies of the truth. She looked up at the darkened windows of the alley, where no candle burned and no face appeared behind the leaded panes, praying that the sleeping weavers would not wake until the cellar hatch was bolted behind them.

The fog along the Scheldt smelled of rotten cabbage and brackish river ice. At the water gate, three Dutch canal boats lay moored against the slippery pilings, their tarred hulls rising and falling with the sluggish winter tide. A lantern hung from the bowsprit of the middle boat, casting a murky yellow ring across the dark grease of the water. Beneath the canvas awning, an old waterman sat on an upturned barrel, puffing on a clay pipe that glowed cherry red in the gloom.

Giles shifted his weight on the shafts of the handcart, easing the iron-rimmed wheels over a sunken curb stone. The laundry hampers groaned under the compact heft of the unprinted paper. Four reams of fine Genoese laid paper, watermarked with a cardinal's hat and the triple keys. Hendrik had paid ten gold crowns for those sheets two years before the siege, storing them in dry pine chests beneath the shop rafters to keep the sizing from turning sour.

"If the gate guards prod the hampers with their rapiers," Giles muttered, his voice barely carrying past his shoulder, "the steel will strike the paper blocks with a dull thud. They will know in a second that no woman ever wore chemises as stiff as millboard."

"They will not prod them," Katrijn replied quietly, keeping her pace even with the barrow. "I tucked three greasy blood puddings and a crock of rancid beef drippings right beneath the top linen cloth. The stench will keep their noses six feet away before they ever think to draw a blade."

The Scheldt was running high with the winter ebb, carrying thick cakes of gray ice that groaned as they collided with the bridge pilings. Giles leaned his weight against the barrow shafts, maneuvering the heavy load through the narrow passage between the dye-house and the rope-walk. The iron tires of the wheels clicked softly over the frozen mud.

Katrijn walked with her hand upon the sacking. Beneath the greasy linen, the four reams of paper were dry and firm. She looked up at the stone battlements of the Steen, where the torchlight of the Spanish watch flickered behind the embrasures. If the sentry challenged them, Giles would not hear the command. She took a deep breath, resting her fingers on the cold hilt of the small bodkin tucked inside her bodice, and kept her eyes fixed upon the dark tunnel of the Sint-Jansvliet gate.

The wheels of the handcart creaked over the frozen mud of the Sint-Jansvliet alley. Giles kept his head down, his broad shoulders braced against the hauling straps as the barrow climbed the slight rise toward the water gate. The river fog lay thick between the gables, smelling of tarred cordage, horse manure, and cold Scheldt silt. Katrijn kept pace beside the load, her knuckles resting against the coarse burlap cover that hid the reams of French paper.

Giles set down the barrow shafts and paused beneath the stone archway of the Steen. His breath billowed in the winter frost, white and heavy as woodsmoke. He listened for the clank of Spanish halberds along the quay wall, his jaw set in a hard, defiant line as Katrijn checked the canvas wraps around the paper bundles.

"Unload the hampers," Katrijn said, drawing the iron cellar key from her pocket. "We have four hundred octavos to fold before the frost freezes the canal."

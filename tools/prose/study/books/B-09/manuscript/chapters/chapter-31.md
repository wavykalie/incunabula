# Chapter 31: The Confrontation on the Silt

The Spanish mastiff lunged on its iron chain, its claws scrabbling on the gray river ice, its throat raw with savage barking.

Katrijn did not take a step back.

She bent down, picked up one of the fresh octavo pamphlets from the severed hamper, and held it upright in her right hand. The morning breeze off the Scheldt caught the sixteen pages of Dutch rag paper, fluttering the margins with a dry, sharp rustle like iron foil.

Behind Moretus's beadles, across the frozen silt of the Crane Wharf, twenty men were descending the timber stairs from the quayside.

They were harbor dockers, grain shovelers, and ship caulkers in grease-stained woolen jerkins, carrying their heavy timber-hooks, iron pry-bars, and pitch ladles. They had heard the barking of the hound across the silent river basin; they had seen the four guild partisans leveled on the ice; and they had seen the woman in the black widow's bonnet standing alone before the proctor.

"Let the dog go, Master Moretus," Katrijn said, her voice clear and cutting through the frost. "Let the dog tear the throat of a printer's widow on the public silt of Antwerp. There are thirty dockers behind your back who watched Hendrik die on the Grote Markt. Let them see what the Stationers' Guild does to a woman who carries paper."

Moretus did not turn his head, but his jaw hardened.

He raised his hand. The Spanish sergeant jerked the chain, hauling the mastiff back until the beast sat choking in the snow, its red tongue dripping slobber onto the ice.

"You have no standing here, Katrijn," Moretus hissed, his voice trembling with contained venom. "You are an outlaw. A harborer of sedition. The provost's writ gives me the right to hang you from the yardarm of the crane without a magistrate's seal."

"Read the fifth page, Proctor," Katrijn answered. She extended her hand, holding the open pamphlet toward him. "Read Article III to the dockers. Tell them how many gold ducats you drew from the Spanish paymaster at the Italian Bourse on the fourteenth of May. Tell them who bought the iron chains that lock the river at Calloo while their children eat boiled weeds in the cellar."

A tall, broad-shouldered man in a tarred leather apron stepped forward from the advancing crowd of dockers.

He had gray hair tied with a hemp cord, his face deeply scarred by thirty years of handling river cables. In his right hand he carried an iron cargo hook sixteen inches long, its point honed to a razor edge.

It was Master Dirck, the head porter of the Crane Wharf.

He stopped three paces from Moretus's beadles, his cold blue eyes moving from the proctor's marten fur to the white pamphlets scattered across the ice.

"What paper is that, mistress?" the porter asked, his voice low as grinding granite.

Master Dirck stepped closer to the sledge, his boots crunching in the crusted snow. He reached into the broken hamper, lifted a handful of the white octavos, and spread them like playing cards across his leather palm. The black Brevier ink stood out sharp against the gray river light: *A True Record of the Smashed Presses.*

"I have known this woman since she was an apprentice girl in her father's shop on the Kammenstraat," Dirck shouted to the dockers behind him, his voice rolling across the ice like thunder. "She has buried her husband in the ditch, and she has kept her children on turnip peels while the aldermen drank malmsey wine with the Spanish colonel. If any man lays a finger on her smock, he will answer to forty iron hooks from the Crane Wharf."

Moretus's beadles looked at the wall of dockers advancing across the ice, their eyes darting from Master Dirck's iron cargo hook to the dark water of the open river channel behind them. They were four men armed with ceremonial staves and halberds, facing seventy harbor workmen whose hands were hardened by a lifetime of handling heavy timber and stone.

"Put down the partisans, boys," Master Dirck warned, his voice low and steady as grinding millstones. "You are the sons of Antwerp weavers and cobblers. Do you want your mothers to see your bodies floating in the Scheldt for the sake of four hundred Spanish ducats?"

Katrijn dipped a clean rag into cold river water and wiped the face of the brass rule. A compositor's fingers grew numb after three hours in an unheated room. When the frost got into the joints of the thumb, a worker would mistake an *n* for an *u* or set a letter upside down without feeling the notch on the nick. She blew warm breath onto her reddened fingers, rubbing them against the coarse wool of her kirtle to restore the circulation before lifting the next line into the stick.

"The truth of Antwerp, Master Dirck," Katrijn said, turning to face the dockers. "The record of who sold the bread from your mouths."

# Chapter 23: The Children's Lookout

The linen thread was tied around Katrijn's left wrist.

It was a piece of strong, three-ply bookbinder's twine, waxed with beeswax and run through a small iron staple in the floorboards beneath the coal chute. From there, it traveled thirty feet upward through the dark chimney casing into the garret above the kitchen, where eight-year-old Clara sat on a three-legged stool in the freezing cold.

The rule was simple.

If a single lantern passed the alley mouth, one gentle tug: the watch was on the quayside.

If soldiers halted at the dyer's stoop, three sharp jerks: the bailiffs were at the door.

Katrijn stood at the composing stone, collating the printed sheets of signature B. Her wrist twitched.

A single tug.

She froze with a sheet suspended between her fingers. Giles stopped the ink-balls two inches above the forme, holding his breath in the gloom.

Above them, through the brick vault, came the muffled scrape of iron-shod boots on the frosty cobblestones. Two men were walking slowly down the Sint-Jansvliet alley, their voices low, grumbling against the freezing northern wind that had turned the gutter water into solid glass.

Katrijn looked up toward the ceiling.

In her mind, she saw Clara sitting in the dark garret, wrapped in her father's old woolen cloak, her small bare feet tucked beneath her skirts to keep them from freezing to the pine floor. Five-year-old Pieter would be sleeping beside her on the straw mattress, covered with sheep wool offcuts and empty flour sacks, his thumb in his mouth, coughing softly in his sleep.

A child of eight should have been sleeping in a warm bed with a feather bolster, dreaming of Saint Nicholas marzipan and gingerbread kings.

Instead, Clara was guarding a secret press with a piece of bookbinder's thread tied to her thumb, watching the street lanterns through a knothole in the shutter. She knew that if she dropped the twine or fell asleep from cold, her mother would be dragged to the Steen prison before morning.

The thread tightened again. A long, steady pull, held for three heartbeats, then released.

The soldiers had passed the dyer's wall. They were marching toward the canal bridge.

Katrijn let out her breath in a long, silent plume of white vapor.

She looked down at the sheet in her hands. It was the tenth page of *A True Record*, listing the names of the thirty-four Antwerp citizens who had been executed on the Grote Markt since the Duke of Parma laid siege to the Scheldt.

Among them was Jan De Smet, the weaver who had refused to pay the Spanish war contribution; Maeyken Claes, the widow who had baked bread for the Dutch garrison at Calloo; and Hendrik Vane.

Every name had cost an executioner's fee of three guilders, paid from the town treasury and entered into the municipal ledger under the heading of *Sundry Expenses for the King's Justice.*

She untied the twine from her wrist, walked to the ladder, and climbed to the trapdoor beneath the kitchen floor. She pushed the wood up two inches, slipped her hand through the opening, and set two cold rye crusts and a slab of dried salt pork onto the kitchen floorboards.

"Clara," she whispered through the gap.

A small, cold hand reached through the darkness, taking the food from her fingers.

"The soldiers went to the bridge, Mother," the girl's whisper came down through the floorboards. "They had a lantern on a pole. The dog barked at the water butt, but the sergeant pulled him away."

"Eat the pork, child," Katrijn whispered back. "Give the soft crust to Pieter when he wakes. We have eighty sheets left to pull. When the morning bells ring, it will be done."

"I am not cold, Mother," Clara said, her voice trembling slightly in the dark. "I have Father's cloak."

In the garret above, Clara sat wrapped in Hendrik's woolen cloak, her small knees pulled tight to her chest on the three-legged stool. The draft whistled through the gaps between the roof slates, dusting her dark hair with tiny crystals of dry snow that blew in from the northeast. She held the bookbinder's thread looped twice around her thumb. She felt the faint, rhythmic vibrations that traveled up from the cellar floor.

Every time the heavy screw descended thirty feet below, a tiny tremor passed through the chimney masonry, buzzing through the brickwork like a sleeping hornet. Clara counted the blows in her head: two hundred and sixty... two hundred and sixty-one. Each stroke was one more loaf of bread, one more cartload of dry peat from the bogs, one more morning that the black-coat men could not take them.

In the dark garret, Clara held the linen thread between her fingertips, her breath forming tiny clouds of frost against the rough roof rafters. Through a knothole in the wooden gable, she watched the street lanterns of the civic patrol move slowly across the snow-dusted cobblestones of the Sint-Jansvliet quayside.

The soldiers were marching in pairs, their halberd blades gleaming in the torchlight, their yellow wool sashes sodden from the sleet. Clara did not move. She sat motionless on her stool, her small hand steady on the twine. She felt the quiet pulse of the press working thirty feet below her feet.

Katrijn stepped into the coal cellar and reached behind the stacks of peat fuel. Her fingers touched the cold surface of an earthenware jug hidden inside an empty herring cask. She pulled the stopper. The bitter scent of vitriol and nut-gall rose from the dark fluid. It was iron gall ink, mixed two years before the siege for penning the guild rolls. If the bailiffs smashed the printing press, she would copy the names onto parchment slips by hand, letter by letter, until her fingers froze to the quill.

From the street above, a dog barked three times, sharp and panicked. Katrijn blew out the rush-light and stood motionless in the absolute dark, listening to the drip of condensation along the barrel vault. A squad of Spanish pikemen marched past the grating. The iron ferrules of their eighteen-foot ash pikes clinked against the cobblestones with a cold, metallic rhythm. One soldier cursed his sergeant in the rough dialect of Castile, his leather boots splashing through the filthy slush of the gutter. Katrijn kept her hand pressed over her mouth until the iron clatter faded toward the fish market.

She waited in the darkness of the coal vault until the street was silent again. The smell of damp peat and cold stone was suffocating. She wiped the soot from her fingers onto her wool apron, counted twenty heartbeats in the quiet, and slipped back toward the stone cellar where Giles stood waiting by the press bed.

She leaned against the rough brickwork of the cellar arch, her boots wet with slush. Down in the darkness, the canal water gurgled through the stone culverts, carrying the refuse of the dye-works out into the gray river.

Katrijn closed the trapdoor, slid the wooden bolt into its iron slot, and climbed back down into the cellar.

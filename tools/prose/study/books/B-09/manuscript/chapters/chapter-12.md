# Chapter 12: The Children by the Hearth

The kitchen smelled of damp lime and cold ash.

Katrijn knelt before the low fireplace, coaxing the tiny blue flame of two peat briquettes with a wooden shingle. In the iron pot hanging from the crane, half a pint of oatmeal simmered in three cups of canal water, thickened with a thumb-sized lump of salted mutton tallow that Huybrechts had thrown into her apron on market morning. It was thin, greasy food, gray as river foam, but it was warm.

Clara sat on the low bench, her woolen stockings pulled tight over her knees to ward off the draft that whistled under the floorboards. She held Pieter on her lap. The boy was five, but his legs were thin as birch twigs, his skin pale and translucent under the eyes where the winter cough had settled.

"Is the black-coat man coming back, Mother?" Clara asked, her voice small in the hollow chimney.

"No," Katrijn said. She stirred the porridge with a peeled alder spoon. "The proctor has gone to his dinner at the guildhall. He will not trouble us today."

"He looked at my shoes," Pieter whispered, burying his nose in Clara's woolen bodice. "He smelled like church candles."

"He smelled of cloves," Clara said, her dark eyes sharp and observant. "He had a silver box with little holes in the top. When Father was in the shop, the men from the Town Hall had silver boxes like that. They put sweet dust on their coats so they would not smell the printers' ink."

Katrijn did not answer. She ladled the hot gruel into two wooden bowls, set them on the bench between the children, and handed each a horn spoon.

"Blow upon it," she commanded gently. "It will burn your lips."

The children ate in silence, scraping the bowls until the horn spoons clicked against the bare wood. For eight months, hunger had been their constant companion: not a sudden, dramatic famine, but the slow, grinding erosion of daily life in a city under siege. First the white wheaten bread had disappeared from the bakers' tables; then the butter and the Gouda cheese; then the salted herrings had doubled in price, until a single fish cost more than an apprentice's daily wage. Now, with Parma's army throwing up earthen redoubts along both banks of the Scheldt, the river was choked with Spanish gunboats, and the great grain storehouses at the water gate were nearly empty.

When Pieter finished his porridge, he leaned his head against Clara's shoulder and closed his eyes, his breath coming in faint, wheezing whistles.

Clara waited until her brother's hands went limp with sleep. Then she leaned toward Katrijn, her gaze dropping to the floorboards beneath the dresser where the trapdoor to the coal cellar lay hidden under a heap of empty wool sacks.

"Mother," the girl whispered.

Katrijn paused with the wooden spoon in her hand. "Yes, child?"

"There was a man in the alley yesterday," Clara said, her voice barely louder than the draft in the chimney. "Before the noon bell. I was looking through the small window above the water butt."

Katrijn's fingers tightened on the alder wood. "What kind of man?"

"He had a black coat with dark velvet on the collar," Clara said. "Not like the proctor. His coat was torn at the hem, and he had dirty boots with brass buckles. He stood by the coal grate for a long time. He knelt in the gutter and looked through the iron bars into the dark."

"Did he speak to you?"

"No. I stayed behind the shutter like you told me. But he had a little piece of chalk in his pocket. When he stood up, he made a white cross on the corner of the dyer's wall, right beside the cellar chute."

Katrijn felt the skin across her scalp prickle with cold terror.

A chalk mark.

In the old days, before the Duke of Alba marched his tercios into Brabant, a white cross on a door meant the plague had visited the house. But under the Spanish military council, a chalk mark on an alley wall meant something far more lethal: it was the mark of the provost marshal's spies, pointing out suspected houses to the night raiding parties before the curfew sweeps began.

She set the wooden bowl on the hearth, stood up, and walked to the small grimy window that looked out into the side lane.

Through the greenish bottle-glass panes, the alley lay deserted in the freezing rain. Water poured from a cracked lead gutter onto the cobblestones, splashing against the stone footings of the dyer's house. At the corner of the masonry, three feet above the curb, she could see it: a crude, diagonal cross drawn in white lime chalk, half-washed by the drizzle but still clearly visible against the dark, soot-stained brickwork.

Moretus had not come to her kitchen on an idle suspicion.

The guild proctor was weaving his net. He had posted his watchers along the Sint-Jansvliet; he had marked the house; and he had come in person to test her nerves and see if she would break or flee. If she packed her bundles and took the children toward the gates, the bailiffs would seize her on the bridge; if she remained, the night watch would smash the cellar hatch the moment they had proof of the press.

She walked back to the bench where Clara sat guarding her sleeping brother.

Katrijn knelt on the cold floorboards, took Clara's small, rough hands in her own, and looked straight into her daughter's eyes.

"Clara," she said softly.

"Yes, Mother?"

"If there is a loud noise at the street door tonight—if you hear iron boots or the sound of wood breaking—you do not run to the cellar. Do you understand me?"

Clara's lower lip trembled, but she nodded. "I take Pieter."

"You take Pieter by the hand," Katrijn instructed, her voice steady and deliberate. "You open the small scuttle in the garret roof behind the peat stack. You climb onto the dyer's leads, walk along the gutter to the brewery roof, and climb down the cooper's ladder into the malt yard. You go straight to Master Huybrechts's bakery on the Meir. You show him this."

She reached into her bodice, pulled out the cold pewter guild token of Hendrik Vane, and pressed it into Clara's woolen pocket.

"Tell Huybrechts that Hendrik's children are on the street," Katrijn whispered. "He will curse you, and he will curse your father's memory, but he will not let the Spanish take you. He will hide you in his flour bins until the morning carts leave for Zeeland."

Clara looked down at her pocket, her eyes wide with tears that did not spill. "And you, Mother? Will you come to the brewery?"

"I have work on the stone," Katrijn said. She stroked the girl's tangled hair with her ink-stained thumb. "Giles and I have four hundred voices to wake before the frost takes the river. When the voices have spoken, Hendrik will sleep in peace, and we will buy all the white bread in Brabant."

Katrijn sat on the low stool, watching the frost feather across the corners of the small bottle-glass window panes. Outside, the dusk was deepening into a bruised, purplish gray over the chimney pots of Sint-Jansvliet. Across the street, an old woman was nailing an oiled linen cloth over her broken window frame to keep out the draft from the river.

In the corner of the hearth, Pieter had arranged three dry pine kindling sticks into the shape of a press gallows. He had found a scrap of gray paper in the wood basket, and he was pressing the flat of his thumb against the wood, making a soft *clack-clack* sound with his tongue like the turning of a rounce. Clara watched him in silence, her hand resting protectively on his shoulder.

"Father told me that when the river opens in the spring, the English ships bring oranges from Portugal," Clara murmured, looking up at Katrijn. "Round yellow oranges wrapped in tissue paper that smells of sweet cloves. Will the ships come this year, Mother?"

Katrijn sat on the bench beside Clara, watching Pieter sleep with his head in his sister's lap. The boy's face was thin, the skin around his cheekbones stretched tight as drumhead parchment, his small chest rising and falling in shallow, rapid breaths. She pulled the hem of Hendrik's old woolen cloak over his bare ankles to keep out the draft that whistled under the door.

On Saint Nicholas Eve two years before, the kitchen on the Vrijdagmarkt had been warm, filled with the aroma of roasted chestnuts, spiced ginger biscuits, and dark Spanish raisins. Hendrik had built a small wooden puppet theatre for Pieter, carved with little figures of the Prince of Orange and the sea captains of Flushing. Now the puppet theatre was firewood in a bailiff's stove, and the only toy Pieter had was a charred piece of pine kindling.

Moretus stepped closer to the workbench, his glove trailing along the edge of a pine shelf where dry bundles of parchment offcuts were stacked. He leaned over an open drawer of two-line initials, picking up an ornate letter *D* cut with foliage and a dancing satyr. He turned the boxwood block in his white fingers, holding it to the candlelight to inspect the grain.

"Antwerp was once the intellectual heart of the Spanish Netherlands, Master Giles," Moretus remarked, his voice dropping to a smooth, purring cadence. "In my father's day, fifty presses turned from sunrise to dusk between the Kipdorp and the canal. Christopher Plantin printed the Royal Polyglot Bible with royal subsidies, twelve hundred sheets an hour, eight languages side by side in perfect register. That was order. That was piety and commerce united under the crown of His Catholic Majesty."

He laid the boxwood initial back into the drawer with a gentle, precise tap. "Now look at our streets. Half the masters have fled to Leiden or London. Those who remain hide in basements like sewer rats, printing foul little broadsides on scrap paper with muddy ink, whispering mutiny into the ears of starving cobblers. Do you truly imagine that a pamphlet can stop Parma's cannon, Giles? Or do you imagine that when the Spanish storm the walls, the Duke will pause to ask whether a printer was merely following orders?"

Moretus walked to the corner of the printshop, where an iron press from Nuremberg stood under a dust-cloth. He pulled the coarse linen aside with two fingers, exposing the heavy bronze spindle and the massive iron platen. His gaze moved across the maker's mark stamped into the cross-beam with cold, aristocratic scrutiny.

"In Frankfurt last autumn, the Emperor's commissioners seized twelve hundred pamphlets printed in Basel," Moretus said quietly, his voice echoing in the stone room. "They burned the books in the public square before the cathedral. Then they broke the printer's thumb with an iron pliers so that he could never lift a composing stick again. That is how the Holy Roman Empire treats those who spread heresy among the commons. Do you believe Antwerp will be treated with greater mercy when Parma's tercios march through the Red Gate?"

Moretus stepped back toward the door, his hand resting on the silver pommel of his rapier. "The Council of Troubles made twenty thousand martyrs, Master Giles," he said, his voice quiet as snowfall upon a church roof. "The Duke of Alba believed that fear was the only sovereign remedy for rebellion. My master, the Prince of Parma, is wiser. He offers the town its ancient trade if you will bend your necks to the King. But if you persist in scattering seditious libels through the alleys, he will leave Antwerp a desert of broken bricks."

Moretus turned his cold gaze toward the window, where the first gray light of dawn was breaking over the frozen Scheldt. "You mistake my purpose, Giles," he said softly, smoothing the dark fur on his collar. "The King does not seek to destroy Antwerp. He seeks to prune the wild branches that the vineyard may bear fruit for the Church. Those who bow to the pruning knife will flourish; those who resist will be cast into the fire."

She kissed Clara's forehead, wrapped her woolen cloak around her shoulders, and lifted the trapdoor into the darkness of the cellar below.

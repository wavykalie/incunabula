# Chapter 29: The Frozen Canal

The Scheldt was a desert of gray ice.

In ordinary winters, the great river of Antwerp was a highway of three hundred masts: Dutch grain hoys from Zeeland, English merchantmen with broadcloth from London, Portuguese caravels laden with spices and Brazilian sugar, their colorful pennants snapping in the westerly wind.

Today, under the iron grip of the November frost and the Spanish blockade, the river lay dead.

From the quayside wharves of Antwerp across to the low, flooded dikes of the Flanders shore, the water was frozen two feet deep: a jagged, desolate waste of pressure ridges, tilted ice floes, and frozen slush that creaked and groaned with a hollow, subterranean rumble whenever the ocean tide pushed beneath the shelf.

Fog hung low over the ice, gray and freezing, muffling the sound of the church bells that began to toll the six o'clock morning bell from the Cathedral tower.

Katrijn and Giles moved across the ice in silence.

The iron runners of the sledge scraped over the crusted snow with a steady, whispering hiss: *hiss-shhh, hiss-shhh.* Giles leaned into the leather chest strap, his clogs finding purchase in the frozen ruts left by earlier carts, his breath coming in white plumes that froze into ice crystals on his collar.

Behind them, the stepped gables and church spires of Antwerp rose out of the mist like a city of stone ghosts.

To their left, three hundred paces down the channel, the dark silhouette of the Spanish pontoon bridge loomed through the fog.

Even from this distance, the scale of Alexander Farnese's siege works was terrifying. Thirty-two armed river ketches sat locked in the ice, their masts stripped of sail, connected by massive timber beams and iron chains that formed a floating wall eight feet high across the entire channel. At each end of the stockade, an earthen redoubt bristled with brass cannons; and in the middle of the river, mounted on an artificial island of sunken barges, rose the great wooden fort of Saint Maria, its watchtowers commanding every inch of the frozen current.

Katrijn kept her eyes fixed on the Crane Wharf ahead.

Under the curfew orders, all traffic across the ice was forbidden without a military pass from the Spanish provost. But between the Sint-Jansvliet basin and the docks, a natural depression in the riverbed had created a hidden channel—a "silt-gut"—where the winter ice had cracked and refrozen into a sunken road beneath the high stone walls of the river warehouses.

If they stayed in the gut, they were invisible to the sentries on the pontoon bridge; but if they were trapped in the cut by a patrol from the quayside, there was no escape through the vertical stone bulkheads.

"Hold the runners," Giles muttered, halting in his tracks.

Katrijn stopped, her hand tightening on the rear crossbar of the sledge.

Through the drifting fog, fifty yards ahead where the sunken channel opened onto the mudflats of the Crane Wharf, she heard the sound of iron boots.

It was not the heavy march of common soldiers; it was the light, rapid stride of men wearing fine leather shoes, accompanied by the distinctive, rattling clatter of guild staves and the wet panting of a hound.

A lantern beam flickered through the mist.

"Moretus," Katrijn whispered, her heart turning to stone in her breast.

"We cannot turn the sledge," Giles rasped, his eyes darting to the ten-foot ice ridges that walled the gut on both sides. "The runners will jam in the pressure cracks."

"Do not turn," Katrijn commanded, her voice dropping into a flat, steady calm that astonished her own ears. "Walk straight forward. Keep your head down. If they speak, let me answer."

She reached beneath her woolen shawl, found the hilt of the kitchen knife tucked into her apron string, and loosened the steel in its sheath.

The ice beneath their clogs was thick, scarred by the heavy iron runners of the peat sledges and the hooves of the artillery horses that had hauled cannons to the river batteries. In the middle of the channel, where the ocean tide pushed beneath the frozen crust, the ice shelf groaned with a deep, hollow booming that echoed from the stone bulkheads of the quayside.

Katrijn looked toward the Crane Wharf. The three massive timber cranes stood against the gray dawn sky like gallows trees, their iron pulleys dripping long icicles that reached toward the frozen silt below.

The river ice creaked beneath the sledge runners with a deep, hollow resonance that vibrated through the soles of Katrijn's clogs. In the center of the Scheldt, where the flood tide pushed against the frozen barrier, great blocks of green ice had buckled upward into jagged walls four feet high, dusted with fine powdery snow that blew in from the North Sea.

Giles leaned into the hauling traces, his breath coming in rhythmic puffs of steam, his scarred hands gripping the crossbar with unyielding strength as they navigated the narrow, winding channel of the silt-gut toward the Crane Wharf.

The handcart groaned over the icy ruts of the Sint-Jansvliet. Giles pulled the hauling traces tight across his chest, his wooden clogs slipping on the frozen horse manure that littered the gutter. The fog was freezing as it settled, coating the timber gables and the iron shop-signs with a brittle crust of white rime.

Katrijn walked two paces ahead, her eyes scanning the dark entries of the dye-houses. A stray cat darted from beneath a brewer's dray, its eyes glowing green in the dim light of a street shrine. She held her breath, watching the iron lantern of the night watchman swing three streets away along the canal wall. The watchman cried the fourth hour of the night, his chant muffled by the freezing mist: *Four of the clock and all quiet in the town.*

Giles pulled the handcart into the shadow of an arched stone portal. His chest heaved with deep, ragged breaths of cold air. Katrijn waited beside him, watching the dark canal water lap against the slime-covered stairs. A dead dog floated past the embankment, bloated and stiff with winter ice.

The iron wheels of the handcart clicked against the curbstone as Giles guided the shafts through the narrow alley. The freezing mist clung to their wool cloaks in a fine mesh of icy diamonds.

They pushed the sledge forward into the widening mist.

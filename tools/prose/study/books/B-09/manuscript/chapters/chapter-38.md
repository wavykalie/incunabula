# Chapter 38: The Memory of Hendrik

At twilight, the snow began to fall over Antwerp.

It fell in large, soft, silent flakes that settled upon the stepped gables of the merchant houses, dusting the slate roofs of the guildhalls and capping the stone buttresses of the Cathedral of Our Lady in pure white velvet.

Katrijn stood at the small diamond-paned window of the garret, looking out across the rooftops toward the Grote Markt.

The market square was quiet now.

The four thousand citizens who had gathered before the town hall had dispersed to their parish wards, carrying the sixteen-page octavos into every kitchen, workshop, and brewery in the city. The Spanish garrison had withdrawn behind the ramparts of the southern citadel, their guns silent, their commanders stunned by the sudden, unified defiance of the guilds.

On the town hall balcony, the imperial flag of the Habsburgs had been taken down; in its place fluttered the old maritime ensign of Antwerp: the white castle with the two severed hands of Brabo.

Katrijn took Hendrik's broken silver watch from her bodice.

She held the round case in her palm. The balance spring inside was silent, its steel hair curled into a useless knot, but the silver back had been polished until it reflected the pale twilight snow.

"They know, Hendrik," she whispered against the glass.

For eight months, her greatest dread had not been the gallows or the torture chamber; it had been the silence. The terrible, suffocating fear that Hendrik's name would be erased from the city roll, that his life would be recorded only as an asterisk in a Spanish inquisitor's register, a heretic forgotten in an unmarked ditch outside the Red Gate.

Now, four hundred copies of his memorial were in the hands of the people he had served.

Whenever an apprentice set a composing stick in an Antwerp shop, whenever a shipwright read a charter on the quays, whenever a mother sang a Dutch psalm by her evening fire, Hendrik's name would be spoken.

Not as a victim. Not as a criminal.

As a master craftsman who had held the line of truth against thirty thousand pikemen and thirty pieces of gold.

Behind her, the low wooden door of the garret opened.

Giles stood on the threshold. He had washed his face at the yard pump; the coal soot was gone from his cheeks, though the purple scar along his left ear remained sharp and prominent against his pale skin. He wore a clean woolen smock that Katrijn had mended for him, and carried an iron oil-can and a bundle of clean rags in his hands.

"The dyer's cellar is sealed with fresh mortar," Giles said, his voice quiet. "The neighbors helped me brick up the sewer pipe this afternoon. No bailiff will find the iron screw under the coal."

Katrijn turned from the window. "And the lye jar?"

"The lead sorts are safe at the bottom," Giles smiled, his eyes bright in the twilight. "I buried the jar beneath the cabbage bin in the yard. In the spring, when the frost leaves the ground, we can wash the sorts in vinegar and set a new case."

Katrijn walked to the boy, took his scarred hands in hers, and held them tight.

"You pulled well, Giles," she said.

The boy looked down at his crooked fingers, his lip trembling with an emotion he had kept locked behind his teeth for eight months. He nodded once, wiped his nose with his sleeve, and looked out the window at the falling snow.

"He would have liked the print," Giles said softly. "The Brevier was clean. Not a single halo."

From the high garret window, the roofs of Antwerp looked peaceful beneath the blanket of new snow. The spires of Saint Paul's and Saint Michael's rose into the purple twilight, their stone crosses capped in white rime. On the Meir, the torches of the civic trainbands moved through the streets, not to search for heretic presses, but to guard the grain storehouses for the common bread.

Hendrik's name was clean. It was written in four hundred books that would live in the chests of Antwerp long after the Spanish army had retreated across the Meuse.

From the garret window, Katrijn looked out over the quiet roofs of Antwerp as the twilight deepened into purple night. The snow was falling in large, silent flakes, covering the scars of the siege with an unbroken mantle of white velvet.

Hendrik's name was spoken in every guildhall in the city tonight. He was no longer a condemned heretic rotting in a ditch; he was the master printer whose courage had broken the silence of a besieged city.

Lucas Van Hout stood on the town hall balcony, looking out over the crowded expanse of the Great Market. The square was packed with thousands of citizens: weavers in their blue wool smocks, guild wardens in fur-trimmed cloaks, women with starving infants wrapped in shawls, and dockers leaning on their iron boat-hooks. In the center of the square, an apprentice stood upon an overturned beer tub, reading the clandestine broadside aloud at the top of his lungs.

The boy's voice carried across the frozen rooftops, clear and defiant. He read the names of the five magistrates who had received Spanish pensions. He read the exact sums paid in gold ducats. He read the secret agreements to deliver the river bastions to Parma's engineers. With every sentence, a low, ominous rumble rose from the crowd, like the sound of surf breaking against an iron breakwater. Van Hout saw Inspector Moretus standing by the portico below, his face pale as chalk, surrounded by six arquebusiers who held their matchlocks ready with trembling hands.

From his vantage point upon the town hall steps, Van Hout watched the crowd surge toward the stone portico. The faces of the weavers were dark with fury, their wool caps waving in the cold air like a sea of blue cloth. In the front ranks, three burly smiths carried iron sledges, their leather aprons stained with forge soot.

"Let the aldermen answer!" a voice roared from the center of the market square. "Let Van Hout show the gold florins he took from the Spanish commander!" The cry was taken up by five hundred throats, echoing from the high timber gables of the guildhalls until the stone statues on the cathedral porch seemed to tremble with the fury of the town.

The roar of the crowd in the Great Market rose like the sound of a winter storm breaking against the harbor sea-wall. The weavers shouted for the magistrates, their angry voices echoing between the guildhall gables.

"Yes," Katrijn said, looking out at the white roofs of Antwerp. "The Brevier was clean."

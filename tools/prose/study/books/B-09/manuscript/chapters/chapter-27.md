# Chapter 27: Folding the Octavos

The folding of the sheets had taken place during the fifth hour of the night, in the breathless silence before the weavers rioted on the Sint-Jansvliet.

It was the binder's craft: the transformation of flat, printed linen rag into the portable, pocket-sized weapon known as the octavo.

A broadsheet was a proclamation; it was nailed to church portals and town hall pillars where any bailiff with a halberd could tear it down with a single stroke. But an octavo was a conspirator's book. It was sixteen pages printed upon a single sheet of paper, folded three times upon itself, cut at the edges, and stitched through the spine with waxed thread so that it could be slipped inside a workman's woolen sleeve, concealed in a woman's bodice, or tucked into the lining of a merchant's boot without making a bulge.

Katrijn sat on the low stool before the pine bench, holding the bone folder in her right hand.

The tool was carved from the rib bone of an ox, polished glass-smooth by thirty years of handling, its edges thin and rounded so that it could crease the damp paper without tearing the fragile linen fibers.

Giles stood beside her, taking the damp sheets from the drying lines.

He laid the first sheet flat upon the pine timber, the print facing upward: signature A, containing the title page, the preface, and the opening four articles of the indictment.

"First fold," Katrijn said.

Her hands moved with the swift, rhythmic economy of long habit.

She took the right-hand margin of the sheet with her left thumb, brought it over to meet the left-hand margin, aligning the folio numbers at the foot of the pages with absolute precision. She held the edges together with her left fingers, took the bone folder in her right hand, and drew the polished edge down the center fold with a firm, decisive stroke: *Zzzzp.*

The paper creased with a clean, sharp snap. The sheet was now a quarto of four leaves.

"Second fold."

She turned the folded paper ninety degrees, brought the top edge down to meet the bottom edge, checking that the running titles at the top of the columns aligned to the thickness of a thread. Again the bone folder swept across the fold: *Zzzzp.*

An octavo of eight leaves.

"Third fold."

One final turn, folding the sheet in half once more along its vertical axis, followed by a hard, double-pass of the bone folder across the back. *Zzzzp-zzzp.*

Sixteen pages.

The sheet was now a compact, dense rectangle of paper, six inches high by four inches wide, perfectly aligned, ready for the binder's needle.

Clara had sat at the end of the bench, her small fingers holding the awl.

The eight-year-old girl had learned to pierce the signatures when she was six, sitting on Hendrik's knees while he bound the city merchant ledgers. She took each folded gathering from Katrijn's pile, placed it against the wooden guide-jig that Hendrik had carved from Dutch beech, and struck the head of the brass bodkin with a small wooden mallet.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Three clean, round holes through the spine: one at the center, one an inch from the head, one an inch from the tail.

Then came the needle.

Giles held a blunt bookbinder's needle threaded with two strands of Dutch flax twine, drawn through a block of yellow beeswax to make the linen cord slide smoothly through the paper. He passed the needle through the center hole from the inside out, brought it back through the head hole, carried it down the spine to the tail, and tied the two ends off in a tight, flat reef knot at the center of the fold.

He pulled the knot tight with his teeth, snipped the thread with his kitchen knife, and laid the completed pamphlet on the growing stack.

*Fold, crease. Fold, crease. Pierce, stitch, knot.*

In the candlelight, the four of them had worked like the components of a single clockwork engine. No word was spoken. The only sounds in the cellar were the dry scrape of the bone folder across the paper, the light *tap-tap* of Clara's wooden mallet, and the soft *snip* of Giles's knife through the waxed twine.

Every pamphlet was an identical, sixteen-page tract.

On the title page, the Great Primer letters stood out bold and uncompromising:

*A TRUE RECORD OF THE SMASHED PRESSES.*
*Wherein the Betrayal of Antwerp is Laid Open to the Commons.*
*Printed in the Year of Our Lord 1584.*

Beneath the title sat no printer's name and no imperial privilege; there was only a small, stark woodcut that Hendrik had carved ten years before: an anvil beneath an iron hammer, with the Latin motto encircling the border:

*STANTE CHARITATE, VERITAS NON CADIT.*
*While love stands, the truth cannot fall.*

By four o'clock, the stack of completed pamphlets had reached three feet high upon the bench: four hundred copies, tied in bundles of twenty-five with strips of coarse packing linen.

Katrijn had run her hands down the stack, feeling the dense, solid weight of the paper.

In those four hundred little books lay the life of her family, the memory of her husband, and the fate of the city. If they remained in the cellar, they were fuel for a bailiff's bonfire; if they reached the hands of the dockers, they were thirty thousand voices shouting down the walls of the citadel.

Katrijn's bone folder flew across the damp paper with the smooth, rhythmic hiss of an iron plane on dry pine. The French linen stock was tough, resilient, folding with razor sharpness without cracking along the spine. Every gathering was inspected for register: if the folio numbers at the bottom of the page did not align within the width of a pinhead, the sheet was rejected and laid aside for wrapper waste.

Giles had prepared forty lengths of waxed linen thread, each three feet long, knotted at the ends. He sat on an empty herring keg, his fingers moving with swift, mechanical precision, stabbing the needle through the center awl-hole, looping the flax through the head and tail, tying the knots with a single pull of his crooked thumbs.

Katrijn's hands moved with practiced, unthinking precision, drawing the bone folder across the spine of each folded gathering. The French linen paper took the crease with a sharp, resonant snap, forming clean, dense signatures that lay flat upon the pine bench without gaping at the margins.

Clara sat beside her, holding the small brass bodkin in her left hand, striking the head with the wooden mallet: *tap-tap, tap-tap.* Every stroke opened three clean holes through the back of the octavo, ready for Giles's waxed flax twine. By the time the third candle guttered in its saucer, four hundred copies lay stitched, trimmed, and gathered into neat quires.

Moretus stood by the leaded window of the council chamber, watching the smoke rise from the Spanish camp across the frozen polder. His hands were clasped behind his back, his black velvet cloak lined with white ermine that brushed against the floorboards. The silver crucifix on his chest caught the gray daylight from the canal.

"You speak of rights, Alderman," Moretus murmured without turning his head. "You speak of municipal charters granted by Duke Philip the Good two hundred years ago. Those parchments are yellow parchment, eaten by bookworms and dust. In Rome, the Holy Father has blessed the King's enterprise. In Madrid, the Council of State has resolved that this rebellion will be cauterized with fire and sword until not one heretical pamphlet remains in all the Low Countries."

He turned slowly, his pale blue eyes settling upon Van Hout like iron weights. "The printing press is an engine of disorder when left in the hands of common weavers and printers' widows. It scatters sedition among men who ought to be on their knees before the altar. When the Duke of Parma enters the marketplace, every press in Antwerp will be registered with the Guild of Saint Luke, under pain of the gallows. We will smash the wooden screw of every secret printer into kindling, and their type will be cast into bullets for the royal guard."

Moretus poured wine from a crystal flask into a pewter goblet, his hand steady as a sundial's gnomon. He held the wine up to the leaded window, watching the dark red liquid catch the winter daylight. The smell of spiced cloves and cinnamon filled the chilly council chamber.

"You Dutch burghers think with your ledgers, Van Hout," Moretus said with a cold, mocking smile. "You calculate liberty in bushels of rye and barrels of herring. You believe that an empire of three continents can be checked by town charters signed by dead dukes. King Philip has sworn to restore the ancient faith to every corner of these Netherlands, even if he must turn Antwerp into a heap of blackened ash. When the Walloon tercios enter the Meir, they will not ask whether a merchant was an alderman or a rebel. They will take the gold, burn the books, and leave the dead in the gutters."

Moretus adjusted the lace ruff at his neck with two fingers. "You speak of resistance, Alderman, as though Antwerp were an island in the ocean. Parma has twenty thousand Spanish veterans encamped between Beveren and Calloo. His Italian engineers are constructing an iron bridge across the Scheldt that will shut your harbor to every Dutch ship from Zeeland. When the bridge is completed, you will eat your own cart horses before the spring grass appears upon the polders."

She had wrapped the bundles in oilcloth, packed them into the willow fish hampers, and covered them with salt herring.

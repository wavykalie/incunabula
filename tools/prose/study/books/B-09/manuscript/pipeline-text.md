# Chapter 1: The Sound of the Lever

The oak lever groaned against the iron cheeks of the press. In the darkness of the coal cellar beneath Sint-Jansvliet, every pull sounded like a timber beam snapping in a ship's hull.

Katrijn Vane kept her left hand flat on the tympan frame. Her palms were calloused, the skin along her knuckles split from the caustic lye water she used to scour lead sorts after midnight. Above them, through two feet of mortared brick and damp soil, the night carts rumbled down the quayside toward the Scheldt. Each iron-rimmed wheel sent a tremor through the ceiling vault, shaking loose flecks of dry coal dust that settled on the damp linen frisket.

"Hold the rounce," she whispered.

Giles Claes leaned his shoulder against the ash wood crank. He did not speak. His left ear had been ruined eight months ago when the Stationers' Guild bailiffs tore open Hendrik's licensed shop on the Vrijdagmarkt; an iron bar had crushed the cartilage and left him half-deaf to anything softer than a hammer blow. Two fingers of his right hand were stiff, crooked like dried twigs where the sergeant's boots had stamped them into the floorboards. But his shoulders were broad, thickened by four years of swinging twelve-pound sledgehammers and pulling common press levers eighteen hours a day.

He gave the rounce handle a quarter turn. The carriage rolled under the platen with a wet, heavy rattle.

"Dampness on the second ream?" Giles muttered, his voice flat and loud in the enclosed cellar.

"Two passes with the sponge," Katrijn said. She reached into the earthen crock beside her stool, drawing out a folded scrap of paper from Troyes. She laid the sheet across her inner wrist. The linen rag felt cool, pliable as cured pigskin, without the greasy sweat that ruined an impression. "No wetter than morning grass. If the rag takes too much water, the ink bleeds across the margins. If it stays dry, the lead punches blind holes in the fibers."

"Moretus has three patrols along the canal," Giles said. He did not look up from the bed. "I smelled their pipe weed when I brought the peat sacks through the grating at curfew."

"The guild proctor can smell what he pleases," Katrijn said. "He does not sign the baker's ledger on the Meir."

She reached for the twin ink-balls resting in their wooden cups. The sheepskin casings were dark, stained nearly purple from years of walnut oil and chimney soot, stuffed with uncarded wool so tightly they bounced against the stone slab like boiled leather balls. She took a dab of fresh lampblack paste from the ink-block, worked the two balls together in a swift circular roll, and rocked them over the forme. The lead sorts clicked under the leather. It was a precise, rhythmic percussion: eighteen lines of Brevier type locked within an iron chase, held rigid by beveled oak quoins that Hendrik had planed from Dutch timber before the siege began.

Giles spat into the coal dust. He grasped the ash lever with both hands, planted his clogs against the oak sill, and threw his weight backward.

The heavy wooden screw descended through the brass nut. The platen struck the tympan with a dull, muffled thud that traveled down into the flagstones. Katrijn felt the blow in the arches of her feet. For two heartbeats, the press held the sheet in total compression, forcing the thick, boiled nut oil deep into the wet rag grain.

"Up," she said.

Giles eased the bar back. The counterweight creaked on its hemp cord. Katrijn flipped the frisket upward, peeled the damp paper from the tympan points, and held it close to the tallow candle.

The impression was sharp. The black letters stood out against the gray sheet without halo or smudge: three hundred lines of municipal receipts, ordered by a grain merchant who had already fled across the river to Zeeland. It paid four copper stuivers a dozen. Not enough for beef, but enough for two bags of dried parsnips and six bundles of dry marsh peat before the hard freeze locked the canals.

Along the right cheek of the wooden frame, a white hairline crack ran three inches down the grain. It had opened when the bailiffs kicked the press down the cellar stairs eight months ago. Every hard pull widened the gap by the thickness of a fingernail.

Katrijn ran her thumb over the split wood. The timber felt cold and strained.

"The cheek is moving," she said.

Giles wiped his forehead with the greasy sleeve of his linen shirt, leaving a smear of coal soot across his temple. "It will take two oak wedges in the mortise. Tomorrow, before curfew."

"We pull fifty more sheets before midnight," Katrijn said. She laid the fresh print onto the drying poles suspended beneath the vaulted ceiling. "The children have no bread for tomorrow's morning bell."

Giles picked up the wooden mallet from the paper stone. He tapped the iron quoins along the head of the chase, testing the tension of the lead. The sorts gave back a sharp, clear ring: the sound of seasoned metal held in true square.

"The wind has turned north," he said, nodding toward the ceiling grate where cold air poured down into the vault. "The river will be full of grease ice by morning."

"Then the peat carts will not cross the dikes," Katrijn said. She reached for the second dampened sheet, smoothed the linen deckle edge with her thumb, and laid it onto the brass tympan points.

Katrijn stepped to the wooden shelf where Hendrik had kept his ink-stones. The room smelled of cold coal, damp plaster, and old lard. On the shelf sat a small stoneware crock containing two ounces of Venice turpentine and an iron oil-can with a bent brass spout. She picked up the spout and let three drops of nut oil fall into the bronze socket where the rounce shaft passed through the timber cheek.

The press was an old machine, built in Ghent thirty years before the Duke of Alba marched his tercios into Brabant. Hendrik had bought the timbers from an English merchant who was fleeing the Spanish inquisitors. Its oak cheeks had been seasoned in salt water, its screw cut by a hand-chisel from solid Basque iron. When Katrijn had first married Hendrik, the press stood in the front shop on the Vrijdagmarkt beneath the sign of the Golden Compasses, where sunlight poured through twenty diamond panes and students from the Latin school stopped to read proof sheets pinned to the shutters.

Now the Golden Compasses was a heap of charred timber and broken bricks, burned by the provost bailiffs during the August riots. The city council had confiscated Hendrik's paper stores, three hundred reams of fine linen stock from Troyes and Basel, and carted the sheets to the stadhuis to be used for military muster rolls.

Katrijn reached into her apron pocket and drew out an iron bodkin. She used the sharp tip to pick a hardened fleck of dry lye crust from the eye of a lowercase *g* in the twelfth line. The lead sort was old, its shoulder battered by ten thousand impressions, cast from a matrix that Hendrik had bought at the Frankfurt book fair when they were first betrothed.

Hendrik had known the temper of every fount in Brabant. He could run his thumb across a galley in the dark and tell whether the type was cut by Robert Granjon or Claude Garamont by the slant of the italic ascenders. When he stood at the press, he worked with a serene, patient rhythm, singing Dutch psalms beneath his breath while the rounce turned on its greased gudgeons. He used to say that a true printer was both a blacksmith and a scholar, balancing twenty stone of oak pressure against a sheet of paper no thicker than an onion skin.

She checked the copper composing stick once more under the candle flame. The brass setting rule had been notched by Hendrik's grandfather forty years ago, marked with tiny hatch marks for pica and brevier. Every indentation held decades of oil and skin sweat, the dark bronze metal worn concave at the center where a compositor's thumb pressed the sorts tight against the head. In the silence of the shop, the ticking of the wall clock sounded like an iron hammer striking a cold anvil.

She blew upon the composing stick to clear away the fine lead dust that settled in the corners. The metal was icy against her knuckles. Through the draft in the wainscot, she heard the watchman strike the iron triangle at the water gate. Midnight had passed. In seven hours, the carters would begin hauling the frozen barrels of Rhine wine across the square.

She picked up a small tallow candle and held it close to the galley tray. The cold draft from the floorboards made the flame dance, throwing long, trembling shadows of the lead ascenders across the stone. Every sort had been cast by hand in the copper mold, trimmed at the foot with an iron rasp to ensure an even height to paper.

Giles reached for the rounce handle and rolled the carriage back onto the bed.

# Chapter 2: The Bread Line on the Meir

The cold on the Meir cut through Katrijn's woolen cloak like river reed.

Rain fell in a gray, greasy mist that turned the horse droppings and canal mud into black paste between the cobblestones. Two hundred women and boys stood in a ragged line before Master Huybrechts's bakery shutters, their wooden pattens clacking against the kerb whenever the press of bodies surged forward. Above them, the unfinished spire of the Cathedral of Our Lady rose into the low clouds, its great bronze bells silent under the curfew order of the military council.

Katrijn kept her hands jammed into her apron pockets. Beneath the coarse linen, her fingers curled around six copper stuivers and a folded pewter token stamped with the guild seal of Saint Luke. The metal was icy to the touch.

Beside her, Maeyken, the wife of an armorer from the Kipdorp gate, blew into her red fists.

"Three guilders for a bushel of rye yesterday at the New Bourse," Maeyken muttered, not turning her head. Her breath came in short puffs of white steam. "The German mercenaries at the redoubt took four grain hoys coming down the Rupel. They threw the carters into the reeds and dragged the wheat to their camp at Beveren. My oldest boy went down to the quayside at dawn to rake grain sweepings from the bilges. The Spanish sentry hit him across the ribs with a pike butt."

"Parma has closed the dikes," a weaver behind them rasped. His face was gray, lined with blue dye stains deep in the pores of his neck. "They say thirty thousand Spaniards are digging trenches from Calloo across the polder. They have cut five hundred pine trees from the Kempen to build a floating stockade across the Scheldt. No grain barge from Zeeland will reach the Crane Wharf by Christmas."

"Hush, Jacob," a woman snapped, glancing toward the corner of the cloth hall. "The provost has four Walloon sergeants in the tavern porch."

Two pikemen in rusted iron breastplates stood beneath the eaves of the Golden Fleece, their ten-foot ash shafts planted in the gutter. They did not watch the crowd; they watched the wagons rattling out of the cattle market, their yellow wool sashes sodden from the drizzle. Everyone in Antwerp knew the sashes. They were the color of Spanish muster rolls, the color of the garrison that had sacked the town eight years before and burned a thousand houses to the cellars.

A wooden bolt slid back with a sharp crack. The bakery shutter swung upward.

Master Huybrechts appeared behind the oak counter, wearing a flour-dusted leather apron and a wool cap pulled down over his ears. Behind him, the bread shelves were three-quarters bare. Instead of the round, golden loaves of Brabant wheat that used to fill the baskets on market mornings, there were only flat, gray trenchers of coarse rye, dark as peat and heavy with bean meal.

"One loaf to a household," Huybrechts shouted over the murmur of the crowd. He laid a heavy iron butcher's cleaver on the cutting board. "Six stuivers a loaf. Cash on the timber. No tally sticks. No credit for widow or church."

A roar of angry voices went up from the line.

"Six stuivers!" a dyer's widow cried, waving an empty basket. "It was four on Tuesday! You are grinding dried peas and sweepings into the flour, Huybrechts!"

"Take it or eat river grass," the baker snarled, slamming his fist onto the board. "The Spanish have burned the mills at Doel. If I cannot buy firewood from the forest carters, the ovens go cold tomorrow night. Six stuivers, or get out of my doorway."

Katrijn moved forward as the line shuffled. When her turn came, she laid four copper coins and the pewter token on the damp timber.

Huybrechts stared at the token. He pushed it back with the flat of his thumb. "What is this trash?"

"My husband's guild token," Katrijn said, her voice quiet and level. "Master Hendrik Vane, sworn printer of the Vrijdagmarkt. The council book gives master craftsmen two stuivers credit on the town reserve."

The baker looked up. His eyes were small, red-rimmed from wood smoke and sleepless nights. For a second, a flicker of uneasy recognition passed across his fleshy face. He looked past her shoulder toward the two Walloon pikemen standing beneath the tavern porch.

"Hendrik Vane has no credit on the town books, mistress," Huybrechts said in a low voice, leaning across the counter so the women behind could not hear. "Your husband died on the scaffold eight months ago with an imperial writ pinned to his shirt. The magistrate crossed his name out of the burger roll before the executioner had cleaned his sword. If Inspector Moretus sees me taking a condemned printer's token for flour, he will nail my ears to the pillory."

Katrijn did not take the token back. She looked directly into Huybrechts's watery eyes. "My children are five and eight. They have had nothing since yesterday's noon bell but boiled water and parsnip peelings. You knew Hendrik twenty years. He printed your daughter's marriage psalm on Italian linen rag and charged you not a single stuiver for the binding."

The baker's jaw tightened. He glanced once more toward the street corner, grabbed a small, misshapen rye loaf from beneath the counter shelf, and shoved it roughly into her basket. With a swift swipe of his grease-stained apron, he swept the four copper coins into his drawer and tossed the pewter token onto the wet cobblestones at her feet.

"Take it and go," he whispered hoarsely. "Do not come to my window again with that dead man's lead. Go by the wool market. The town bailiffs are checking passes at the Meir bridge."

Katrijn knelt in the mud, picked up the cold pewter token, and wiped the black gutter slush against her cloak.

She tucked the token deep into her bodice against her skin. She wrapped the rye loaf in her coarse woolen apron to protect the crust from the drizzle, pulled her hood low over her forehead, and stepped out into the crowded street.

Along the market colonnade, three women from the lace weavers' guild were arguing with a butter merchant from Brabant. The merchant had only two stoneware crocks of sour lard left in his handcart, and he was demanding sixteen stuivers a crock. A carter had dropped his horse by the fountain; the animal lay in the mud, its ribs protruding through its sodden hide, breathing in shallow gasps while two boys stripped the leather traces from its collar.

Katrijn walked with her eyes fixed on the stones before her clogs.

She did not look at the Spanish sentries on the bridge; she did not look at the black iron crosses painted on the doors of the abandoned merchant houses where the fever had taken the clerks. She thought only of the small kitchen, the two children sitting on the bench, and the forty sheets of damp paper hanging on the cellar cords.

Across the street, two carters from the Rupel were trying to haul a timber wagon through the black mud. The wheels had sunk to the axles in the gutter, and the driver was beating the shire horse with a knotted hemp rope, his curses mixing with the cold drizzle that dripped from the shop eaves. A crowd of barefoot boys stood on the kerb, watching the wagon with hollow, hungry eyes, waiting to see if a sack of malt would split upon the stones.

The price of bread was the pulse of the siege. When the Duke of Parma had first laid his earthen redoubts across the polders in July, a six-pound loaf of rye had cost two copper stuivers. By September, when the Spanish took the river fort at Liefkenshoek, it was four. Now, with the north wind blowing freezing sleet off the North Sea and the dikes broken to flood the meadows, money had ceased to have meaning. An armorer on the Meir had traded his silver pocket watch for ten pounds of dried split peas and two salt herrings.

A gray-haired woman wrapped in a torn dyer's blanket leaned against the stone post beside Katrijn, her hands tucked deep inside her sleeves to keep them from the freezing drizzle. Her name was Betken, the widow of a sailmaker who had died of the camp fever in the autumn.

"They say the Spanish commander at Calloo has promised two barrels of flour to any carter who brings a list of the reformed printers to his headquarters," Betken murmured, her voice barely louder than the patter of the rain. "My nephew was at the meat market this morning. He saw three Spanish sergeants nailing a red placard to the shambles wall, offering forty silver guilders for information on the secret press of the Sint-Jansvliet. God help whoever has the timbers in their cellar, Katrijn. If the bailiffs find the screw, they will hang the children with the mother."

Across the frozen cobbles of the Steen, an old fishmonger was scraping fish scales from a split pine bench with a dull iron scraper. The scales flew into the freezing gutter like silver coins. The man muttered curses into his wool scarf, his boots bound with greasy hemp rope to keep the mud from his chilblains. Two Spanish sentries watched him from the guard post by the gate, their matchlocks smoking in the damp river air, their eyes dull with cold and vinegar beer.

Behind the timber gables of the Kammenstraat, two starved crows fought over a frozen cabbage stalk in the snow. An apprentice baker in a patched wool tunic hurried past them, his apron tucked up into his belt to keep the hem out of the black street mud. He cast a fearful glance toward the Spanish guardhouse at the corner where three Walloon pikemen warmed their hands over a smoking iron brazier.

"They searched the grain mill at the Red Gate before sunrise," the boy whispered as he passed Betken. "The provost-marshal seized twenty sacks of barley meal intended for the poor-relief. They say Parma has promised all confiscated grain to the Italian cavalry officers at Beveren. If a man wants bread this winter, he must pay in Spanish silver or chew river weeds."

Betken blew on her chapped fingers, her eyes watching the Spanish sergeant who stood on the steps of the weigh-house. "They took my brother's cart two days ago," she muttered, pulling her shawl tighter around her throat. "He had six sacks of oats brought in from the polders before the sluices were cut. The bailiffs gave him a scrap of Spanish paper with a red seal, promising payment in gold when the King's army occupies the town hall. Paper promises will not fill a pot with porridge when the winter freezes the canal."

She turned down the narrow alley that led toward the canal, shielding the bread beneath her arm.

# Chapter 3: The Midnight Knock

The three knocks came at the fourth hour after curfew.

They were not the frantic, heavy blows of the Spanish watch, whose sergeants kicked the lower panels with their iron-tipped boots until the lime mortar crumbled from the lintel. These were slow, measured taps: the dry rap of an ivory walking cane against the weathered oak hatch that opened from the alley into the cellar coal chute.

In the corner beneath the brick vault, Giles froze with an iron wrench suspended over the press spindle. His breath stopped. His eyes darted to the wooden mallet resting on the paper stone.

Katrijn held up her hand. She extinguished the tallow candle between two wet fingers, plunging the cellar into darkness broken only by the faint orange glow of three charcoal briquettes dying in the iron brazier. The scent of hot tallow and bitter lye rose thick in the still air.

The cane tapped again. Twice. Then a pause, followed by a light scrape of wood along the iron grating.

"Wait by the timber prop," Katrijn whispered.

She picked up the small kitchen knife she used for trimming deckle edges from damp paper. She moved silently across the flagstones, her wool stockings making no sound against the damp silt that seeped from the canal wall. She reached the bottom of the ladder, pressed her ear against the rough pine planking of the hatch, and listened.

Above her, in the alley, water dripped from an overhanging gutter into the cobbles. A man was breathing heavily, his chest rattling with the wet cough of someone who spent his winters in drafty guildhalls.

"Mistress Vane," a voice murmured through the chink where the cellar door met the foundation stone. The voice was low, patrician, modulated with the formal cadence of the town hall chambers. "Open the latch. I am alone."

Katrijn's hand tightened on the knife haft. "The watch shoots anyone found in the alleys after the nine o'clock bell."

"The night watch knows my livery," the man replied through the wood. "Pull the bolt, Katrijn. I carry neither bailiff nor steel."

Katrijn reached up and drew the heavy oak bolt. The iron hinges, greased with rancid bacon fat, turned without a squeak. She pushed the door upward three inches.

A man knelt in the drizzle of the alley. He wore a heavy traveling cloak of dark Flemish broadcloth, its hem lined with wet beaver fur that smelled of musk and street rain. A black velvet bonnet was pulled low over his temples, shading his brow, but the dim light of the street lantern at the alley head caught the heavy gold chain looped twice across his woolen doublet and the pale, fleshy curve of an alderman's jowls.

It was Lucas Van Hout.

He did not wait for an invitation. He squeezed his bulk through the narrow opening, his leather boots scraping down the ladder rungs until he stood on the cellar floor. He reached up, pulled the trapdoor shut, and let the oak bar drop firmly into its iron brackets.

He stood breathing hard in the darkness, brushing rain droplets from his fur collar.

"Light your tallow, woman," he whispered, his voice shaking with cold or terror. "I will not speak to a shadow in a coal hole."

Katrijn struck a spark from the flint into a dry wad of rag flax, blew the red ember into flame, and touched it to the candle wick. The yellow light bloomed across the cellar vault.

It illuminated the common press standing in the center: the massive upright oak cheeks, the iron screw, the sheepskin ink-balls hanging from their hooks, and the wet sheets of municipal receipts draped over the drying cords like ghost shrouds. Behind the press, half-hidden in the shadow of the coal bin, Giles Claes stood with his back pressed against the brickwork, his right hand gripping an iron crowbar three feet long.

Van Hout's gaze flicked to Giles, then to the crowbar, then to the press. A faint, bitter twitch touched the corner of his thin mouth.

"Hendrik's press," the alderman said, his eyes scanning the beveled joints and the iron-shod carriage. "Moretus swore to the council that his men had split the cheeks with axes and thrown the lead sorts into the Scheldt."

"They broke the table and smashed two frames," Katrijn said. She did not lower the knife. "Giles dragged the screw and the brass nut through the sewer ditch while the bailiffs were drinking Hendrik's cellar beer. What do you want here, Alderman? The Spanish provost pays fifty guilders for the head of an unlicensed printer. You can summon your guard from the quayside with a blast of your whistle."

Van Hout pulled off his wet leather gloves, revealing pale fingers adorned with a single heavy gold signet ring engraved with the three chevrons of his family crest. He reached into the inner fold of his cloak, drew out a heavy leather purse tied with waxed hemp twine, and tossed it onto the smooth marble composing stone.

The bag landed with a sharp, heavy chink. Several silver coins spilled from the mouth, rolling across the black ink-scratched stone before coming to rest against an iron composing stick.

They were carolus guilders. Thick, heavy silver coins bearing the profile of the old Emperor.

Katrijn stared at the silver. In eight months she had not seen five guilders together in one hand. Three of those coins would buy two sacks of rye flour from the Baltic merchants at the water gate; ten would buy four cartloads of Kempen peat, enough to keep Clara and Pieter warm through the deepest freeze of January.

"Thirty pieces," Van Hout said quietly. He pulled his velvet bonnet from his head, revealing thinning gray hair plastered to his forehead with sweat. "Twenty now. Ten more when the work is delivered across the river to the water gate at Saint Michael's."

"Delivered to whom?"

"To the men who can still save this miserable city," Van Hout said. He pulled a rolled packet from beneath his arm. It was wrapped in oiled silk, tied with black ribbon, and sealed with plain red wax that bore no armorial impression. He laid it beside the silver. "Four hundred copies. Octavo format, sixteen pages. It must be set, printed, folded, and stitched before Monday dawn."

Giles took a step forward from the shadows, the iron bar held low at his hip. His damaged ear was purple in the candlelight. "We print receipts. We print bills of lading for the salt merchants. We print nothing that brings the provost's cord to our necks."

"Be silent, boy," Van Hout snapped, though his voice lacked the haughty authority of the council chamber. He looked back at Katrijn, his eyes hollowed with dark circles. "You know what is happening on the river. Alexander Farnese has brought his siege lines to the polders. His Italian engineers are driving oak piles into the Scheldt mud at Calloo. In four weeks, his bridge will close the channel from bank to bank. If that bridge stands, no grain fleet from England or Holland will enter Antwerp. We will be eating rats and boiled saddle leather before the ice thaws."

"That is the council's affair," Katrijn said, her heart hammering against her ribs. "The council took thirty thousand guilders from the burgers to build the river forts. What has that to do with my cellar?"

"The council is rotten," Van Hout whispered, stepping closer to the composing stone. His breath smelled of stale wine and clove lozenges. "The guild deacons have taken Spanish silver. Three of the magistrates have signed secret letters to Parma, promising to surrender the city gates the moment the river bridge is finished. They have starved the garrisons on the polders, withheld powder from the river redoubts, and allowed the grain hoys to be seized so the mob will riot for bread and demand surrender."

He tapped the oiled silk packet with a trembling finger.

"This is the record," the alderman said. "The dates of their meetings at the tavern on the Cow Gate. The names of the nine Stationers' Guild proctors who drew Spanish pensions. The account of the bribes paid to smash every printing press that dared publish the truth of what was negotiated at Brussels. The people must see the signatures. If the citizens do not know who has sold them, they will open the gates to the pikemen and Antwerp will burn as it burned in seventy-six."

Katrijn looked from the packet to the silver coins gleaming in the candlelight.

"Who wrote this?" she asked.

Van Hout pulled his fur collar tight against his neck. "A patriot."

"There are no patriots in Antwerp," Katrijn said flatly. "Only men who want to keep their warehouses and men who want to hang their neighbors. If Moretus finds this text on my bed, my children will starve in the street while the executioner burns my hands with hot pincers."

"If you do not print it, your children will starve anyway," Van Hout said, his voice dropping to a harsh, ragged rasp. "Look at the silver, Katrijn. Look at it. Thirty guilders buys life until the spring fleet arrives. Refuse me, and what will you do? Print tallow tallies for two stuivers a week while Parma's cannons batter the walls? You have until Monday."

He turned toward the ladder, planted his boot on the first rung, and looked down over his shoulder.

"Lock the hatch," he said. "The watch passes every half-hour."

Van Hout rubbed his pale hands together over the glowing charcoal. His heavy gold signet ring caught the red embers, casting tiny sparks of yellow light across the damp flagstones. He looked around the vault with the nervous, darting gaze of a patrician who had spent his life in tapestried council chambers and now found himself five feet beneath the city gutter.

"Parma has thirty Italian engineers working day and night on the dikes at Calloo," the alderman whispered, his voice trembling. "They have built thirty-two flat-bottomed barges, mounted with twenty-four-pound brass cannons cast at Mechelen. When the floating stockade is joined from bank to bank, they will drive iron chains across the river boom. The Sea Beggars in Flushing have forty hoys loaded with wheat, but Admiral Treslong will not risk his ships against ninety Spanish guns in a narrow channel."

Katrijn watched the alderman's face. She saw the sweat standing on his forehead beneath the gray hairs. "You knew this in August, Alderman. When Hendrik stood before your bench, you knew the Spanish bridge was coming."

"We thought there would be an accommodation," Van Hout said, his voice cracking into a ragged plea. "The Prince of Orange was dead, shot on the stairs at Delft. The States-General had no money to pay the German mercenaries. Every magistrate in Antwerp was trying to save his own skin, Katrijn. If we had not surrendered the vernacular presses, Moretus would have opened the gates to the Walloon regiment that night."

Van Hout took a deep breath, his chest rattling with a dry, hollow cough. He pulled a small silver snuff-box from his velvet pocket, unlatched the lid with his thumbnail, and took a pinch of dried marjoram powder between his fingers. The aromatic herb filled the cold vault with a faint scent of summer gardens, cutting through the bitter smell of coal smoke and wet wool.

"I sat with Alexander Farnese in his tent at Beveren three weeks ago," the alderman whispered, his eyes wide in the candlelight. "The Prince of Parma does not scream or rant like the Duke of Alba. He speaks with the smooth, terrifying politeness of an Italian cardinal. He poured Spanish wine into a silver cup, set it before me, and smiled. He told me that Antwerp was a beautiful garden that had grown wild with heretical thorns, and that his master the King had sent him with the pruning shears. He said that if we did not surrender the river dikes by Christmas, he would leave not one stone standing upon another from the Kipdorp to the water gate."

Van Hout looked down at his own manicured nails, stained with purple ink at the cuticle. For thirty years he had written records for the Great Council, logging the shipments of English wool, the tolls on Baltic grain, and the excise duties on French wine. He had believed that the law was an iron cage that kept men safe from their own wild appetites. Now the iron cage had turned into a slaughterhouse pen, and the butchers wore Spanish lace at their wrists.

"I watched them string up the baker from the Kammenstraat last Tuesday," the alderman whispered, his throat working convulsively. "They took him because he had given three crusts of rye bread to a preacher's orphan. The provost-marshal did not even bother to read an indictment. They looped the greasy cord over the meat hook on the wall of the exchange, hauled him up until his toes danced eighteen inches above the mud, and left him there until dusk. The children threw turnip peelings at his breeches. That was when I knew that the law in Antwerp was dead, Katrijn. The ink on the parchment meant nothing. Only the hemp cord remained."

Van Hout rubbed his rheumy eyes with the knuckle of his thumb. His velvet cloak was stained at the hem with yellow clay from the trenches outside the Kipdorp bastion. Two days ago he had accompanied the city engineers to inspect the breaches made by Parma's battery. The Spanish cannonballs had pulverized thirty yards of brick rampart, leaving only a mound of shattered masonry and broken timber palisades.

Van Hout unclasped his fur-trimmed cloak and laid it across the back of the pine bench. His doublet was worn thin at the elbows, the blue velvet rubbed bare where his forearms had rested against the council table for thirty years. "We had sixty merchant hulks at the quays when the spring fleet arrived from Spain," he said in a dull, flat monotone. "Now there are only three barges of sour beer and two ketches loaded with firewood. Parma's redoubts command both banks of the Scheldt. A gull cannot fly from Beveren to the sea without Spanish musketeers shooting at its wings."

Van Hout looked down at the parchment list of Antwerp grain merchants, his pen trembling over the signatures. "If I turn these names over to the Spanish provost," he whispered, his voice dry as summer grass, "every granary on the Sint-Michielskaai will be seized by sunset. The weavers will have nothing left to eat but the grass in the churchyard. But if I refuse, Moretus will deliver my brother to the inquisitors in Mechelen before morning."

He pushed the trapdoor open, slipped into the rainy darkness of the alley, and let the oak wood fall shut behind him.

# Chapter 4: The Manuscript Copy

The oilskin packet smelled of stale tobacco and horse leather.

Katrijn carried it to the composing stone, where the yellow light of the tallow candle flickered in the draft from the coal chute. Giles stood beside her, his breath coming in short, uneven rasps through his teeth. He laid the iron crowbar across the timber frame of the press, reached out with his soot-stained thumb, and slit the red wax seal with his thumbnail.

The packet opened like a split pod. Inside lay twelve leaves of fine Dutch rag paper, quarto size, folded lengthwise and covered in tight, flourishing cursive script.

Katrijn unfolded the first sheet. Her eyes, trained by ten years of deciphering scribal crabbed hands and printer's copy, moved across the opening lines:

*A True and Faithful Record of the Treasons Practiced within the City of Antwerp, Wherein Are Set Down the Names of Those Magistrates, Guild Deacons, and Proctors Who Have Received the Gold of Spain to Destroy the Free Presses and Deliver the Gates to the Duke of Parma.*

"Christ," Giles muttered under his breath, crossing himself with his left hand. "He wants us to hang."

"Hush," Katrijn commanded.

She leaned closer to the candle. The ink was iron gall, brownish-black where the pen had bitten deep into the linen fiber, with faint rust-colored halos along the capital letters. The hand was not the hurried scrawl of an apprentice or the coarse script of a shopkeeper; it was an educated, chancery hand, full of Latinate abbreviations, elegant flourishes on the ascenders, and the long, sweeping tails characteristic of municipal secretaries.

On the second leaf, the accusations began in numbered articles:

*Article III. That upon the fourteenth day of May last past, Master Balthasar Moretus, Proctor of the Stationers' Guild, did receive at the house of the Spanish factor near the Italian Bourse the sum of four hundred gold ducats, under covenant that no tract, song, or relation touching upon the closure of the Scheldt should be permitted impression within this jurisdiction.*

*Article IV. That pursuant to said covenant, the said Moretus, accompanied by twelve armed bailiffs and six German mercenaries, did violently enter the dwelling of Master Hendrik Vane upon the Vrijdagmarkt, did smash his common press with iron sledges, scatter his lead types into the canal muck, and deliver the said Vane to the provost marshal under false accusation of publishing vernacular psalters contrary to the King's peace.*

Katrijn's hand began to tremble. The paper rustled between her cold fingers.

Hendrik's name. Written in black ink, set down in the middle of a municipal indictment like an itemized barrel of herring or a sack of malt.

"He knew," she whispered. Her throat felt tight, dry as wood ash. "The man who wrote this sat in the council chamber when Hendrik was dragged before the magistrates. He knew the charge was a lie."

Giles leaned over her shoulder, his squinting eyes following her index finger as it traced the text. "Can you read the ninth article, mistress? That name at the bottom of the leaf?"

Katrijn turned the leaf. Her finger stopped at the margin:

*Article IX. That upon the second day of August, the municipal warrant for the execution of Hendrik Vane, printer, was drawn and signed in the secret committee of the town hall, not by decree of the full bench of aldermen, but by three men acting under direct terror of the Spanish garrison: namely, Jan Van Rooden, Burgomaster; Pieter De Vos, Receiver of Customs; and—*

The third name was written with an elaborate, looping capital *L*, followed by a high, decorative cross-stroke on the *V*:

*—Lucas Van Hout, Alderman and Warden of the Scheldt Dikes.*

The cellar went completely silent.

Outside, the rain beat against the alley stones with a soft, drumming hiss. The coal dust smelled damp, bitter, clinging to the back of Katrijn's palate. On the composing stone, the thirty silver carolus guilders lay scattered where the alderman had thrown them, their stamped imperial faces gleaming dull and cold in the tallow flame.

"Van Hout," Giles said, his voice dropping into a harsh, cracked whisper. "The customer. The man who sat on that stool ten minutes ago."

Katrijn did not answer. She turned the leaf over, staring at the handwriting. The capital *L* on the ninth article had the same distinctive double loop, the same sharp hook at the baseline, as the flourish on the title page. The same hand had drafted the tract. The same hand had written the accusations against Moretus.

And the same hand had signed the parchment strip that sent Hendrik to the block on the Grote Markt.

"He signed it," Giles said. He picked up the iron crowbar, his knuckles turning chalk-white under the black grease. "He signed Hendrik's death, and now he brings his silver down our stairs to buy his own skin. Let me go up the alley, mistress. He cannot have reached the Meir bridge yet. I can take him in the dark behind the butcher's stalls."

"Put the bar down," Katrijn said.

"He killed him!" Giles spat, his chest heaving. "He killed your husband! He watched them take the axe to him in the rain, and now he walks into our cellar like an honest merchant!"

"Put the bar down, Giles!" Katrijn's voice cracked like a whip through the vaulted room.

The boy stopped. His chest rose and fell, his breath ragged, his good ear flushed bright crimson. Slowly, with trembling shoulders, he lowered the iron bar until its tip touched the floor.

Katrijn looked down at the manuscript leaf. Her eyes were dry, burning with a cold, steady fury that ran deeper than tears.

"He signed the warrant," she said, her voice steady as a plumb line. "And now he is terrified that when Parma takes the city, Moretus and the Spanish will hang him anyway to cover their own theft. He wants Antwerp to tear Moretus to pieces before the gates fall. He wants us to do his killing with lead and ink."

"Then we burn it," Giles said, gesturing toward the iron brazier. "We throw his silver into the canal and we burn the paper."

Katrijn reached out and picked up one of the silver coins. She weighed it in her palm. It was cold, heavy, bearing the full weight of a child's winter.

"If we burn it," she said, "Moretus rules the guild. If we burn it, Hendrik remains a heretic and a criminal in the town register forever, and my children starve on parsnip peelings while Van Hout buys his way to Holland on an English hoy."

She laid the coin back on the stone.

Katrijn lifted the third leaf of the manuscript close to the candle flame. The paper was fine, heavy Dutch linen rag, bearing the watermarked crest of the city of Amsterdam: a shield flanked by two lions, with three Saint Andrew's crosses in the center. It was paper that cost three guilders a ream, smuggled across the Spanish siege lines in the double bottom of a butter barrel.

The eighth article named Jan Van Rooden, the Burgomaster. It set down the date, the place, and the exact weight of Spanish gold delivered to his town house on the Meir: six hundred florins, carried in three leather bags by the factor of the Fugger banking house. In return, Van Rooden had agreed to delay the dispatch of the civic trainbands to the river forts, allowing the Spanish engineers forty-eight hours of unmolested work to drive their oak piles into the Scheldt mud.

Giles leaned his chin on his soot-stained hand, staring at the cursive lines of the manuscript with a mixture of reverence and dread. He had never learned to read more than the capital letters of the title fonts and the signature marks at the bottom of the gatherings, but he understood the lethal weight of the paper.

"If we print this, mistress," Giles whispered, his rough voice cracking in his throat, "the dockers will read it. When the boatmen see how Moretus took the Spanish gold, they will take their boat-hooks to the guildhall. There will be blood on the Handschoenmarkt before the ink has dried on the second signature."

Giles picked up an iron oil-can and squirted three drops of lard oil onto the spindle of the oak screw. The drop ran down the threaded groove in a thick brown tear, collecting the black grime that had gathered since Sunday. He wiped his palms on his leather apron, his breath white in the draft that came up through the floorboards from the canal below. The timber joints of the press creaked softly under the frost, the seasoned oak contracting in the winter cold.

Giles tapped the side of the wooden galley with his thumbnail. The sound was solid and dull. The lead types sat firmly against the brass rule, locked in place by two small pine quoins that had been wedged tight with a wooden mallet.

Katrijn examined the tympan hinges. The brass pins had worn oval after ten years of steady labor, but Giles had shimmed the gap with two slips of beaten sheet-copper. The frame closed without play, falling square against the bed with a muffled clatter.

"Take the lower cases from the wall," she told Giles. "We have sixteen pages of Brevier to set before Monday, and every letter of his name must be straight."

# Chapter 5: The Signature on the Warrant

Katrijn waited until Giles had climbed the ladder to his straw pallet in the coal bin.

When his heavy, uneven breathing settled into the rhythm of exhausted sleep, she took the tallow stub and walked to the far corner of the vault, where the brick floor met the damp foundation of the dyer's house. Behind three loose peat briquettes, deep in a cavity carved into the lime mortar, sat an iron-bound oak tin box no larger than a church missal.

She pulled the box into her lap. Her hands were cold, the skin stiff around the nails.

Inside lay Hendrik's silver watch, its balance spring broken during his arrest; his copper guild seal, filed flat by the provost's smith; two bone buttons from his best woolen doublet; and a folded strip of sheepskin parchment, stained with grease and yellowed around the creases.

It was the extract of the execution warrant, delivered to her by a merciful clerk three days after the head fell into the sawdust on the Grote Markt.

She unfolded the sheepskin on the composing stone.

The script on the warrant was formal, cramped, written in Latin by the town clerk: *In nomine Regis... Hendrik Vane, typographus... propter libros seditiosos et haereticos... capite plectatur.* At the foot of the document, beneath the seal of the city of Antwerp, were three signatures.

The first was Jan Van Rooden, Burgomaster, his hand shaky and thin.

The second was Pieter De Vos, a heavy, blunt slash of ink.

The third was Lucas Van Hout.

Katrijn laid the handwritten manuscript of *A True Record* directly beside the sheepskin warrant. She brought the tallow flame within an inch of the two texts.

There was no difference. None.

The capital *L* on the death warrant had the same haughty, sweeping loop at the summit, curving backward like the prow of a Spanish galley before plunging down into the heavy baseline stroke. The *V* in *Van Hout* was formed with a tiny, nervous hesitation—a stutter of the quill where the alderman's hand had paused before committing his family name to blood.

She touched the sheepskin with her forefinger.

She remembered the morning of the execution. It had been raining, that same cold, greasy November drizzle that was falling on Antwerp tonight. They had brought Hendrik out from the Steen prison in his linen shirt, his arms bound behind his back with hemp cord. He had looked across the crowd toward the colonnade of the town hall, searching for her face among the pikemen. When he saw her holding five-year-old Pieter by the hand, he had not spoken; he had simply nodded once, his lips moving silently in the words of the twenty-third psalm.

On the balcony above the scaffold, three men had stood beneath the velvet canopy to witness the execution. One of them had worn a dark cloak with a wet beaver fur collar. One of them had turned his head away when the executioner lifted the broadsword from the black baize cloth.

Lucas Van Hout.

Katrijn sat in the silence of the cellar, her palms pressed flat against the cold marble stone.

Her husband was buried in unconsecrated silt outside the Red Gate, his body tossed into a ditch with two horse thieves and an English smuggler. His books were ashes beneath the town hall gallows. And the man who had bought his death with a quill stroke had stood in her kitchen tonight, shivering with fear, asking her to risk her own life and her children's bread to save his municipal reputation.

She picked up the manuscript leaf.

"You coward," she whispered to the empty stone.

She did not weep. Tears were a luxury for women who had flour in their bins and husbands by the fire. She folded the death warrant, placed it back into the tin box with Hendrik's broken watch, and slid the box into her apron pocket.

Then she took her composing stick from the rack, pulled the Brevier case toward her chest, and reached for the lead sorts.

She adjusted the brass slide to twenty-four picas. Her left hand held the wood firmly, her thumb ready to support the growing line of type. With her right hand, she drew an uppercase *T* from the top partition. The metal clicked against the rule.

It was the first letter of the indictment.

She remembered the feast of Saint Luke ten years before, when Hendrik had received his master's charter from the guild. He had brought home a gallon of Rhine wine and a basket of sweet almond bread from the French baker on the Kipdorp. They had sat in the upper parlor with the windows open to the summer breeze, listening to the chime of the Cathedral bells, while Hendrik planed the beveled edges of his first wooden composing stick.

"A printer's ink never dies, Katrijn," he had told her that night, his arm around her waist as they looked out over the gables of the Vrijdagmarkt. "A stone wall can be knocked flat by a culverin. A king's decree can be revoked by his heir. But once a sentence is pressed into paper with good oil, it lives in a hundred chests, in a hundred towns, and no tyrant has an arm long enough to gather all the pages back."

Katrijn took Hendrik's copper composing rule from the tin box. It was eight inches long, notched with small engraved marks at every pica em, worn smooth at the corners where his thumb had rested for twenty years. She laid the copper rule against the left-hand margin of the execution warrant, measuring the distance between the clerk's rubric and Van Hout's signature.

Exactly two inches. The same margin that Van Hout had left on the opening page of his seditious tract. A man could disguise his cursive letters with flourishes and extra loops, but he could not disguise the subconscious geometry of his hand: the habit of spacing, the angle of the pen-knife trim, the hesitation before the capital letter that revealed his inner fear.

Katrijn rubbed her knuckles where the lye had cracked the dry skin into red fissures. She blew out the smoking tallow wick and slipped the iron key into the lining of her wool bodice, pressing the cold metal against her ribs.

She did not look at the candle; she looked at the lead. In the quiet of the cellar, letter followed letter with the cold, measured precision of an artisan building a scaffold.

# Chapter 6: The Chemistry of Lampblack

The iron kettle had to be scoured to bare metal before the oil touched the heat.

If a crust of old linseed varnish remained on the interior curve, the boiled oil would scorch, turning the ink into a stringy, foul-smelling rubber that dragged across the lead type and tore the surface fibers from damp paper.

Katrijn knelt beside the charcoal brazier, scraping the iron kettle with a handful of river sand and a piece of coarse sackcloth. Her thumbs were raw, gray with coal soot and the biting grease of old pitch. Beside her, Giles fed three dry peat blocks into the coals, using a small brass blowpipe to coax the red embers into a steady, smokeless flame.

"Two pints of nut oil," Katrijn said, pointing to the stoneware jug near the coal bin. "Walnut, not linseed. Linseed yellows within forty days on rag paper. If this tract is to sit in the guildhall through the winter, the black must hold its bite without greasing the margins."

Giles lifted the jug, pulled the wooden stopper with his teeth, and poured the pale, viscous oil into the kettle. It fell with a soft, heavy slurp, coating the iron bottom in a golden film.

Making printer's ink was a filthy, hazardous trade. In the licensed shops on the Vrijdagmarkt, master printers kept their boiling sheds outside the town walls, out on the dikes near the roperies, because the fumes of boiling oil and turpentine were so inflammable that a single stray spark could send an entire timber workshop into the sky in three minutes. Here, in a closed cellar with a brick ceiling and no chimney other than the narrow coal chute leading to the alley, every pint of oil boiled was a wager against suffocation and fire.

"Keep the wet linen blanket ready," Katrijn warned, her voice tight. "If the oil flashes, throw the blanket over the rim. Do not use water. Water will throw the burning pitch into our faces."

Giles nodded. He held a square of heavy sailcloth, soaked in canal water, folded across his knee.

The kettle began to warm. A faint, sweet scent of crushed walnuts rose into the cold air, followed quickly by the bitter, pungent tang of scorched vegetable fat. Katrijn stirred the oil with an iron rod, watching the surface change. First came tiny, glassy bubbles that rose from the bottom and popped silently; then a thin white vapor that curled upward, stinging her nostrils and making her eyes water.

"Bring the pitch," she commanded.

From a wooden tub, Giles measured four ounces of black pine resin and two lumps of Venetian turpentine, each the size of a walnut. He dropped them into the boiling oil.

The mixture hissed violently. A cloud of thick, yellow-gray smoke billowed from the kettle, tumbling across the cellar vault like sea fog. Katrijn coughed into the sleeve of her gown, her lungs burning, but she did not drop the iron rod. She stirred continuously in wide, steady circles, feeling the resistance of the liquid increase as the resin dissolved into the heated oil.

The oil was thickening. When she lifted the rod, the dark syrup ran down the iron shaft in a thin, gleaming thread that held together for four inches before breaking into heavy droplets.

"The bread crust," she said.

Giles handed her a stale crust of dry rye bread, skewered on an iron skewer. Katrijn plunged the bread into the center of the boiling kettle. The crust crackled and turned instantly black, smoking fiercely in the fat. This was the ancient printer's test: if the bread browned without burning to ash, the oil was hot enough to temper; if it burst into flame, the batch was ruined and ready to flash.

The crust held. Katrijn withdrew the skewer and tossed the smoking bread onto the flagstones.

"Draw the fire," she whispered, her voice hoarse from the acrid fumes.

Giles used an iron shovel to drag the glowing peat coals away from beneath the kettle, scattering them onto the stone floor where they died in the damp silt. Katrijn lifted the heavy iron pot with two rag-wrapped handles, setting it carefully onto a flat stone slab to cool.

Now came the soot.

In the licensed shops of the Plantin press, ink-makers used fine German lampblack, collected in conical linen tents from the smoke of burning resin torches: pure, velvety carbon that felt like silk between the fingers and carried no grit. Katrijn had no German lampblack. She had two canvas sacks of chimney soot, bought from the sweep who cleaned the flues of the Saint Elizabeth hospital brewery. It was coarse, smelling strongly of sulfur and burned malt, mixed with tiny flakes of brick mortar that had to be ground into submission.

She brought out the muller: a heavy, bell-shaped stone pestle made of polished Belgian granite, its flat base worn glass-smooth by thirty years of ink-grinding.

She poured three ladles of the warm, syrupy varnish onto the center of the marble grinding slab. Over the oil, she sifted four handfuls of the black soot through a fine wire sieve, watching the dark powder settle onto the fat like a velvet blanket.

"Work the stone," she told Giles.

Giles gripped the granite muller with both hands, pressing his entire weight down upon the slab. He moved the heavy stone in tight, figure-eight loops, grinding the soot particles into the tempered oil.

The sound was a harsh, rhythmic rasp: *scritch-scritch-scritch* against the marble. At first, the mixture was stiff, crumbly, resisting the stone like wet loam; but as Giles circled and pressed, his broken knuckles white under the strain, the heat of the friction broke down the carbon grains. The paste began to sheen. It turned from a dull, chalky gray into a deep, glossy, impenetrable black that caught the candlelight like polished obsidian.

Katrijn took a wooden palette knife and scraped the ink from the edges of the slab back into the center, beneath the muller.

"Finer," she said. "If there is grit in the paste, it fills the counters of the lowercase *e* and *a* within twenty impressions. A reader will not trust a tract if the letters look like squashed flies."

Giles did not pause. Sweat poured down his face, dripping from his chin onto his linen smock, cutting pale channels through the black soot on his cheeks. He ground the stone for forty minutes without stopping, his breath coming in rhythmic grunts, until the paste was so smooth that when Katrijn drew the palette knife across the surface, it left a mirror-bright furrow that did not break or separate.

She dipped her little finger into the ink.

She rubbed it against the back of her left wrist, spreading the black film thin against her pale skin. The ink was dense, opaque, intensely black, with a greasy, elastic tack that clung to the skin without running into the pores. It smelled of pine pitch, walnut meat, and deep winter smoke.

"It will hold," she said.

She used the wooden knife to scrape the black paste into two earthenware jars, packing it down tightly to keep the air from forming a dry skin over the top. She covered each jar with a circle of damp sheepskin parchment, tying it off with waxed hemp twine.

Outside, far across the city, the great bell of Saint Michael's began to toll the six o'clock morning bell.

The street carts were beginning to move along the Sint-Jansvliet quayside. The curfew was lifted for the day. Above their heads, the children would be waking in the garret, their bare feet pattering across the drafty floorboards as they searched for peat to light the hearth.

Katrijn wiped her hands on a scrap of waste paper. The black stain remained deep in the grain of her palms, around the cuticles and beneath the nails: the permanent brand of the trade, which no lye or pumice stone could ever fully wash away.

Giles leaned over the grinding slab, his breath coming in rhythmic, whistling rasps. The heat of the charcoal brazier and the friction of the granite muller had softened the black paste into a rich, velvety butter that clung to the stone in thick, glossy ribbons. Katrijn watched the surface of the ink, checking for the tiny pinhead bubbles that showed the presence of water or unmixed fat.

To test the tack, she took an ivory bodkin, dipped its point into the center of the paste, and lifted it four inches into the air. The ink drew out into a fine, elastic thread that held together without breaking, swaying gently in the draft from the coal chute. This was the true printer's string: resilient, dense, capable of coating the lead face with a microscopic film that would not pool in the counters of the letters.

Making ink was an inheritance that had passed from master to journeyman since the days of Gutenberg. In the great shops of Paris and Basel, the ink-makers were sworn to secrecy by guild oaths, forbidden to write down the exact proportions of nut oil to pine resin under penalty of a heavy fine. If the varnish was too thin, it spread across the rag paper in an oily halo, turning the white margins into dirty yellow parchment within six months. If it was too thick, it pulled the face from the damp linen, filling the counters of the letters with sticky, gummy clots that ruined the fount.

Katrijn watched the muller move in Giles's hands. The granite stone was grinding the last stubborn particles of soot into the tempered oil with a steady, hypnotic rhythm. Under the friction of the stone, the mixture was warming, releasing a rich, deep, resinous scent that smelled of ancient forests and hot pitch.

Boiling the nut oil was the most hazardous hour in any printing house. If a single drop of water fell into the iron pot while the oil was foaming, the cauldron would erupt in an explosion of liquid fire that could gut a timber shop in ten minutes. Hendrik had kept a heavy square of wet sailcloth folded beneath the hearth bench for that single terror. He had taught Katrijn how to smother the mouth of the vessel with the wet canvas without breathing the choking black smoke that billowed from the scorching linseed.

She watched Giles stir the varnish with an iron rod. The oil had reached the consistency of warm honey, stringing from the tip of the rod in long, elastic amber threads that cooled into transparent beads upon the hearth stone. Giles lifted a small handful of ground pine resin from a wooden bowl and tossed it into the boiling pool. The resin crackled and hissed, releasing a pungent cloud of resinous steam that made Katrijn cough into her sleeve. When the pine resin had melted into the oil, Giles swung the pot off the coals and set it upon a bed of cold ashes to cool before adding the Frankfurt soot.

The smell of the melted rosin clung to the cellar bricks like pine smoke. Katrijn watched Giles work the granite muller across the marble slab. The stone moved with an unhurried, circular grind, reducing the coarse soot into an impalpable black film. With each rotation, the black varnish grew richer and darker, catching the yellow candlelight on its glassy surface like polished ebony.

Hendrik had always said that ink was the life-blood of a printing house. Without good varnish, even the finest matrices cut by Claude Garamont were merely useless blocks of lead. The oil had to bind the soot to the rag paper without bleeding into the margins or drying upon the ink-balls before the pressman could strike the forme.

Katrijn stirred the cooling varnish with a wooden spatula carved from apple wood. The oil was thick as honey now, clinging to the grain of the wood in smooth, dark ribbons. In the great printshops of Lyon and Venice, ink-makers spent four years learning how to judge the temper of the oil by the sound of the bubbles as they broke upon the surface. Hendrik had known the craft by smell alone. He could walk into the cellar in total darkness and tell whether the resin had dissolved completely by the sharpness of the pine scent in the air.

Katrijn watched the dark pool of varnish cool in the iron kettle. When Giles lifted the wooden paddle, the oil hung in a thick, shimmering curtain that broke into slow amber tears. The resin had bound with the boiled linseed into a dense, viscous medium that would hold the soot particles in permanent suspension, defying the damp cellar air and the rot of the centuries.

"Eat some rye crust," she told Giles, nodding toward the loaf on the shelf. "Then wash the Brevier cases with lye. We begin setting the title page at dusk."

# Chapter 7: The Cloaked Meeting

The churchyard of Saint Paul lay locked in shadow behind the Dominican convent.

The wind had shifted from the north, carrying the cold, stagnant stench of the city moat across the mud graves. Between the buttresses of the brick choir, the gravestones stood tilted in the wet grass, their carved cherubs and Dutch inscriptions coated in green slime.

Katrijn pulled her hood tight around her ears. She stood with her back against the granite plinth of a merchant's tomb, her hands tucked deep inside her woolen cloak, her right hand resting on the hard wooden handle of her kitchen knife.

A figure emerged from the cloister passage.

Lucas Van Hout walked with a slow, heavy tread, his feet sinking into the soggy turf. He wore the same dark broadcloth cloak, but tonight he carried no ivory cane; his right hand was thrust into his fur-lined pocket, and he glanced repeatedly toward the narrow lane that led to the Cattle Market.

He stopped three paces from her tomb slab.

"You are late, Mistress Vane," he murmured, his breath forming a pale cloud in the twilight.

"I have two children to lock into their beds," Katrijn said. "And the provost has posted four Walloon pikemen at the corner of the Dominican gate. Why did you summon me here instead of coming to the cellar?"

Van Hout looked around at the dripping yew trees. His face was pale, his eyes sunken, his jowls gray in the dusk.

"Moretus has doubled the watchers along the canal," he whispered. "A dyer on Sint-Jansvliet reported the smell of boiling pitch and walnut varnish from the lower vaults this morning. The Stationers' Guild proctors have searched three cellars near the brewery wharf already. If I am seen entering your alley, we will both be hanging from the Steen gibbet before noon."

Katrijn took a step forward from the shadow of the tomb.

"You signed his warrant, Alderman."

The words fell between them like iron weights onto stone.

Van Hout went entirely still. The wind caught the hem of his cloak, rustling the wet broadcloth against the granite slab. He did not ask whom she meant. He looked down at his leather boots, where the yellow grass clung to the buckles.

"I signed forty warrants that week," he said, his voice flat, exhausted. "The Duke of Parma had thirty hostages from the merchant families in the citadel at Calloo. My own sister's sons were in the irons, Katrijn. Parma sent their signet rings to the council chamber in a leather bag, with a letter promising that if the city did not suppress the seditious pamphlets and vernacular tracts within seven days, the boys would be hanged from the yardarms of his galleys."

"Hendrik was no seditionist," Katrijn said, her teeth clenched. "He was a master printer. He printed the psalms in Dutch so the common people could sing in their kitchens without asking a Spanish priest for leave."

"In wartime, there is no difference between a psalm and a cannon shot," Van Hout said, lifting his head. His eyes were bright with a desperate, defensive anger. "Do you think I wanted your husband's blood on my ledger? I knew Hendrik. He bought his paper from my brother's mill in Brabant. But the council was divided. Moretus brought the proof—the printed sheets, the witnesses from the guild—and laid them on the stadhuis table before the Spanish commissioner. If I had refused my signature, I would have joined Hendrik on the scaffold, and my sister's sons would be rotting in the Scheldt mud."

"So you gave them my husband to save your nephews."

"I gave them a printer to save thirty lives!" Van Hout hissed, leaning forward until Katrijn could smell the sour wine on his lips. "And what did it buy? Eight months of delay! And now the Spanish bridge is nearly closed, Moretus is drawing his pension from Brussels, and the city is sold from the inside! If you want vengeance for Hendrik, print the tract! Print it, scatter it through the guildhalls, let the dockers and weavers see who has sold their bread, and Moretus will not live to see the Spanish troops enter the gates!"

Katrijn stared at the alderman.

She saw the terror in him: not the grand, tragic agony of a martyr, but the miserable, squalid panic of a bureaucrat who had compromised until there was nothing left to trade, and now found the executioner's noose slipping over his own head.

"You want to use Hendrik's press to wash your name," she said.

"My name is already damned," Van Hout said bitterly. "God will judge what I signed in the council chamber. But the people can still burn Moretus's warrants before the citadel gates open. Have you set the type?"

"The first eight pages are in the chase," Katrijn said. "We need more paper. Two reams of Troyes rag. The sentries are searching carts at the Meir bridge."

Van Hout reached into his pocket and drew out a folded paper stamped with the red wax seal of the municipal admiralty.

"A pass," he said, pressing it into her cold hand. "It declares the cart to be carrying hospital linen for the pest-house at Saint Elizabeth. The sergeant at the bridge will not open bundles of soiled plague sheets. Take the paper through the Saint Jans gate before tomorrow's curfew."

Katrijn looked at the seal. It was genuine: the two crossed anchors and the lion of Brabant.

"And the remaining ten guilders?" she asked.

"When the four hundred copies are delivered to the water gate," Van Hout said. "Not a stuiver before."

He turned without another word, pulled his hood low over his brow, and vanished into the dark cloister passage that led toward the town hall.

A cold gust of wind rustled the dead leaves in the churchyard ditch, scattering drops of freezing water across Katrijn's hood. From the distant quayside, the muffled sound of a sentry's challenge drifted through the mist, answered by the sharp clank of an iron halberd against stone.

"The Duke of Parma has thirty thousand veterans in the camps at Beveren," Van Hout whispered, his eyes darting toward the cloister gate. "Spanish pikemen, Walloon musketeers, Italian sappers who dug the trenches at Maastricht. They have had no pay for four months. If Antwerp falls by storm, there will be no quarter given. The soldiers will sack the merchant houses, burn the churches, and slaughter every Protestant artisan from the redoubt to the water gate."

Van Hout looked up into the dark branches of the yew trees, where the rain dripped from the needles with a soft, mournful rustle. His heavy jowls quivered in the cold air, his teeth chattering against each other with a dry click.

"Do you remember the Spanish Fury of seventy-six, Katrijn?" he whispered, his eyes haunted by the memory. "The mutinous tercios breaking through the Saint George gate with burning torches. Eight thousand burgers slaughtered in their beds in three days. The Great Market running knee-deep in blood while the Town Hall burned above our heads. I stood in my father's cellar on the Meir and listened to the women screaming in the street for forty-eight hours without stopping. When Parma marched his army to Calloo, I swore on the Holy Sacrament that I would sign whatever was demanded of me before I saw Antwerp put to the sword a second time."

A patrol of Walloon arquebusiers marched down the alley behind the cloister wall, their iron boots clattering on the wet flagstones. Their officer called out the watchword in rough French, his halberd scraping the brick archway as he passed. Katrijn waited beneath the dripping eaves of the linden tree until the iron footsteps died away toward the riverfront.

Katrijn pulled her woolen hood tight against the wind. The rain had turned into sleet, rattling against the leaded panes of the cloister windows like handfuls of river sand. Beside the wall, a starved mongrel dog chewed on an old leather boot-heel, its ribs showing sharp through its matted pelt.

The wind drove the rain through the bare branches of the churchyard elms. Katrijn waited by the dripping buttress, listening to the chime of the cathedral bells as they struck the fifth hour of the afternoon across the wet gables of the city.

Katrijn stood alone among the tombs. The wind howled through the buttresses of Saint Paul's, cold and merciless, carrying the distant rumble of the Spanish siege cannons from the polders across the river. She tucked the pass into her bodice, pulled her cloak around her shivering shoulders, and walked out into the lane.

# Chapter 8: The Spanish Garrison Decree

The drums began at the third hour of the afternoon on the Grote Markt.

It was not the cheerful, rattling march of the civic trainbands, but the slow, heavy, menacing thump of Spanish kettle-drums, muffled in wet calfskin. Three hundred burgers stood clustered beneath the stepped gables of the guildhalls, their breath rising in gray columns into the freezing November mist.

Katrijn stood on the lower steps of the Town Hall, her basket of dried herrings resting against her knee. Beside her, Giles leaned against a stone pillar, his head tilted so his good right ear faced the central platform.

On the wooden scaffold where Hendrik had died eight months before, six Spanish halberdiers in polished steel morions formed a semicircle. In their center stood the imperial herald, wearing a yellow tabard emblazoned with the black double-headed eagle of the Habsburgs, and beside him, wrapped in a fur-trimmed robe of crimson velvet, stood Inspector Balthasar Moretus.

The herald unrolled a broad parchment scroll weighted with lead seals.

His voice cut across the silence of the square, high and nasal, translated sentence by sentence into Dutch by a clerk who stood shivering at his elbow:

"By decree of His Excellency, Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma and Piacenza, Governor-General of the Low Countries for His Catholic Majesty Philip, King of Spain, Defender of the Faith."

A low, sullen murmur rippled through the crowd of weavers and dockers. Two Spanish pikemen stepped forward, leveling their iron points toward the cobblestones, and the crowd fell silent again.

"Whereas certain malignant persons, heretics, and rebels against the King's authority do continue within this city of Antwerp to print, scatter, and publish false, defamatory, and seditious libels, tending to the disturbance of the King's peace and the encouragement of rebellion:

"It is hereby commanded that from this day forward, all printing presses, wooden or iron, all types, matrices, punches, and composing sticks, together with all stores of printing paper and ink, shall be declared contraband of war, save only those operated under express license and seal of the Sworn Proctor of the Stationers' Guild, Master Balthasar Moretus.

"Whosoever shall harbor, conceal, operate, or assist in the operation of any unlicensed or secret press within the walls, liberties, or suburbs of Antwerp, shall be held guilty of high treason, and shall without appeal or benefit of clergy be delivered to the executioner to be hanged by the neck until dead, and his goods confiscated to the King's treasury.

"And furthermore: to any burger, apprentice, journeyman, or servant who shall discover and deliver into the hands of the guild proctor any such secret press, or the person of any unlicensed printer, there shall be paid from the royal exchequer the sum of two hundred gold florins, and a full pardon for all past offenses against the Crown."

Two hundred gold florins.

A sharp intake of breath hissed through the market square. In a city where weavers were boiling leather offcuts for broth and a loaf of rye cost half a week's wages, two hundred florins was a fortune large enough to buy a brick house on the Meir and three grain barges on the river.

Katrijn felt Giles's shoulder stiffen against the pillar.

She did not turn her head. She looked straight ahead, toward the platform, where Inspector Moretus stood with his hands folded inside his wide fur sleeves.

The proctor was watching the crowd. His dark, intelligent eyes moved slowly from face to face, measuring the reactions of the artisans and apprentices. He carried a small silver pomander ball in his right hand, lifting it occasionally to his nose to ward off the smell of the unwashed weavers. When his gaze swept past the steps where Katrijn stood, it lingered for half a heartbeat on her black widow's bonnet, then moved on toward the butcher stalls.

"The King's peace," the herald finished, rolling the parchment scroll with a dry rattle. "God save the King."

The drums rolled once more, three sharp, thunderous beats that shook the leaded glass windows of the guildhalls. The soldiers shouldered their halberds, turned in lockstep, and marched down the scaffold steps toward the town hall gates.

The crowd began to disperse, talking in hushed, frightened whispers.

"Two hundred florins," Giles muttered, his voice barely audible beneath the clatter of wooden clogs on the wet cobblestones. "Every beggar on the quayside will be sniffing cellar gratings tonight."

"Let them sniff," Katrijn said, picking up her herring basket. "They do not know the smell of walnut varnish from dyer's grease."

Around the platform, the crowd of artisans stood frozen in the cold. Katrijn saw Master Baetens, the deacon of the wool weavers, standing by the fountain with his arms folded across his chest, his jaw set in a hard, dangerous line. Beside him, two young apprentices from the cooperage had their hands inside their woolen jackets, gripping the handles of their draw-knives.

The two hundred gold florins offered by the Duke of Parma hung in the mist like an iron weight. It was enough money to feed ten families through a three-year siege. In every alley, in every taproom, neighbors would look at neighbors with suspicious, calculating eyes, wondering who kept a box of lead sorts behind his peat stack or who smelled of boiled oil in the morning queue.

On the scaffold steps, the Spanish herald rolled the parchment decree with a sharp snap of dry sheepskin, sliding the document into an embossed leather tube slung across his chest. The two Walloon halberdiers stepped back into formation, their iron breastplates clanking against their belt buckles as they turned toward the town hall porch.

Inspector Moretus remained on the platform for three heartbeats longer. He looked out over the crowded market square, his eyes moving slowly over the caps of the weavers and apprentices. He lifted his silver pomander ball to his nostrils, inhaled the scented clove water, and let his cold, calculating gaze rest once more upon the stone archway where Katrijn stood beside the pillar.

She walked down the steps into the crowd, her head held high, her pace steady and unhurried as she turned down the lane toward the canal.

# Chapter 9: The Composing Stick

The composing stick was carved from seasoned walnut, its brass setting rule polished until it reflected the candlelight like yellow glass.

Katrijn held the stick in her left hand, her thumb resting against the brass slide that fixed the line measure to twenty-four pica ems. With her right hand, her fingers moved across the compartments of the wooden type case with the swift, unthinking precision of an organist touching ivory keys.

*T-h-e.*

Upper case for the capital *T*. She plucked the lead sort from the upper left compartment of the case, turned it between her forefinger and thumb so the nick faced upward, and seated it firmly against the slide. Then the lowercase *h* from the center bin, then the *e*.

In printing, the world was built in reverse.

Every letter had to be set upside down, reading from left to right across the stick, so that when the ink met the damp paper under the platen, the impression would emerge clean and true for the human eye. To an apprentice, it was a torment of backwards reading that took two years to master; to Katrijn, who had grown up in her father's shop on the Kammenstraat and set her first Latin grammar at fourteen, it was second nature. She read the lead sorts faster than she read the scribal pages of Van Hout's manuscript.

*A-c-c-u-s-a-t-i-o-n.*

The lead alloy was cold against her fingertips. It was Hendrik's alloy: three parts soft English lead, one part antimony for hardness, and half a part tin to give the face a sharp, clean cast that did not crush under the press screw. The metal had a faint, greasy, metallic scent that lingered in the pores of the skin for days.

Beside her, on the low stool, the candle guttered in an earthen saucer.

Behind the press, Giles was sorting through the sorts from the old Dutch bible that Hendrik had dismantled before his arrest, separating the worn, battered characters from the clean, sharp letters cast from the French matrices. Every lowercase *e* was precious; in the Dutch tongue, the *e* appeared four times more often than any other letter, and their cases were already running low.

"How many lines on the third page?" Giles asked, holding a bent sort up to the light.

"Thirty-six lines to the page," Katrijn said, her fingers never stopping their rhythm. *Sort, click, sort, click.* "Two columns of Brevier. We have sixteen pages in the pamphlet. That is five hundred and seventy-six lines of lead to set before we lock the chase."

She reached the end of the line.

The letters did not quite fill the measure; there was a gap of three points between the final period and the brass slide. She reached into the space box, selected two thin copper spaces, and inserted them between the words *magistrate* and *betrayal*. She tested the tightness with her thumbnail. The line held firm: not so tight that the lead would bow upward under the quoins, not so loose that a letter would drop through the carriage when Giles rolled the rounce.

She lifted the completed line from the stick with both hands, using her index fingers and thumbs to clamp the lead rectangle into a rigid block, and transferred it to the wooden galley tray resting on the stone.

She looked down at the lines she had set.

Reading the lead upside down, the cold metal spelled out the names of the men who ruled Antwerp:

*Balthasar Moretus, Proctor.*
*Jan Van Rooden, Burgomaster.*
*Lucas Van Hout, Alderman.*

Each letter was an indictment that carried the penalty of the rope. If the watch broke down the hatch while these galleys were on the stone, there would be no trial, no mercy, no appeal to the Great Council of Mechelen. The provost marshal would drag them straight to the Grote Markt, and the two hundred gold florins of the Duke of Parma would be paid to whoever had pointed the finger.

Her gaze shifted to the fifth line from the bottom of the galley.

There, set in the same sharp Brevier lead, was Hendrik's name:

*Master Hendrik Vane, printer, wrongfully condemned and executed by terror of the Spanish garrison, August 2, 1584.*

She touched the face of the letters with the tip of her forefinger.

Hendrik had spent fifteen years building his shop. He had saved every copper coin from his apprentice days, bought his first small press from a printer in Ghent who had fled the Inquisition, and traded his mother's silver spoons for two sets of Robert Granjon's italic matrices. He had believed, with the stubborn, naive faith of a Dutch craftsman, that a book was an honest thing that could hurt no man who did not fear the truth.

"They will never forgive this," Giles said softly from the shadows.

Katrijn did not look up from the galley. "Who?"

"Moretus. The council. The Spanish." Giles laid a handful of clean sorts on the case rim. "If this tract reaches the street, they will hunt every cellar in the city until they find who had the matrices."

"They are hunting anyway," Katrijn said. She picked up the composing stick again, set the slide, and reached for an uppercase *W*. "Moretus has the bailiffs and the Spanish pikemen. But we have the lead."

She set the next word: *Woe.*

The type cases were arranged in the traditional German order: the upper case holding the capitals, accented letters, and figures; the lower case holding the small letters, spaces, and punctuation marks. The compartments were sized according to the frequency of the letters in the Dutch tongue: the *e* box was four inches wide, occupying the center of the bench, while the *x*, *z*, and *q* sat in narrow, neglected corners at the top edge.

Katrijn's fingers moved with a rhythmic, fluttering speed that seemed detached from her conscious mind. *Sort, nick, slide. Sort, nick, slide.* The composing stick grew heavy in her left palm as line after line of lead locked into place, each metal rectangle bearing the inverted shadow of a word that would shake the city.

Katrijn reached into the lower case, her fingers selecting two copper hair-spaces from the small partitioned tray beside the rule. She inserted the thin metal slips between the words *tyranny* and *truth*, testing the tension with her thumbnail until the line was tight enough to stand upright on its own lead feet without falling.

A line of type was an architectural arch built of metal and pressure. If one sort sat a fraction of a hair higher than its fellows, it would puncture the damp paper under the platen; if one sort was worn thin at the shank, the entire line would bow outward in the chase, spilling fifty lead letters into the ink-stone like broken teeth. She ran her fingertip across the tops of the characters, feeling the unbroken, glass-smooth level of the face.

She picked up a thin slip of boxwood reglet and slid it between the fifth and sixth lines of the column. The wood was bone dry, salvaged from an old book-binder's press, planed down to a thickness of two points. In the cold cellar air, the lead sorts and the wooden reglets breathed together, expanding and contracting with every change in dampness. A careless compositor would forget the moisture of the cellar walls, only to watch the type spring out of the chase like dried peas when the wooden wedges dried under the stove.

She laid three sheets of waste parchment over the galley to protect the type from the dust that sifted between the ceiling planks. In the stillness of the cellar, she could hear the faint sound of church bells tolling the fourth hour of the night from the spire of Saint James across the canal.

She took a small pinch of bone ash from the earthenware jar and dusted the setting stick. The white powder kept the compositor's sweat from tarnishing the lead sorts, preserving the crisp relief of the tiny serifs through twenty lines of composition.

The lead sorts clicked against the walnut wood, steady, precise, relentless in the quiet of the underground vault.

# Chapter 10: The Search on the Canal

The handcart had a loose iron tire on the left wheel that clattered over the cobblestones with every rotation.

Giles had greased the axle with mutton fat and wrapped the iron rim in strips of old linen sackcloth, but in the dead silence of the curfew hours, every bump against the granite kerb sounded to Katrijn like a musket shot echoing off the canal walls.

The November fog had rolled in thick from the Scheldt, smothering the city in a wet, freezing shroud that smelled of dead shellfish and coal smoke. Across the Sint-Jansvliet basin, the hulls of the moored peat barges were dark, hulking shapes looming through the mist, their rigging dripping steady beads of water into the black canal below.

Katrijn walked two paces ahead of the cart, her woolen shawl pulled over her nose and mouth. Under her cloak, her right hand gripped the municipal pass that Van Hout had given her in the churchyard.

In the belly of the two-wheeled barrow lay two reams of fine Troyes rag paper—one thousand sheets of heavy, white linen stock, packed into four bundles and wrapped in oilcloth to keep the river dampness from swelling the margins. On top of the paper, covering the bundles completely, lay six wicker laundry hampers filled with foul, blood-stained bandages, soiled sheets, and gray woolen shrouds discarded from the fever ward of the Saint Elizabeth hospital.

The stench of vinegar, dried blood, and carbolic soap was sharp enough to make Katrijn's eyes burn in the cold.

"Hold the cart," she whispered, raising her hand.

They had reached the foot of the Sint-Jansvliet bridge.

Through the drifting fog, the yellow light of an iron brazier gleamed from the stone arch. Two Spanish soldiers sat on empty salt casks beside the parapet, their twelve-foot pikes propped against the bridge rail, warming their gloved hands over the glowing coals. A third man—a sergeant in a buff leather coat and an iron breastplate—leaned against the bridge pillar, smoking a short clay pipe that glowed red in the mist.

"Walloon," Giles muttered behind her, his voice barely audible above the rattle of the wheel. "Not Spanish. He understands Dutch."

"Keep your mouth shut," Katrijn said. "Look at the cobblestones. If he speaks to you, point to your ear."

She stepped onto the bridge incline, her wooden pattens clicking sharply on the wet stones. Giles followed, his head down, his shoulders hunched as he pushed the heavy cart up the slope.

The sergeant lifted his head. He took the clay pipe from between his yellow teeth, spat a stream of brown juice into the gutter, and stepped into the center of the roadway, his hand resting on the hilt of his heavy broadsword.

"Halt the barrow," he barked in rough, French-accented Dutch.

Giles dropped the cart shafts. The barrow settled with a dull thump.

The sergeant walked slowly around the front of the cart, his iron-shod boots scraping the stones. When he caught the rank, sweetish stench of the hospital linens, his face twisted in disgust. He pulled his leather collar up over his nose and stepped back two paces.

"What is this filth?" he demanded, pointing with the stem of his pipe.

Katrijn stepped forward, curtseying low as a widow of the artisan class was expected to do before a crown officer. She drew the folded paper from her bodice and held it out with both hands.

"Linens for the pest-wash, Captain," she said, her voice trembling with just enough fear to sound natural. "From the fever ward at Saint Elizabeth. The council has ordered all bedding from the plague cellar burned or boiled in lye at the salt meadows outside the Red Gate."

The sergeant took the paper gingerly by its dry corner, holding it toward the light of the brazier.

He could not read the Latin script—few mercenaries in Parma's regiments could read more than their pay chits—but he knew the heavy red wax seal of the municipal admiralty and the bold signature of the city magistrate. His thumb rubbed the edge of the seal, testing its hardness.

"The Red Gate is closed until dawn," the sergeant said, glaring at her through the fog. "No carts pass the walls after the nine o'clock bell."

"The hospital warden swore the pass would clear us to the laundry wharf on the canal, my lord," Katrijn pleaded, keeping her eyes fixed on the muddy buckles of his boots. "The dead wagons are bringing three more bodies from the hospital cellar tonight. If the bedding is not washed in the lye vats before tomorrow's noon bell, the sisters cannot admit the wounded soldiers from the polder forts."

The mention of wounded soldiers seemed to carry weight. The sergeant grunted, handed the paper back to her, and turned toward the two men by the brazier.

"Pedro," he shouted in Spanish. "Bring the pike."

One of the soldiers rose from his salt cask, picked up his heavy ash pike, and walked over to the barrow. He reversed the weapon, holding the fifteen-pound ash shaft like an iron crowbar.

Katrijn's breath caught in her throat.

If the iron point penetrated the wicker hampers and pierced the oilcloth beneath, it would strike the hard, dense edges of two reams of Dutch-milled rag paper—paper that did not yield like soft linen, but rang solid as seasoned oak.

The soldier jabbed the blunt iron butt of the pike into the top hamper.

A squelch of wet, vinegar-soaked rags answered the thrust. The stench of hospital sickness billowed from the disturbed basket, so foul that the Spanish soldier cursed in his native tongue, pulled the weapon back, and spat into the canal.

"Basta," the soldier growled, waving his hand toward the opposite bank. "Es mierda del hospital. Let them go, Sargento."

The sergeant took a long pull on his clay pipe, blowing a stream of blue smoke into the cold air.

"Take your pestilence across the bridge, woman," he snarled. "And if I catch you in the lanes after the morning bell, I will throw you and your idiot boy into the moat."

"God preserve your lordship," Katrijn murmured, bowing her head.

She tapped Giles on the shoulder. The boy gripped the cart shafts, lifted the barrow with a grunt of strain, and leaned into the leather strap. The cart creaked forward over the bridge crest, past the glowing brazier, and down the western slope into the dark, narrow alleys of the Sint-Jansvliet quarter.

They did not speak. They did not look back.

They pushed the cart through three twisting alleys, their footsteps drowned by the steady dripping of the fog from the gables, until they reached the low wooden door of the abandoned dyer's house.

Giles dropped the shafts. He collapsed against the damp brick wall, his forehead pressed against his forearm, his breath coming in dry, painful sobs. His hands were shaking so violently that he could not unhook the barrow traces.

Katrijn reached down, took his soot-stained wrists in her hands, and squeezed them until the bone gave under her fingers.

"We have the paper, Giles," she whispered in his good ear.

The boy looked up. His eyes were wide, dark with the remembered terror of the bridge, but in the dim light of the alley, his jaw set hard as iron.

The handcart creaked as Giles guided the iron-shod wheels over the slick granite incline of the bridge. The fog was so dense that the water of the Sint-Jansvliet canal was invisible beneath them, marked only by the hollow sloshing of the tide against the stone piers and the smell of dead eels in the mud.

The Walloon sergeant took a deep breath of his clay pipe, blowing a cloud of gray smoke into the drizzle. He looked at Katrijn with the dull, contemptuous indifference of a foreign mercenary who had seen fifty Flemish towns starve and burned two dozen villages along the Meuse. To him, she was only another gray, shivering woman in a black hood, dragging the rotting bedding of plague victims to the ditch.

Giles pushed the two-wheeled barrow down the narrow descent of the Sint-Jansvliet bridge, his wooden clogs sliding on the wet river silt that coated the cobblestones. The fog was thickening, swirling in white, ghostly eddies around the dark timber gables of the dyer's house, muffling the distant chime of the Cathedral clock as it struck the second hour of the morning.

Katrijn walked with her hand resting on the canvas cover of the laundry hampers, feeling the solid, dense weight of the paper reams beneath the soiled linen bedding. One thousand sheets of French rag paper. Enough to print four hundred copies of the truth. She looked up at the darkened windows of the alley, where no candle burned and no face appeared behind the leaded panes, praying that the sleeping weavers would not wake until the cellar hatch was bolted behind them.

The fog along the Scheldt smelled of rotten cabbage and brackish river ice. At the water gate, three Dutch canal boats lay moored against the slippery pilings, their tarred hulls rising and falling with the sluggish winter tide. A lantern hung from the bowsprit of the middle boat, casting a murky yellow ring across the dark grease of the water. Beneath the canvas awning, an old waterman sat on an upturned barrel, puffing on a clay pipe that glowed cherry red in the gloom.

Giles shifted his weight on the shafts of the handcart, easing the iron-rimmed wheels over a sunken curb stone. The laundry hampers groaned under the compact heft of the unprinted paper. Four reams of fine Genoese laid paper, watermarked with a cardinal's hat and the triple keys. Hendrik had paid ten gold crowns for those sheets two years before the siege, storing them in dry pine chests beneath the shop rafters to keep the sizing from turning sour.

"If the gate guards prod the hampers with their rapiers," Giles muttered, his voice barely carrying past his shoulder, "the steel will strike the paper blocks with a dull thud. They will know in a second that no woman ever wore chemises as stiff as millboard."

"They will not prod them," Katrijn replied quietly, keeping her pace even with the barrow. "I tucked three greasy blood puddings and a crock of rancid beef drippings right beneath the top linen cloth. The stench will keep their noses six feet away before they ever think to draw a blade."

The Scheldt was running high with the winter ebb, carrying thick cakes of gray ice that groaned as they collided with the bridge pilings. Giles leaned his weight against the barrow shafts, maneuvering the heavy load through the narrow passage between the dye-house and the rope-walk. The iron tires of the wheels clicked softly over the frozen mud.

Katrijn walked with her hand upon the sacking. Beneath the greasy linen, the four reams of paper were dry and firm. She looked up at the stone battlements of the Steen, where the torchlight of the Spanish watch flickered behind the embrasures. If the sentry challenged them, Giles would not hear the command. She took a deep breath, resting her fingers on the cold hilt of the small bodkin tucked inside her bodice, and kept her eyes fixed upon the dark tunnel of the Sint-Jansvliet gate.

The wheels of the handcart creaked over the frozen mud of the Sint-Jansvliet alley. Giles kept his head down, his broad shoulders braced against the hauling straps as the barrow climbed the slight rise toward the water gate. The river fog lay thick between the gables, smelling of tarred cordage, horse manure, and cold Scheldt silt. Katrijn kept pace beside the load, her knuckles resting against the coarse burlap cover that hid the reams of French paper.

Giles set down the barrow shafts and paused beneath the stone archway of the Steen. His breath billowed in the winter frost, white and heavy as woodsmoke. He listened for the clank of Spanish halberds along the quay wall, his jaw set in a hard, defiant line as Katrijn checked the canvas wraps around the paper bundles.

"Unload the hampers," Katrijn said, drawing the iron cellar key from her pocket. "We have four hundred octavos to fold before the frost freezes the canal."

# Chapter 11: The Proctor's Visit

The rap at the kitchen street door came at noon, while Katrijn was paring cold turnip roots into an earthen pot.

She did not startle. In the months since the Vrijdagmarkt raid, she had trained her nerves to obey the silence of the workshop. She wiped the iron paring blade on her linen apron, laid it on the cutting board beside three parsnip tops, and smoothed her black woolen skirt before walking to the threshold.

Through the heavy oak door, she heard the scrape of fine Spanish leather shoes on the damp stoop.

She drew the bolt.

Inspector Balthasar Moretus stood on the doorstep, framed against the gray light of the Sint-Jansvliet quayside. Behind him, standing in the muddy gutter, waited two uniformed guild beadles in brown woolen tabards, each holding a five-foot ash staff tipped with the silver shield of Saint Luke.

Moretus smiled. It was a cold, practiced grimace that did not disturb the smooth skin around his hazel eyes. He wore a heavy traveling cloak of dark green broadcloth, lined with marten fur, and carried a pair of perfumed kidskin gloves tucked beneath his left arm.

"Mistress Vane," he said, touching the brim of his black velvet bonnet. His voice was soft, purring with the educated cadence of the Plantin counting house. "A chilly midday for the quays. May an officer of the guild warm his fingers at your hearth for three minutes?"

Katrijn stepped aside, bowing her head just enough to observe guild custom. "The hearth has only peat ashes, Master Proctor. But the threshold is open to the council."

Moretus stepped into the narrow kitchen, his marten fur brushing against the low doorframe. He glanced once around the room, taking in every object: the three earthenware plates on the oak dresser, the iron pot over the cold hearth, the scrubbed pine table where eight-year-old Clara sat winding gray wool yarn into a wooden ball. Five-year-old Pieter sat on the floor by the chimney corner, his small knees drawn to his chest, clutching a piece of charred firewood.

Moretus walked to the table. He stood looking down at Clara, who did not lift her eyes from the yarn.

"A dutiful daughter," Moretus remarked, his fingers lightly tapping the tabletop. "Hendrik's brow, I think. Let us pray she inherits her mother's prudence rather than her father's stubborn errors."

"Hendrik answered to the law on the Grote Markt, Master Proctor," Katrijn said, standing beside the dresser with her hands folded over her apron. "The guild took his presses, his paper stock, and his metal. There is nothing left in this house for the bailiffs to inventory."

"The guild took what was listed on the sheriff's register, mistress," Moretus said softly, turning to face her. He pulled a small silver vinaigrette box from his velvet pocket, unlatched the pierced lid, and inhaled the sharp scent of cloves and vinegar. "Yet there are rumors that drift across the Meir like river fog. Printers' devils in the taverns speaking of cheap Dutch tracts. Scraps of seditious doggerel found beneath the arch of the water gate. One hears that certain broken presses have grown new wooden limbs in the cellars of Sint-Jansvliet."

"We live on parish soup and what sewing I can find from the sailmakers," Katrijn replied. Her heart beat with a steady, dull rhythm against her ribs. "If there are presses in the canal vaults, they belong to the rats."

Moretus took two slow paces toward her. He reached out with his right hand, his perfumed fingers surprisingly strong, and took Katrijn's right wrist.

He lifted her hand into the gray light of the window.

His thumb moved across her knuckles, pressing into the rough, cracked skin where the lye water had eaten away the flesh around the joints. He turned her hand over, examining her fingertips and the margins of her nails.

Beneath the nail of her forefinger, deep in the groove where the cuticle met the bed, lay a microscopic crescent of lampblack: a stain so dark and permanent that three days of scrubbing with pumice stone had failed to lift it.

Moretus held her wrist suspended between them. His thumb rubbed the dark crescent with slow, deliberate pressure.

"Lampblack," the proctor murmured, his eyes locking onto hers. "Not chimney soot, Mistress Vane. Chimney soot is gray, gritty with coal ash. This has the silky bite of boiled oil and fine pine soot. The mark of the composing stick. The black brand of the devil's wooden box."

Katrijn did not pull her hand away. If she flinched, the two beadles on the stoop would have their crowbars into her floorboards within twenty seconds.

"The dyer's widow on the corner paid me three stuivers yesterday to clean her iron ink kettles," Katrijn said, her voice flat and cold as river ice. "She boils walnut skins and copperas to blacken mourning wool for the burgomaster's household. If the guild forbids a starving widow from scouring pots for bread, you had better write the prohibition into your register and nail it to the church door."

Moretus stared at her for five long seconds. The silence in the kitchen was broken only by the click of Clara's wooden yarn bobbin.

Slowly, the proctor released her wrist. He drew a fine cambric handkerchief from his cuff, wiped his thumb where it had touched her skin, and tucked the cloth back into his sleeve.

"The Duke of Parma has thirty thousand men across the Scheldt, Mistress Vane," Moretus said, his purr dropping into a dry, venomous whisper. "When the river stockade is joined, this city will starve until the burgers eat their own boots. The men who pay you thirty silver guilders for tracts will not be there to pull your neck from the hemp cord. Remember that when you hear the night carts on the stones."

He turned on his heel, walked through the doorway without looking back at the children, and stepped out onto the wet quayside. The two beadles fell in behind him, their heavy ash staves clattering against the cobblestones as they marched toward the canal bridge.

Moretus turned his gaze to the wooden dresser where three earthenware bowls and an iron candlestick sat in a row. Behind the plates leaned a small leather-bound book, its calfskin cover stained with water and rubbed thin at the corners. The proctor walked over, reached out with two gloved fingers, and lifted the volume by its spine.

"A Latin grammar, Master Proctor," Katrijn said, her voice completely flat. "Printed in Cologne twelve years ago. Hendrik bought it for three stuivers at the churchyard book fair to teach Clara her conjugations."

Moretus flipped the leaves with his thumb. The dry rag paper crackled in the cold room. He paused at the colophon, examining the printer's mark: a simple woodcut of an olive tree encircled by a serpent. For half a heartbeat, his eyes narrowed as if searching for a hidden watermark in the margins. Then, with a faint sniff of distaste, he tossed the book back onto the dresser.

Moretus stood in the center of the kitchen, his marten fur cloak pulled tight across his chest, his eyes sweeping the bare whitewashed walls. He pointed with his kidskin glove toward the ceiling beam where two dried turnip bunches hung from a wooden peg.

"The guild of Saint Luke has seventy sworn masters in this city, Mistress Vane," the proctor said, his voice dropping into a smooth, didactic cadence. "Master Plantin operates twenty-two presses on the Vrijdagmarkt. He employs eighty journeymen, fifty compositors, and ten punch-cutters from Paris. He has printed the Royal Polyglot Bible in eight folio volumes for His Catholic Majesty. That is printing: an ordered, sacred hierarchy protected by the imperial privilege. What you and Hendrik practiced was not a craft; it was a contagion. A reckless, heretical fever that brings the executioner's broadsword into the market square."

Katrijn examined the tympan cloth on the outer frame. The linen had slackened in the river damp, sagging like an old sail. She took an iron pin and tightened the corner cord, drawing the coarse weave taut until it twanged under her thumb like a lute string. If the tympan lacked tension, the paper would drag across the inked face before the platen made contact, producing a fuzzy, smeared impression known in the craft as a monk's ghost.

Katrijn rubbed a pinch of powdered chalk over the surface of the leather tympan to absorb any stray grease. If oil touched the clean linen paper, the printed letters would smudge into illegible grey smears before the sheet could be hung to dry.

Katrijn smoothed the sheepskin covering of the outer tympan with her palm. The leather was supple, rubbed with almond oil each Saturday to prevent the cellar frost from stiffening the hide into brittle cardboard.

Katrijn walked to the door, slid the heavy iron bolt into place, and leaned her forehead against the cold oak timber.

# Chapter 12: The Children by the Hearth

The kitchen smelled of damp lime and cold ash.

Katrijn knelt before the low fireplace, coaxing the tiny blue flame of two peat briquettes with a wooden shingle. In the iron pot hanging from the crane, half a pint of oatmeal simmered in three cups of canal water, thickened with a thumb-sized lump of salted mutton tallow that Huybrechts had thrown into her apron on market morning. It was thin, greasy food, gray as river foam, but it was warm.

Clara sat on the low bench, her woolen stockings pulled tight over her knees to ward off the draft that whistled under the floorboards. She held Pieter on her lap. The boy was five, but his legs were thin as birch twigs, his skin pale and translucent under the eyes where the winter cough had settled.

"Is the black-coat man coming back, Mother?" Clara asked, her voice small in the hollow chimney.

"No," Katrijn said. She stirred the porridge with a peeled alder spoon. "The proctor has gone to his dinner at the guildhall. He will not trouble us today."

"He looked at my shoes," Pieter whispered, burying his nose in Clara's woolen bodice. "He smelled like church candles."

"He smelled of cloves," Clara said, her dark eyes sharp and observant. "He had a silver box with little holes in the top. When Father was in the shop, the men from the Town Hall had silver boxes like that. They put sweet dust on their coats so they would not smell the printers' ink."

Katrijn did not answer. She ladled the hot gruel into two wooden bowls, set them on the bench between the children, and handed each a horn spoon.

"Blow upon it," she commanded gently. "It will burn your lips."

The children ate in silence, scraping the bowls until the horn spoons clicked against the bare wood. For eight months, hunger had been their constant companion: not a sudden, dramatic famine, but the slow, grinding erosion of daily life in a city under siege. First the white wheaten bread had disappeared from the bakers' tables; then the butter and the Gouda cheese; then the salted herrings had doubled in price, until a single fish cost more than an apprentice's daily wage. Now, with Parma's army throwing up earthen redoubts along both banks of the Scheldt, the river was choked with Spanish gunboats, and the great grain storehouses at the water gate were nearly empty.

When Pieter finished his porridge, he leaned his head against Clara's shoulder and closed his eyes, his breath coming in faint, wheezing whistles.

Clara waited until her brother's hands went limp with sleep. Then she leaned toward Katrijn, her gaze dropping to the floorboards beneath the dresser where the trapdoor to the coal cellar lay hidden under a heap of empty wool sacks.

"Mother," the girl whispered.

Katrijn paused with the wooden spoon in her hand. "Yes, child?"

"There was a man in the alley yesterday," Clara said, her voice barely louder than the draft in the chimney. "Before the noon bell. I was looking through the small window above the water butt."

Katrijn's fingers tightened on the alder wood. "What kind of man?"

"He had a black coat with dark velvet on the collar," Clara said. "Not like the proctor. His coat was torn at the hem, and he had dirty boots with brass buckles. He stood by the coal grate for a long time. He knelt in the gutter and looked through the iron bars into the dark."

"Did he speak to you?"

"No. I stayed behind the shutter like you told me. But he had a little piece of chalk in his pocket. When he stood up, he made a white cross on the corner of the dyer's wall, right beside the cellar chute."

Katrijn felt the skin across her scalp prickle with cold terror.

A chalk mark.

In the old days, before the Duke of Alba marched his tercios into Brabant, a white cross on a door meant the plague had visited the house. But under the Spanish military council, a chalk mark on an alley wall meant something far more lethal: it was the mark of the provost marshal's spies, pointing out suspected houses to the night raiding parties before the curfew sweeps began.

She set the wooden bowl on the hearth, stood up, and walked to the small grimy window that looked out into the side lane.

Through the greenish bottle-glass panes, the alley lay deserted in the freezing rain. Water poured from a cracked lead gutter onto the cobblestones, splashing against the stone footings of the dyer's house. At the corner of the masonry, three feet above the curb, she could see it: a crude, diagonal cross drawn in white lime chalk, half-washed by the drizzle but still clearly visible against the dark, soot-stained brickwork.

Moretus had not come to her kitchen on an idle suspicion.

The guild proctor was weaving his net. He had posted his watchers along the Sint-Jansvliet; he had marked the house; and he had come in person to test her nerves and see if she would break or flee. If she packed her bundles and took the children toward the gates, the bailiffs would seize her on the bridge; if she remained, the night watch would smash the cellar hatch the moment they had proof of the press.

She walked back to the bench where Clara sat guarding her sleeping brother.

Katrijn knelt on the cold floorboards, took Clara's small, rough hands in her own, and looked straight into her daughter's eyes.

"Clara," she said softly.

"Yes, Mother?"

"If there is a loud noise at the street door tonight—if you hear iron boots or the sound of wood breaking—you do not run to the cellar. Do you understand me?"

Clara's lower lip trembled, but she nodded. "I take Pieter."

"You take Pieter by the hand," Katrijn instructed, her voice steady and deliberate. "You open the small scuttle in the garret roof behind the peat stack. You climb onto the dyer's leads, walk along the gutter to the brewery roof, and climb down the cooper's ladder into the malt yard. You go straight to Master Huybrechts's bakery on the Meir. You show him this."

She reached into her bodice, pulled out the cold pewter guild token of Hendrik Vane, and pressed it into Clara's woolen pocket.

"Tell Huybrechts that Hendrik's children are on the street," Katrijn whispered. "He will curse you, and he will curse your father's memory, but he will not let the Spanish take you. He will hide you in his flour bins until the morning carts leave for Zeeland."

Clara looked down at her pocket, her eyes wide with tears that did not spill. "And you, Mother? Will you come to the brewery?"

"I have work on the stone," Katrijn said. She stroked the girl's tangled hair with her ink-stained thumb. "Giles and I have four hundred voices to wake before the frost takes the river. When the voices have spoken, Hendrik will sleep in peace, and we will buy all the white bread in Brabant."

Katrijn sat on the low stool, watching the frost feather across the corners of the small bottle-glass window panes. Outside, the dusk was deepening into a bruised, purplish gray over the chimney pots of Sint-Jansvliet. Across the street, an old woman was nailing an oiled linen cloth over her broken window frame to keep out the draft from the river.

In the corner of the hearth, Pieter had arranged three dry pine kindling sticks into the shape of a press gallows. He had found a scrap of gray paper in the wood basket, and he was pressing the flat of his thumb against the wood, making a soft *clack-clack* sound with his tongue like the turning of a rounce. Clara watched him in silence, her hand resting protectively on his shoulder.

"Father told me that when the river opens in the spring, the English ships bring oranges from Portugal," Clara murmured, looking up at Katrijn. "Round yellow oranges wrapped in tissue paper that smells of sweet cloves. Will the ships come this year, Mother?"

Katrijn sat on the bench beside Clara, watching Pieter sleep with his head in his sister's lap. The boy's face was thin, the skin around his cheekbones stretched tight as drumhead parchment, his small chest rising and falling in shallow, rapid breaths. She pulled the hem of Hendrik's old woolen cloak over his bare ankles to keep out the draft that whistled under the door.

On Saint Nicholas Eve two years before, the kitchen on the Vrijdagmarkt had been warm, filled with the aroma of roasted chestnuts, spiced ginger biscuits, and dark Spanish raisins. Hendrik had built a small wooden puppet theatre for Pieter, carved with little figures of the Prince of Orange and the sea captains of Flushing. Now the puppet theatre was firewood in a bailiff's stove, and the only toy Pieter had was a charred piece of pine kindling.

Moretus stepped closer to the workbench, his glove trailing along the edge of a pine shelf where dry bundles of parchment offcuts were stacked. He leaned over an open drawer of two-line initials, picking up an ornate letter *D* cut with foliage and a dancing satyr. He turned the boxwood block in his white fingers, holding it to the candlelight to inspect the grain.

"Antwerp was once the intellectual heart of the Spanish Netherlands, Master Giles," Moretus remarked, his voice dropping to a smooth, purring cadence. "In my father's day, fifty presses turned from sunrise to dusk between the Kipdorp and the canal. Christopher Plantin printed the Royal Polyglot Bible with royal subsidies, twelve hundred sheets an hour, eight languages side by side in perfect register. That was order. That was piety and commerce united under the crown of His Catholic Majesty."

He laid the boxwood initial back into the drawer with a gentle, precise tap. "Now look at our streets. Half the masters have fled to Leiden or London. Those who remain hide in basements like sewer rats, printing foul little broadsides on scrap paper with muddy ink, whispering mutiny into the ears of starving cobblers. Do you truly imagine that a pamphlet can stop Parma's cannon, Giles? Or do you imagine that when the Spanish storm the walls, the Duke will pause to ask whether a printer was merely following orders?"

Moretus walked to the corner of the printshop, where an iron press from Nuremberg stood under a dust-cloth. He pulled the coarse linen aside with two fingers, exposing the heavy bronze spindle and the massive iron platen. His gaze moved across the maker's mark stamped into the cross-beam with cold, aristocratic scrutiny.

"In Frankfurt last autumn, the Emperor's commissioners seized twelve hundred pamphlets printed in Basel," Moretus said quietly, his voice echoing in the stone room. "They burned the books in the public square before the cathedral. Then they broke the printer's thumb with an iron pliers so that he could never lift a composing stick again. That is how the Holy Roman Empire treats those who spread heresy among the commons. Do you believe Antwerp will be treated with greater mercy when Parma's tercios march through the Red Gate?"

Moretus stepped back toward the door, his hand resting on the silver pommel of his rapier. "The Council of Troubles made twenty thousand martyrs, Master Giles," he said, his voice quiet as snowfall upon a church roof. "The Duke of Alba believed that fear was the only sovereign remedy for rebellion. My master, the Prince of Parma, is wiser. He offers the town its ancient trade if you will bend your necks to the King. But if you persist in scattering seditious libels through the alleys, he will leave Antwerp a desert of broken bricks."

Moretus turned his cold gaze toward the window, where the first gray light of dawn was breaking over the frozen Scheldt. "You mistake my purpose, Giles," he said softly, smoothing the dark fur on his collar. "The King does not seek to destroy Antwerp. He seeks to prune the wild branches that the vineyard may bear fruit for the Church. Those who bow to the pruning knife will flourish; those who resist will be cast into the fire."

She kissed Clara's forehead, wrapped her woolen cloak around her shoulders, and lifted the trapdoor into the darkness of the cellar below.

# Chapter 13: The Galleys of Accusation

The proof sheet lay on the tympan, wet and gray under the candlelight.

Katrijn held the wooden reading glass in her right hand, moving the convex lens line by line across the rough impression of the third signature. The ink was dense, black as pitch, biting into the Troyes rag paper with razor sharpness. Beside her, Giles held a tallow candle close to the bed, shielding the flame from the draft with his palm.

"Line fourteen," Katrijn muttered. "A turned letter. The lowercase *n* in *pension* is set as a *u*."

She took her brass bodkin from her pocket, inserted the needle-point between the lead sorts, and levered the offending letter from the chase. She flipped it in her fingers, reseated it with the nick facing true, and pressed it home with her thumb.

Then her eyes moved to the head of the seventh article.

*Article VII. Concerning the Betrayal of Master Hendrik Vane upon the Vrijdagmarkt.*

Katrijn's breath stopped in her throat.

When Van Hout had delivered the manuscript, she had scanned the opening indictments of the magistrates and the Duke of Parma's secret subsidies. But here, laid out in the cold geometry of Brevier lead, was the detailed account of the raid that had ended her life.

*That upon the twenty-eighth day of February, information was laid before the Inquisitorial Commission by Master Dirck Coornhert, Deacon of the Stationers' Guild and sworn printer to the Holy See;*

*Who did depose that the said Hendrik Vane had received from a merchant of Dordrecht four reams of English paper and a copy of the vernacular psalms translated by Peter Dathenus;*

*And who did receive from Inspector Balthasar Moretus the sum of eighty gold crowns, drawn from the secret treasury of the Spanish garrison, upon condition that he should deliver the key to Vane's outer warehouse at the hour of midnight.*

Katrijn's fingers went cold on the brass bodkin.

Dirck Coornhert.

Not a Spanish informer. Not a drunken carter or a bribed apprentice from the quays.

Dirck Coornhert had been Hendrik's oldest companion in the trade. They had served their apprenticeships together under Christopher Plantin thirty years ago, sleeping beneath the same press carriage on the Kammenstraat, sharing the same bread and ink-pots. When Coornhert's oldest daughter died of the sweating sickness, Hendrik had carved her memorial woodcut with his own hands and refused to take a single silver stuiver for the work.

"Coornhert," Giles whispered, his scarred face twisting into a grimace of pure revulsion. "He sat by our hearth on the Vrijdagmarkt three days before the bailiffs came. He drank Hendrik's beer and kissed Clara on the forehead."

"Eighty gold crowns," Katrijn said, her voice flat, hollowed of all resonance. "That was the price of Hendrik's head. Four score crowns to pay for Coornhert's new front room on the Meir."

She looked down at the galley of type locked in the iron chase.

This was what the printing press was made for. Not for Latin grammars that gathered dust on monastery shelves, not for the flattering dedications that court poets wrote to buy patron silver. It was an iron hammer designed to strike the lead into the linen fiber, making the lie indelible, dragging the secret crime from the dark council chambers into the cold, blinding light of the market square.

"Check the fourth column," Katrijn said, her voice rising with a hard, cutting clarity. "Look for doublets in the Latin citations. We pull the first hundred sheets before the two o'clock bell."

Katrijn leaned her elbows on the pine bench, staring at the wet proof sheet under the yellow candle flame. The text was brutal in its precision. It listed the dates of four secret meetings held at the guildhall of Saint Luke, where Deacon Coornhert had met with the commissioners of the Duke of Parma to negotiate the seizure of twelve independent printshops.

In exchange for his cooperation, Coornhert had been promised the royal monopoly on all Catholic liturgical printing in Brabant once the city fell: two thousand Latin breviaries, four thousand missals, and five hundred folio bibles to be printed on imported Italian paper with royal subsidies from Madrid. The death of Hendrik Vane had not been a matter of religious zeal; it had been a business transaction negotiated across a velvet tablecloth over cups of Spanish sack.

Katrijn ran her finger down the galley of Brevier type, checking the signature marks at the foot of each page: *A3, A4, B1, B2.* The lead sorts sat true and level in the chase, held firmly by the beveled oak quoins that Giles had planed from Dutch heartwood. There was no bowed line, no high space that would ink the paper margin, no turned letter in the Latin legal citations.

The eleventh article detailed the destruction of three secret printshops in Ghent and Bruges, where royal commissioners had seized the lead founts and melted them down into musket balls for the Spanish siege lines. In every case, the raid had been arranged through secret letters carried by the proctors of the Stationers' Guild, paid from the confiscated estates of the condemned printers.

Katrijn reached beneath the bottom shelf of the washstand and checked the tin flask of almond oil. The oil was clear and sweet, reserved for oiling the hinges of the frisket so that no metal shriek would echo through the stone walls into the alley above.

Giles took the reading glass from her hand, his jaw set, and bent his head over the lead.

# Chapter 14: The Tavern on the Cow Gate

The taproom of the Red Lion on the Cow Gate smelled of stale rye beer, wet wool, and the greasy smoke of tallow candles stuck into empty stoneware jugs.

Giles sat in the darkest corner beneath the timber stair, his back pressed against the lime-washed brickwork, an earthen mug of sour small beer resting between his hands. His rough wool cap was pulled low over his damaged left ear, and he kept his ink-blackened fingers wrapped around the earthenware clay so the guild spies could not see the marks on his knuckles.

Around him, thirty harbor dockers, grain carters, and river bargemen sat crowded on the rough pine benches, their voices rising in a sullen, angry roar over the clatter of pewter tankards.

"Parma has driven thirty piles into the channel at Calloo," an old shipwright with a salt-gray beard shouted, slamming his fist onto the trestle. "My brother was on the scouting hoy from Zeeland yesterday. He saw the Spanish palisade stretching from the Brabant shore three hundred paces into the current. They have thirty-two armed ketches anchored in line, lashed together with iron ship-chains thick as my thigh. No grain fleet from Holland will break that timber wall."

"The burgomaster says the grain stores in the citadel will last until Easter," a young carter muttered, staring into his beer.

"The burgomaster lies through his gold teeth!" a dyer rasped from the bench beside the hearth. His apron was stained crimson with madder root. "There is no grain in the citadel! The Spanish garrison took four hundred sacks of rye out of the town magazines on Tuesday night and loaded them onto wagons for the camp at Beveren. The aldermen are filling their own cellars while our children eat turnip skins and chaff. My sister buried her youngest boy this morning. Starved to the bone, he was."

A heavy silence fell over the table, broken only by the crackle of green willow logs in the fireplace.

A stranger in a carter's frieze coat leaned forward from the end of the bench. He had a scarred lip and the squinting eyes of a man who worked with river ropes.

"They say there is a paper coming," the carter whispered, glancing over his shoulder toward the taproom door where the tavern keeper stood wiping mugs.

Every man at the table went still.

"What paper?" the shipwright asked.

"A printed tract," the carter murmured, lowering his voice until the dockers had to lean across the beer puddles to hear. "A brother of the Saint Nicholas guild saw a proof sheet in a warehouse near the brewery wharf. It gives the names. It tells which magistrates took the Spanish ducats to close the river redoubts, and which guild deacons sold their master printers to Moretus's bailiffs."

"Moretus," the dyer spat, crossing his fingers against the evil eye. "That dog has twenty thousand guilders in the Spanish bank at Genoa. He walks the Meir with his marten fur like a prince, while Hendrik Vane rots in a ditch outside the walls."

"Hendrik Vane was an honest craftsman," the old shipwright said, his voice deep with remembered respect. "He printed the maritime charters for the water guilds when the Spanish governors tried to steal our river rights ten years ago. He never charged the boatmen a brass mite for the seal. When Moretus broke his press, the heart went out of the Vrijdagmarkt."

Giles sat motionless in his corner, his thumbs digging into the rough clay of his mug.

He felt the heavy surge of pride and grief tightening his throat. In this foul, smoke-blinded tavern, among men who smelled of river mud and sweat, Hendrik's name was not the name of a condemned heretic; it was a rallying cry, an open wound that Antwerp had not forgotten.

"If the paper comes," the shipwright continued, leaning both elbows onto the timber, "we will not let Moretus burn it. If the paper names the traitors on the council, we take our boat-hooks and iron rammers and we march on the town hall. We open the city magazines ourselves, and we hang the Spanish pensioners from their own window sashes."

"Aye!" two carters growled, their hands dropping to the wood knives in their belts.

The tavern door swung open with a bang of cold wind and sleet.

Two uniformed sergeants of the civic night watch stepped into the taproom, their iron helmets dripping rain, their halberds held upright. The tavern fell dead silent. The keeper immediately picked up an earthen jug and rushed forward with a servile bow, offering two mugs of double beer on the house.

The sergeants did not take the beer.

Their eyes swept the crowded room, cold, suspicious, searching the shadows beneath the stairs. Giles kept his head down, staring into the dark dregs of his cup, his breath steady and slow.

"Curfew bell rings in twenty minutes," the lead sergeant announced, his voice ringing harsh against the low beams. "Clear the benches and douse your fires. The provost has doubled the guard at the Cow Gate. Any man found on the quays after the bell will spend the night in the Steen irons."

The dockers rose slowly, grumbling under their breath, shuffling toward the door with their heads down.

At the long trestle table near the beer kegs, three bargees from the Rupel were arguing over the depth of water on the drowned meadows. One of them had drawn a rough map in spilled beer on the oak boards, dipping his forefinger into the foam to trace the line of the Spanish dikes from Calloo to Beveren.

"The Spanish have thirty-two gunboats anchored in the river," the bargee growled, tapping the wet table. "Each hoy has four twenty-four-pound brass culverins mounted in the bows. If a Dutch ketch tries to pass the boom, the gunners will blow her timbers into kindling before she can come about. The only way through that timber wall is fire. If the Zeelanders do not send burning ships down on the ebb tide, Antwerp will eat horse meat before the snow melts."

A sailor in a salt-stained frieze jacket slammed his pewter tankard onto the table, splashing foam across the wet boards. He had a gold hoop in his left ear and the weathered, squinting eyes of a man who had sailed the North Sea fisheries from Dunkirk to the Shetlands.

"The Spanish fleet at Dunkirk has thirty-six armed pinnaces ready to put to sea," the sailor growled, wiping his mouth with his sleeve. "They have two thousand Walloon pikemen embarked on the transports. If the Sea Beggars cannot break the river palisade at Calloo before the deep freeze sets in, Antwerp will be invested from the land and the sea together. The only bread you will eat this winter will come from Parma's grain wagons, and you will pay for it on your knees before the inquisitor's crucifix."

The ink-balls hung from iron pegs driven into the cellar ceiling timbers. Giles took down the pair, running his palms over the stretched sheepskin pelts. The leather was tanned with alum and dog-dung, supple as kidskin, padded inside with carded wool that had been boiled twice to remove the lanolin grease. If the pelt was too dry, it would shed flakes into the galley; if the wool was too damp, the ink would curdle upon the type like sour cream.

"The wool is holding the frost, mistress," Giles whispered, his rough voice muffled by his sleeve. "I will warm the balls over the charcoal brazier before we ink the forms. Cold leather makes a patchy sheet, and this text must be read by men who have only rush-lights to see by."

Giles dipped the tip of his finger into the tallow pot and greased the wooden tracks of the press bed. The seasoned beechwood rails slid smoothly under the frame, without the telltale iron shriek that might carry through the floorboards to the street above.

Giles placed the iron chase upon the imposing stone, testing the squareness of the corners with a bronze rule. If the chase bowed by even half a millimeter under the pressure of the wooden quoins, the forme would spring in the middle during printing.

Giles waited until the taproom was half empty. Then he slid from the bench, pulled his wool cap over his scarred ear, and slipped out through the side scullery into the freezing darkness of the alley.

# Chapter 15: Sledgehammers in the Night

The iron blows began at the stroke of two in the morning.

In the coal cellar beneath Sint-Jansvliet, the sound arrived not through the air, but through the earth: a rhythmic, shuddering percussion that vibrated through thirty feet of wet alluvial soil and mortared brick.

*Thump. Thump. Craaaack.*

Katrijn dropped the ink-ball she was working. Giles caught the rounce handle before it could spin backward on the carriage. Both stood frozen in the yellow candlelight, their heads tilted toward the ceiling vault.

"Where is it?" Giles breathed.

"Three alleys east," Katrijn whispered. "Near the cooperage on the Predikherenrei."

*Smash.*

The sound of splintering oak was unmistakable. It was the heavy, catastrophic demolition of a timber common press being broken with fourteen-pound iron sledgehammers. First the sound of the oak gallows cracking under the blows; then the screech of the iron screw being wrenched sideways out of its brass box; then the hollow, bell-like ring of the platen striking the stone floorboards.

A man's voice began to scream in the distant street.

It was a high, terrified shriek that rose above the sound of the rain, cut short by the dull thud of an iron pike butt against bone. Then came the barking of watch dogs, the shouting of Spanish commands in the cold air, and the harsh clatter of iron-shod boots running along the canal quay.

"Master Visscher," Katrijn whispered, her hands turning to ice. "He had a small wooden bench press hidden in his cooper's shed. He printed religious broadsheets for the English cloth merchants."

Giles reached for the tallow candle, pinching out the wick between two wet fingers.

The cellar plunged into absolute blackness. The cold air rushed into Katrijn's lungs, smelling of damp coal, river silt, and the acrid pine soot of their fresh ink. She stood with her hand resting against the upright timber cheek of Hendrik's press. Under her palm, the oak frame felt cool, solid, alive with the tiny vibrations of the soldiers' boots passing along the quayside above.

She moved to the ladder, climbed three rungs, and pressed her eye to the small ventilation slit where the coal chute met the street curb.

Through the iron grating, the Sint-Jansvliet alley was illuminated by the flickering red glare of pitch torches.

Six Spanish pikemen stood at the corner of the lane, their steel breastplates gleaming in the rain, their pikes held at the ready to bar the passage. In the center of the lane, two guild beadles dragged a man through the mud by the collar of his linen shirt.

It was Master Visscher.

His face was covered in blood where a halberd blade had grazed his temple; his spectacles hung broken around his neck by a scrap of twine. Behind him, two soldiers carried the wreckage of his trade: an iron composing stick, three wooden cases of Brevier lead, and a bundle of damp printed sheets that fluttered in the freezing wind like white birds.

Inspector Balthasar Moretus walked slowly behind the soldiers.

The proctor was wrapped in his crimson marten-lined cloak, his hands tucked warm inside his fur muff. He did not look at Visscher. He stopped in the center of the alley, directly opposite the dyer's house, and looked down at the cobblestones.

Katrijn's breath locked in her chest.

Moretus was standing four feet from the cellar grating. She could see the droplets of rain clinging to the brim of his velvet bonnet; she could smell the sharp, perfumed clove water that drifted from his gloves.

The proctor looked at the wall of the dyer's house.

In the red torchlight, the white chalk cross that the spy had drawn beside the cellar chute stood out bright as a fresh scar against the dark brickwork.

Moretus lifted his walking cane, pointing with its silver tip toward the white cross.

A sergeant of the Walloon watch stepped forward, his halberd resting on his shoulder. "Shall we batter the cellar door, Master Proctor? The informer reported smoke from the grate yesterday noon."

Katrijn's heart hammered against her ribs with such violence that she feared the men in the alley would hear it through the iron bars. Her fingernails bit into the oak rung of the ladder until the wood bruised her flesh.

Moretus lowered his cane.

He looked at the dark, silent facade of the dyer's house, where no candle burned in the windows and no smoke rose from the chimney.

"Not tonight, Sergeant," Moretus said, his voice carrying clearly through the brick slit. "Visscher is the small fish. His type was cast in London; he has no connection to the council libels. If we kick down every coal hole on Sint-Jansvliet tonight, the real press will take warning and scatter its lead into the canal before we can seize the formes."

He turned back toward the main quayside, tapping the cobblestones with his cane.

"Leave the watchers at the bridge," Moretus commanded. "If anyone carries paper or ink across the canal before dawn, seize them without question. Take Visscher to the Steen. Let the executioner test his fingers on the thumbscrews until he tells us who cut his matrices."

"And the dyer's cellar?" the sergeant asked.

"Watch the grating," Moretus replied, his voice fading as he walked into the rain. "When the rat moves, we will take the whole nest together."

The torchlight flickered away down the quayside. The shouts of the soldiers grew distant, swallowed by the drumming rain and the wind that howled off the Scheldt.

Katrijn climbed down the ladder in the dark.

She reached out with her hands, found Giles's arm in the blackness, and gripped his forearm. The boy was trembling from head to foot, his teeth chattering against each other with a dry, clicking rattle like lead sorts in a case.

"They did not touch the door," Giles choked out.

"They have marked the house," Katrijn said, her voice low and hard as granite. "Moretus knows the press is on this street. He is waiting for us to pull the sheets so he can catch the ink wet."

She struck flint and steel, blowing the red spark into the tallow wick until the yellow flame leaped up once more.

The common press stood before them, its iron screw gleaming dull and heavy, its carriage loaded with the chase holding the first signature of *A True Record*.

"How many reams of paper are dampened?" she asked.

Giles wiped the cold sweat from his face with his sleeve. "Two reams. One thousand sheets."

Through the cold ground came the heavy, splintering crash of a wooden paper chest being overturned onto flagstones, followed by the metallic chime of several hundred lead sorts scattering across the floor like dry peas. Katrijn knew that sound. It was the music of a ruined workshop: the labor of three generations crushed beneath an iron boot in five minutes.

In the street above, a woman began to scream. It was Visscher's daughter, her voice rising in frantic, shrill shrieks as two Walloon pikemen dragged her down the alley stoop by her woolen hair. A sergeant shouted an oath in French; then came the sharp, sickening sound of an ash staff striking flesh, and the screaming stopped, replaced by the heavy sobbing of a child in the dark.

Through the cold ground came the sound of a handcart being loaded in the alley: the heavy, hollow thump of oak press timbers thrown into a barrow, followed by the clanking chime of broken iron screw components. Two soldiers were laughing in French, joking about the five silver stuivers they had found in Visscher's salt crock.

Katrijn knelt on the flagstones in the black vault, her arm wrapped around Giles's shaking shoulders. She did not speak; she held her breath in the frozen air, listening as the iron-rimmed wheels of the barrow rolled away down the lane toward the quayside, leaving the Sint-Jansvliet in a dead, suffocating silence.

Giles gripped the wooden rounce handle with both hands, his calloused palms biting into the polished ash. The gears engaged with a deep, hollow groan of dry beech-wood teeth. The carriage slid forward beneath the heavy cast platen, the iron iron-tracks groaning under forty stone of pressure.

Katrijn watched the paper feed. Each sheet of laid linen entered the frame like a white flag of truce and emerged stamped with the black, irrevocable indictments of the town council. Across the top line, the heavy gothic capitals spelled out the names of the five aldermen who had pocketed Parma's florins. With every pull of the lever, Giles was striking another nail into the coffin of the Spanish regency. Katrijn could hear the blood thumping behind her temples, each heartbeat keeping time with the rhythmic heave of the press-timber.

The ink-balls struck the lead forme with a wet, rhythmic thump that sounded like a baker kneading dough on an oak table. Giles moved with mechanical precision, rocking the leather pelts across the lines of type until every seraph and accent was coated in a flawless coat of black varnish.

Katrijn laid the damp paper onto the tympan points, folded the frisket down with a snap of her wrist, and rolled the carriage beneath the platen. With a sharp groan of the ash lever, the screw turned. Forty stone of oak pressure pressed the linen fibers down onto the inked characters. When the platen lifted, the page appeared: clean, black, and permanent as an epitaph on a tombstone.

Giles leaned his full weight against the wooden bar, his shoulder muscles knotting beneath his coarse linen shirt. The press creaked with a deep, resonant groan as the platen bore down upon the tympan. Katrijn stood ready at the bench, holding the wooden peel that printers used to lift the fresh sheets onto the drying cords. With every pull of the lever, another page of the secret manifesto was born, stamped in black ink that would outlast the Spanish garrisons.

"Then we do not sleep," Katrijn said. She picked up the twin sheepskin ink-balls from their cups. "We pull the impression tonight. Every hour we wait, Moretus draws his rope an inch tighter around our throats."

# Chapter 16: The Metallurgy of Type

The iron ladle rested on the glowing peat embers, its bowl filled with molten metal that shivered like mercury in the heat.

Katrijn bent over the crucible, holding a small horn spoon in her left hand to skim the dross from the surface. A crust of yellowish-gray oxide gathered at the rim—the slag of cheap English lead and impurities from old pewter—which she drew off and dropped into an earthen saucer, where it hissed and hardened into dull gray powder.

"More antimony," Giles said, holding out an iron pair of pincers gripping a jagged lump of silvery, crystalline ore the size of a pigeon's egg.

"Only half," Katrijn warned. "If you put too much antimony into the melt, the metal becomes brittle as glass. The fine serifs of the lowercase *f* and *t* will snap under the pressure of the platen on the fortieth pull."

Giles tapped the lump with a small smith's hammer, breaking off a fragment that fell into the molten lead with a soft *plip*.

The alloy of the printer was a delicate, dangerous secret. Lead alone was too soft; it would mash flat like tallow beneath the screw after a single day's run. Tin alone was too malleable, running too slowly into the fine channels of the matrix. It was the antimony—that toxic, brittle, crystalline semi-metal mined in the mountains of Germany—that gave the alloy its miraculous property: it expanded slightly as it cooled in the mold, forcing the liquid metal into the deepest crevices of the punched copper matrix, ensuring that every hairline serif and delicate curve emerged with razor-sharp fidelity.

Katrijn took her casting mold from the wooden bench.

It was Hendrik's hand mold, crafted by a precision punch-cutter in Lyon: two interlocking halves of seasoned boxwood lined with polished iron plates, held together by a steel bow spring. In the throat of the mold sat the tiny copper matrix, stamped with the reversed face of a Brevier lowercase *e*.

They had run out of *e* sorts while setting the twelfth page of the indictment.

Without sixty more *e*s, the press was dead. You could not write a Dutch sentence without that vowel; the tongue was built upon it, from the definite articles to the plural verbs.

Katrijn took the iron casting ladle by its turned wooden handle.

Her movement was swift, practiced, repeated ten thousand times in her youth. She dipped the lip of the ladle into the silvery pool of molten alloy, lifted two drams of liquid metal, and brought it to the funnel-shaped orifice of the hand mold.

With a sudden, coordinated jerk of her left wrist, she poured the metal and tossed the mold upward in a quick, lifting snap.

That upward jerk was the founder's art: it drove the heavy molten lead down into the tiny chamber before the chilled iron could freeze the metal, forcing the liquid into the face of the copper matrix.

*Clack.*

She released the steel bow spring. The mold fell open in two halves.

A tiny, gleaming sliver of lead dropped onto the wooden tray: a perfect Brevier sort, complete with its cast face, its body, its nick, and a long, tapering jet of waste metal at the foot.

Katrijn picked up the sort with a pair of brass tweezers.

The metal was hot enough to scorch the skin, but she held it close to the candle flame, examining the face with her magnifying lens. The tiny oval loop of the *e* was clear and open; the crossbar was straight as a drawn line; the serif at the foot had no air bubble or cold-shut flaw.

"Break the jet," she told Giles.

Giles took the hot sort in his gloved fingers, snapped the waste jet off with a sharp twist, and ran the base of the lead across a fine flat file to bring the sort to the exact height-to-paper of the rest of the fount.

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

Katrijn poured, jerked, opened, and released. One sort every eight seconds. The cellar filled with the sweet, sickening metallic fumes of vaporized lead and antimony, making her throat dry and her tongue taste of cold copper coins. But she did not pause.

Katrijn dipped the iron ladle back into the crucible. The molten alloy was gleaming like liquid quicksilver beneath the thin skin of yellowish dross. She reached for the small stoneware box of powdered sal-ammoniac that Hendrik had kept for purifying the melt, taking a pinch between her fingers and scattering the white crystals across the liquid surface.

The powder crackled with a sharp, pungent puff of white smoke that smelled of burned horn and salt. The slag pulled away to the edges of the bowl, leaving the pool of lead, antimony, and tin pure, bright, and mirror-smooth. She brought the hand mold to the ladle lip, her muscles braced for the rhythmic snap of the casting jerk.

Katrijn took the newly cast *e* sort from the brass tweezers, laid it upon the smooth marble test stone, and ran a steel height-to-paper gauge across the face. The gauge slid over the letter with a faint, musical hiss, neither catching on the serif nor rocking on the shank. The height was true to the thousandth part of an inch: the exact French standard of the Plantin workshops.

The balance of the metal was everything. A fount that had too much soft lead would crush flat under the platen after forty pulls, turning the clean letters into black, illegible blots; a fount that had too much brittle antimony would shatter like dried clay when the screw descended. She looked at the eighty-two newly cast characters resting on the pine board, bright and sharp as newly struck silver groats.

The smell of fresh printer's ink was as distinct as newly baked bread. It was the smell of boiled walnut oil, crushed soot, and pine rosin, sharp enough to cut through the reek of rotting vegetables in the cellar drains.

Katrijn rubbed her knuckles over the rough surface of the work-table. A film of white lime dust had settled across the boards from the cellar ceiling. Through the floor, she could hear the ice cracking against the canal wall like gunshots in the freezing twilight.

By the time the iron ladle was empty, eighty-two clean, newly minted *e* sorts lay cooling on the pine board, ready to spell the treason of the Stationers' Guild.

# Chapter 17: Van Hout's Second Payment

Alderman Lucas Van Hout arrived through the cellar chute carrying a willow basket covered with a wet flour sack.

He fell the last two feet from the ladder, his heavy boots striking the floor with a wet thud that sent coal dust billowing into the air. He did not brush his cloak. He dropped the basket onto the composing stone, collapsed onto the low oak stool, and leaned his head against the brick vault, breathing with a harsh, rattling wheeze that shook his heavy shoulders.

Katrijn stood over him, holding her candle high.

"You look like a man who has seen his own shroud, Alderman."

Van Hout pulled off his velvet bonnet and buried his face in his hands. His fingers were shaking violently. When he lifted his head, his eyes were bloodshot, his cheeks gray as ditch silt in the candlelight.

"The pontoon is closed," he whispered, his voice trembling so hard that his teeth clicked.

Katrijn stared at him. "The Scheldt?"

"The bridge of boats," Van Hout said, his hand clutching his chest. "Parma's engineers drove the final anchors into the mud off Calloo at noon today. Thirty-two armed ketches, lashed with iron chains. A timber parapet eight feet high, mounted with ninety brass cannons. Two floating forts at each end, bristling with Spanish culverins. A seagull cannot fly down the river channel without drawing musket fire."

Giles took a step forward from the shadow of the press. "The Dutch fleet? The Sea Beggars from Zeeland?"

"They cannot pass," Van Hout grovelled, shaking his head. "Admiral Treslong brought forty hoys up the estuary on the flood tide. The Spanish batteries blew three galleys out of the water before they reached the boom. The rest turned back toward Flushing. Antwerp is an island, Katrijn. A locked cage in the middle of a frozen bog."

The silence in the vault was suffocating.

For months, the citizens had clung to the desperate hope that the Dutch fleet would break the blockade before the river froze. Now, with Alexander Farnese's floating stockade locked across the current, the city was severed from the sea. No wheat from England. No salt from France. No gunpowder from Amsterdam.

"The council met in secret session at the stadhuis two hours ago," Van Hout said, his voice dropping to a terrified murmur. "Burgomaster Van Rooden laid the Spanish terms on the table. Parma demands unconditional surrender. Re-establishment of the Catholic mass. Total submission to the royal inquisitors. The expulsion of every Protestant artisan who will not swear allegiance to the King within sixty days."

"And the aldermen?" Katrijn asked, her voice cold. "Will they sign?"

"They are preparing the signatures tonight," Van Hout said bitterly. "They will open the gates before the snow melts. They have already drawn the lists of the men to be sacrificed to the provost marshal to satisfy the King's mercy."

He reached out, his hand trembling, and pulled the flour sack from the willow basket on the stone.

Inside lay three thick slabs of salt pork, cured in white brine; two loaves of wheaten bread; a bag of dried apples; and a small pigskin purse tied with red silk cord.

He pushed the purse toward her.

"Ten more guilders," he said. "The second payment, as I swore. There is food for the children. Enough to keep them until the gates open."

Katrijn looked at the silver, then at the salt meat, then into the alderman's hollow eyes.

"Why do you bring this now?" she asked. "If the city is surrendered tomorrow, your anonymous tract will not save you. Moretus will take the stadhuis, and Parma's sergeants will hang every man who spoke against the King."

Van Hout gripped the edge of the stone table, his knuckles turning chalk-white.

"Because I will not die a cur," he snarled, a sudden, desperate flare of aristocratic pride breaking through his terror. "I signed Hendrik's warrant because I was a coward who wanted to save his sister's boys. I thought if I gave the Spanish a printer, they would spare the city. But they take the printer, and then they take the river, and then they take the gates, and at the end of it all, they hang the magistrate who served them!"

He pointed with a shaking finger at the iron chase locked on the bed of the press.

"Print it, Katrijn," the alderman pleaded, his voice cracking with tears. "Let the burgers see who sold the river boom! If we must burn, let Antwerp burn from our own fire, not from the Spanish match!"

Katrijn reached out and picked up the pigskin purse. She did not open it. She dropped it into the deep pocket of her apron, beside Hendrik's broken watch.

Van Hout reached into his pocket and drew out a small leather memorandum book, bound in black morocco leather and secured with a silver clasp. He opened it to a leaf covered in tight, nervous scribbles, holding it toward the candle flame with a shaking hand.

"Parma's headquarters at Beveren received two couriers from Madrid on Tuesday," the alderman whispered. "King Philip has rejected the petitions of the merchant bench. The King writes that he would rather rule over a desert of dead heretics than an empire of rich rebels. When the Spanish troops march through the Kipdorp gate, every church that held Protestant services will be purified with lye and holy water, and three hundred leading citizens will be delivered to the provost's gallows."

Van Hout reached into his broadcloth cloak and drew out an unsealed letter written in Italian, its parchment surface folded twice and stamped with the private seal of the Duke of Parma: three lilies surmounted by a ducal coronet. He held the document toward Katrijn with trembling fingers.

"The Italian sappers have finished the floating redoubts on the river," the alderman whispered, his face drawn and gray. "The fort of Saint Maria in the center of the current mounts sixteen forty-eight-pound culverins. They have laid thirty-two iron chains across the channels between the barges. No ship from Zeeland can pass that wall alive. If this tract does not turn the city to arms before Monday, Antwerp is lost."

Katrijn took down three dried herrings from the chimney beam, wrapping them in an unprinted corner of a broadsheet. She set the fish on the corner of the composing stone beside a heel of hard rye bread. The bread was green with mold along the crust, but Giles carved the rot away with his pocket-knife, chewing the hard bread with slow, stoic patience.

"In eighty-one," Giles muttered between bites, "we ate boiled cabbage and horse-flesh for four weeks when the French prince besieged the southern bastions. A man can endure an empty belly if he knows that the gates are barred against the butcher. But when the men who hold the keys are selling the bolts to the enemy, even a loaf of white wheat bread would taste of ditch-water."

Katrijn sat on an upturned nail keg, watching Giles chew his bread. The cold cellar air smelled of wet stone and coal smoke. Outside the brick vault, the muffled sounds of the morning watch died away into the foggy distance toward the harbor.

Giles swallowed the last crust of rye bread and took a swig of cold canal water from an earthen jug. He wiped his lips with the back of his hand, picked up his bodkin, and turned back to the galley stone.

"We have set twelve pages," she said. "The remaining four will be in lead before the morning bell. Be at the Saint Michael's water gate on Monday night with your porters, Alderman. The four hundred copies will be waiting."

# Chapter 18: The Cold Frost on the Roof

The frost fell upon Antwerp like an iron shroud during the third watch of the night.

It did not arrive with wind or blizzard; it came in the dead, breathless silence of a clear northern sky, dropping the temperature until the water in the earthen washing crocks froze solid to the bottom and the moisture in the brick walls expanded, popping the lime mortar from the ceiling joints with sharp, dry snaps like distant pistol shots.

In the coal cellar, Katrijn's breath formed thick, white clouds that hung motionless in the freezing air.

She stood beside the ink-block, holding one of the sheepskin ink-balls in her hands. The leather, which had been soft and supple twelve hours before, was hard as a frozen turnip. When she pressed her thumb into the sheepskin, the uncarded wool within did not yield; the boiled nut oil and pine soot had congealed into a stiff, gummy tar that tore from the leather in brittle, rubbery flakes.

"We cannot pull," Giles said. He stood beside the brazier, his teeth chattering so violently that his words were blurred. "The ink will not distribute. If we rock the balls across the forme, the cold tar will pull the lead sorts right out of the chase."

Katrijn touched the face of the locked type in the iron chase.

The metal was icy, coated in a microscopic sheen of white rime that had settled from their breath. If cold paper met cold lead under the platen, the ink would sit on the surface like grease on ice, smudging into an illegible smear the instant a finger touched the margins.

"Bring the charcoal kettle," she commanded.

Giles used the iron tongs to lift three glowing peat coals from the brazier, dropping them into a shallow iron dish mounted on three short legs. He placed the dish beneath the bed of the common press, directly below the iron chase holding the sixteen pages of Brevier type.

A faint, dry warmth began to rise through the oak ribs of the carriage.

Katrijn held the two ink-balls suspended six inches above the glowing coals, turning them slowly in her wrists like roasting apples on a spit. The scent of hot sheepskin and toasted wool rose into the vault, mingled with the pungent, heavy aroma of warmed walnut oil and pitch.

Slowly, the congealed paste began to soften.

The black surface lost its dull, leathery crust, taking on a rich, gleaming, liquid sheen that caught the red glow of the embers. Katrijn worked the twin balls together in a swift, rolling circle, beating them against each other with a soft, rhythmic *thwip-thwip-thwip* until the ink was distributed across both leather domes in a thin, elastic film that held its tack without running.

"The paper," she said.

From beneath the damp linen cloths on the bench, Giles drew out the first gathering of dampened Troyes rag paper.

The linen sheets were cold, stiffened by the frost into sheets that crackled like thin birch bark. Katrijn took the top sheet, laid it between two dry woolen blankets, and passed a warm soapstone iron across the cloth. The dampness within the fibers turned to gentle steam, relaxing the linen until the sheet became soft, pliable, ready to drink the warm oil from the lead.

She laid the sheet upon the tympan points.

The two brass pins pierced the paper margins with tiny, clean punctures that would ensure perfect register when the sheet was turned for the second impression. She folded down the frisket—the parchment frame cut with sixteen rectangular windows that protected the white margins from stray ink—and nodded to Giles.

"Beat the forme," she said.

Giles stepped to the press bed, holding the twin warm ink-balls.

He rocked the leather surfaces across the lead sorts with a rolling, circular motion, his shoulders moving in a practiced, muscular rhythm. *Click-roll, click-roll.* The black paste transferred from the sheepskin to the raised faces of five thousand lead characters. Under the warm charcoal heat, the letters took the ink evenly, glowing like rows of polished jet beads set in an iron frame.

Katrijn turned the wooden rounce handle.

The carriage rolled beneath the heavy oak platen with a smooth, rumbling clatter. Giles seized the ash lever with both hands, planted his clogs against the oak sill, and threw his entire weight backward.

The wooden screw groaned in its brass nut. The platen struck the tympan with a heavy, solid *thump* that reverberated through the frozen flagstones. For three seconds, Giles held the bar pinned against the cheek, his muscles straining, forcing the warm, resinous ink deep into the damp pores of the French linen rag.

"Up," Katrijn whispered.

Giles eased the bar back. The counterweight creaked on its hemp rope.

Katrijn wound the rounce, rolled the carriage out from beneath the platen, and lifted the tympan frame. With gentle, calloused fingers, she peeled the paper from the brass points and held the printed sheet up to the yellow flame of the tallow candle.

It was magnificent.

The Brevier letters stood upon the white rag paper with an astonishing, razor-sharp clarity: dense, jet-black, without a single blur, halo, or broken serif. The margins were clean, snowy white; the alignment was true to the fraction of a millimeter; the ink had bitten into the fiber so deeply that the impression could be felt on the reverse side like raised embroidery.

At the head of the page, the title stood out in bold, magnificent Great Primer lead:

*A TRUE RECORD OF THE SMASHED PRESSES.*

Katrijn looked at the print, her eyes burning with cold tears that did not fall.

Outside, above their heads, the city of Antwerp lay frozen in the grip of thirty thousand Spanish pikemen. The canals were turning to solid ice; the river was blocked by iron chains; the aldermen were preparing the instruments of surrender in the town hall.

But here, in the dark coal vault beneath the dyer's house, the dead man's press had spoken.

The cold in the vault was so intense that the tears in Katrijn's eyes froze along the rims of her eyelids. When she breathed through her nose, the membranes inside her nostrils stiffened, crackling like dry parchment. Giles had tied rags around his wooden clogs to keep his feet from sticking to the frozen flagstones, but his breath came in great, wheezing gasps that formed clouds of white frost on the oak gallows of the press.

Every twenty pulls, they had to halt the work to warm the ink-balls over the charcoal dish. If the sheepskin cooled below the temperature of fresh bread, the boiled walnut oil congealed instantly into a hard, gummy resin that refused to leave the leather, pulling the damp rag paper away from the tympan pins in torn, shredded ribbons.

The frost on the brick ceiling vault was growing thicker, forming delicate, fern-like crystals of white rime that glittered in the candlelight. Every breath they took left a white plume that lingered in the still, frozen air like sea fog. Giles's fingers were stiff with cold, the skin along his knuckles split into deep, bleeding fissures where the iron wrench had scraped the bone.

Katrijn kept the charcoal embers glowing in the iron dish beneath the press carriage. The gentle warmth rose through the oak timber, keeping the iron chase and the lead sorts warm enough to receive the ink without chilling the boiled nut oil into gummy tar. She moved with steady, relentless precision, laying sheet after sheet upon the tympan points.

The ink-stone was a slab of gray Belgian marble, three inches thick, reclaimed from the ruin of a sacked monastery chapel in sixty-six. Its surface had been ground perfectly flat with sand and water until it was smooth as ice. Giles poured two spoons of black varnish onto the center of the stone, taking the ink-balls in his hands and rocking them across the pool with a steady, rolling motion that printers had called the ball-dance for two hundred years.

The black mixture sizzled beneath the sheepskin, aerating the thick oil and spreading the pigment in a fine, uniform film no thicker than a moth's wing. When Giles lifted the leather pelt, it made a sharp sucking sound, like a boot pulling free from deep river mud. He touched the skin to the tip of his finger. The ink adhered in a tiny, tacky cone, black as pitch, without a drop of grease or oil separation. It was ready for the forme.

"The text is long, mistress," Giles whispered, wiping a bead of sweat from his scarred forehead. "If we pull three hundred sheets before sunrise, my shoulders will feel as if two Spanish pikemen had beaten me with ash poles. But the ink will bite deep into the linen. Even if Moretus scrubs the paper with lye water, the letters will remain readable to the bone."

The black ink on the marble slab was as smooth as melted butter. Giles picked up the stone muller once more, giving the pool fifty final rotations until the friction of the granite warmed the varnish to blood heat. In the warm oil, the soot particles dissolved completely, leaving no grit or dry specks to foul the delicate ascenders of the italic fount.

Katrijn inspected the ink under the lantern. It was jet black, without a trace of the brown greasy stain that cheap ink-makers left upon the paper. When this text was printed, the words would stand out against the white linen like iron nails driven into a lime-washed wall. Men would read them by the smoky light of tallow candles in the taverns of the Kammenstraat, and they would recognize the truth before the first word was finished.

The ink-stone was glossy as a mirror beneath the yellow flame of the oil dip. Giles worked the two sheepskin pelts against each other with a steady rolling motion, distributing the black paste until the surface of the leather was as uniform as a fresh coat of pitch on a ship's plank. Katrijn watched his hands move with instinctive grace. For twenty years Giles had lived in the rumble of the press room, his senses attuned to the physical resistance of metal and wood.

"Hang the sheet on the third line," Katrijn told Giles, her voice steady and proud. "We have three hundred and ninety-nine more to pull before the frost breaks."

# Chapter 19: The Watch at the Grating

Giles climbed down the ladder from the coal chute with his face white as bleached linen.

He did not speak until his boots touched the stone floor. He caught Katrijn by the sleeve of her gown, his breathing ragged, his good ear flushed dark with blood.

"Two bailiffs," he whispered, pointing up toward the ceiling. "At the corner of the dyer's house. They have iron lanterns and clubs."

Katrijn laid her composing stick on the stone. "Did they touch the grating?"

"They have a dog," Giles said. His fingers were digging into her forearm. "A Spanish mastiff on an iron chain. The dog put its nose to the iron bars and began to whine. One of the bailiffs scraped the frost from the curb with his dagger and knelt down to smell the grate."

"What did he smell?"

"The charcoal," Giles said. "The warm nut oil. You cannot hide the smell of boiling pitch in a frost like this. It rises through the chute like chimney smoke."

Katrijn walked to the ladder, climbed quietly to the second rung, and listened.

Above her head, through the brickwork, she heard the low, guttural growl of the mastiff, followed by the heavy rasp of a man's voice speaking in Flemish:

"There is fire below, Pieter. I tell you, I smell the fat."

"The dyer has been in Zeeland since Michaelmas," a second voice replied, rough and impatient. "The house is sealed by the municipal sheriff. It is only the sewer gas from the canal ditch."

"Sewer gas does not smell of warm walnut oil," the first bailiff growled. "Go to the bridge house. Tell Sergeant Mendoza to bring four men with crowbars and a lantern. If the unlicensed press is in this vault, the two hundred gold florins are ours before the morning bell."

Katrijn climbed down the ladder.

Her face was calm, her movements measured and deliberate.

"We have twenty minutes," she said.

Giles picked up the heavy iron crowbar from the press bed, his eyes wild with desperate fury. "I will go up the chute. When the bailiff kneels to look through the bars, I will drive the iron through his throat."

"The press?" he choked out. "The lead cases? Hendrik's matrices?"

"The timber can be broken," Katrijn said, her fingers closing around the willow handles of the second basket. "The lead can be melted into bullets. But four hundred copies of the truth are already in the wicker, Giles. And the truth does not burn."

Katrijn held the candle to the mouth of the sewer opening. The brick barrel vault, built two centuries before by the guild of dyers, was four feet in diameter, coated in a thick, velvety crust of black canal mold that smelled of stagnant river mud, sour vinegar, and old copperas. A narrow trickle of freezing ditch water ran down the center of the invert, carrying flecks of gray ice toward the Scheldt.

She inspected the brick archway: the mortar was old lime, crumbly at the joints, but the arch was solid, capable of supporting the weight of a creeping body without caving under the alley cobbles above.

"The tide will turn at four," she told Giles. "We must have the two hampers through the pipe and hidden beneath the brewery wharf before the water rises above the sill. If the water enters the conduit, the four hundred copies will turn to gray pulp in ten minutes."

She watched the candle gutter in its puddle of grease. The wick sputtered, sending up a thin thread of foul-smelling smoke that curled toward the blackened beams of the ceiling.

The wick of the tallow lamp hissed as a drop of condensation fell from the vaulted stone overhead. Katrijn watched the yellow flame shrink to a blue bead before flaring bright again against the dark bricks.

She pushed him toward the dark mouth of the sewer pipe, picked up the second hamper, and blew out the candle.

# Chapter 20: The Proofreader's Tear

Before she pulled the oilcloth over the second hamper, Katrijn took the final printed signature sheet and held it close to the dying embers of the brazier.

It was three o'clock in the morning.

Above them, through the frozen ground, the Spanish mastiff paced along the alley curb, its claws clicking against the frost. In the dark corner behind the coal bin, Giles was on his knees with an iron trowel, digging through the frozen mud that blocked the entrance to the sewer conduit.

Katrijn smoothed the damp paper across her knees.

Her fingers were blackened to the second knuckle with the indelible lampblack paste; her thumb was split along the nail where the brass composing stick had pressed against the flesh for twenty hours. Her eyes burned with sleeplessness and the bitter smoke of peat ash.

She read the final paragraph of the tract, set in the large, elegant Saint Augustin italics that Hendrik had bought in Antwerp before the Spanish troops closed the gates.

*And let it be recorded before Almighty God and the citizens of this afflicted town,*

*That when the magistrates fled to their country houses and the guild deacons took the gold of the foreigner to break the free word,*

*There were craftsmen who would not bow their necks to the yoke.*

*Among whom let the name of Master Hendrik Vane, printer of the Vrijdagmarkt, be held in everlasting honor and remembrance:*

*Who chose the block and the executioner's steel rather than put his master's mark to a lie,*

*And whose memory shall outlive the stones of the citadel and the names of the men who sold his blood.*

Katrijn sat in the silence of the dying vault.

A single tear—cold, heavy, clear as well water—gathered in the corner of her right eye, tracked down her soot-stained cheek, and fell upon the damp paper.

It struck the white margin, half an inch below Hendrik's name.

The drop of water soaked into the French linen rag, spreading in a tiny, circular halo that dampened the fibers without dissolving the boiled nut oil of the print. The black ink, tempered with Venetian turpentine and baked pine soot, held its bite against the moisture, as sharp and permanent as a chisel cut in granite.

She touched the wet paper with the tip of her forefinger.

Eight months of terror. Eight months of hiding in cold cellars, of paring turnip skins for Clara and Pieter, of scrubbing lead sorts with caustic lye until her skin bled, of bowing her head in the street before the Spanish sergeants who had kicked Hendrik's severed head into the market sawdust.

It was finished.

The dead man had his record. It was not carved on a marble monument in the Cathedral, where Spanish priests would deface the letters with their iron chisels; it was printed in four hundred copies of honest ink on Dutch paper, ready to be scattered into the hands of dockers, weavers, carters, and merchants across the Low Countries.

Every copy was an arrow aimed at Moretus's throat.

Every sheet was an unanswerable witness that would stand in the guildhalls and taverns long after Alexander Farnese's pikemen were rotting in the Flanders mud.

"The conduit is clear, mistress," Giles whispered from the darkness of the coal bin.

His voice was hoarse, choked with mud and coal dust. He stood in the shadow of the sewer mouth, his face smeared with black silt, holding the wicker handles of the first hamper.

Katrijn folded the proof sheet carefully, slid it into her bodice next to Hendrik's broken watch and the municipal execution warrant, and picked up the second hamper of pamphlets.

"Take the lead," she said.

She did not look back at the wooden common press standing alone in the center of the vault.

Katrijn looked at the lines of Brevier lead locked in the iron chase. In those five thousand small metal characters lay the craft that had built Antwerp: the art that Christopher Plantin had brought from France, that Dirk Martens had planted in Alost, that had turned the Low Countries into the printing house of Europe. The Duke of Parma could bring thirty thousand tercios from Spain; he could build bridges across the Scheldt; he could fill the ditches with the bodies of heretics.

But he could not kill the printed word. Once the ink was pressed into the wet rag fibers, it lived beyond the power of pikemen and inquisitors. It would cross the sea in the pockets of sailors; it would be read by candle flame in secret chambers in London, Geneva, and Amsterdam; it would bear witness against the tyrants long after the bones of the Duke of Parma were dust in the cathedral vault of Madrid.

Katrijn sat in the silence of the cellar, her hand resting upon the damp paper sheet where her tear had dried into the linen fibers. Above her head, through thirty feet of frozen ground, the city of Antwerp lay locked in the sleep of exhaustion and fear. The sentries were pacing their beats along the dikes; the aldermen were huddled over their secret ledgers in the stadhuis; the children were shivering beneath thin wool blankets in a hundred garrets.

She looked at the name of Hendrik Vane set in lead. A name was all that a craftsman left to his children: not land, not gold, not stone houses on the Meir, but the clean memory of an honest trade practiced without shame before God and man.

Katrijn gathered the proofs into an apron pocket and secured the drawstring. She looked back at the cold stone vault where Hendrik had labored for twenty years. Every scratch upon the workbenches, every ink-stain on the floorboards, every worn tooth in the rounce spoke of a lifetime spent in patient, quiet defiance against ignorance and tyranny.

"If they take the shop, Giles," she whispered, her hand resting on the oak cheek of the press, "they will break the timbers with iron sledges. They will melt the type into musket balls to shoot our neighbors on the ramparts. But they cannot melt what has already flown into the hands of the guilds. A thousand printed sheets are a thousand witnesses that no Spanish fire can burn."

Katrijn tucked the bundle of printed proofs into the leather pouch beneath her skirt. She checked the iron bolt on the cellar hatch. Outside, the freezing rain continued to fall upon the cobblestones with a steady, dreary hiss, washing away the tracks of the night carts toward the canal.

She waited in the doorway until the footsteps of the Spanish watch faded toward the river. The air in the lane tasted of frozen mud and woodsmoke, sharp enough to make her chest ache with every breath.

She climbed into the mouth of the sewer pipe, pulled the wicker basket behind her into the cold, reeking mud, and crawled forward into the frozen darkness beneath Antwerp.

# Chapter 21: The Rounce and the Tympan

The rhythm of the press took hold of their bones at four in the morning.

In a common press, printing was not a trade of thoughts; it was a brutal, relentless dance of muscle, timber, and iron. It required two bodies moving in exact synchronization, where a hesitation of two seconds spoiled a sheet of Troyes rag paper and a misaligned finger meant broken knuckles under fifty stone of descending oak platen.

Katrijn stood at the tympan frame, her apron pinned high to keep the hem clear of the ink-stone.

Giles stood opposite the bed, his feet planted wide on the damp flagstones. He held the two sheepskin ink-balls, their leather warm from the charcoal dish, working the black walnut paste across the domes with quick, circular twists of his wrists.

"Beat," Katrijn said.

Giles stepped forward. He rocked the ink-balls over the forme with a circular rolling motion, his elbows high, striking the lead sorts twice in the center and twice at the margins. *Click-slap, click-slap.* The black paste covered the sixteen pages of Brevier type in an even, velvet skin.

Katrijn laid the damp sheet onto the brass tympan points.

Her hands moved with machine-like certainty: left hand taking the corner of the rag paper from the damper pile, right hand guiding the deckle edge against the gauge mark. The cold linen settled onto the sharp brass pins with a tiny puncture. She dropped the frisket frame down over the paper, trapping the sheet against the parchment mask that shielded the white margins.

With her left hand, she folded the tympan down onto the forme.

With her right hand, she seized the wooden rounce handle and spun the crank two turns forward.

The carriage rolled beneath the platen with a heavy, wet clatter of iron wheels.

"Pull," she commanded.

Giles caught the ash lever with both hands. He threw his chest back, bending his knees, using his entire weight to drive the screw home.

The iron screw turned in the brass nut. The platen struck the tympan with a deep, shuddering blow that drove through the sole of Katrijn's clogs. For one second, two seconds, the pressure held: three thousand pounds of force squeezing the boiled walnut varnish deep into the damp rag fibers, transferring the black characters from lead to linen.

"Up," she said.

Giles eased the lever back. The counterweight creaked on its hemp line.

Katrijn spun the rounce handle two turns backward. The carriage rolled out onto the stone rails. She lifted the tympan frame, swung the frisket upward, and peeled the printed sheet from the brass pins with her calloused thumb.

She did not look at the text; she looked at the drying line suspended from the cellar ceiling.

With a long ash wood peel—the T-shaped baker's shovel of the printer—she lifted the damp sheet by its center fold and hung it over the taut hemp cord beside forty others.

The paper fluttered in the draft, smelling of fresh boiled oil, pine pitch, and river dampness.

"Again," she said.

*Beat. Lay. Roll. Pull. Up. Roll. Peel. Hang.*

One impression every twelve seconds. Five impressions a minute. Three hundred sheets an hour.

The cellar grew hot despite the freezing frost outside. Sweat poured from Giles's neck, soaking through his coarse linen smock until the cloth stuck to his shoulder blades like wet skin. His breath came in heavy, rhythmic gasps through his parted teeth, his broken fingers locking around the ash lever with mechanical fury. On his left temple, the purple scar where the bailiff's iron bar had crushed his ear drum stood out swollen and dark.

Katrijn's back burned as if an iron rod were pressed against her spine.

Her knuckles were swollen, red and raw from the cold paper and the sharp edges of the lead chase. Every time she lifted the tympan frame, the muscles across her shoulders screamed with fatigue. But she did not slow. If they stopped to rest, the ink on the ink-block would chill in the cellar air, turning stiff and gummy; if the paper dried on the pile, the fibers would refuse the impression and the text would blur into a gray, illegible smudge.

They were at sheet one hundred and eighty.

The drying cords beneath the vaulted ceiling were filling rapidly, draped in white linen octavos that hung like ghost banners in the dim candlelight. Every sheet bore the indictment: the names of the aldermen, the sums paid by the Spanish treasury, the betrayal of Hendrik Vane on the Vrijdagmarkt.

Giles paused for half a second between pulls, wiping his forehead with his soot-stained forearm.

"The rounce is stiffening," he wheezed. "The iron gudgeons on the carriage are eating into the oak rails."

Katrijn reached for the earthenware pot of rancid hog fat on the shelf, scooped a gob of grease with two fingers, and smeared it along the grooves of the wooden ribs.

"Pull," she said.

Every twenty pulls, Katrijn wiped the brass tympan points with an oiled rag to prevent the buildup of damp paper fibers. If the points became clogged with wet lint, they would tear the sheet as it was lifted, leaving ragged, fuzzy holes in the paper margin that would ruin the register when the second side was backed up.

The ink-block was a flat slab of polished Belgian slate, four inches thick, bedded into a heavy wooden frame against the south wall. Giles kept his palette knife moving across the stone, spreading the walnut varnish in thin, glossy waves, ensuring that no crust formed along the edges where the cold air crept down from the coal chute. His smock was soaked through with sweat, black soot running down his neck in dark channels.

The ink-balls bounced against the forme with a steady, rhythmic cadence like the heartbeat of an iron beast. Giles worked the twin sheepskin pads in alternating circles, his shoulders rolling with muscular precision, transferring the black walnut varnish from the slate block to the lead letters without a single splash on the timber bed.

Katrijn watched the brass pins of the tympan pierce the clean rag paper. With every sheet she laid, the stack on the damper pile grew smaller, while the white banners hanging from the drying cords multiplied across the cellar vault. The smell of fresh boiled oil and damp linen rose thick in the enclosed space, warm and heavy against the freezing cellar draft.

The ink-pelts smelled of stale lye and tallow. Giles took a horn comb from his apron pocket and scraped the wool fleece through the neck opening of the ball-stock, teasing out the hardened clumps of dried varnish that had formed during the night. A good ink-ball was responsive to the touch. If the stuffing bunched into hard knots beneath the leather, the pressure against the form would be uneven, leaving light spots in the center of the broadsheet and thick black blurs at the margins.

Katrijn watched the shadows lengthen across the lime-washed bricks. Through the small iron grate at street level, she could see the heavy wooden wheels of the artillery wagons rumbling toward the river dikes. The King's forces were digging saps through the polders at Calloo, draining the water meadows with iron shovels so that the Spanish heavy horse could charge across the frozen turf. Every rumble of the wagon wheels vibrated through the foundation of the house, setting the lead sorts rattling softly inside the wooden cases like dice in a leather cup.

Katrijn looked at the iron composing stick in her hand. The lead sorts felt warm from the friction of her fingers. On the street above, an artillery wagon rumbled over the bridge, its iron rims shaking the foundations of the warehouse until the dust fell from the rafters like dry snow.

Katrijn checked the level of the oil lamp. The reservoir was half full of dark rapeseed oil, smelling of scorched seeds and iron soot. In three hours the oil would be spent, leaving them to compose by the smoky wick of a tallow dip.

Giles spat into his palms, gripped the ash lever, and threw his weight against the wood.

# Chapter 22: The Cracked Spindle

The sound was not a crack; it was a screech of dry iron grinding against brass.

Giles braced his good shoulder against the iron screw, heaving upward with all his remaining strength to relieve the pressure on the brass nut.

Katrijn lifted the heavy maul with both hands.

She swung the mallet in the cramped cellar vault, striking the oak wedge with a solid, thunderous blow: *CLANG.*

The wedge sank an inch into the split. The timber groaned, resisting the iron band, but the fracture held. She struck again, driving the wedge home until the split cheek compressed back into the mortise joint, forcing the iron screw back into true vertical alignment.

She reached down, seized the ash lever, and pulled.

The screw turned. The iron threads bit into the brass box with a stiff, heavy groan, but they held. The platen leveled itself above the bed, hanging square and true once more.

Giles wiped the coal soot from his mouth with the back of his hand. He stepped to the bed, took the twin ink-balls, and worked them over the charcoal embers.

"The timber has taken the wedge," Giles grunted, leaning his shoulder against the upright gallows. "The grain is tight. It will hold for the final signature if we do not throw our weight too hard against the bar."

She checked the catch on the sliding timber tray beneath the stone bed. The iron bolt was greased with mutton suet, sliding into its notched housing without a click.

Katrijn slid the brass bodkin into her hair to keep the curls from her eyes. The metal was cold against her scalp. She checked the alignment of the wooden quoins with a light tap of her knuckles.

"Sheet two hundred and forty-one," he said.

# Chapter 23: The Children's Lookout

The linen thread was tied around Katrijn's left wrist.

It was a piece of strong, three-ply bookbinder's twine, waxed with beeswax and run through a small iron staple in the floorboards beneath the coal chute. From there, it traveled thirty feet upward through the dark chimney casing into the garret above the kitchen, where eight-year-old Clara sat on a three-legged stool in the freezing cold.

The rule was simple.

If a single lantern passed the alley mouth, one gentle tug: the watch was on the quayside.

If soldiers halted at the dyer's stoop, three sharp jerks: the bailiffs were at the door.

A single tug.

She froze with a sheet suspended between her fingers. Giles stopped the ink-balls two inches above the forme, holding his breath in the gloom.

Each stroke was one more loaf of bread, one more cartload of dry peat from the bogs, one more morning that the black-coat men could not take them.

In the dark garret, Clara held the linen thread between her fingertips, her breath forming tiny clouds of frost against the rough roof rafters. # Chapter 24: The Alderman's Panic

# Chapter 27: Folding the Octavos

The folding of the sheets had taken place during the fifth hour of the night, in the breathless silence before the weavers rioted on the Sint-Jansvliet.

It was the binder's craft: the transformation of flat, printed linen rag into the portable, pocket-sized weapon known as the octavo.

A broadsheet was a proclamation; it was nailed to church portals and town hall pillars where any bailiff with a halberd could tear it down with a single stroke. But an octavo was a conspirator's book. It was sixteen pages printed upon a single sheet of paper, folded three times upon itself, cut at the edges, and stitched through the spine with waxed thread so that it could be slipped inside a workman's woolen sleeve, concealed in a woman's bodice, or tucked into the lining of a merchant's boot without making a bulge.

Katrijn sat on the low stool before the pine bench, holding the bone folder in her right hand.

The tool was carved from the rib bone of an ox, polished glass-smooth by thirty years of handling, its edges thin and rounded so that it could crease the damp paper without tearing the fragile linen fibers.

Giles stood beside her, taking the damp sheets from the drying lines.

He laid the first sheet flat upon the pine timber, the print facing upward: signature A, containing the title page, the preface, and the opening four articles of the indictment.

"First fold," Katrijn said.

Her hands moved with the swift, rhythmic economy of long habit.

She took the right-hand margin of the sheet with her left thumb, brought it over to meet the left-hand margin, aligning the folio numbers at the foot of the pages with absolute precision. She held the edges together with her left fingers, took the bone folder in her right hand, and drew the polished edge down the center fold with a firm, decisive stroke: *Zzzzp.*

The paper creased with a clean, sharp snap. The sheet was now a quarto of four leaves.

"Second fold."

She turned the folded paper ninety degrees, brought the top edge down to meet the bottom edge, checking that the running titles at the top of the columns aligned to the thickness of a thread. Again the bone folder swept across the fold: *Zzzzp.*

An octavo of eight leaves.

"Third fold."

One final turn, folding the sheet in half once more along its vertical axis, followed by a hard, double-pass of the bone folder across the back. *Zzzzp-zzzp.*

Sixteen pages.

The sheet was now a compact, dense rectangle of paper, six inches high by four inches wide, perfectly aligned, ready for the binder's needle.

Clara had sat at the end of the bench, her small fingers holding the awl.

"No bailiff will put his fingers through two inches of pickled herring," Giles muttered, wiping his greasy hands on his smock. "The proctors have velvet doublets. They will not soil their cuffs for a fish cart."

"Moretus does not soil his cuffs," Katrijn said, lashing the wicker lid with hemp twine. "He has men who eat herring heads for breakfast and pick their teeth with rusty nails. If they stop us on the ice, let them take the top fish. Tell them we are taking the keg to the carters at the Saint Jans redoubt."

She checked the knots on the timber sledge.

The sledge was built of rough ash poles, five feet long, with two curved runners shod with strips of hoop iron that Hendrik had forged to haul paper reams from the canal barges. On the flat bed of the sledge, the two wicker hampers sat lashed side by side, covered with a filthy, frozen horse blanket that smelled of stale manure and wet straw.

To anyone looking down from the quayside cranes, it was nothing more than an ordinary fish-sledge: the daily, miserable traffic of two starving artisans dragging food from the river warehouses to the city markets.

Katrijn pulled her woolen hood low over her forehead, concealing her face in shadow.

Beside her, Giles took the leather hauling strap, looped it across his chest over his right shoulder, and gripped the crossbar with his scarred hands.

"The tide is on the turn," Giles said, squinting through the river fog toward the middle of the Scheldt. "The ice will be groaning on the channel shoals in an hour."

"Then we walk now," Katrijn said.

Giles adjusted the ash crossbars of the timber sledge, checking that the iron runners were seated true in their wooden mortises. The sledge was low, flat, built to slide smoothly over river ice and packed snow without tipping when dragged through the pressure ridges off the quayside.

The smell of the Portugal sea salt was sharp enough to make their nostrils sting in the freezing air. The gray crystals were encrusted around the willow twigs of the baskets, forming a white, crystalline frost that looked completely natural to any harbor watchman inspecting the morning carts.

The salt herrings were layered two deep across the tarred sailcloth, their gray scales glistening with frozen brine in the dim light beneath the wharf pilings. Giles packed the coarse Portugal rock salt into every corner of the baskets, until the wicker weave was completely hidden beneath a crust of white crystals that looked like river frost.

Katrijn pulled the hemp cords tight across the wicker lids, tying them off in double reef knots that would not slip when the sledge bumped over the pressure ridges of the frozen Scheldt.

Giles picked up an iron crowbar and wedged the beveled tip beneath the stone hearth. He pulled back with the full weight of his muscular torso, levering the heavy sandstone slab upward two inches until the hollow space beneath the floorboards was exposed. Inside the dark recess lay two pine boxes bound with iron straps, containing Hendrik's finest founts of roman and italic type, packed in dry oats to keep them from rusting in the cellar damp.

Giles replaced the stone slab over the secret cavity beneath the hearth, sweeping dry ashes across the seam with an old broom of birch twigs until the joint was invisible under the gray dust.

She stepped out from the shelter of the timber pilings onto the open ice of the canal basin, her wooden pattens gripping the crusted snow, her right hand resting on the rear crossbar of the sledge to steady the load.

# Chapter 29: The Frozen Canal

The Scheldt was a desert of gray ice.

In ordinary winters, the great river of Antwerp was a highway of three hundred masts: Dutch grain hoys from Zeeland, English merchantmen with broadcloth from London, Portuguese caravels laden with spices and Brazilian sugar, their colorful pennants snapping in the westerly wind.

Today, under the iron grip of the November frost and the Spanish blockade, the river lay dead.

From the quayside wharves of Antwerp across to the low, flooded dikes of the Flanders shore, the water was frozen two feet deep: a jagged, desolate waste of pressure ridges, tilted ice floes, and frozen slush that creaked and groaned with a hollow, subterranean rumble whenever the ocean tide pushed beneath the shelf.

Fog hung low over the ice, gray and freezing, muffling the sound of the church bells that began to toll the six o'clock morning bell from the Cathedral tower.

Katrijn and Giles moved across the ice in silence.

The iron runners of the sledge scraped over the crusted snow with a steady, whispering hiss: *hiss-shhh, hiss-shhh.* Giles leaned into the leather chest strap, his clogs finding purchase in the frozen ruts left by earlier carts, his breath coming in white plumes that froze into ice crystals on his collar.

Behind them, the stepped gables and church spires of Antwerp rose out of the mist like a city of stone ghosts.

To their left, three hundred paces down the channel, the dark silhouette of the Spanish pontoon bridge loomed through the fog.

Even from this distance, the scale of Alexander Farnese's siege works was terrifying. Thirty-two armed river ketches sat locked in the ice, their masts stripped of sail, connected by massive timber beams and iron chains that formed a floating wall eight feet high across the entire channel. At each end of the stockade, an earthen redoubt bristled with brass cannons; and in the middle of the river, mounted on an artificial island of sunken barges, rose the great wooden fort of Saint Maria, its watchtowers commanding every inch of the frozen current.

Katrijn kept her eyes fixed on the Crane Wharf ahead.

Under the curfew orders, all traffic across the ice was forbidden without a military pass from the Spanish provost. But between the Sint-Jansvliet basin and the docks, a natural depression in the riverbed had created a hidden channel—a "silt-gut"—where the winter ice had cracked and refrozen into a sunken road beneath the high stone walls of the river warehouses.

If they stayed in the gut, they were invisible to the sentries on the pontoon bridge; but if they were trapped in the cut by a patrol from the quayside, there was no escape through the vertical stone bulkheads.

"Hold the runners," Giles muttered, halting in his tracks.

Katrijn stopped, her hand tightening on the rear crossbar of the sledge.

Through the drifting fog, fifty yards ahead where the sunken channel opened onto the mudflats of the Crane Wharf, she heard the sound of iron boots.

It was not the heavy march of common soldiers; it was the light, rapid stride of men wearing fine leather shoes, accompanied by the distinctive, rattling clatter of guild staves and the wet panting of a hound.

A lantern beam flickered through the mist.

"Moretus," Katrijn whispered, her heart turning to stone in her breast.

"We cannot turn the sledge," Giles rasped, his eyes darting to the ten-foot ice ridges that walled the gut on both sides. "The runners will jam in the pressure cracks."

"Do not turn," Katrijn commanded, her voice dropping into a flat, steady calm that astonished her own ears. "Walk straight forward. Keep your head down. If they speak, let me answer."

She reached beneath her woolen shawl, found the hilt of the kitchen knife tucked into her apron string, and loosened the steel in its sheath.

The ice beneath their clogs was thick, scarred by the heavy iron runners of the peat sledges and the hooves of the artillery horses that had hauled cannons to the river batteries. In the middle of the channel, where the ocean tide pushed beneath the frozen crust, the ice shelf groaned with a deep, hollow booming that echoed from the stone bulkheads of the quayside.

Katrijn looked toward the Crane Wharf. The three massive timber cranes stood against the gray dawn sky like gallows trees, their iron pulleys dripping long icicles that reached toward the frozen silt below.

The river ice creaked beneath the sledge runners with a deep, hollow resonance that vibrated through the soles of Katrijn's clogs. In the center of the Scheldt, where the flood tide pushed against the frozen barrier, great blocks of green ice had buckled upward into jagged walls four feet high, dusted with fine powdery snow that blew in from the North Sea.

Giles leaned into the hauling traces, his breath coming in rhythmic puffs of steam, his scarred hands gripping the crossbar with unyielding strength as they navigated the narrow, winding channel of the silt-gut toward the Crane Wharf.

The handcart groaned over the icy ruts of the Sint-Jansvliet. Giles pulled the hauling traces tight across his chest, his wooden clogs slipping on the frozen horse manure that littered the gutter. The fog was freezing as it settled, coating the timber gables and the iron shop-signs with a brittle crust of white rime.

Katrijn walked two paces ahead, her eyes scanning the dark entries of the dye-houses. A stray cat darted from beneath a brewer's dray, its eyes glowing green in the dim light of a street shrine. She held her breath, watching the iron lantern of the night watchman swing three streets away along the canal wall. The watchman cried the fourth hour of the night, his chant muffled by the freezing mist: *Four of the clock and all quiet in the town.*

Giles pulled the handcart into the shadow of an arched stone portal. His chest heaved with deep, ragged breaths of cold air. Katrijn waited beside him, watching the dark canal water lap against the slime-covered stairs. A dead dog floated past the embankment, bloated and stiff with winter ice.

The iron wheels of the handcart clicked against the curbstone as Giles guided the shafts through the narrow alley. The freezing mist clung to their wool cloaks in a fine mesh of icy diamonds.

They pushed the sledge forward into the widening mist.

# Chapter 30: The Ambush on the Ice

Inspector Balthasar Moretus stood at the mouth of the ice channel, his crimson marten-lined cloak wrapped tight against the freezing river wind.

Beside him stood four armed beadles of the Stationers' Guild, their woolen tabards belted over rusted iron breastplates, each holding a six-foot ash partisan with a curved billhook blade. A fifth man—a burly sergeant of the Spanish garrison with an iron morion helmet—held the chain of the brindled mastiff, which strained toward the sledge, its black lips drawn back over yellow fangs, barking with a deep, echoing roar that bounced off the granite walls of the Crane Wharf.

The sledge came to a halt ten paces from the proctor.

Giles stood in the hauling traces, his head lowered, his hands resting on the ash crossbar, his breathing slow and heavy.

Moretus did not move. He carried his silver pomander ball in his left hand, holding it to his nostrils against the stench of the river silt. His hazel eyes swept over the sledge, taking in the dirty horse blanket, the two wicker hampers, and the two shivering figures standing in the gray dawn mist.

"A diligent widow," Moretus said, his purring voice carrying clearly across the silent ice. "To haul fish across the frozen Scheldt before the morning bell has finished tolling. I did not know the Saint Elizabeth hospital bought its pickled herrings from the river mud, Mistress Vane."

Katrijn stepped forward from the rear of the sledge, curtsying on the rough ice.

"The hospital has forty sick carters in the wards, Master Proctor," she said, her voice steady and deferential. "The fish stalls on the Meir were cleared by the soldiers yesterday. We bought two hampers from the Zeeland hoy that sits locked in the ice off the salt meadow."

"From a Zeeland hoy?" Moretus smiled, stepping slowly forward, his fine leather shoes crunching in the crusted snow. "A Zeeland hoy that slipped through the Duke of Parma's blockade in the dark? How remarkable that a starving printer's widow should find an enemy barge when thirty-two Spanish gunboats could see nothing."

He stopped beside the first wicker hamper.

He lifted his ivory walking cane, using the silver tip to push the filthy horse blanket aside, revealing the willow weave of the basket beneath.

"The dyer's cellar was empty when my sergeants kicked the door down an hour ago," Moretus remarked, looking at her with cold, mocking amusement. "The press was gone. The cases were overturned; the lead sorts were scattered into the coal bin. But my men found two things of great interest upon the stone, Katrijn."

He reached into his pocket and drew out two objects.

The first was a scrap of proof paper, torn from the margin of the eighth page, stained with a single fingerprint of black lampblack ink.

The second was a small iron-bound tin box.

Katrijn's breath caught in her throat. Her hand involuntarily touched her apron pocket, finding it empty. In the panic of the sewer escape, she had left the box on the stone behind the ink-block.

"Your husband's watch," Moretus said softly, dangling the silver case by its broken brass chain. "And the copy of his execution warrant. Signed by our good friend, Alderman Lucas Van Hout."

He dropped the watch into his velvet pocket with a contemptuous click.

"The alderman is already in the irons at the stadhuis," Moretus continued, his voice dropping into a harsh, triumphant rasp. "He confessed everything when the provost showed him the thumbscrews in the council cellar. He named the press. He named the widow. He named the boy who dragged the lead through the sewers."

The proctor turned to the four beadles.

"Cut the ropes," he commanded. "Let us see what kind of fish Master Hendrik Vane's widow carries to the hospital."

One of the beadles stepped forward with a broad butcher's knife, slitting the hemp lashings that bound the first hamper to the sledge crossbars. He seized the willow lid and ripped it open.

The gray, salt-crusted herrings spilled across the crusted snow, slumping onto the ice in a foul, greasy heap.

The beadle plunged his hand into the basket, digging through the remaining fish until his fingers struck the hard, flat surface of the tarred sailcloth beneath. He drew his knife across the canvas with a violent, tearing slash.

The white edges of four hundred folded octavo pamphlets sprang out through the severed cloth like white teeth.

Moretus reached into the opening with his gloved fingers.

He pulled out a single sixteen-page pamphlet, turned it to the gray dawn light, and read the bold Great Primer letters on the title page:

*A TRUE RECORD OF THE SMASHED PRESSES.*
*Wherein the Betrayal of Antwerp is Laid Open to the Commons.*

The proctor's face went white.

The smooth, purring arrogance vanished from his features, replaced by a sudden, violent rage that distorted his thin lips into a snarl. His hazel eyes moved down the first page, scanning the numbered articles until they stopped at Article III: his own name, set down in razor-sharp Brevier lead, detailing the four hundred gold ducats he had drawn from the Spanish paymaster at the Italian Bourse.

"You printed this," Moretus whispered, his voice trembling with fury.

He crumpled the pamphlet in his fist, crushing the linen paper into a tight ball, and tossed it into the snow at Katrijn's feet.

"You miserable, heretic gutter-bitch," he snarled, stepping toward her with his walking cane raised to strike. "You dare set my name in lead? You dare accuse the sworn officers of the King's guild before the commons?"

Katrijn did not move. She did not flinch from the raised cane.

She stood upright on the frozen Scheldt, her hands resting flat against her woolen apron, looking directly into the face of the man who had ordered her husband's death.

"The letters are straight, Master Proctor," she said, her voice ringing clear and cold as an iron bell across the river ice. "There is not a turned sort in the entire fount."

Moretus lowered his cane, his chest heaving, his fingers twitching with venomous rage.

"Seize them," he shouted to the Spanish sergeant and the beadles. "Tie their hands with the hauling ropes! Drag them to the Red Gate! I will have the executioner build the fire on the Grote Markt before the noon bell rings!"

Inspector Moretus stood motionless on the ice, his hand resting on the hilt of his ivory cane, his hazel eyes fixed upon Katrijn's face. The freezing river wind whipped the hem of his crimson cloak around his boots, but he seemed not to feel the cold. His lips were drawn back in a thin, pale sneer that showed his small, sharp teeth.

"You thought yourself clever, Mistress Vane," the proctor murmured, his voice silky with malice. "To hide your dead husband's press in a dyer's coal hole. Did you think the Stationers' Guild would forget the man who printed the psalms? Did you think thirty pieces of Van Hout's silver would buy you a free passage across the river?"

The four guild beadles stood in the snow with their ash partisans leveled at Giles's chest, their faces pale beneath their iron morions. They had recognized Katrijn; they had seen the dead printer's widow standing on the ice with four hundred copies of the truth at her feet, and they knew that every word on those printed pages was an accusation that carried the penalty of the rope.

Moretus took a step closer, his kidskin gloves trembling with uncontrollable fury. His gaze dropped to the open pamphlet lying in the snow, where his own name stood out in bold, sharp Brevier lead: *Master Balthasar Moretus, Proctor of the Stationers' Guild, four hundred gold ducats drawn from the Spanish paymaster.*

The cellar beneath the dyer's warehouse was colder than the street. Frost had formed on the stone vaulting, sparkling like salt in the candlelight. Giles set the heavy oak base of the press onto two square balks of seasoned pine, leveling the frame with small wooden wedges driven home by taps from an iron hammer. The timber stood true and plumb, its massive uprights capable of resisting the immense torque of the hand lever.

Katrijn unpacked the paper reams from the bottom of the fish hampers. The sheets were cold to the touch, the linen fibers crisp and dry. She laid twelve sheets at a time onto a clean wool blanket, spraying them with a fine mist of clean rainwater from a brass syringe to soften the sizing before printing. If the paper was printed dry, the ink would sit upon the surface like soot on glass, rubbing off on the reader's thumb; if it was properly dampened, the lead face would press deep into the linen fibers, embedding the word into the very heart of the rag.

"The proclamation is set in twenty-four-point capitals, mistress," Giles whispered, pointing to the copper galley on the bench. "The letters are tall enough to be read from across a tavern table. When the weavers gather at the Meat Shambles tomorrow morning, they will see the signatures of the traitors before the guild wardens can pull the placards down."

"Let them read it," Katrijn said, her fingers smoothing the damp linen against the sheepskin tympan. "Let every mother who buried a child of the hunger fever see how the aldermen sold our bread to the Spanish commander for gold florins. When the morning comes, Antwerp will know who opened the gates."

The cellar floor was paved with blue Tournai flagstones, cold and slippery with condensation. Giles adjusted the wooden stays of the press, driving two iron spikes into the joints between the paving stones to hold the frame rigid against the torque of the lever. The oak timbers groaned under the tension, settling into place with a sharp, dry snap.

Katrijn opened a dry pine chest and lifted out the ink-pelts. She had greased the sheepskin with neat's-foot oil before leaving the Sint-Jansvliet, wrapping the balls in damp linen cloths to keep the leather soft. She pressed her thumb into the fleece. The wool yielded with an elastic resilience that showed the stuffing had remained warm inside the packing. She picked up the wooden ball-stock and handed it to Giles, who wiped his palms on his leather apron and took his place before the inking stone.

"The proclamation will be printed by dawn," Katrijn said, her voice quiet in the hollow vault. "Three hundred copies. One for every guildhall, one for every church porch, and one for every tavern on the quays. When the morning tide turns, the town council will have to choose between the Spanish gold and the vengeance of the weavers."

Giles drove two more oak wedges beneath the foot of the press frame, testing the stability of the timber with his heavy fist. The massive uprights stood firm as the trunk of an old churchyard oak. Katrijn arranged the composing rules upon the stone shelf, laying each brass strip in order of length. The cellar was cold and damp, but the iron stove was beginning to radiate a faint warmth from the burning peat turf.

Giles picked up an iron oil-can and lubricated the bronze bushings of the rounce spindle with three drops of refined lard oil. The gears engaged without a sound, sliding on the seasoned beechwood track with the smooth, silent glide of a boat entering calm water. Katrijn laid out the fresh sheets on the dampening cloth, ready for the platen.

The four beadles stepped forward, their halberd points leveled at Giles's chest, as the Spanish sergeant unlocked the chain of the mastiff.

# Chapter 31: The Confrontation on the Silt

The Spanish mastiff lunged on its iron chain, its claws scrabbling on the gray river ice, its throat raw with savage barking.

Katrijn did not take a step back.

She bent down, picked up one of the fresh octavo pamphlets from the severed hamper, and held it upright in her right hand. The morning breeze off the Scheldt caught the sixteen pages of Dutch rag paper, fluttering the margins with a dry, sharp rustle like iron foil.

Behind Moretus's beadles, across the frozen silt of the Crane Wharf, twenty men were descending the timber stairs from the quayside.

They were harbor dockers, grain shovelers, and ship caulkers in grease-stained woolen jerkins, carrying their heavy timber-hooks, iron pry-bars, and pitch ladles. They had heard the barking of the hound across the silent river basin; they had seen the four guild partisans leveled on the ice; and they had seen the woman in the black widow's bonnet standing alone before the proctor.

"Let the dog go, Master Moretus," Katrijn said, her voice clear and cutting through the frost. "Let the dog tear the throat of a printer's widow on the public silt of Antwerp. There are thirty dockers behind your back who watched Hendrik die on the Grote Markt. Let them see what the Stationers' Guild does to a woman who carries paper."

Moretus did not turn his head, but his jaw hardened.

He raised his hand. The Spanish sergeant jerked the chain, hauling the mastiff back until the beast sat choking in the snow, its red tongue dripping slobber onto the ice.

"You have no standing here, Katrijn," Moretus hissed, his voice trembling with contained venom. "You are an outlaw. A harborer of sedition. The provost's writ gives me the right to hang you from the yardarm of the crane without a magistrate's seal."

"Read the fifth page, Proctor," Katrijn answered. She extended her hand, holding the open pamphlet toward him. "Read Article III to the dockers. Tell them how many gold ducats you drew from the Spanish paymaster at the Italian Bourse on the fourteenth of May. Tell them who bought the iron chains that lock the river at Calloo while their children eat boiled weeds in the cellar."

A tall, broad-shouldered man in a tarred leather apron stepped forward from the advancing crowd of dockers.

He had gray hair tied with a hemp cord, his face deeply scarred by thirty years of handling river cables. In his right hand he carried an iron cargo hook sixteen inches long, its point honed to a razor edge.

It was Master Dirck, the head porter of the Crane Wharf.

He stopped three paces from Moretus's beadles, his cold blue eyes moving from the proctor's marten fur to the white pamphlets scattered across the ice.

"What paper is that, mistress?" the porter asked, his voice low as grinding granite.

Master Dirck stepped closer to the sledge, his boots crunching in the crusted snow. He reached into the broken hamper, lifted a handful of the white octavos, and spread them like playing cards across his leather palm. The black Brevier ink stood out sharp against the gray river light: *A True Record of the Smashed Presses.*

"I have known this woman since she was an apprentice girl in her father's shop on the Kammenstraat," Dirck shouted to the dockers behind him, his voice rolling across the ice like thunder. "She has buried her husband in the ditch, and she has kept her children on turnip peels while the aldermen drank malmsey wine with the Spanish colonel. If any man lays a finger on her smock, he will answer to forty iron hooks from the Crane Wharf."

Moretus's beadles looked at the wall of dockers advancing across the ice, their eyes darting from Master Dirck's iron cargo hook to the dark water of the open river channel behind them. They were four men armed with ceremonial staves and halberds, facing seventy harbor workmen whose hands were hardened by a lifetime of handling heavy timber and stone.

"Put down the partisans, boys," Master Dirck warned, his voice low and steady as grinding millstones. "You are the sons of Antwerp weavers and cobblers. Do you want your mothers to see your bodies floating in the Scheldt for the sake of four hundred Spanish ducats?"

Katrijn dipped a clean rag into cold river water and wiped the face of the brass rule. A compositor's fingers grew numb after three hours in an unheated room. When the frost got into the joints of the thumb, a worker would mistake an *n* for an *u* or set a letter upside down without feeling the notch on the nick. She blew warm breath onto her reddened fingers, rubbing them against the coarse wool of her kirtle to restore the circulation before lifting the next line into the stick.

"The truth of Antwerp, Master Dirck," Katrijn said, turning to face the dockers. "The record of who sold the bread from your mouths."

# Chapter 32: The Voice of the Dockers

Master Dirck reached down with his left hand, picked up one of the printed octavos from the snow, and shook the gray salt crystals from the cover.

The four guild beadles raised their ash partisans, their billhook blades gleaming in the pale morning light, but they did not step forward. Forty dockers, boatmen, and smiths were gathering in a dense semicircle on the ice behind the porter, their heavy iron tools resting against their thighs, their breath forming a thick, gray cloud of steam in the freezing air.

"Step back, porter," Moretus commanded, his voice sharp with aristocratic authority. "That paper is high treason against the King. Whosoever touches it falls under the imperial ban."

Dirck did not step back. He looked down at the title page, then turned the first leaf with his calloused, salt-chapped thumb.

"I have no Latin, Master Proctor," the porter said, his voice flat and dangerous. "I am an ignorant docker who has unloaded grain for twenty years at this wharf. But my brother Jan is a sworn ship's carpenter who can read a bill of lading as well as any clerk in the stadhuis."

He turned, tossing the pamphlet across the heads of the crowd to a lean, middle-aged carpenter who stood by the timber crane with a broad axe resting on his shoulder.

The carpenter caught the paper against his chest.

He took his spectacles from a tin case in his pocket, hooked the brass wire loops behind his ears, and stepped onto the lower crossbeam of the crane platform so his head rose five feet above the crowd.

The silence on the frozen Scheldt was total.

Even the mastiff ceased its growling, sensing the sudden, lethal tension that held fifty armed working men motionless upon the ice.

The carpenter cleared his throat, spat into the snow, and began to read aloud from Article III:

"That upon the fourteenth day of May, Master Balthasar Moretus, Proctor of the Stationers' Guild, did receive at the house of the Spanish factor the sum of four hundred gold ducats, under covenant that no tract or song touching upon the closure of the Scheldt should be permitted impression within this town."

A deep, guttural growl rose from the throat of forty dockers.

"Go on, Jan!" a carter shouted from the rear, shaking an iron pry-bar. "Read the rest!"

The carpenter turned to Article IV, his voice rising, carrying with trumpet clarity across the gray ice to the walls of the river warehouses:

"That pursuant to said covenant, the said Moretus, accompanied by twelve armed bailiffs and six German mercenaries, did violently enter the dwelling of Master Hendrik Vane upon the Vrijdagmarkt, did smash his common press with iron sledges, and did deliver the said Vane to the provost marshal under false accusation of heresy, to the end that the closure of the river should remain hidden from the burgers!"

The crowd surged forward two paces.

The four guild beadles took a hurried step backward, their halberd shafts trembling in their gloved hands, their faces going pale beneath their iron morions.

"Lies!" Moretus shrieked, his voice cracking into a high, furious screech. "Rebel lies written by a disgraced alderman! I serve the King's council! I serve the peace of Antwerp!"

"You serve the Spanish garrison, you thieving dog!" Master Dirck roared, taking a step toward the proctor, his iron cargo hook raised level with Moretus's throat. "Hendrik Vane printed the water charters that kept our children fed when Alba tried to tax the herring fleet! You broke his press with sledges, and you took four hundred Spanish ducats to sell the river!"

"Seize him!" Moretus screamed to the Spanish sergeant. "Fire upon them!"

The Spanish sergeant looked at the forty dockers. He looked at their iron hooks, their sledgehammers, their caulking axes. He looked at his single matchlock musket, its slow-match smoldering in the freezing mist.

The ship's carpenter turned the second leaf of the pamphlet, his spectacles gleaming in the pale morning sun. Around the crane platform, seventy men stood in absolute silence, listening to the itemized list of bribes: four hundred ducats for Moretus, six hundred for the Burgomaster, three hundred for the Warden of the Redoubt.

"And Article XII!" the carpenter shouted, his voice ringing against the stone walls of the river warehouses. "That the grain hoys from Holland were halted at the boom by order of the secret council, to the end that the price of rye should rise to eight guilders a bushel, yielding a profit of forty thousand florins to the magistrates who held the grain reserves!"

The ship's carpenter turned the third leaf of the pamphlet, holding it toward the rising sun so the light illuminated the bold Brevier type. His voice rang out with the rhythmic cadence of an elder reading the psalms in church, carrying the names of the traitors across the frozen basin to the ears of two hundred citizens who were crowding the quayside balustrade.

"And Article XVII!" he shouted, his arm raised toward the stadhuis towers in the distance. "That on the second day of August, Master Hendrik Vane was executed on the Grote Markt, not for any heresy against Christ, but because he refused to surrender his matrices to the proctors who had sold the city gates to the foreigner!"

Moretus pulled a folded parchment from his embroidered sleeve and laid it upon the table. The seal was red Spanish wax, impressed with the twin pillars and the motto of Emperor Charles V. His fingernails were clean and square, trimmed short in the courtly style of Brussels.

"I offer you safe conduct across the lines, Van Hout," Moretus whispered, leaning forward so that his breath brushed the alderman's cheek. "A barge with eight oarsmen, manned by Walloon sailors who know the secret cuts through the drowned marshes of Saftingen. Before dawn, you could be in Bergen op Zoom, far beyond the reach of Parma's guns. You have sixty thousand guilders in bills of exchange deposited with the Fuggers in Augsburg. Why stay in this doomed city to starve on dried horsehide? Surrender the names of the underground compositors, and the barge is yours."

Van Hout looked down at the red seal. His heart was hammering against his ribs like a bird caught in a chimney flue. He remembered the smell of the wet peat in Katrijn's cellar, the resolute silence of the deaf pressman, and the small hands of the children wrapping sheets in greasy oilcloth. If he gave Moretus the street address, the red wax on the parchment would be paid for in children's blood.

Van Hout looked down at the parchment passport on the table. The wax seal was cold and brittle beneath his fingertips. He could hear the sound of the wind rattling the heavy leaded casements of the town hall, whistling through the stone gargoyles like an angry flute.

"You offer me flight, Moretus," the alderman said, his voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "You offer me an exile's bread in Germany, paid for with the lives of the apprentices who trusted my word. I have been a coward for twelve months, signing warrants that made my stomach turn to water. But I will not buy my skin with the blood of Katrijn Vane and her children."

Van Hout looked down at his ink-stained fingers. The silver snuff-box felt heavy in his pocket, a relic of forty peaceful years in the magistrates' chamber that seemed as distant as the reign of the Roman emperors.

The sergeant took a slow, deliberate step backward. He did not level the musket. He lowered the ash stock to the ice, unhooked the mastiff's chain, and turned toward the river stairs without speaking a single word.

# Chapter 33: The Flight of Moretus

The circle of dockers closed with the slow, crushing momentum of an advancing river dike.

Moretus backed away across the ice, his fine leather shoes slipping on the crusted snow, his crimson marten-lined cloak trailing in the dirty slush. The ivory walking cane had dropped from his hand; his kidskin gloves were stained with mud where he had stumbled against an ice ridge.

"Dirck," the proctor gasped, his hands outstretched in desperate, frantic entreaty. "Listen to reason. I have fifty guilders in my purse. I can give you the safe-conduct for the Zeeland boats. I can speak to the burgomaster—"

"The burgomaster is a Spanish pensioner like yourself," Master Dirck said, his iron cargo hook held steady before him. "And you have no safe-conduct for the ice, Master Proctor."

Behind Moretus, two young boatmen were already cutting through the ice with their heavy iron ice-chisels.

*Chink. Chink. Chunk.*

The sharp steel drove into the gray surface thirty paces behind the proctor, opening a jagged, black hole four feet wide where the deep, freezing current of the Scheldt boiled beneath the ice shelf, dark and murderous with river mud.

"The ice hole," a caulker shouted, his face contorted with eight months of starvation and rage. "Let the guild proctor test the water he sold to Parma! Throw him in! Let him swim down to Calloo with his Spanish ducats in his pockets!"

A roar of furious agreement went up from fifty throats.

The four guild beadles dropped their ash partisans onto the ice with a frantic clatter of iron, turned on their heels, and ran like terrified sheep toward the stone stairs of the Crane Wharf, scrambling over the frozen masonry without looking back at their master.

Moretus was left alone on the ice.

He stood twenty feet from the open ice hole, his breath coming in ragged, hysterical gasps, his eyes wide and white with the absolute, unvarnished terror of a man who realized that his gold, his titles, and his imperial warrants were meaningless against the raw, muscular fury of the men he had starved.

He looked at Katrijn.

She stood beside the sledge, her hands folded in her apron, her face motionless as carved oak.

"Katrijn," Moretus choked out, his voice cracking into a high, pathetic squeak. "Tell them. Tell them you stole the text. Tell them it was a forgery."

Katrijn looked at him.

Her eyes were cold, clear, devoid of mercy and devoid of malice.

"Hendrik forgave his executioner upon the scaffold, Master Moretus," she said, her voice carrying over the mutter of the crowd. "He forgave the man who swung the broadsword. But he did not forgive the man who sold the truth for forty pieces of foreign gold."

She turned to Master Dirck.

"Do not drown him in the river," Katrijn said. "The Scheldt is foul enough without a traitor's carcass. Let him run to the citadel. Let him run to Alexander Farnese and tell his Spanish master that Antwerp has read the book."

Master Dirck stared at Katrijn for three seconds. Then he lowered his iron cargo hook, spat into the snow, and looked at Moretus.

"You have two minutes, proctor," the porter said, his voice flat as ditch water. "Run toward the redoubt before my boys decide your cloak is worth more than your skin."

Moretus did not wait for a second warning.

He gathered the skirts of his crimson cloak in both hands, turned, and fled across the frozen Scheldt toward the distant palisade of the Spanish pontoon bridge. His boots slipped and skittered across the crusted snow; he fell once onto his hands and knees, scrambled up with a whimper of terror, and ran headlong into the morning fog like a hunted hare, his marten fur flapping behind him in the river wind.

A thunderous roar of laughter and jeers went up from the dockers.

Master Dirck turned back to the sledge. He reached into the severed wicker hamper, lifted two bundles of the printed octavos, and held them high above his head.

"Jan," he shouted to the carpenter on the crane. "Take twenty copies to the Saint Nicholas guildhall. Tell the carters to hitch their horses. Pieter, take two bundles to the meat market. Tell the weavers on the Predikherenrei that Hendrik Vane's press has answered the Spanish decree."

The dockers surged around the sledge, their rough, calloused hands reaching into the wicker hampers, taking the white pamphlets with an eager, reverent hunger that exceeded their desire for bread.

Within ten minutes, the two wicker hampers were bare to the willow bottom.

Four hundred copies of *A True Record* were moving through the fog, carried beneath woolen jerkins and tucked inside coarse leather aprons, fanning out through the gates, alleys, and guildhalls of the besieged city.

Katrijn stood on the empty sledge runners, looking out across the gray ice where Moretus's tracks vanished into the northern mist.

Giles stood beside her, his shoulders straight, his hand resting on the ash crossbar, his scarred ear pale in the rising sun.

"We must go to the cellar," Giles said softly.

Moretus reached the first pressure ridge of the frozen Scheldt, his boots slipping on the glazed ice, his hands clawing at the rough hummocks of frozen slush. Behind him, the roar of the crowd on the Crane Wharf rose in a wave of mocking derision that echoed off the brick gables of Sint-Jansvliet.

He looked toward the Spanish pontoon bridge half a mile down the current. The thirty-two armed ketches were silent in the morning mist, their guns pointed toward the sea, powerless to protect a fleeing guild proctor from the rage of his own citizens. His marten-lined cloak tore on an iron mooring ring as he scrambled past a frozen barge, but he did not pause to look back.

The ice shelf groaned beneath Moretus's desperate feet as he scrambled across the pressure ridges toward the palisade of Calloo. The fine marten fur of his cloak was torn, dragged through freezing mud and salt slush; his ivory cane lay broken in the snow behind him, kicked into the open ice hole by an angry caulker.

From the Crane Wharf, seventy voices roared with derisive laughter that echoed off the stone walls of the river warehouses, watching the grand inquisitor of the Stationers' Guild flee like a cornered rat into the gray mist of the Spanish lines.

The ink-pelts were wearing thin at the crown. Giles held the leather up to the candle flame, checking for pinholes where the stuffing might burst through under pressure. With a bone needle and waxed shoemaker's thread, he took four quick stitches through the seam, securing the wool fleece tight against the wooden core. The smell of hot pitch rose from the brazier where he kept a tin cup of resin warm to seal the thread ends.

Katrijn watched him work with steady admiration. For twenty years Giles had lived in the roar of the press, his ears deafened by the thunder of the oak bed and the clanking of the iron rounce. He had lost his hearing, but his hands had gained a miraculous sensitivity to weight and balance. He could tell within half an ounce whether a sheet of paper had received the correct volume of ink, merely by the drag of the pelt against the lead face.

"Look at the seventh line of the second page," Katrijn said, leaning over the stone and pointing with her bodkin. "The ligature for *ct* has a cracked loop. If the ink clogs the break, the word *fact* will read as *fart* to any drunken docker who buys the sheet at the tavern door."

Giles leaned down, his squinting gray eyes an inch from the metal. He picked up an iron pincers, plucked the defective sort from the galley with a surgeon's deftness, and dropped a fresh sort from the case into the vacant space. With his thumb, he drove the lead home against the brass rule until it seated flush against the shoulder of the adjacent letter. He looked up at Katrijn and nodded once, his scarred lips tightening in a grim, silent grin.

Giles leaned his massive bulk over the composing stone, checking the alignment of the columns with a boxwood rule. His fingers were covered with black ink up to the knuckles, the varnish worked deep into the grain of his scarred palms. He had worked twenty years at the press without a holiday, through the Spanish Fury, through the French Fury, through the great frosts that froze the Scheldt from bank to bank.

Katrijn watched his concentration with profound respect. In the silent world where Giles lived, every movement had its own precise, physical language. He knew the weight of a sheet of paper by the air it displaced as it settled upon the tympan; he knew the thickness of the ink by the drag of the sheepskin against the metal sorts; he knew the balance of the timber press by the vibration that traveled up through his boots into his knees.

"We have enough paper for four hundred sheets, Giles," she whispered, touching his broad shoulder to gain his attention. She held up four fingers in the candlelight. Giles looked at her hand, nodded with a curt, resolute jerk of his chin, and reached for the wooden lever with a look of fierce, unyielding pride.

Giles picked up an iron pincers and extracted a damaged sort of lowercase *m* from the fourth line of the page. The lead letter was bruised at the shoulder, worn down by ten thousand impressions on Hendrik's old press. He dropped the damaged type into the waste-box and selected a crisp replacement from the upper case, pressing it into place between the setting rule and the wooden reglet with his thumb.

Katrijn watched the compositors' fingers dance across the partitioned compartments of the upper case, plucking capital letters from the small wooden boxes with instinctive speed. Each lead sort was a weapon in the silent war against the Spanish council, cast in the secret furnace and sharpened by the cold truth of the printed word.

"Yes," Katrijn said. "We have the press to dismantle before the garrison arrives."

# Chapter 34: The Disassembly of the Press

The common press had to be broken into pieces that looked like common firewood.

In the coal cellar beneath Sint-Jansvliet, Katrijn and Giles worked with the frantic, methodical speed of shipwrights abandoning a sinking hull.

The morning light was filtering through the ventilation slit above the coal chute, pale and gray, carrying the distant, confused roar of church bells and shouting crowds from the center of the city. The pamphlets were already circulating on the Meir; in two hours, the Spanish commander in the citadel would order his pikemen to scour every alley for the press that had cast the lead.

Giles knocked the oak wedges from the split cheek-piece with four heavy blows of the maul.

*Crack. Clack.*

The upper crossbeam loosened in its mortise joints. Katrijn reached up, unscrewed the brass nut from the throat of the gallows, and lowered the massive iron screw spindle into Giles's waiting arms.

The screw was five feet long, forged of solid Spanish iron, weighing more than eighty pounds. It was the heart of the press: the irreplaceable tool that could not be carved by a common carpenter or bought without an inquisitor's permit.

Giles carried the heavy screw to the corner of the vault, rolled it into the deep coal bin, and shoveled three hundredweight of black anthracite slack over the metal until not an inch of the polished iron thread remained visible.

Next came the platen.

Katrijn unhooked the suspension cords from the brass ears, lifting the heavy oak block from the bed. She carried it to the hearth, set it upon the flagstones, and smeared its smooth face with black chimney soot and grease until the square timber looked like a discarded butcher's chopping block.

The carriage rails were unbolted from the frame; the rounce drum was slipped from its iron gudgeons; the tympan and frisket frames were stripped of their parchment masks and cut into firewood strips that went into the peat basket.

Now came the lead.

The sixteen pages of Brevier type remained locked in the iron chase on the stone.

Katrijn did not take time to distribute the sorts back into their wooden cases. If the soldiers found a single composing stick or a handful of lead sorts in a drawer, it was sufficient for the gallows.

She took the heavy brass mallet, struck the beveled oak quoins, and loosened the chase.

With both hands, she swept five thousand lead characters—the letters that had spelled the treason of the aldermen, the names of the Spanish pensioners, and the memory of Hendrik Vane—into a large earthenware pickle jar filled with cloudy lye water.

The lead sorts splashed into the caustic liquid with a heavy, metallic rattle, sinking to the bottom beneath three inches of yellow grease.

To a Spanish sergeant searching the cellar, it was nothing more than an earthen crock of scouring water used for washing wool.

Katrijn wiped the marble composing stone with a rag soaked in turpentine, removing the last traces of black ink, until the stone looked like an ordinary kitchen pastry slab.

She stood in the center of the empty cellar.

The great oak cheeks of the common press lay flat against the wall, looking like discarded roof joists from an old barn. The floor was swept clean of coal dust; the drying cords were down; the ink-balls were buried in the silt of the sewer pipe.

Hendrik's press was dead.

It was no longer a machine of iron and oak; it had dissolved back into the raw, silent elements of wood, stone, and metal from which it had been born.

"The hatch," Giles whispered from the ladder.

Giles used an iron cold-chisel to shear the lead seals from the carriage rails, dropping the metal scraps into the coal heap where they vanished in the black dust. Katrijn took the two sheepskin ink-balls from their wooden cups, slit the leather seams with her kitchen knife, and shook the greasy wool stuffing into the iron brazier, where it burned with a thick, gray smoke that smelled of boiled fat.

The brass matrix mold, the boxwood composing stick, and the four pairs of tweezers were wrapped in an oiled sheepskin and pushed through the gap in the chimney flue behind the kitchen hearth, three feet above the damper where no soldier's pike could reach them.

Katrijn swept the last traces of lead antimony dust from the composing stone with a horsehair brush, catching the gray particles in a piece of folded rag paper and dumping them into the coal heap. In the center of the vault, the oak frame of the press lay disassembled in four innocent timber balks, looking like common floor joists prepared for a carpenter's repair.

The iron screw spindle was buried beneath four feet of black anthracite slack; the brass box was concealed in the chimney flue; and the lead sorts were resting at the bottom of the lye crock, safe from the eyes of any bailiff who might search the cellar.

The cathedral bells were tolling the third hour of the morning, their heavy bronze chime muffled by the thick winter fog that hung over the Scheldt. Down in the cellars, the sound was reduced to a deep, visceral throb that Katrijn felt in the marrow of her shinbones. Outside the grating, two dogs fought over a scrap of salted leather in the gutter, their savage growls echoing between the timber gables before an iron shutter banged shut above them.

Katrijn blew out the rush-light and waited in the darkness of the archway. The frost bit through her leather shoes, numbing her toes until they felt like wood. Across the alley, an iron shutter banged once in the winter wind, creaking on its rusted hinges with a sound like a distant scream.

She blew upon her cold fingers to warm the stiff joints. The iron composing stick felt like an icicle against her palm, but she kept her eyes fixed on the lead sorts in the upper case.

Katrijn picked up her woolen shawl, wrapped it around her soot-stained shoulders, and climbed up through the floorboards into the kitchen above.

# Chapter 35: The Pamphlet in the Cathedral

At the eighth hour of the morning, a single copy of *A True Record* was nailed with three iron horseshoe tacks to the great north door of the Cathedral of Our Lady.

The portal, carved with three hundred stone saints and crowned with the royal arms of Brabant, faced directly onto the Handschoenmarkt. By nine o'clock, five hundred citizens had gathered before the oak timber, their woolen hoods pressed together in the freezing drizzle, their voices rising in a deafening, tumultuous murmur that drowned the chanting of the priests within.

A captain of the Spanish pikemen stood before the portal with six halberdiers, his drawn broadsword in his hand, shouting commands to clear the square.

No one moved.

The dockers from the Crane Wharf stood in the front rank, their heavy timber hooks held at their chests, their shoulders locked together in a wall of tarred leather that refused to yield to the soldiers' blades.

"Read it, Master Huybrechts!" a dyer shouted from the colonnade.

Master Huybrechts, the baker of the Meir, stood three paces from the oak door, his white flour-dusted apron tied over his Sunday coat, his spectacles perched on his broad nose.

He was not a rebel. He was an elder of the bakers' guild, a churchwarden, a man who had paid his municipal excises for thirty years without a word of complaint. But this morning, with his bins empty of grain and his neighbors eating boiled chaff, the baker stood reading the printed lines with a loud, ringing voice that echoed off the cathedral towers:

"Article IX. That upon the second day of August, the execution warrant for Master Hendrik Vane, printer, was drawn and signed in the secret committee of the town hall, by three men acting under direct terror of the Spanish garrison: namely, Jan Van Rooden, Burgomaster; Pieter De Vos, Receiver of Customs; and Lucas Van Hout, Alderman!"

A roar of furious indignation broke from the crowd.

"Van Rooden!" a weaver shrieked, waving an iron hammer. "To the stadhuis! Tear down his gables!"

"To the town hall!" fifty voices took up the cry.

The great bronze bell of the Cathedral—the *Karolus*, which had remained silent since the siege began—began to toll with a slow, thunderous, terrifying rhythm.

*BONG. BONG. BONG.*

It was the storm-bell: the ancient civic alarm that summoned every guild brother in Antwerp to arms in defense of the municipal charters.

Within twenty minutes, the entire city was in motion.

From the Kipdorp gate to the water redoubts, four thousand artisans, boatmen, weavers, and shopkeepers poured into the streets, their guild banners unfurled against the gray sky: the shipwrights with their crossed anchors, the smiths with their iron anvils, the dyers with their crimson shields.

They carried no Spanish flags; they carried the black lion of Brabant.

And in the pocket of nearly every third man in the advancing crowd, folded small and creased at the edges, lay a sixteen-page octavo pamphlet printed on French rag paper, its black ink sharp and unanswerable in the morning light.

Inside the town hall on the Grote Markt, the emergency session of the municipal council had dissolved into screaming panic.

Burgomaster Jan Van Rooden sat in his high carved chair beneath the imperial canopy, his face gray as tallow, his trembling hands clutching the armrests as the roar of the crowd outside shook the leaded glass windows of the chamber.

On the mahogany table before him lay three copies of *A True Record*.

Beside the papers sat two senior officers of the Spanish garrison, their hands resting on their sword hilts, demanding that the aldermen immediately sign the orders to deploy the Walloon regiments to clear the market square with grape-shot.

"We cannot fire upon the guilds," an old alderman from the cloth hall groaned, burying his face in his velvet sleeves. "The entire trainband has joined them! The captains have refused to muster the company!"

The oak double doors of the council chamber flew open with a violent crash.

Alderman Lucas Van Hout walked into the hall.

He wore no velvet bonnet; his gray hair was disheveled; his broadcloth cloak was torn at the lining and spattered with river silt. He carried no sword and no walking cane.

The copy of the pamphlet nailed to the north door of the Cathedral fluttered in the freezing wind, its three iron horseshoe nails driven deep into the ancient oak carvings of the Last Judgment. Below the paper, five hundred artisans and shopkeepers stood shoulder to shoulder, passing copies from hand to hand across the square.

Two young weavers from the Predikherenrei had climbed onto the stone pedestal of the central fountain. They were reading the tenth article aloud in turns, their voices carrying across the market roofs, while women held their children up to hear the names of the aldermen who had sold the city grain.

The crowd before the Cathedral north door had grown to more than a thousand burgers by midday. Weavers, smiths, carters, and merchant clerks stood shoulder to shoulder in the freezing mist, their woolen hoods dusted with snow, listening as copy after copy of the tract was read aloud from the steps.

The great bronze bell of the Cathedral—the *Karolus*—continued to toll its slow, thunderous summons across the stepped gables of the city, calling every guildsman to the Grote Markt to demand the truth from the municipal council.

Giles checked the alignment of the frisket sheet. The paper mask had been cut from a thick sheet of French rag paper with a sharp shoemaker's knife, leaving rectangular apertures exactly the size of the text columns. The frisket kept the unprinted margins of the broadsheet immaculate, shielding the clean linen from any stray ink that might have spattered onto the furniture or the iron chase.

Katrijn checked the register pins on the tympan frame. Two needle-sharp brass points projected from the wood, positioned to pierce the paper at the center fold. When the second side of the sheet was printed, the pressman would impale the puncture holes on the same brass pins, ensuring that the lines of type on the front and back aligned with geometric perfection. If the register was off by a sixteenth of an inch, the letters on the reverse side would show through the paper, creating a distracting shadow that strained the reader's eyes.

The ink-pelts rocked across the forme with a steady, hypnotic slap. Giles worked with the furious energy of a man fighting for his life, his breath whistling through his broken teeth in rhythmic gasps. The black letters shone upon the lead face like wet coal in a newly opened vein.

Katrijn fed the sheets of laid paper one by one. The damp linen caught the ink with hungry absorption, drinking the boiled oil into its fibers. With every pull of the rounce, the truth took physical form in four hundred identical impressions, multiplying beyond the power of any magistrate to recall or burn.

Giles checked the tension of the tympan cord with his thumb. The cord twanged tight and true, holding the sheepskin cover rigid against the outer frame of the press.

He walked down the center aisle between the rows of magistrates, his leather boots ringing on the marble flags, until he stood directly before the burgomaster's chair.

# Chapter 36: The Van Hout Confession

Lucas Van Hout stood before the full bench of aldermen with his head held high.

The roar of four thousand citizens in the Grote Markt outside beat against the stone walls of the town hall like a rising sea. Through the high arched windows, the smoke of torches and the red glare of the weavers' braziers cast flickering shadows across the carved portraits of the ancient dukes of Brabant.

Burgomaster Van Rooden leaned forward, his watery eyes bulging with fury.

"Alderman Van Hout," the burgomaster snarled, his voice shaking. "You enter this chamber unsummoned. The guild proctor reports that your library has been the source of the most venomous, heretical libels ever published within this city. What say you to this charge?"

Van Hout looked down at the mahogany council table.

He picked up one of the printed copies of *A True Record*, turned it in his fingers, and laid it back beside the municipal seal.

"The charge is true, Master Burgomaster," Van Hout said, his voice loud, resonant, filling the high vaulted ceiling without a tremor.

A gasp of shocked disbelief echoed through the chamber. Two aldermen rose from their benches, their hands clutching their fur collars, staring at their colleague as if he had unbuttoned his shirt to reveal an assassin's dagger.

"You confess?" the Spanish military commissioner demanded, rising from his chair with his hand on his rapier. "You admit you commissioned this sedition?"

"I admit that every word set down upon these sixteen pages is the undeniable truth," Van Hout said, turning to face the Spanish commissioner. "I admit that on the fourteenth of May, this council sat in secret committee and received four hundred gold ducats to close the river redoubts to our own fleet. I admit that we starved the garrison at Calloo to force a surrender. And I admit that on the second day of August, I signed the death warrant of Master Hendrik Vane, an innocent craftsman whose only crime was that he would not sell his press to our cowardice."

The chamber erupted into furious shouting.

"Madman!" an alderman screamed. "He has sold his senses!"

"Traitor to the King!" the Spanish officer shouted, half-drawing his blade.

"I am a traitor to the King, sir," Van Hout answered, his voice rising with an astonishing, terrifying clarity that silenced the room. "And I am a traitor to God and to the people of Antwerp. For eight months I have sat in this velvet chair, signing warrants of blood, trading the lives of honest artisans to preserve my spice warehouses and my family name. I thought that if we compromised with foreign steel, we could save the city's trade. But you cannot save a city by feeding its best men to the executioner."

He reached into his pocket, drew out the heavy gold chain of his municipal office, and laid it upon the center of the mahogany table beside the pamphlet.

"The crowd is on the steps, gentlemen," Van Hout said, pointing toward the doors. "They do not want my blood; they want the truth. If you send your Walloon mercenaries to fire upon them, they will burn this town hall to the water vaults before the noon bell rings."

Burgomaster Van Rooden's face was chalk-white. He clutched his chest, his breath coming in shallow, wheezing gasps.

"Who printed it, Lucas?" the burgomaster whispered, leaning forward over the table. "Name the shop. Name the press. If you give us the printer, the Duke of Parma may yet show mercy to your sons."

Van Hout looked into Van Rooden's pleading, terrified eyes.

For a long second, his memory flashed back to the dark coal cellar on Sint-Jansvliet: the smell of boiling pitch, the sound of the oak lever groaning against the iron cheeks, the woman with the ink-stained knuckles who had thrown his Spanish gold back into his face.

He smiled. It was the first clean, genuine smile that had touched his lips in eight months.

"The press was dismantled two hours ago, Burgomaster," Van Hout said quietly. "Its timbers are firewood; its lead is scattered in the river silt. And the printer who set the type has no name that can be reached by your warrants."

He turned to the two town beadles standing at the door.

"Take me to the Steen," the alderman commanded. "I will wait for the King's justice in the irons."

He held out his wrists.

Van Hout took the paper draft of his secret accounts from his inner pocket, laid it on the council table, and pushed it with his thumb into the center of the mahogany board, directly beneath the gold seal of the Duchy of Brabant.

"Here are the receipts, gentlemen," the alderman said, his voice quiet, steady, cutting through the murmurs of the magistrates like a cold chisel through lead. "Every payment, every courier, every letter carried between the stadhuis and the Spanish camp at Beveren. You may send me to the Steen or you may send me to the block, but you cannot unwrite what four hundred presses have already told the world."

Van Hout looked down at the silent magistrates of Antwerp, his hands resting flat upon the mahogany council table. For eight months, fear had ruled the stadhuis; for eight months, men had whispered in corners, signing warrants in secret committees, trading their neighbors' blood to save their own velvet robes.

"You may call the Spanish pikemen from the citadel, gentlemen," the alderman said, his voice echoing in the vaulted ceiling. "You may build a scaffold before these doors. But the text is printed in four hundred copies, and by nightfall there is not a child in Antwerp who will not know who sold the river boom to Alexander Farnese."

The ink on the printed sheets smelled like roasted walnuts and damp linen. Katrijn laid the fresh broadsides upon the drying cords that stretched across the vault beneath the ceiling. She used split-willow clips to pin the sheets two by two, keeping the wet faces outward so that the ink could dry without offsetting onto the adjacent leaves.

Through the stone floorboards, she heard the muffled tramp of marching boots. A patrol of Parma's Walloons was moving along the canal bank, their heavy iron greaves clanking against their scabbards. Giles froze with his hands upon the lever, his broad back rigid as an oak beam. The tallow candle flickered in the draft from the flue, casting long, wavering shadows across the lime-washed walls.

For five long minutes, neither of them moved. The water in the drain trickled with a steady, monotonous gurgle. A rat scuttled across the pile of peat turf, its claws clicking on the dry moss. Then the sound of the iron boots faded toward the Sint-Jansvliet bridge, followed by the distant bark of an arquebus discharged across the river dikes.

Katrijn let out her breath in a long, shuddering sigh. She stepped back to the stone and picked up the ink-balls. "We have fifty sheets left to pull, Giles," she whispered, her voice steady and clear in the cellar gloom. "Before the dawn bells ring at Saint Walburga, every fish-seller at the docks will have the names in their baskets."

The cellar ceiling was hung with rows of drying lines made of braided horsehair. Katrijn pinned the broadsides two by two with wooden pegs, moving along the rows with practiced, silent speed. The sheets hung like white banners in the gloom, their black gothic headlines declaring the treachery of the five aldermen in letters two inches high.

Giles paused to wipe his face on his shirt sleeve, his broad chest glistening with sweat despite the freezing draft from the cellar vents. He picked up an iron oil-can and squirted three drops onto the spindle of the screw, easing the friction of the iron against the oak nut. The heavy timber press was warm from the labor of four hours, smelling of heated ash, lard oil, and fresh paper sizing.

"The dawn bells are ringing at Saint Michael," Katrijn said, pointing to the ceiling. The faint vibration of the bells trembled through the stone arches. "The boatmen will be arriving at the quays to unload the morning turf. It is time to bundle the sheets and clear the stone."

Katrijn inspected the dried broadsides under the lantern. The ink was rich and black, biting deep into the damp linen rag without feathering or bleeding into the margins. Across the top of each sheet, the bold roman capitals spelled out the indictment of the municipal magistrates in words that no citizen could misread. Before noon, the placards would be nailed to the church doors of Saint George and Saint James for all the town to see.

The ink-pelts struck the lead forme with a solid, echoing thud that made the dust dance upon the cellar rafters. Giles pulled the oak rounce with steady, rhythmic power, his shoulders gleaming with sweat in the cold vault. With each impression, four hundred words of defiance were pressed into the damp rag paper, ready to be scattered among the guildhalls before noon.

The beadles did not snap the iron cuffs on his hands. They stepped beside him with their staves lowered, bowing their heads in silent, stunned reverence as they escorted Alderman Lucas Van Hout through the council doors into the roaring sea of the market square.

# Chapter 37: The Bread and the Hearth

The kitchen was warm.

For the first time since the frost had begun, a fire of dry red peat from the Kempen burned high in the chimney, casting a bright, cheerful orange light across the whitewashed brickwork and the scrubbed pine table.

On the center of the table sat a round loaf of Brabant wheaten bread—white, golden-crusted, smelling of honest yeast and summer grain—beside a brown earthenware crock of fresh Dutch butter and a smoking trencher of boiled salt beef.

Clara and Pieter sat side by side on the bench, their cheeks flushed pink from the hearth heat, their wooden plates piled high.

Pieter had butter on his chin. He ate with both hands, chewing with a ferocious, silent delight, his horn spoon forgotten beside his bowl. Clara ate more slowly, savoring every mouthful of the soft white bread, her dark eyes watching Katrijn as she moved between the dresser and the fire.

"Is the black-coat man gone, Mother?" Pieter asked through a mouthful of beef.

"He is gone, child," Katrijn said, wiping his chin with the corner of her clean linen apron. "He has gone to Calloo to live with the soldiers."

"And the alderman?" Clara whispered, leaning forward. "Did the alderman get on the ship?"

"The alderman is in the town hall," Katrijn said softly. She reached out and smoothed Clara's hair, tucking a loose brown strand behind the girl's ear. "He is speaking to the burgers."

Beneath the third stone of the hearth, buried deep in the dry sand beneath the chimney breast, sat the thirty silver carolus guilders that Van Hout had thrown upon the composing stone on the night of the midnight knock.

The thirty guilders were enough.

The silver would purchase four sacks of rye, two bags of dried peas, and three carts of dry peat from the northern bogs. It was enough to keep the children fed, clothed, and warm through the deepest freeze of January and February, until the spring thaw opened the dikes and the river ran free once more.

Katrijn sat on the low stool beside the fire.

She picked up a slice of the white bread, spread it with yellow butter, and took a bite. The taste was rich, sweet, dissolving upon her tongue like a forgotten memory of peace.

She looked down at her hands resting in her lap.

The skin along her knuckles was still red, split from the caustic lye water; the crescent of lampblack still darkened the cuticle of her forefinger, permanent as a dyer's mark.

She did not scrub it.

Pieter drank his warm broth from the wooden bowl, his cheeks pink and glowing from the hearth fire. Clara broke a crust of white bread into small pieces, feeding them to her brother with the patient, gentle care of an older sister who had known eight months of cold hunger.

Beneath the hearthstone, the thirty silver carolus guilders lay safe in the dry sand. Katrijn reached out and touched the children's heads, her hands warm from the fire, the smell of fresh wheaten bread filling the small kitchen like a blessing from heaven.

The kitchen was quiet now, filled with the warmth of the glowing peat fire and the rich, comforting aroma of boiled beef and yeast bread. Pieter lay asleep on the bench, his head resting on Clara's knee, his breathing deep and peaceful in the firelight.

Katrijn sat beside them, her hands resting in her lap, looking at her two children. For eight months she had fought the cold, the hunger, and the terror of the scaffold; now, beneath the third hearthstone, the thirty silver guilders were safe, and her children were fed.

Katrijn counted the silver coins into a linen purse. The thirty carolus guilders were heavy in her palm, cold as river pebbles. She tied the drawstring with a double slipknot and slipped the purse into the secret pocket stitched inside her underskirt. With thirty guilders, an honest printer could buy two sacks of rye flour, ten pounds of salt pork, and six bundles of dry peat turf from the smuggling ketches that ran the Spanish blockade by night.

Katrijn tied the linen purse securely into the hem of her petticoat, feeling the heavy silver coins press against her calf with every step she took across the cold cellar stones.

She laid her hands flat against the rough linen of her apron, listening to the crackle of the peat fire and the steady, contented breathing of her children.

# Chapter 38: The Memory of Hendrik

At twilight, the snow began to fall over Antwerp.

It fell in large, soft, silent flakes that settled upon the stepped gables of the merchant houses, dusting the slate roofs of the guildhalls and capping the stone buttresses of the Cathedral of Our Lady in pure white velvet.

Katrijn stood at the small diamond-paned window of the garret, looking out across the rooftops toward the Grote Markt.

The market square was quiet now.

The four thousand citizens who had gathered before the town hall had dispersed to their parish wards, carrying the sixteen-page octavos into every kitchen, workshop, and brewery in the city. The Spanish garrison had withdrawn behind the ramparts of the southern citadel, their guns silent, their commanders stunned by the sudden, unified defiance of the guilds.

On the town hall balcony, the imperial flag of the Habsburgs had been taken down; in its place fluttered the old maritime ensign of Antwerp: the white castle with the two severed hands of Brabo.

Katrijn took Hendrik's broken silver watch from her bodice.

She held the round case in her palm. The balance spring inside was silent, its steel hair curled into a useless knot, but the silver back had been polished until it reflected the pale twilight snow.

"They know, Hendrik," she whispered against the glass.

For eight months, her greatest dread had not been the gallows or the torture chamber; it had been the silence. The terrible, suffocating fear that Hendrik's name would be erased from the city roll, that his life would be recorded only as an asterisk in a Spanish inquisitor's register, a heretic forgotten in an unmarked ditch outside the Red Gate.

Now, four hundred copies of his memorial were in the hands of the people he had served.

Whenever an apprentice set a composing stick in an Antwerp shop, whenever a shipwright read a charter on the quays, whenever a mother sang a Dutch psalm by her evening fire, Hendrik's name would be spoken.

Not as a victim. Not as a criminal.

As a master craftsman who had held the line of truth against thirty thousand pikemen and thirty pieces of gold.

Behind her, the low wooden door of the garret opened.

Giles stood on the threshold. He had washed his face at the yard pump; the coal soot was gone from his cheeks, though the purple scar along his left ear remained sharp and prominent against his pale skin. He wore a clean woolen smock that Katrijn had mended for him, and carried an iron oil-can and a bundle of clean rags in his hands.

"The dyer's cellar is sealed with fresh mortar," Giles said, his voice quiet. "The neighbors helped me brick up the sewer pipe this afternoon. No bailiff will find the iron screw under the coal."

Katrijn turned from the window. "And the lye jar?"

"The lead sorts are safe at the bottom," Giles smiled, his eyes bright in the twilight. "I buried the jar beneath the cabbage bin in the yard. In the spring, when the frost leaves the ground, we can wash the sorts in vinegar and set a new case."

Katrijn walked to the boy, took his scarred hands in hers, and held them tight.

"You pulled well, Giles," she said.

The boy looked down at his crooked fingers, his lip trembling with an emotion he had kept locked behind his teeth for eight months. He nodded once, wiped his nose with his sleeve, and looked out the window at the falling snow.

"He would have liked the print," Giles said softly. "The Brevier was clean. Not a single halo."

From the high garret window, the roofs of Antwerp looked peaceful beneath the blanket of new snow. The spires of Saint Paul's and Saint Michael's rose into the purple twilight, their stone crosses capped in white rime. On the Meir, the torches of the civic trainbands moved through the streets, not to search for heretic presses, but to guard the grain storehouses for the common bread.

Hendrik's name was clean. It was written in four hundred books that would live in the chests of Antwerp long after the Spanish army had retreated across the Meuse.

From the garret window, Katrijn looked out over the quiet roofs of Antwerp as the twilight deepened into purple night. The snow was falling in large, silent flakes, covering the scars of the siege with an unbroken mantle of white velvet.

Hendrik's name was spoken in every guildhall in the city tonight. He was no longer a condemned heretic rotting in a ditch; he was the master printer whose courage had broken the silence of a besieged city.

Lucas Van Hout stood on the town hall balcony, looking out over the crowded expanse of the Great Market. The square was packed with thousands of citizens: weavers in their blue wool smocks, guild wardens in fur-trimmed cloaks, women with starving infants wrapped in shawls, and dockers leaning on their iron boat-hooks. In the center of the square, an apprentice stood upon an overturned beer tub, reading the clandestine broadside aloud at the top of his lungs.

The boy's voice carried across the frozen rooftops, clear and defiant. He read the names of the five magistrates who had received Spanish pensions. He read the exact sums paid in gold ducats. He read the secret agreements to deliver the river bastions to Parma's engineers. With every sentence, a low, ominous rumble rose from the crowd, like the sound of surf breaking against an iron breakwater. Van Hout saw Inspector Moretus standing by the portico below, his face pale as chalk, surrounded by six arquebusiers who held their matchlocks ready with trembling hands.

From his vantage point upon the town hall steps, Van Hout watched the crowd surge toward the stone portico. The faces of the weavers were dark with fury, their wool caps waving in the cold air like a sea of blue cloth. In the front ranks, three burly smiths carried iron sledges, their leather aprons stained with forge soot.

"Let the aldermen answer!" a voice roared from the center of the market square. "Let Van Hout show the gold florins he took from the Spanish commander!" The cry was taken up by five hundred throats, echoing from the high timber gables of the guildhalls until the stone statues on the cathedral porch seemed to tremble with the fury of the town.

The roar of the crowd in the Great Market rose like the sound of a winter storm breaking against the harbor sea-wall. The weavers shouted for the magistrates, their angry voices echoing between the guildhall gables.

"Yes," Katrijn said, looking out at the white roofs of Antwerp. "The Brevier was clean."

# Chapter 39: The Sinking of the Pontoon

The explosion shook the bed of the Scheldt at the turn of the February tide.

It was not the thunder of siege artillery; it was a catastrophic, earth-shattering detonation that rattled the roof slates of Antwerp three miles away and sent a column of fire five hundred feet into the midnight sky.

The Dutch Sea Beggars had struck the Spanish bridge.

From the quayside walls of Sint-Jansvliet, Katrijn and Giles stood among three thousand cheering citizens, watching the southern horizon burn with orange and crimson flame.

Two weeks after the circulation of *A True Record*, the political balance of the siege had collapsed. The municipal council, confronted by the exposed evidence of Moretus's bribery and the open insurrection of the water guilds, had refused to ratify the terms of surrender. The town gates remained locked against the Spanish tercios; the bread stores in the municipal magazines had been seized and distributed to the common bakers; and an armed delegation of river boatmen had slipped across the flooded polders to contact the Dutch fleet at Flushing.

The plan had been conceived by an Italian engineer in the service of the Prince of Orange: the *Finis Belli*, a floating mine-ship loaded with seven thousand pounds of fine gunpowder, brick masonry, and iron scrap, disguised as an ordinary grain hoy and drifted down the Scheldt on the ebb tide.

The ship had struck the center of Alexander Farnese's pontoon bridge off Calloo.

Through the winter darkness, the river was an inferno.

Thirty-two Spanish ketches were blown to splinters; the great floating fort of Saint Maria was torn in half, its brass cannons hurled into the river mud; and a breach three hundred yards wide was torn through the center of the stockade, opening the channel to the sea.

A roar went up from the walls of Antwerp:

"The Beggars! The Beggars are through!"

Dawn broke over a river that was no longer dead.

Through the drifting smoke and the floating wreckage of the Spanish palisade, the white sails of the Dutch relief fleet appeared in the river mouth: forty armed hoys from Zeeland, their decks piled high with sacks of Baltic rye, barrels of salt pork, and kegs of gunpowder, flying the orange, white, and blue colors of freedom.

The siege was broken.

The Spanish army remained entrenched in the flooded polders of Calloo, but their iron grip on the Scheldt was shattered. Antwerp was linked once more to the ocean, to the free ports of Holland, and to the printing houses of London and Amsterdam.

In the market square, the citizens dragged the iron pillory from the center of the Grote Markt, smashing the timber posts with sledgehammers, tossing the fragments into a victory bonfire that roared before the steps of the town hall.

Master Dirck, the head porter of the Crane Wharf, stood atop an empty beer barrel, holding a frayed, soot-stained copy of *A True Record* in his right hand.

"This was the hammer!" the porter shouted to the thousands of artisans and dockers who crowded the square. "This was the spark that woke the river! Honour to the free word! Honour to Master Hendrik Vane!"

The crowd roared with a thunderous, unified voice that shook the bronze bells of the Cathedral.

Katrijn stood on the lower steps of the cloth hall, holding Clara and Pieter by their hands.

She did not join in the shouting. She watched the bonfire smoke curl upward into the clear blue winter sky, her heart filled with a deep, serene, unshakeable peace.

The dead man had won his battle.

The Dutch relief fleet dropped anchor in the middle of the Scheldt before the Crane Wharf, their decks crowded with armed sailors and sacks of Baltic wheat. Forty broad-beamed hoys from Flushing and Middelburg, their masts hung with pine boughs and orange pennants, their crews singing the psalms of Clement Marot across the open water.

The Spanish bridge was broken. The thirty-two armed ketches had been burned to the waterline, their blackened timbers drifting down the current to the North Sea, while the citizens of Antwerp lined the quays three deep to haul the sacks of grain onto the city wagons.

The Dutch relief fleet sailed through the shattered center of the Spanish palisade on the morning flood tide, their decks loaded with four thousand sacks of Baltic rye and two hundred barrels of salt pork from Zeeland.

From the Crane Wharf to the water gate, three thousand citizens lined the river walls, cheering with a thunderous roar that shook the Cathedral bells as the first grain hoys dropped anchor before the city quays.

Giles cleaned the lead sorts with a stiff bristle brush dipped in a trough of boiling lye. The caustic solution hissed against the greasy metal, dissolving the black varnish and lifting the ink from the deep counters of the letters. The fumes of the potash rose into the cold air, stinging his nostrils with a sharp, alkaline bite. When the metal was clean and shining like freshly cast pewter, he rinsed the forme in three changes of clear canal water to prevent the lye from corroding the copper matrices.

Katrijn watched the water drain from the stones. The press was disassembled piece by piece: the heavy oak platen, the iron screw, the polished granite bed, and the two massive upright cheeks. Each timber was wrapped in oiled sacking and tied with hemp rope, ready to be lowered into the bilge of the smuggling ketch moored behind the timber wharf. Before the Spanish heralds could post their rewards on the cathedral doors, Hendrik's press would be floating toward the open waters of the North Sea, bound for the free printshops of Middelburg.

The canal boat rocked beneath their weight as they lowered the timber frame into the hold. Giles stood knee-deep in the bilge water, his strong arms guiding the oak platen into its cradle between two barrels of pickled herring. The smell of salt brine, tarred rope, and damp river silt filled the narrow space beneath the hatches.

Katrijn handed down the two pine cases containing the matrices. Her fingers were raw from the cold, but her pulse was steady and calm. Hendrik's type was safe beneath the cargo of fish. In three days, the ketch would be sailing past the Spanish forts at Lillo into the wide waters of the Zeeland estuary, where no King's officer could search a Dutch vessel without a broadside from an Orange privateer.

Giles tightened the hemp ropes around the dismantled wooden cheeks of the press, tying each knot with a double half-hitch. The seasoned oak timbers were secure, ready to withstand the swell of the estuary.

The press had spoken; the lie had broken; and her children were standing in the sunlight of a free city.

# Chapter 40: The First Clean Page

The spring thaw arrived in the second week of April.

The ice melted from the canals with a soft, musical gurgling, carrying the gray winter slush out into the deep green current of the Scheldt. Sunlight—warm, golden, smelling of wet earth and budding willow trees—poured through the high arched windows of the upper workshop on the Sint-Jansvliet quayside.

The shop had once belonged to an old sailmaker who had retired to Zeeland.

Now, the clean pine floorboards were scrubbed white with lye; the brick walls were freshly lime-washed; and in the center of the room, standing proud and solid in the morning light, stood the reassembled common press.

Giles had planed the split oak cheek with an adze, reinforcing the joint with two forged iron straps bolted clean through the heartwood.

The iron screw spindle, freshly cleaned of coal dust and greased with sweet olive oil, turned in its brass nut with a smooth, silent glide that required no more force than the push of two fingers. The twin sheepskin ink-balls hung from their hooks beside the stone, their leather newly re-stuffed with washed fleece, soft and bouncy as young lambs.

Katrijn stood at the composing stone.

She held the walnut composing stick in her left hand, her fingers moving across the compartments of a brand-new upper and lower case of English Pica type, bought with the remaining silver guilders from the merchant fleet at the water gate.

The metal was clean, bright as newly minted silver, casting tiny sparkles of light across the white paper pile.

She set the letters one by one, upside down and left to right, her thumb guiding each sort against the brass rule with rhythmic precision:

*P-R-I-N-T-E-D.*

She did not set an anonymous text. She did not set a Latin indictment or a municipal receipt.

She set her own title page:

*THE COVENANT OF CRAFTSMEN.*
*Being an Account of the Free Liberties and Guild Rights of the City of Antwerp.*
*Printed by Katrijn Vane, Widow and Master Printer.*
*At Her Shop upon the Sint-Jansvliet.*
*Anno Domini 1585.*

She transferred the completed lines to the iron chase, locked the oak quoins with three firm taps of the wooden mallet, and slid the chase onto the bed of the carriage.

Giles stepped to the press, his scarred face glowing in the spring sunlight.

He took the twin ink-balls, worked the fresh walnut ink across the leather domes, and rocked them over the forme. The clean Pica letters took the black oil with a sharp, glistening sheen.

Katrijn laid a fresh sheet of white Dutch rag paper onto the tympan points.

She folded down the frisket, turned the wooden rounce handle two turns forward, and nodded to Giles.

"Pull," she said.

Giles caught the ash lever with both hands, bent his knees, and threw his weight against the wood.

The platen descended. The timber struck the bed with a firm, solid, magnificent blow that echoed through the sunlit workshop.

"Up," Katrijn commanded.

Giles eased the bar back. Katrijn rolled the carriage out, lifted the tympan, and peeled the paper from the points.

The impression was perfection.

The black letters stood out against the snowy linen sheet with razor-sharp authority, clean, beautiful, permanent, bearing her own name beneath the mark of the hammer and the anvil.

The upper workshop smelled of fresh pine shavings, clean linseed oil, and spring rain. Through the open leaded windows, the sound of the river came up from the quayside: the clinking of anchor chains, the shouts of the boatmen, the song of the larks rising over the green dikes of the polder.

Katrijn ran her hand over the oak carriage of the press. The timber was smooth, seasoned, solid as stone. Beside her, Giles was beating the new sheepskin ink-balls against the block, his face bright with the pride of a master pressman who had seen the battle through to the end.

The sunlight poured through the leaded glass windows of the upper shop on Sint-Jansvliet, warm and golden, smelling of fresh river water and blooming willow dikes. The reassembled common press stood in the center of the clean pine floorboards, its iron screw gleaming with fresh oil, its oak cheeks straight and true.

Katrijn held the printed sheet up to the morning breeze. The black Pica letters stood out against the snowy Dutch rag paper with bold, razor-sharp authority: *Printed by Katrijn Vane, Widow and Master Printer, 1585.* The press was free, the city was alive, and her name was set in lead for the world to see.

The ketch dipped into the cold gray swell of the Western Scheldt, the salt spray whipping across the deck like handfuls of gravel. Katrijn stood by the tiller beside the Dutch master, her wool cloak pinned tight against the biting gale. Behind them, the great spire of Antwerp Cathedral rose against the smoky horizon, a pale finger of stone pointing into the winter clouds, surrounded by the smudge of a hundred Spanish campfires.

Down in the dark cargo hold, Giles sat on an upturned cask of salt herring, his deaf head resting against the rough oak planking of the hull. Between his knees, he held the pine chest containing Hendrik's matrices and the two copper composing sticks. The timber hull creaked and strained against the surge of the North Sea tide, but the metal inside was dry and safe.

Katrijn reached into the pocket of her cloak and took out the final proof sheet. The ink had dried hard and black upon the rag paper, the sharp Garamont types biting deep into the linen. She folded the sheet into four and tucked it inside the breast of her bodice, right against her skin. The Spanish army had taken the walls, the Duke of Parma had proclaimed the King's peace, and the priests were chanting Te Deum in the looted cathedral. But the words had flown across the provinces. In Middelburg, in Leiden, and in Amsterdam, the presses were already turning.

The wind shifted into the north-east, bringing the smell of salt marsh and open sea across the deck. Katrijn stood by the rail, watching the distant low dunes of Zeeland rise from the gray water like sleeping whales. In her pocket, the proof sheet rustled softly against her wool kirtle. The ink was dry now, hard as stone, permanent as the North Sea tide. The Duke of Parma had won the gates of Antwerp, but the words that had broken his victory were already flying north on every Dutch ketch that ran the river. In Middelburg, the presses would begin printing before nightfall, and the story of the unlicensed press would be read in every free town from Flushing to the Zuiderzee.

Katrijn looked back at the distant spire of Antwerp, fading into the sea mist behind the low islands of Zeeland. The salt wind tasted clean and cold upon her lips, sweeping away the soot and sulfur of the siege.

Katrijn carried the fresh print to the open window, holding it up to the sun to dry in the warm spring breeze that blew off the free river.

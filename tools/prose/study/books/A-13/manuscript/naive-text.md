# The Night Books

### Chapter 1

The buffer had a pull to the left like a horse that had learned its plowman was tired.

Martha Gould kept her heels flat against the green linoleum and let the torque settle into the tendon behind her right knee. It was three-twenty in the morning, which was the hour when the heating in Ward 7 gave up on the radiused Victorian cast-iron radiators by the high windows and began to hiss dry, lukewarm air from the overhead trunking instead. The building was three distinct layers of time crushed together on the slope above the canal: red Accrington brick from 1894 at the root, pre-cast aggregate panels from the mid-seventies where the geriatric beds sat, and the blue tinted glass of the new Ambulatory Care wing tacked onto the north elevation like a greenhouse glued to a prison.

At three-twenty, the ward smelled of things that had been boiled too often: sheets that had lost their tooth in the industrial laundry out at Skelmersdale, cabbage steam lingering from midday trays, and the faint, sweetish vinegar of floor stripper that never quite lifted from the skirtings.

Martha worked in six-foot arcs. She was fifty-eight, with small, square hands that smelled perpetually of sodium hypochlorite, no matter how many times she scrubbed them with the pink carbolic cake she kept on her own basin at home. Her hair was the color of a wet slate, cut straight below the ears with a pair of dressmaker's shears every six weeks over the kitchen sink, and she wore two pairs of thin cotton socks under her black rubber-soled clogs to keep the damp of the ground floor from setting into her arches.

In Bed 4, old Albert Garside was talking to the wall again.

Albert had worked thirty-eight years at the wagon-sheet works down by the junction, dipping canvas into hot linseed oil until his lungs crackled like dried gorse whenever he drew breath. His daughter had come on Sunday, stayed seven minutes by the wall-clock, left a net bag of satsumas on the metal locker, and gone back down the hill to catch the four-fifteen bus to the new housing by the retail park.

"The three-inch rivets," Albert said. His voice was not loud; it had the dry, rustling scrape of a dead leaf being dragged across gravel. "Not the flat-head. The button-head for the flange. You tell him. Tell him I’ve got the chalk marks on the plate."

Martha let the buffer swing through its half-circle. The pad was a coarse nylon weave, dark brown with grease and gray wax. She guided the machine with her hips, never using her forearms if she could use the swing of her body, because thirty-one years of night domestic work had taught her that arms gave out by forty-five, but hips would carry you into your sixties if you didn't fight the motor.

She waited until the buffer reached the edge of the rubber bumper on Albert's bed frame. Then she clicked the switch on the handle with her right thumb. The motor died down through a three-note descending moan, and the sudden quiet of the bay rushed in: the rhythmic click-whistle of the oxygen concentrator beside Bed 2, the wet snoring of the man with the tracheostomy in Bed 6, and the rain throwing handfuls of gravel against the single-glazed sash windows that overlooked the mortuary yard.

Martha reached into the left-hand pocket of her navy nylon tabard.

Her notebook was a narrow reporter’s pad, three inches by five, bound at the top with a black wire spiral. The cardboard cover had peeled at the corners until the gray pulp showed through, and the pages were ruled with thin, faint blue lines that had blurred where tea had spilled on them two winters ago. She carried a three-inch stub of HB pencil sharpened at both ends with a Stanley knife.

She did not turn on the bed lamp. She knew the spacing of the lines by touch.

*Garside,* she wrote, holding the pad against the metal upright of the drip stand. *03:22. The three-inch rivets. Not the flat-head. The button-head for the flange. Chalk on the plate.*

She didn't know what a flange was, though her father had likely worked them when the boiler shop was open down at the ironworks. It didn't matter. What mattered was that Albert had spent seventy-four years on the earth, forty of them in grease, and when the fever came up at three in the morning, the things that stayed in his throat were the lengths of iron he had set into the side of a coal wagon in 1968.

Two beds down, Mrs. Cleary was weeping into her pillow, small, rhythmic hiccups that sounded like a puppy under a blanket.

"He's under the bridge," Mrs. Cleary whispered to nobody. "He took the red scarf. He said he was going for the bread, but the bakery shuts at five. He's been standing by the canal since five."

Martha flipped the page.

*Cleary. 03:25. The red scarf. Bakery shuts at five.*

She didn't know who the man with the red scarf was. Mrs. Cleary’s admission card on the end of the bed said *Widow. Next of kin: Social Services.* There was nobody to tell about the scarf, nobody who would care that fifty years ago someone had walked down toward the locks and never come back up the cobbles. But if it wasn't written down, it simply went into the radiator vents and disappeared into the rain over the railway arches. A life was an enormous, untidy accumulation of small debts, bruised knees, wet overcoats, and forgotten names; when a person came into Ward 7, the doctors stripped it all down to a blood pressure reading, a creatinine clearance rate, and a yellow DNR wristband taped over the pulse point.

The notebook kept what the charts threw away.

Martha tucked the pad back into her tabard, clicked the buffer back to life with a jolt that pulled at her left bicep, and took the machine into the narrow alley between Bed 4 and Bed 5.

At four in the morning, the night sister, Sister Hallowes, came down the ward with the plastic drug tray. Hallowes was twenty-six, with sharp, pale clavicles that showed above the V of her scrub top and hair pulled back so tight into a donut at the crown of her head that her eyebrows seemed perpetually raised in cold surprise. She was an agency nurse from Newcastle who had been at St. Jude’s for six weeks and still called the domestic staff "cleaner" instead of their names.

"You're making a racket with that drum," Hallowes said without looking up from her little paper cups of liquid morphine and halved paracetamol.

"The pad's worn on the rim," Martha said, keeping her voice at the flat, low register she used for charge nurses, site coordinators, and policemen. "I put a requisition in to stores three weeks back. Mr. Vane hasn't signed it off."

"Well, don't bang the lockers. Bed 3's just settled. If he starts howling for his tea again, I'm leaving him to you."

"Bed 3 doesn't howl for tea," Martha said mildly, guiding the machine six inches clear of the castors. "He howls for his sister. His sister's been dead since the freeze of sixty-three."

Hallowes stopped with a blister pack half-popped in her thumb. She looked at Martha, her small, pale face tightening with the distinct annoyance of someone who had a degree in health sciences and twelve thousand pounds of student debt being corrected by a woman in rubber shoes whose pension would barely buy her coal.

"Just keep it down," Hallowes said.

She moved off down the bay, the soles of her trainers squeaking on the fresh wax Martha had laid two hours earlier.

Martha did not mind Hallowes. Agency nurses were like starlings; they blew in with the bad weather when the Trust couldn't fill the rota, they complained about the tea bags in the staff room, they left unrinsed specimen pots in the sluice sink, and by the time the daffodils came up along the ambulance ramp they were gone to Leeds or Manchester to work forty hours at time-and-a-half on the surgical wards. They left no mark on the place.

The hospital belonged to the walls, to the dry-rot under the linen room floorboards, and to the women who carried the keys to the cleaning cupboards.

At five-fifteen, Martha finished the cross-corridor and took her bucket into the sluice room at the north end of Ward 7.

The sluice room was the only room on the floor that had never been modernized. The Trust had run out of capital funds in 2008, just as the contractors reached the service ducts behind the bedpan washer, so the walls were still clad in the heavy white glazed tiles from the nineteen-thirties, craze-cracked like old teacups, with lead-soldered waste pipes running down the corners into the concrete floor.

On the low shelf beneath the macerator sat the blue plastic recycling crate.

It was supposed to hold empty cardboard kidney dishes, packaging from sterile dressing packs, and the tear-off sheets from the bottom of examination couches. That was what the Trust circular on green waste had mandated three years ago, when the management had put up the laminated posters showing a drawing of a polar bear standing on a very small iceberg next to the sentence: *Every sheet saved helps St. Jude’s reach Net Zero.*

Martha put her mop into the ringer, set both feet on the pedal, and bore down with her full weight until the gray water stopped running from the yarn into the lower tank. Then she turned to the bin.

Sitting on top of the crushed cardboard was a buff-colored cardboard folder, eight inches by thirteen, tied with a length of rotting red cotton tape. Across the front, written in the broad, black felt-tip of someone who had worked the wards when nurses still wore starched caps, were the words:

*WARD 4 — NIGHT REPORT & CASUALTY TRANSFERS — OCTOBER 1997 TO MARCH 1998.*

Martha’s chest gave a single, hard thump, like an empty bucket dropped onto flagstones.

She stood very still. The macerator hummed on its wall bracket, a steady, low-frequency vibration that rattled the brass tap on the sink. The red tape on the folder was frayed at both ends, the knot tied in a double bow that only the old school of sisters used—the sort who had been trained under Sister Tutor MacIntyre before the nursing school was pulled down to make room for the multi-storey car park.

Someone had pulled it out of the filing cupboard behind the sisters' station. The cupboard was supposed to be kept locked with a brass Chubb key that hung on a hook inside the medicine press, but the lock had been jimmied with a butter knife during the pandemic when they were turning Ward 4 into an overflow respiratory unit, and it had never been repaired.

Martha reached out. Her fingers were damp from the mop handle, cold at the tips where the blood ran slow. She touched the buff card. The surface was powdery with thirty years of coal dust and boiler ash that had drifted through the service risers from the sub-basement.

Under the red tape, someone had scribbled in modern purple ballpoint: *REDUNDANT ARCHIVE — DISPOSAL / GDPR REVIEW.*

The handwriting was not Sister Hallowes's. It was too small, too upright, the letters formed with the pinched, aggressive efficiency of someone who had done an MBA at an ex-polytechnic and wore lanyard badges that said *Operations Transformation Lead.*

Nicholas Vane.

Martha lifted the file. It had the weight of two bricks. The paper inside was not the thin, glossy laser sheets they used now that tore if you looked at them; it was forty-pound ledger stock, ruled with double red lines for times and admissions, each page signed at the foot by the night superintendent in ink that had turned the color of dried liver.

She didn't open it. She didn't need to open it to know what was between those two boards.

October 1997 to March 1998.

November fell right in the center.

She looked toward the sluice room door. The small square of reinforced wire-glass in the upper panel was dark; the corridor beyond was empty except for the yellow warning triangle she had left outside the linen store: *CAUTION — WET SURFACE.*

From down the hall came the rattle of the breakfast trolley coming off the service lift. That would be Terry, who had the speech impediment and the bad hip, bringing up seventy bowls of cold cornflakes and the silver-plated tea urns that had the dent in the side where an orderly had dropped one down the lift shaft during the strike in seventy-nine.

Martha took two steps to the cleaning trolley parked against the wall.

On the bottom tray, beneath the three-gallon drum of pine disinfectant and the stack of folded yellow duster cloths, sat her shopping bag. It was an old woven polypropylene bag from the Co-op, blue and green checks, with handles made of folded plastic webbing that dug into your fingers if you carried more than three tins of corned beef. Inside it was her plastic lunch container—two rounds of white bread with pasteurized cheese spread and a tomato that had gone soft—and her spare cardigan.

She slid the buff ledger out from under the red tape. She did not take the folder itself; the cardboard covers were too stiff, too easily noticed if someone glanced into the bottom of the trolley. She used her thumbnail to snap the cotton cords that held the ledger leaves to the spine.

The block of pages came free with a dry, papery *snick*, like a loaf of bread being broken in two.

She slid the paper into the bottom of the Co-op bag, laid the folded acrylic cardigan across the top, and placed the plastic lunch container squarely over the center. The empty buff folder she dropped back into the recycling bin, burying it beneath three empty cardboard kidney bowls and a sodden paper towel that had been used to wipe up spilled glucose.

Her hands did not shake.

A woman who had spent thirty-one years cleaning up the things human bodies dropped onto vinyl when they were frightened or dying did not get the shakes from five quires of twenty-six-year-old paper.

She picked up her mop, wheeled the bucket out into the cross-corridor, and walked toward the day room to start on the skirting boards before the day shift arrived to tell her she was in their way.

### Chapter 2

Nicholas Vane did not look like a man who worked in a hospital.

He wore suits that looked as though they had been ordered from a catalogue that catered to German architects: narrow-legged trousers of dark charcoal wool that ended two inches above the welt of his shoes, leaving a gap of sharp patterned sock, and jackets with thin lapels that stayed unbuttoned even when he was walking through the draughty draft-lobbies between the towers. He carried an iPad in a gray felt sleeve and had three different colors of highlighter pen clipped into his breast pocket alongside a laminated identity badge that said *N. VANE — DEPUTY DIRECTOR OF ASSET UTILISATION & GOVERNANCE.*

Martha had watched him for three weeks. He had arrived at the start of October, right when the Trust board had announced that the deficit for the third quarter had touched nine million pounds and that "non-clinical operational efficiencies" would have to be identified before the regional audit team arrived from Leeds in the second week of December.

At seven-fifteen, as Martha was signing the key register at the domestic supervisor’s desk in the sub-basement, Vane was standing by the double fire doors that led out to the loading bay.

With him were two men who did not belong to the hospital staff at all.

They wore navy blue cargo trousers and high-visibility vests with the words *SHRED-CON AUDIT SERVICES — SECURE ONSITE DESTRUCTION* printed across their shoulder blades in silver reflective tape. Behind them, backed right up to the concrete lip of the loading dock with its diesel engine idling in heavy, blue-white puffs against the morning rain, was an eight-ton Mercedes lorry. The body of the truck was a solid steel box with no windows, only a hydraulic hopper at the rear and a pair of heavy, four-inch steel rotating cutters visible through the mesh intake screen.

The noise of the cutters was a low, hungry growl that traveled through the floor slabs into the soles of Martha’s clogs: *chug-chug-crunch, chug-chug-crunch.*

"Everything before 2004," Vane was saying. He was speaking into a small black headset clipped over his left ear, his thumb flicking across the glass of his iPad while he talked to the driver. "There is no requirement under the ten-year rule for general nursing day-books, and the Caldicott principles are quite clear regarding legacy records containing unredacted patient identifiers. If it’s not an active litigation file or a pediatric retention, it goes into the bins today."

"The boxes in three-B are tied with wire, mate," the driver said. He was chewing a matchstick, his hands buried deep in the pockets of his high-vis vest. "You want us to cut the wire, or will the drum take the ties?"

"The drum takes wire," Vane said without looking up from his screen. "It takes staples, box files, and three-ring binders up to two inches. Just tip the gray wheelie-bins straight into the lift. We need the basement cleared by sixteen hundred. The Trust executives have a site walk-through on Thursday morning with the private finance consortium, and I want the entire archive corridor painted white before they see it."

"Painted white," the driver repeated, not sounding impressed. "Right you are, boss."

Martha stood by the key cabinet, the brass ring of Ward 7 keys heavy in her left palm.

Her domestic supervisor, Mrs. Noonan, was an enormous, wheezing woman of sixty-two who had two bad knees, an artificial hip that clunked when the barometric pressure dropped, and thirty-five years of service that gave her the right to sit on a low stool behind the key board and drink instant coffee from a chipped mug that said *WORLD’S GREATEST GRANDMA.*

"Look at him," Noonan whispered behind her hand, not looking at Vane, her eyes fixed on the small black-and-white portable television that was showing the morning news on the shelf above the electric kettle. "Thirty-two years old, Martha. Thirty-two. My Kevin’s older than him and Kevin’s still living in my back bedroom playing them games on the television. That one's on sixty-eight grand a year plus his car allowance. They brought him in from the health authority down in Birmingham to get rid of the paper."

"He's getting rid of the records," Martha said. Her voice was flat, level, the voice she used when she was asking for more bleach.

"He's getting rid of everything," Noonan said, taking a loud sip of her coffee. "The paper, the old iron beds in the tunnel, the laundry press they shut down in ninety-six. He says it’s all *square-footage liabilities.* That’s what he told the union steward on Monday. Said every square foot of floor space that isn't generating clinical income is costing the Trust forty-eight pence a week in heated air."

She snorted into her cup.

"Heated air. I told him, I said, 'Mister Vane, there hasn't been heated air in three-B since the coal boilers came out during the miners' strike.' He didn't even look at me. Just typed something into that little telephone he carries."

"How long are the shredder men staying?" Martha asked.

She had her Co-op bag between her feet on the concrete floor, her ankle pressed against the bulge of the ledger pages through the plastic.

"All week," Noonan said. "They’ve got three lorry-loads to do. Every old record from the old fever hospital, the sanatorium books from out at Highfield, the whole lot. If it’s older than ten years and nobody’s sued over it, it goes in the hopper. He says the new computer system doesn't have room for paper that hasn't been scanned, and scanning costs four bob a sheet, so it's cheaper to turn it into cardboard boxes for the supermarkets."

Noonan reached out with a thick, yellowed fingernail and tapped the key register.

"Sign your number, love. You’re three minutes over. If the site manager catches you hanging about the basement, he’ll dock your quarter-hour. You know what they’re like since the payroll went down to Bristol."

Martha took the ballpoint tied to the counter with a greasy piece of parcel string.

She wrote *M. GOULD — DOMESTIC — 07:18.*

Her signature was three small, jagged strokes that looked like a row of barbed-wire pickets. She had signed the same register in the same sub-basement five nights a week since November 1993, when her father had finally died of the lung-rot and her mother’s knees had given out at the wool-sorting warehouse, leaving them thirty-two pounds short every Thursday on the rent for the house on the estate.

Thirty-one years.

When she started, the ward sisters still wore navy blue wool capes lined with scarlet serge, the doctors wore tweed jackets that smelled of pipe tobacco under their white coats, and every death on the ward was recorded in three separate places by hand: the admission day-book, the sister’s report, and the black leather register that stayed in the safe in the chapel of rest behind the mortuary.

Now there was only the terminal on the nurse's desk that needed a six-digit password that changed every forty days, and Nicholas Vane standing by the loading bay with his felt-sleeved iPad, watching eighty years of human misery being chewed into three-millimeter gray flakes by an eight-ton lorry with an engine that smelled of fried diesel oil.

"Morning, Gould," Vane said as she picked up her bag.

He had turned around from the shredder lorry. His eyes were small, pale gray, behind glasses with thin titanium frames that had no rims at the bottom. He didn't look at her face; he looked at her shoes, at the rubber clogs that were slightly greyed with dried floor-seal around the welt.

"Mr. Vane," Martha said.

"Ward 7 was four minutes late with the domestic sign-off on the terminal this morning," he said. His voice was very light, completely devoid of the flat northern vowels that filled the hospital corridors; it was the smooth, rootless English of someone who had lived in three different business parks between Reading and Derby before he was thirty. "The domestic supervisor needs to ensure the digital log is closed before the shift hand-over at seven hundred. When the record hangs open, the system flags the bed spaces as uninspected on the regional dashboard."

"The buffer has a bad pad, Mr. Vane," Martha said. She did not lower the Co-op bag. She held it with both hands against the front of her tabard, the weight of the paper resting directly over her bladder. "The pad takes five minutes longer to cut the wax when the edge is ragged. I put a ticket in."

Vane touched the glass of his screen with a manicured index finger.

"The pad replacement cycle has been extended to forty-five days as part of the consumables review," he said without looking at her. "The old thirty-day cycle was established under the direct-labor agreement in nineteen-ninety-two. It’s entirely inconsistent with current floor-wear metrics. Just adjust your transit speed across the bay."

"My transit speed," Martha said.

"The speed at which you push the unit," he said, looking at her then, his eyes flat and clear as two clean microscope slides. "The equipment is self-propelled, Gould. It doesn't move any slower because the pad has thirty percent less fleece. You simply need to maintain the forward cadence."

Martha looked at him for two seconds.

She had seen eight chief executives come through those doors. She had seen the one who had the heart attack in the lift during the junior doctors' strike in ninety-six; she had seen the one who was escorted off the premises by two fraud squad detectives from the regional constabulary in 2004; she had seen the one who wore three different pastel-colored silk scarves and spent four hundred thousand pounds changing the hospital's logo from a red cross on a shield to three blue wavy lines that were supposed to represent *the journey of wellness.*

All of them were gone.

The hospital was still here. The linoleum was still here, and the mold under the skirting boards was still the same mold her mother had scrubbed with ammonia when the place was called the Municipal Infirmary.

"Yes, Mr. Vane," Martha said.

She turned and walked toward the fire exit that led out to the service road behind the mortuary.

As she pushed the steel bar of the door, the shredder lorry behind her let out a sudden, high-pitched screech as the steel teeth caught something hard—a brass eyelet from an old binder, or the corner of a tin index box. The engine dipped, black smoke puffed against the wet brickwork of the light-well, and then the machine found its bite again, grinding down through sixty years of names with a steady, heavy *crunch-crunch-crunch* that rattled the glass in the emergency door until she let it swing shut behind her.

### Chapter 3

The estate began where the railway cutting ended.

It was called the Broadwater Estate, though there was no water within three miles except the canal, which was green with duckweed and choked with shopping trolleys from the Asda, and the rain that collected in the hollows of the flat-roofed concrete garages behind the community center.

The houses were three-bedroom terraces of sand-faced brick, built by the Corporation in 1954 to rehouse the families cleared from the cellar-dwellings behind the gasworks. Over seventy years, the brick had turned the color of an old dog's teeth, and the small front gardens had mostly been paved over with council gravel or filled with rusted Vauxhall Astras sitting on concrete blocks with their front wheels missing.

Martha walked with her head down, her blue plastic Co-op bag held tight against her ribs under her waterproof mac.

The drizzle was the sort that didn't look like much in the air but had your knees soaked through your trousers within twenty yards of the bus stop. She didn't take the bus. The fare was two pounds forty each way, which worked out to twenty-four pounds a week—nearly one hundred pounds a month. One hundred pounds was the difference between her mother having the small corner room at Highfield Lodge with the window that looked out onto the rhododendrons, or being moved into the four-bed shared unit on the ground floor where the radiator clanked all night and the woman in the next bed screamed every time the nurses changed her incontinence pad.

She turned into Moor Lane.

Number 14 was the ground-floor flat of a two-storey block that had once been an eight-room house before the council divided it in the eighties. The front door was peeling green gloss over an aluminum draught-excluder that had lost its rubber strip three winters ago.

Martha unlocked the two Mortice deadbolts—one at eye level, one four inches from the floor—and stepped inside.

The smell of her flat was always the same: paraffin from the superser heater she used when the gas bills got too high, damp woolen coats drying on the backs of chairs, and the faint, sweet trace of the lavender soap her mother had used for forty years, though her mother hadn't slept in that bedroom since the stroke in the spring of twenty-one.

She locked both deadbolts behind her, dropped the brass chain into its slot, and took off her shoes.

Her feet were white and swollen inside her socks, the skin along the sides of her heels ridged with yellow calluses that smelled of rubber. She didn't put on her slippers immediately. She walked in her stockinged feet across the brown floral carpet into the kitchen, carrying the Co-op bag by its handles like a woman carrying a half-stone of potatoes from the market.

The kitchen was narrow, seven feet by nine, with an east-facing window that looked directly into the creosoted timber fence of the garden next door.

The sink was a double-drainer made of pressed white enamel that had chipped down to the black cast iron around the plughole. Below it, behind two sliding doors of cream-colored hardboard that ran in plastic tracks that had long since gummed up with grease, was the dark space where the lead waste-pipe went through the wall.

Martha knelt on the linoleum.

Her right knee gave its customary double-click—*tock-tock*—as she bent. She slid the right-hand hardboard door along its groove until it hit the stop.

The space under the sink smelled of damp copper, old mouse-droppings, and the bitter lemon oil of the cleaning wax she used on her own furniture. In the far corner, behind a plastic bucket filled with half-used cakes of carbolic soap, three spare plungers, and a row of glass jars containing screws and washers her father had saved from the wagon works, sat the bag.

It was not a modern bag.

It was a heavy, yellow plastic carrier bag from Morrisons, the thick kind with the punched-out handles they had stopped giving out at the checkouts in the late nineties, when the supermarkets first started talking about the sea turtles. The yellow plastic had become brittle with age, cross-hatched with hundreds of tiny white stress-cracks where the folds were sharp. Around the neck of the bag, wrapped six times with two-inch brown industrial parcel tape that had dried to the consistency of peanut brittle, was a label written in indelible laundry marker:

*HOUSEHOLD SUNDRIES — PLUMBING.*

Martha reached in.

Her hand went past the rusted stopcock, past the cold, curving belly of the lead U-bend that always sweated when the frost came, into the dry space behind the wooden bearer that supported the draining board.

She didn't take the bag out. She didn't need to. Her fingers knew every contour through the plastic: the stiff, rounded corners of four bound ledger volumes, each two inches thick, laid flat one on top of the other like paving slabs.

Volume One: *WARD 2 (MEN’S SURGICAL) — NIGHT OCCURRENCES — 1989 TO 1991.*  
Volume Two: *ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY — TRAUMA ADMISSIONS REGISTER — 1993 TO 1995.*  
Volume Three: *MORTUARY & PATHOLOGY TRANSFERS — 1995 TO 1997.*  
Volume Four: *WARD 4 (ACUTE OBSERVATION) — SPECIAL INCIDENTS & POLICE TRANSFERS — NOVEMBER 1997 TO FEBRUARY 1999.*

Four books.

For twelve years, every time the hospital had an archive clearance—when they pulled down the old isolation block in 2012, when they cleared the cellars under the chapel in 2016, when the new computer system was installed in 2020—Martha had watched the gray confidential waste sacks being filled by junior clerks who didn't care what was in them as long as they hit their forty bags an hour.

She had never stolen money. She had found five-pound notes tucked into the slippers of dead men; she had found gold wedding rings that had rolled under the radiator pipes in the private rooms; she had found unopened bottles of single malt whisky left for the consultants by wealthy farmers from the valley. Every penny, every ring, every bottle she had taken straight to the sister’s office and laid on the desk with a written note.

"Martha Gould’s as honest as an anvil," old Sister MacIntyre used to say when the domestic supervisors were having their quarterly reviews. "You could leave the crown jewels on her mop bucket and find them there at six in the morning with the dust wiped off them."

And it was true.

Except for the paper.

Paper wasn't property. The hospital had made that clear enough when they hired the shredder lorries: paper was waste, like the grease in the kitchen traps or the dirty dressings that went into the yellow clinical bags to be burned at the incinerator by the sewage works. If nobody wanted it, if they were paying thirty pounds a ton to have it ground into gray fluff and pulped in a vat of caustic soda at Ellesmere Port, then taking it wasn't stealing.

It was simply salvage.

Martha pulled the new bundle of pages out of her Co-op bag.

She laid the thick sheaf on the kitchen table beside the salt cellar. The paper was cold to the touch, smelling faintly of the damp cellar air it had lived in since Tony Blair had his first year in Downing Street.

She turned the pages with her thumb.

The handwriting in the night book was Sister Catherine Boyd’s. Boyd had been fifty in 1997, a huge, silent woman from County Mayo who wore steel-rimmed spectacles tied behind her ears with a length of knicker-elastic and who never let a house officer leave the ward until he had signed for every milligram of diamorphine he had drawn from the safe. Her hand was heavy, angular, the blue Quink ink biting deep into the fibrous paper with the pressure of a steel nib:

*12th November 1997. 23:15. Ward quiet. Seven post-op admissions from Theatre B. Bed 4 (Hargreaves) settled after 10mg Omnopon. Bed 9 (Mellor) confused, attempted to climb bed-rails at 01:20. Cot-sides applied. Dr. Sharma attended 02:45, confirmed chest infection.*

Martha turned three pages.

Her breath had gone shallow in her throat, small and tight like the sound Albert Garside made when the mist came off the canal.

*14th November 1997.*

The page was cleaner than the others. There were fewer admissions recorded in the top half; it had been a Friday night, cold and sleeting, the sort of night when the drunks stayed in the pubs along the dock road until closing time and didn't start coming through the casualty doors until after midnight.

At the foot of the page, beneath the entry for an elderly woman who had scalded her shins with a burst hot-water bottle, was the handwriting that Martha had looked for across twelve years of recycling bins, coal cellars, and abandoned filing cabinets.

It wasn't Sister Boyd’s hand.

The ink was different: thin, black, cheap ballpoint ink from a pen that had skipped on the down-strokes. The handwriting was loose, nervous, slanted violently to the right, the work of a junior doctor whose hand was shaking after sixteen hours on his feet without a cup of tea.

*23:40. ADMISSION: GOULD, Liam. Male. Age: 24.*  
*Brought in by Const. 412 & Const. 884 (Central Division). Found collapsed rear of Railway Hotel, Commercial St. Marked facial trauma, bilateral periorbital hematoma, respiratory rate 8/min. GCS 7 on arrival.*  
*Casualty Officer note (Dr. P. Henderson): Probable blunt force injury, intracranial bleed cannot be excluded. Referred Ward 4 for urgent neuro-observation pending surgical registrar review (Mr. S. Rao).*  
*00:15. Patient placed Side Room 2. Restraints applied to left wrist per police instruction (Sec. 136). Pulse 52, BP 90/60.*  
*01:30. Patient vomiting. Suction unavailable in Side Room 2 (unit removed to Theatre 1 for emergency bronchoscopy). Sister Boyd notified.*  
*02:10. Dr. Henderson paged re: falling oxygen saturation. No response.*  
*02:45. Registrar (Mr. Rao) paged re: dilated right pupil. Switchboard reports Mr. Rao in Theatre with road traffic collision.*  
*03:40. Patient found in arrest by Staff Nurse Miller during routine 4-hour observations. Pupils fixed, dilated. No pulse detected.*  
*03:42. Crash call sounded. Resuscitation attempted by Dr. Henderson & Team 3. Asystole confirmed at 04:05.*  
*04:15. Pronounced LIFE EXTINCT by Dr. P. Henderson.*

Across the bottom of the entry, drawn with a thick red china marker that had left waxy flakes on the paper, was a diagonal line four inches long.

Below the line, written in the broad, purple ink of an administrator whose signature was simply three loops and a dash:

*LOG VOID. NOT TO BE TRANSCRIBED TO INCIDENT REGISTER. SUPERSEDED BY TRUST SPECIAL INQUIRY REPORT 97/41. ALL WORKING PAPERS TO BE TRANSFERRED TO LEGAL SERVICES (SUITE 4, ADMIN BLOCK) PER CHIEF EXECUTIVE DIRECTIVE. DO NOT RELEASE TO CORONER WITHOUT WRITTEN CLEARANCE FROM LEGAL.*

Martha stared at the red line.

The clock on her mantelpiece—an eight-day mantel clock with a brass pendulum that her parents had received for their silver wedding in 1972—ticked twice, heavy and loud in the small kitchen.

*Superseded.*

She remembered the morning of the fifteenth of November, 1997, as clearly as if she were looking through the wire-glass of the sluice room door.

The rain had been turning to sleet against the kitchen window. Her mother had been standing at the stove, sixty-two years old then, with her hair still dark at the nape and her wool-sorter’s apron tied around her waist, frying three thick slices of bread in bacon fat for Liam’s breakfast before he went down to his shift at the wagon works.

Liam was twenty-four. He had his father’s shoulders, broad and slightly rounded at the base of the neck, and his mother’s small, straight nose that never seemed to get red in the frost. He had gone out at eight on Friday night to meet two lads from the plating shop at the Black Dog by the canal basin. He had eleven pounds in his pocket, his union card, and a packet of Player’s No. 6.

At nine o'clock on Saturday morning, two men had come up the path to Number 14.

They were not hospital people. They wore heavy dark blue macs over grey suits, with the small silver badges of the Borough Police pinned into their lapels.

They had sat on the brown moquette sofa in the front room, their hats held between their knees, while her mother stood by the coal grate with her hands wrapped in her tea towel.

"Mrs. Gould," the older one had said—Detective Sergeant Clitheroe, a man whose brother had been at school with Martha’s uncle. "I’m very sorry to have to tell you this. Your lad’s been found down by the locks."

Her mother hadn't moved. She hadn't even dropped the tea towel.

"He's at the infirmary?" she had asked.

"No, love," Clitheroe had said, looking down at his hat. "He never got to the infirmary. He was found in the water by the coal-chutes. Looks like he had a skinful at the Dog, took the towpath to cut through to the estate, lost his footing on the cobbles, and went in by the weir. He’d been in the basin all night. The cold took him before he could get his boots off."

"He didn't have no boots on," her mother had said, her voice small and hard like a pebble. "He had his shoes. The brown ones with the crepe soles he bought for the dance."

"Aye," Clitheroe had said. "The shoes. The water was very cold, Mrs. Gould. The coroner’s officer will be in touch on Monday about the formal identification. There’s no need for you to go up to the hospital. There’s nothing up there for you."

*He never got to the infirmary.*

That was what the police had said. That was what the coroner’s report had said in three lines of nonpareil type in the *Borough Guardian* on the following Thursday: *Liam Gould, 24, apprentice boilermaker, of Moor Lane. Death by misadventure due to immersion in fresh water following accidental fall.*

And her mother had believed it.

For twenty-six years, Evelyn Gould had kept Liam’s brown crepe-soled shoes in a cardboard shoe-box at the back of her wardrobe, wrapped in tissue paper from the Co-op drapery department, because she believed her boy had died in the cold water of the canal basin with nobody near him but the moorhens and the coal-barges.

Martha looked down at the paper on her kitchen table.

*Brought in by Const. 412 & Const. 884.*  
*Patient placed Side Room 2. Restraints applied to left wrist per police instruction.*  
*Crash call sounded. Resuscitation attempted.*  
*Pronounced LIFE EXTINCT by Dr. P. Henderson.*

He hadn't died in the canal.

He had died forty feet from the cleaning cupboard where Martha Gould had been mixing pine disinfectant with hot water from the copper boiler, while Sister Boyd filled out the morphine register and the junior doctor looked for a suction machine that had been taken away to Theatre 1 to save someone else.

### Chapter 4

Highfield Lodge sat on the ridge above the city, three miles from the hospital and two miles from the estate, right where the suburban semi-detached houses began to give way to the rough pasture and gritstone walls of the moor.

It had been built in the eighteen-eighties as a home for retired master weavers, with high stone gables, leaded lights in the upper sashes, and a heavy porch of dressed freestone that smelled of damp moss and wet leaves. In the nineteen-nineties, a private healthcare firm from Surrey had bought it, built a two-storey breeze-block wing out the back with twenty-four single rooms that had beige fire doors and yellow call-buttons beside every bed, and renamed it *The Highfield Lifestyle Community for the Third Age.*

Martha arrived at ten-forty.

She had slept three hours on the horsehair sofa in her living room, without taking off her woolen trousers, her head resting on a cushion she had embroidered in domestic science class in 1981. Her mouth tasted of copper and cold tea.

She wore her good coat: dark brown tweed with plastic horn buttons that her mother had bought from the winter sale at Lewis's in 1989. It was too heavy for the drizzle, but it had deep pockets that could hold two apples and a small tin of butterscotch drops without bulging.

In the small vestibule inside the automatic sliding doors, the manager’s office had a brass plate beside the bell: *MRS. H. CORFIELD — GENERAL MANAGER.*

Before Martha could reach the internal door that led through to the resident's lounge, the frosted glass panel slid back.

"Martha," Mrs. Corfield said.

She was forty-five, with blonde hair that had been lightened to the color of dry hay and very white, very square porcelain veneers on her upper teeth that gave her mouth the permanent, frozen brightness of an estate agent's photograph. She was wearing a plum-colored wool dress with a gold name badge pinned over her left breast.

"Have you got two minutes, love? Just while you're passing."

Martha stopped with her hand on the brass handle of the inner door.

"I’ve got the quarter-hour before they bring the dinners in," she said.

"That's grand. Come in and take the weight off your feet. I’ll shut the door; the draft from the car park’s dreadful today."

The office smelled of plug-in vanilla air freshener and the hot electronic smell of a laser printer that had been running off copies of the monthly newsletter: *AUTUMN HARVEST ACTIVITIES AT HIGHFIELD LODGE.*

Mrs. Corfield did not sit behind her desk. She sat in the small tub chair opposite Martha, her knees pressed together, her hands folded over a buff file with a yellow Post-it note stuck to the top corner.

"How was she yesterday?" Martha asked.

"Oh, she’s bright as a button, Martha. Truly she is. She had two helpings of the bread-and-butter pudding at lunchtime, and she was telling young Gemma all about the wool-sorting down at the warehouse. Gemma loved it. She said it was like listening to the wireless."

Mrs. Corfield paused.

The bright, porcelain smile did not move, but her shoulders dropped half an inch, the posture of a woman who had delivered the same three-paragraph speech to forty-five different daughters in this exact room over the last eighteen months.

"It’s about the local authority review, Martha."

Martha looked at the yellow Post-it note.

On it, written in blue felt-tip: *GOULD, E. — ROOM 12 — SHORTFALL £284.50/WK.*

"The council had their quarterly panel on Thursday," Mrs. Corfield said, her voice dropping into the soft, rhythmic cadence of a professional condolence. "They’ve reviewed the Band 3 support allocations across the borough. Because Evelyn’s mobility score hasn't dropped since July—she's still managing the four steps to the dining room with her frame, which is wonderful, really, we're all so proud of her—they’ve classified her as *Moderate Residential* rather than *Enhanced Nursing.*"

"She can't dress herself," Martha said. Her voice was flat, level. "Her left side's been dead since the second stroke. She can't get her knickers up over her hip without someone holding her elbow."

"I know, love. And our staff do that with an open heart, you know they do. Gemma and Sarah treat her like their own gran. But the funding formula doesn't look at dressing. The formula looks at clinical interventions. Because she doesn't have a catheter and she’s not on the pressure-relief mattress, the council's contribution drops from four hundred and ninety pounds to three hundred and twenty from the first of December."

Martha calculated the numbers.

She had the arithmetic of her mother’s maintenance etched into her brain like the numbers on a butcher’s slate.

Her own wages: eleven pounds forty-two an hour. Thirty-seven and a half hours a week on the night shift gave her four hundred and twenty-eight pounds gross, which worked out to three hundred and forty-two pounds take-home after the pension deduction and the union dues she never cancelled because the union was the only thing that stopped the Trust from moving her to the day shift.

The night differential was two pounds fifteen an hour—eighty pounds a week.

Overtime: she worked two Saturday nights a month on the acute surgical ward when the agency staff didn't show up, which brought in another one hundred and twenty pounds after tax.

Her mother's state pension: one hundred and sixty-nine pounds fifty.

The room at Highfield Lodge: seven hundred and forty pounds a week.

"The shortfall is two hundred and eighty-four pounds," Martha said. "A week."

"Two hundred and eighty-four pounds and fifty pence," Mrs. Corfield corrected gently, as if the fifty pence were a small, friendly flower she was placing on a grave. "We do have the four-bed shared unit down on the garden level. That’s fully covered by the basic local authority tariff, Martha. There wouldn't be any top-up at all. Not a penny."

"The garden level," Martha said.

"It’s very nice," Mrs. Corfield said quickly, her hand reaching out to touch Martha’s wrist, though she stopped her fingers two inches short of the brown tweed sleeve. "It’s very social. Mrs. Armitage is in there, and old Mrs. Preece from the sweetshop on Moor Lane. They have the television on all day, and the care assistants are right outside the door in the substation."

Martha didn't need Mrs. Corfield to describe the garden level.

She had cleaned floors like the garden level for thirty years.

The garden level was where the linoleum was nineteen years old, where the smell of stale urine stayed in the fiberboard wardrobes no matter how much pine oil the girls used, and where the old people who had nobody to sign the cheques sat in four high-backed vinyl armchairs facing a wall-mounted screen that showed morning game shows with the volume turned down so the night staff could sleep in the office.

Her mother was eighty-two.

Every morning at nine o'clock, her mother looked out of the corner window of Room 12 at the three rhododendron bushes by the stone gateposts. She knew that when the buds turned purple, it was May; she knew that when the leaves curled up like cigars, the frost had got into the valley; she knew that if she sat very still with her hands in her lap, the gardener who came on Wednesdays would touch his cap to her through the glass.

If you put Evelyn Gould in the four-bed room with the game shows and the artificial leather, she would be dead before the rhododendrons opened. She would simply stop swallowing her porridge, turn her face to the beige wall, and let the phlegm settle in her bronchial tubes until the night sister called the undertaker from the cooperative.

"I’ll pay it," Martha said.

Mrs. Corfield blinked. The porcelain smile wavered for a split second, then snapped back into position with a small, sharp inhalation through the nose.

"Martha, love... it’s over eleven hundred pounds a month. On top of what you’re already paying for the laundry supplement and the chiropody."

"I do the Saturday overtimes," Martha said. "The Trust is offering double-time for the weekend infection-control deep cleans until Christmas. Mr. Vane wants the old isolation rooms stripped back to the plaster before the audit team arrives."

"Nicholas Vane?" Mrs. Corfield looked up, her eyebrows lifting with the sudden, sharp interest of someone who read the regional health authority bulletins over her morning muesli. "Are you under Nicholas Vane at St. Jude’s?"

"He's the new manager," Martha said. "The one with the iPad."

Mrs. Corfield leaned forward, her voice dropping into a conspiratorial whisper that smelled of cinnamon mints.

"He was at the Queen’s Hospital in Burton before this, you know. My brother’s on the clinical commissioning group down there. Vane outsourced the entire hotel services contract—cleaning, catering, portering, the lot—to an outfit called *Sovereign Facilities.* Cut two hundred and forty staff off the NHS pension in one afternoon. My brother said he’s a hatchet man, Martha. They only bring him in when they want to prep a hospital for a private management buyout."

Martha looked down at her hands.

The skin around her cuticles was stained with the faint, yellow-brown tinge of the wax stripper she had used on Ward 7 three hours ago.

"He hasn't outsourced Ward 7 yet," she said.

"Well, just be careful, love," Mrs. Corfield said, standing up and closing the buff folder with a soft, final slap of card on card. "We need the first month’s top-up by the twentieth of November. If the payment isn't in the account by the twenty-fifth, the system automatically generates the room reallocation notice. It’s not me, Martha. You know it’s not me. It’s head office in Guildford. Everything’s computer-driven now. The regional director doesn't even look at the names; he just looks at the red boxes on the spreadsheet."

"The twentieth," Martha said.

She stood up, her tweed coat heavy against her thighs, and walked out into the corridor toward Room 12.

### Chapter 5

Her mother was sitting by the window.

She had her blue wool cardigan on, the one Martha had knitted from three balls of double-knitting wool she had found in the remnant bin at the market in 2018, and her white hair had been brushed back into a small, neat knot at the base of her skull. Her left arm lay across her lap like a dry branch, the fingers curved inward toward the palm, motionless and yellow in the pale morning light that came off the gritstone ridge.

On the small table beside her sat an untouched cup of tea with a thick skin of gray milk across the surface, and a photograph in a small silver-plated frame.

The photograph showed a boy of twelve standing on the towpath by the canal locks. He was wearing short trousers of heavy gray flannel, a knitted jersey with horizontal stripes that had been darned at both elbows, and he was holding a three-spoke bicycle wheel between his knees while he grinned at the camera with his front teeth missing.

"Mam," Martha said.

Evelyn did not turn her head immediately. Her eyes were milky at the edges with the arcus senilis that the doctor said was nothing to worry about—just the fat in the blood settling into the cornea after seventy years of eating dripping on toast.

"He's late," Evelyn said.

Her voice was thin, reedy, like the whistle of an old kettle that had lost its lid.

"Who's late, Mam?"

"Liam," the old woman said, her eyes fixed on the rhododendron bushes outside the glass. "He went down to the forge to see about the apprentice money. Mr. Clitheroe told him they were taking two on for the plating shop. He said if he got the job, he’d buy me the electric toaster from the Co-op. The one with the two slots and the little bell that rings when the bread pops up."

Martha pulled the vinyl-covered stool over from the wardrobe and sat down.

She took her mother’s right hand—the good hand, the warm hand that had scrubbed wool until the skin had peeled away from the quick of her nails—and held it between both her palms.

"The forge isn't open today, Mam," Martha said softly. "It’s raining up on the moor."

"He had his scarf," Evelyn muttered, her fingers tightening around Martha’s thumb with unexpected strength, the fierce, blind grip of a child holding onto a banister in the dark. "The red one your auntie Mary made him from the wool she brought back from Wales. I told him, I said, 'Liam, don't you go near the coal-chutes with that scarf on. If the wind takes it, it’ll pull you right into the cogs.' He laughed at me, Martha. You know how he laughs. Just like his father. He said, 'Mam, there’s no cogs big enough to pull a Gould into the canal.'"

Martha looked at the photograph on the table.

Twenty-six years.

Every day since the fifteenth of November, 1997, Evelyn Gould had lived in the forty-eight hours before Liam’s death. She had forgotten the death itself; the second stroke had wiped the funeral away like a wet cloth over a slate, leaving only the waiting—the eternal, gray Friday afternoon when the bacon fat was in the pan and the kettle was on the trivet and the boy was just around the corner, his boots clicking on the wet flagstones by the railway bridge.

*Patient placed Side Room 2. Restraints applied to left wrist per police instruction.*

Why had they put the restraints on him?

Liam had never been in a fight in his life. He was the boy who wouldn't drown the kittens when the cat had four behind the wash-house; he had carried them three miles in an old biscuit tin to find an old woman who lived by the allotments who took in strays. If he had eleven pounds in his pocket and his union card, why was he behind the Railway Hotel with his face smashed in?

And why had Detective Sergeant Clitheroe—whose brother had played football with her uncle, who had sat in their parlor and drank their tea—told her mother that Liam had never reached the hospital?

Martha reached into her pocket.

Her fingers touched the corner of her spiral notebook. She had slipped a single sheet of paper from the 1997 ledger into the back cover: the carbon copy of the casualty admission transfer, the thin, pink sheet that smelled of the chemical wax they used to make NCR copies before the laser printers came in.

On the bottom of the pink sheet, almost invisible unless you held it up to the daylight that came through the sashes, was a second signature, scrawled beneath Dr. Henderson’s name:

*Police Escort: PC 412 (J. Hallows).*

Hallows.

Martha sat very still.

The name hadn't struck her in the kitchen; it had been just another number, another pair of boots on the hospital linoleum.

Sister Brenda Hallowes on Ward 7.

Brenda Hallowes was twenty-six. Her father had been a sergeant in the Borough Police; he had died four years ago of cancer of the esophagus in the private wing at St. Jude’s, and the family had put a three-inch memorial in the *Borough Guardian* that said: *John Hallows, 1952–2020. Thirty years exemplary service, Central Division. A gentleman and an officer.*

PC 412.

John Hallows had been thirty-five in 1997. He had walked the beat from the canal basin up to the railway station, the man who knew every publican, every bookmaker's runner, and every lad who hung around the billiard hall behind the gasworks.

"Martha?" her mother said.

The old woman’s hand had gone slack in Martha’s grip. She was looking at the small television bracket mounted on the wall above the wardrobe.

"Did you bring the butterscotch?"

"Aye, Mam," Martha said.

She pulled the small yellow tin from her coat pocket, used her thumbnail to pry open the lid, and took out a single square of golden candy wrapped in cellophane. She peeled the wrapper with her left hand, careful not to let the paper rustle, and placed the sweet between her mother’s dry, pale lips.

"It’s good, that," Evelyn murmured, her eyes already closing as the sugar hit her tongue. "Tell Liam there’s two shillings on the dresser if he wants to go to the pictures with the lads. But tell him to take his shoes off in the scullery. I’ve just done the donkey-stone on the step, and I don't want his coal-dirt all over my flags."

"I’ll tell him, Mam," Martha said.

She stayed by the chair until the old woman’s breathing turned heavy and regular, the slow, rattling breath of the brain-tired.

Then she stood up, kissed the cool, papery skin of her mother’s forehead—it smelled of lavender and the starch from the laundry out at Skelmersdale—and walked back down the long corridor toward the automatic doors, her clogs clicking with a hard, dry rhythm against the linoleum that had never seen a buffer like hers in twenty years.

### Chapter 6

At nine-thirty that night, the rain had turned to sleet that blew sideways across the ambulance ramp.

Martha stood in the basement domestic locker room, pulling her navy nylon tabard over her head. The room was four feet below the level of the light-well, with two high, barred windows that looked out onto the tires of the ambulances parked on the slope. The walls were painted that universal municipal color—bilious green on the lower half, curdled cream on the upper—and the forty-eight narrow steel lockers had each been dented by the heels of angry cleaners since the winter of the three-day week.

Terry was sitting on the wooden bench between the lockers, eating a meat-and-potato pie from a paper bag.

"They're... they're... they’re taking two off," he said.

He spoke with his mouth full of gray gravy, his eyes wide behind spectacles that had been mended at the bridge with blue electrical insulation tape.

"Taking two off what?" Martha asked, tying the tapes of her tabard behind her back.

"The... the... the night rota," Terry stammered, his chin working hard to get the words past his tongue. "V-V-Vane was down here at five. With Mrs. Noonan. He had that... that... that gray machine he carries. He told her the... the staffing ratio for domestic services on the night shift is... is thirty percent above the national benchmark."

Martha stopped with her hand on the locker door.

"What did Noonan say?"

"She... she started crying, didn't she?" Terry said, licking a smear of lard from his thumb. "Said her Kevin’s car was... was due its MOT and she couldn't afford to lose the... the shift allowance. Vane told her if she... if she couldn't manage the transition to the... the new productivity model, he’d bring an interim supervisor in from... from the private agency in Leeds."

"He can't bring an agency in," Martha said, her voice dropping into the quiet, dangerous register. "The collective agreement runs until March twenty-seventh. The union branch secretary signed it with the Trust chairman in ninety-nine."

"The... the branch secretary died three year back, Martha," Terry said simply. "They... they never replaced him. They just send a man in a... in a suit from Manchester once every six months to leave leaflets about... about cheap car insurance in the canteen."

He balled up the greasy paper bag and dropped it between his feet.

"He was... he was asking about the... the paper, too."

Martha turned her head. Her locker door was half-open, her street coat hanging from the single wire hook inside.

"What paper?"

"The... the boxes from Three-B," Terry said. "The shredder lorry broke its... its teeth this afternoon. Something jammed the drum. A... a brass lock from an old register. The driver went... went spare. Said it’d cost four hundred quid to get the... the blades ground down at the depot. Vane was down in the archive with a... with a flashlight. He had an old inventory on his... his screen. He was looking for the... the casualty logs from ninety-seven."

Martha reached into her locker.

She touched the bottom shelf, where she kept her spare socks and her plastic tub of carbolic soap.

"Why would he care about ninety-seven?" she asked.

"Something about... about a claim," Terry said, shrugging his thin shoulders inside his blue porter’s smock. "The... the girl in the legal office told the girl in pathology that the... the Trust had an inquiry from the... the Independent Police Ombudsman. Something about an old case they’re... they’re reopening from the... the central lock-up. Vane told the driver if them ninety-seven books weren't in the... the hopper by Friday morning, he’d... he’d withhold the contract payment."

He stood up, his bad hip giving a wet, dull *pop* as he straightened his knee.

"You... you got Ward 7 tonight, Martha?"

"Aye," Martha said. "Ward 7."

"Watch out for... for Brenda Hallowes," Terry said, picking up his porter’s clipboard from the top of the radiator. "She's... she's in a right foul one. The agency told her they’re... they’re capping her hourly rate at twenty-two pound from next week because of the... the deficit. She was kicking the medicine trolley when I... I brought the clean linen up."

Martha shut her locker.

She turned the small brass key in the padlock, checked the shackle with two hard tugs, and dropped the key into her tabard pocket beside her wire-bound notebook.

"I’m not frightened of Brenda Hallowes," Martha said.

"Nobody said you... you was," Terry said, shuffling toward the door. "Just don't... don't let her see your mop bucket. She told the site manager you was... you was using too much hot water from the... the laundry tap."

### Chapter 7

Ward 7 was quieter than usual at midnight.

A sharp frost had set in after the sleet, turning the slush on the fire escapes into sheets of black glass that cracked like pistol shots whenever the wind came over the roof from the moor. The windows were opaque with fern-like patterns of ice on the inside of the glass, the cold radiating off the Victorian brickwork so strongly that the patients in the window beds had pulled their thin acrylic cellular blankets up over their ears.

Martha was working the wax buffer down the central aisle between the bays.

She had learned to listen through the drone of the motor. Over thirty years, her ears had developed the selective sensitivity of an owl in an engine room: she could ignore the seven-hundred-watt hum of the armature, the wet slapping of the nylon pad, and the clatter of the castors on the floorboards, but her brain would instantly register the change in tone when an oxygen line slipped from a nostril, or the sudden, dry click in a dying throat that meant the swallowing reflex had gone.

At Bed 8, a man named Henderson—no relation to the doctor, an old railway shunter who had three toes missing from a frostbite incident in sixty-two—was muttering into his pillow.

"We couldn't get the scotch on," Henderson said. His voice was thick with the sedative the night sister had given him at ten. "The brake-van was rolling toward the main line. I told the guard, I said, 'Jump, Billy, the points are set for the down-coal.' He didn't jump. He was looking for his cap."

Martha didn't stop the buffer.

She slowed her pace, keeping the machine four inches from his bed-curtain, and repeated the words inside her head until they settled into the small, permanent drawer where she kept them:

*Henderson. 00:14. Looking for his cap.*

It was always something small.

People didn't die thinking about their mortgages, or the Queen’s jubilee, or the time they had won forty pounds on the football pools. They died thinking about the three-inch rivets, the red scarf, the two shillings on the dresser, or the guard who had stayed in the brake-van because he didn't want to walk through the engine shed without his greasy woolen cap on his bald head.

At one-fifteen, the light in Sister Hallowes’s office went out.

The office was a glass-sided box set into the corner of the ward like a signalman’s cabin, giving a view down both bays and the cross-corridor that led to the service lift. Through the frosted panels, Martha could see the blue, flickering light of a laptop screen and the pale silhouette of Brenda Hallowes sitting with her elbows on the desk, her head in her hands.

Martha switched off the buffer.

The silence came down like a wool blanket. From Bay 2 came the soft, rhythmic clicking of Albert Garside’s concentrator: *hiss-click, hiss-click.*

Martha walked to the sluice room.

She did not turn on the main strip-light. She left the door two inches ajar so that the yellow light from the corridor emergency lamp cut a narrow path across the cracked white tiles, illuminating the brass taps on the deep sink and the blue recycling bin.

The bin was empty.

Someone had cleared it at ten o'clock—probably Terry, on his first round with the waste-sacks.

Martha leaned against the heavy porcelain rim of the sink. Her back was aching with a deep, dull throbbing that felt as though two hot bricks were being pressed into the small of her spine. She reached into her tabard pocket and took out her notebook.

She turned to the clean page at the back.

*Hallows, PC 412,* she wrote in the dim light, the pencil scratching loud against the paper. *Central Division. 14th November 1997.*

"What are you writing?"

Martha did not jump.

Thirty-one years on the night shift teaches a woman that jumping only spills the bleach.

She closed the notebook with a quiet, deliberate movement of her thumb, slipped it into her pocket, and turned around.

Brenda Hallowes was standing in the doorway.

She had her uniform cardigan on—a dark navy ribbed knit with leather buttons that had the edges scuffed white—and she was holding a blue plastic mug of instant soup that smelled of dried onions and salt. Her face looked very thin in the half-light, the skin under her eyes dark and bruised with the chronic exhaustion that comes from twelve-hour night shifts worked on three hours of broken sleep in a terraced house with two toddlers and a husband who worked the late turn at the parcel depot.

"Just the floor-check," Martha said. "Mr. Vane wants the log signed off by bay."

Hallowes leaned her shoulder against the doorframe. She looked at Martha, her eyes tired and cold, but without the sharp, snappy arrogance she had shown earlier in the week.

"Vane’s a prick," Hallowes said.

It was said with the simple, matter-of-fact exhaustion of a soldier talking about a staff officer three miles behind the line.

"Aye," Martha said. "He is."

Hallowes took a sip of her soup. Her hand was red around the knuckles from the alcohol gel they used fifty times a shift.

Bed 4.

Albert Garside.

### Chapter 8

The mortuary trolley was called the *Bantam.*

### Chapter 9

The domestic changing room in the sub-basement smelled of thirty years of damp wool and wet soap.

### Chapter 10

The man from the housing company was standing on the front path of Number 14 when Martha turned the corner from Moor Lane.

He was twenty-four or five, wearing a navy quilted jacket with the logo of *BROADWATER HOMES LTD* embroidered in red on the chest, a pair of sharp beige chinos that were already splashed with orange mud around the cuffs, and pointed brown leather shoes that were entirely unsuitable for the broken concrete slabs of the estate. If you turn that spindle, you’ll have four hundred gallons of town water through this floor before you can get your phone out of your pocket, and the flat underneath belongs to an old woman who’s on an oxygen cylinder."

He looked up at her from the floor. His eyes were small, brown, slightly wet at the corners with the cold air from the street, and for the first time he seemed to see the size of her wrists—the thick, flat wrists of a woman who had wrung three hundred industrial mops a night for thirty-one years without a holiday except three days when her father was buried.

"The... the Trust requires all non-standard fittings to be replaced by a certified contractor," he said, his voice losing its smooth, managerial slide, slipping back into the flat, nasal accent of the council estates on the south side of the town. Her heart was thumping against her ribs like a bird in a chimney.

Twenty-eight days.

That meant the housing association would be in the flat before Christmas. They would bring two lads with battery saws and a copper-press tool; they would rip out the hardboard doors, pull the wooden bearer away from the wall to get at the lead junction, and throw everything under the sink into a skip on the front lawn before Martha could get home from the night shift.

She walked back into the kitchen.

She knelt down, her knees hitting the floorboards with a dull, heavy thud.

She pulled the Morrisons bag out from behind the pipe. The yellow plastic was cold, stiff, smelling of thirty years of damp lime and copper-rot. She laid it on the kitchen table beside the blue gym bag she had taken from Brenda Hallowes.

Two bags.

One with the hospital's books: the records of what had been done to Liam Gould after they brought him through the service door at midnight.

One with the policeman's book: the record of who had done it in the barrel-room behind the Railway Hotel.

Together, they weighed fourteen pounds.

Fourteen pounds of dry, decaying paper that could put the Medical Director of St. Jude’s in the dock at the Crown Court in Preston, put the Deputy Director of Asset Utilisation in an administrative inquiry, and destroy every pension, every reference, and every shilling of overtime Martha Gould needed to keep her mother from dying on the plastic chairs of the garden level at Highfield Lodge.

She sat down at the table.

She didn't take off her coat. She didn't put the kettle on.

She opened her narrow reporter’s pad, turned to the third page from the front, and looked at the notes she had made three weeks ago, on the night Nicholas Vane had first arrived on Ward 7 with his iPad and his sharp, German-cut trousers:

*Vane. 02:40. Ward 7 corridor. Talking to regional director on phone.*  
*'The legacy estate has twenty-four active historical liabilities that fall outside the ten-year limitation if unredacted records are disclosed under freedom of information. We need the physical destruction completed before the CQC inspection in December, or the foundation trust application will be refused on governance grounds.'*

Martha looked at the date she had written beside the note.

*October 9th.*

The foundation trust application.

That was what it was all about. If St. Jude’s became a foundation trust, the directors could borrow money on the commercial markets, sell off the old fever hospital site to the housing developers for twelve million pounds, and pay themselves forty percent bonuses for "operational transformation." But if the CQC found that the hospital had buried an uninvestigated death in a police cell in 1997, the application was dead, the board was sacked, and Nicholas Vane went back to being an interim manager on six-month contracts in the Midlands.

She took her pencil stub.

She turned to a fresh page at the back of the book.

*The twenty-first of November,* she wrote.

That was the day the care home fees were due.

That was also the day the CQC audit team was scheduled to arrive at St. Jude’s to sign off on the paperless archive.

She had nine days.

### Chapter 11

The drizzle had stopped by two o'clock, leaving a high, white sky that looked like a sheet of unwashed linen stretched tight over the chimney pots of the city.

Martha walked up the long hill toward Highfield.

Her legs were heavy, the muscles along her shins burning with that particular, dull ache that comes from staying on your feet for eighteen hours without lying down. She had left both bags—the Morrisons carrier and the blue gym bag—under her bed, wrapped inside an old wool blanket she had pinned shut with four large steel diaper pins she had saved from Liam’s infancy. It wasn't safe under the bed, she knew that; if the housing officer came back with a master key, he’d look under the valance before he looked anywhere else. But it was safer than under the sink, where the green pipe was already dripping two drops of brown water a minute into the plastic bucket.

Highfield Lodge was warm, almost suffocatingly so, when she pushed through the automatic glass doors.

The radiator in the entrance hall was running at full blast, the air smelling of stewed apples, wet umbrellas, and the floor polish they used to give the vinyl in the foyer a high, commercial gloss that looked clean but was as slick as lard if you had damp soles.

Mrs. Corfield was not in her office. The frosted glass slide was shut, and a small printed cardboard notice hung from the brass hook: *MANAGEMENT IN REGIONAL REVIEW MEETING — PLEASE CONTACT SENIOR CARE LEADER ON DUTY.*

Martha walked down the carpeted corridor toward Room 12.

Through the open doors of the residents' lounge, she could see the garden level stairs. A heavy blue plastic gate had been padlocked across the top of the flight, with a yellow sign that said: *CAUTION — PHASE 2 REFURBISHMENT IN PROGRESS — NO UNAUTHORISED ACCESS.*

Beside the gate stood two men in brown overalls carrying rolls of cheap, gray foam-backed carpet that smelled of petroleum and hot rubber.

"Martha?"

A young woman in a pink tunic came out of Room 10 carrying a plastic laundry hamper.

It was Gemma, the nineteen-year-old senior care assistant who had two small children of her own and who always brought Evelyn three ginger biscuits wrapped in a napkin from the kitchen after the supper trays had been collected.

"How is she, Gemma?" Martha asked, stopping by the fire extinguisher.

Gemma looked down at her laundry hamper. Her face was round, freckled, but her eyes had that narrow, guarded look that Martha had seen on junior nurses when a patient had had a bad turn during the changeover.

"She's... she’s had a bit of a turn this morning, Martha," Gemma said softly, her voice dropping so the men with the carpet wouldn't hear. "About eleven. Dr. Henderson was here."

Martha’s hand closed into a fist inside her coat pocket.

"Dr. Henderson?"

"He's the medical director for the care home group, you know," Gemma said. "He does the monthly clinical oversight for the complex cases. He was doing his round with Mrs. Corfield. They went into Room 12 to look at your mam’s chart."

"What did he want with her chart?"

"He said it was part of the Band 3 reassessment," Gemma whispered, leaning closer. "He looked at her pupils, Martha. He had that little pen-torch he uses. He held her eye open and he asked her about her family. He asked her if she had anyone besides you. He asked if there was any... any surviving male relatives who held power of attorney."

Martha felt the blood drain from her ears.

"What did she say?"

"You know what she’s like when she’s had the stroke medicine, Martha," Gemma said, her face full of genuine pity that made Martha’s stomach turn cold. "She started on about Liam again. She told Dr. Henderson that Liam was coming up from the works with thirty-six shillings for her rent. She told him that Liam had his brown shoes on, and that the police had promised he’d be home before the light went."

Gemma touched Martha’s sleeve.

"Dr. Henderson looked at Mrs. Corfield, Martha. He wrote something on the blue form—the clinical governance form. Mrs. Corfield told me after they went out that Dr. Henderson had recommended an immediate transfer to the Highfield Annex."

"The Annex?" Martha said. "The Annex is the palliative unit."

"He said she was showing signs of advanced terminal agitation," Gemma said, her voice trembling slightly. "He said her cognitive fixation on the deceased sibling was an indicator of late-stage cerebral decline. He told Mrs. Corfield that if she stayed in Room 12, she’d need twenty-four-hour one-to-one nursing, and the local authority would never fund that. He said the Annex was the only place that had the specialized sedation protocols."

*Sedation protocols.*

Martha knew what that meant.

In the old days, they called it the Brompton cocktail: diamorphine, cocaine, and gin, given in a small medicine cup every four hours until the patient stopped asking for their tea and started looking through the ceiling at the angels. Now they used midazolam in a continuous subcutaneous driver, five milligrams every twenty-four hours, increased by two milligrams a day until the lungs filled up with their own secretions and the bed was cleared for the next name on the council waiting list.

"Where is he now?" Martha asked.

"He went about half an hour ago," Gemma said. "In that big black Mercedes he has. He told Mrs. Corfield he’d sign the transfer order from his office at St. Jude’s this afternoon."

Martha did not wait to hear any more.

She walked past the laundry hamper, past the men with the foam-backed carpet, and pushed open the heavy timber door of Room 12.

### Chapter 12

Evelyn was not sitting by the window.

They had put her into the bed, with both chrome-plated cot-sides pulled up and locked into place with spring-pins that had red plastic warning caps on the ends. She was lying flat on her back, without her pillows, her small, white head turned toward the cream distemper of the wall.

On her left forearm, taped over the thin, purple veins with three strips of white micropore tape, was a butterfly needle.

A clear plastic line ran from the needle to a small, battery-operated syringe driver that had been clipped to the headboard of the bed with a Velcro strap. The driver was an old model—a Graseby MS16, the kind the hospital had phased out five years ago because the mechanical drive-screws were known to slip if they got knocked—and its little red LED light was pulsing with a steady, mechanical wink: *one, two, three, inject.*

Martha walked to the bed.

Her fingers went straight to the syringe inside the transparent plastic barrel.

The label on the syringe was written in the round, looping hand of the palliative care nurse who came three times a week from the hospice:

*E. GOULD. Midazolam 10mg / Haloperidol 2.5mg in 24 hrs. Rate: 2mm/hr. Prescribed by: Dr. P. Henderson. 02/11.*

Ten milligrams.

Ten milligrams of midazolam would knock a nineteen-stone docker flat on his back for sixteen hours. For an eighty-two-year-old woman who weighed six stone four and had half a kidney left from a scarlatina infection in 1948, it was a slow, chemical cosh that would have her swallowing reflex gone by midnight.

"Mam," Martha whispered.

She leaned over the cot-side, her hand going to her mother’s cheek.

The skin was cold, waxy, smelling of the antiseptic wipe they had used to clean the vein. Evelyn’s eyelids were half-open, but the eyes beneath were fixed, the pupils contracted to small black pin-heads that did not respond to the light from the window. Her mouth was open, her dry tongue sticking to the roof of her mouth with a faint, clicking sound every time she drew a rattling breath through her nose.

"Mam. It’s Martha."

Evelyn’s good hand—the right hand—did not move from the cellular blanket. The fingers were curled tight into the palm, the knuckles white and stiff like small river stones.

Beside the bed, on the locker where the photograph of Liam usually stood, was a blank square of dust.

The photograph was gone.

Martha opened the drawer of the bedside locker.

Inside lay her mother’s small black plastic rosary, three paper napkins, an unopened tube of zinc-and-castor oil cream, and a buff internal mail envelope from the hospital.

The envelope was unsealed.

Martha pulled it out.

Inside was a single sheet of heavy white bond paper, bearing the printed letterhead of the Trust:

*ST. JUDE’S UNIVERSITY HOSPITALS NHS FOUNDATION TRUST (PROVISIONAL)*  
*OFFICE OF THE MEDICAL DIRECTOR — AMBULATORY CARE WING*

Beneath the crest—the three blue wavy lines that had cost four hundred thousand pounds—was a typewritten memorandum:

*CONFIDENTIAL — VIA COURIER*  
*Date: 2nd November*  
*To: Mrs. H. Corfield, General Manager, Highfield Lodge*  
*From: Mr. N. Vane, Deputy Director of Asset Utilisation & Governance*  
*Countersigned: Dr. P. Henderson, Medical Director*

*RE: GOULD, Evelyn (DOB: 14/05/1942) — RESIDENTIAL STATUS REVIEW*

*Following a review of legacy records held in the Central Archive, it has been identified that the above resident is the sole surviving next-of-kin in relation to an unfinalised clinical incident originating in November 1997 (Ref: SI-97/41).*

*In light of the resident’s deteriorating cognitive status and the imminent transition of St. Jude’s to Foundation status, it is imperative that all outstanding historic liabilities are brought to administrative closure. Under the provisions of the Mental Capacity Act 2005, where a resident lacks capacity to instruct legal representatives, the Trust is prepared to settle all outstanding residential fee shortfalls at Highfield Lodge in perpetuity, conditional upon the formal execution of a Deed of Renunciation by the appointed legal deputy or Court of Protection representative.*

*Dr. Henderson has reviewed the patient clinically this morning and confirms that advanced cognitive decline renders the resident incapable of managing her own affairs. He has initiated appropriate end-stage supportive care.*

*We note that the resident’s daughter, Martha Gould, is currently employed within the Trust’s domestic services directorate. Miss Gould’s employment status is currently subject to review under the market-testing programme for hotel services.*

*We suggest it would be in all parties’ interests if the documentation were executed prior to the arrival of the Care Quality Commission inspection team on 21st November. In the event that Miss Gould refuses to cooperate with the administrative regularization of this matter, the Trust will be unable to support any discretionary hardship funding, and Highfield Lodge will be advised to proceed with the immediate relocation of the resident to the municipal high-dependency unit at Whinmoor.*

Martha read the letter twice.

She did not feel angry.

Anger was something that belonged to people who had choices—people like Nicholas Vane, who threw coffee cups into the waste-paper bins when the computer system was slow, or Brenda Hallowes, who kicked the medicine trolleys when her shift rate was cut by seven pounds.

When you were a domestic cleaner on Ward 7, anger was a luxury that cost three hours’ pay and your name on an incident report.

What she felt was the cold, clean stillness that came over her when she was stripping three coats of old polyurethane seal from a floor that hadn't been touched in twenty years: the knowledge that every square inch of the dirt had to be taken up with the blade, that no chemical would do the work for you, and that if you missed a single patch in the corner by the radiator, the whole floor would show yellow when the new wax went down.

She folded the letter once, sharp along the crease, and slipped it into the inside pocket of her tweed coat, right beside her spiral notebook.

Then she looked at the syringe driver.

The little red light winked: *one, two, three, inject.*

She knew the machine. She had seen Dr. Henderson show the student nurses how to set the rate on Ward 7 three years ago, before the new digital pumps came in.

There was no key. The Graseby MS16 used a small, slotted plastic screw inside the battery compartment to regulate the millimetre-advance per hour.

Martha reached out.

Her square, blunt thumb slid the battery cover back.

Inside, beside the heavy nine-volt zinc-carbon battery, was the small brass potentiometer screw.

She took her Stanley knife from her pocket. She used the tip of the blade—the three-millimeter carbon point—to turn the screw three-quarters of a turn to the left, until the white notch lined up with the number *0.5.*

Two millimeters an hour became half a millimeter.

Ten milligrams of midazolam became two and a half.

Enough to keep the pain from the old hip from waking her; not enough to drown her in her own bronchial mucus before the sun came up over the moor.

She slid the battery cover shut with a quiet, plastic *snick.*

She leaned down over the cot-side, took her mother’s cold, stiff right hand, and pressed the knuckles against her own cheek.

"I’m going to work, Mam," Martha whispered.

Evelyn did not answer. But as Martha turned toward the door, the old woman’s dry, pale lips moved against the pillow, small and faint like the rustle of a moth behind the skirting boards:

"The brown shoes, Martha. Don't let them... don't let them take the shoes."

"Nobody’s taking the shoes, Mam," Martha said.

She pulled the door of Room 12 shut behind her, walked past the empty manager's office without looking at the brass plate, and went out through the automatic doors into the afternoon rain that was beginning to wash the soot from the gritstone gateposts.

### Chapter 13

The Ambulatory Care Wing at St. Jude’s was eleven years old, but it had already begun to smell like an old airport terminal.

The architects had designed it with a four-storey central atrium clad in silver-anodized aluminum panels that rattled whenever the westerly gales hit the glass roof, and the floor was laid with light-gray ceramic tiles imported from Italy that showed every wet footprint from the main entrance to the outpatient pharmacy.

At ten o'clock that night, Martha was not on Ward 7.

She had traded shifts with young Shirley from the third floor—Shirley, whose baby had the chickenpox and who would have had to take unpaid parental leave if Martha hadn't signed the cover sheet in the supervisor’s office.

Ward 7 had been assigned to an agency cleaner from Preston who didn't know the pull on the buffer and would spend the night bumping the machine into the skirting boards and leaving dirty water in the corners.

Martha had requested the Fourth Floor: *Trust Executive Suites & Clinical Governance.*

Mrs. Noonan had been suspicious when Martha asked for the slip.

"You don't want the Fourth, Martha," Noonan had wheezed, her purple knitted cardigan held closed over her stomach with a giant plastic safety pin. "It’s all carpets up there. Heavy wool Wilton from the contract suppliers in Halifax. You have to use that upright Hoover that weighs three stone and pulls your shoulders out of the sockets. And Vane’s up there till midnight half the week, checking them computer dashboards."

"I don't mind the Hoover," Martha had said. "The air's cleaner up there. The radiators don't hiss like the ones on Geriatrics."

Now she was standing on the Fourth Floor landing, the three-stone blue Nilfisk upright vacuum cleaner parked beside her on the gray carpet.

The corridor was forty yards long, wide enough for two executive golf buggies to pass, with walls painted in a soft, non-clinical dove gray. Every ten paces was a black-and-white photograph in a thin aluminum frame, showing historical scenes from the hospital’s past: the opening of the King Edward VII Operating Theatre in 1904; the nurses' hockey team in 1928 with their heavy leather shin-guards; the arrival of the first mobile X-ray van during the blitz of 1941.

None of the photographs showed the coal-cellars.

None of them showed the mortuary yard where the coal-carts used to tip their loads into the boiler risers, or the small, windowless holding rooms behind the old casualty department where the police used to bring the drunks to sleep off their porter before the magistrates' court opened on Monday morning.

At the far end of the corridor, beneath a sign that said *SUITE 402 — MEDICAL DIRECTOR & CLINICAL AUDIT,* a line of bright white LED light cut across the carpet from under the double walnut-veneered doors.

Martha took her spray bottle of multi-surface cleaner and two yellow duster cloths from the shelf of her trolley.

She did not turn the vacuum cleaner on.

The Nilfisk was too loud; it had a high, screaming turbine that sounded like a jet engine taxiing on tarmac, and it would bring a security guard up from the ground floor within four minutes.

She walked in her soft rubber clogs along the edge of the carpet, where the pile was less compressed by the feet of consultants and Trust directors, her body moving with that quiet, drifting ease that domestic cleaners learn after their first five years—the art of being indistinguishable from the furniture.

Through the glass panel beside the door—frosted glass, but with three clear, horizontal lines etched across the center at eye level—she could see the room.

It was three times the size of her living room on the estate.

The desk was an enormous slab of pale oak, clear except for a double-screen monitor on an articulated arm, a telephone with twenty-four programmable extension buttons, and a single, heavy glass tumbler containing two inches of amber liquid that had an ice cube melting in the center.

Behind the desk sat Dr. Peter Henderson.

He was fifty-one now. His hair, which had been dark and curly in the photographs from the nineteen-nineties, was cut very short, silver-white at the temples, combed back with the smooth, expensive precision of a man who paid forty-five pounds for a trim at the hotel salon in Manchester. He wore a crisp white shirt with French cuffs—the gold links shaped like small stethoscopes—and a dark blue silk tie that had been loosened an inch at the collar.

Opposite him, sitting on the corner of the oak desk with one leg swinging, was Nicholas Vane.

Vane still had his iPad in his hand, but he wasn't looking at the screen. He was looking at a thick, buff-colored box file that lay open between them on the wood.

"...the retention schedule was quite clear, Peter," Vane was saying.

His voice came through the acoustic seals around the doorframe—not loud, but clear enough for Martha’s ears, which had spent thirty years separating the sound of a falling drip-stand from the clatter of a tea trolley.

"Any clinical incident involving police presence prior to the 2000 Human Rights Act transition was supposed to have been archived under the legal privilege exemption," Vane said. "It should never have been in the general ward series in Three-B. If the domestic staff hadn't been salvaging waste from the recycling crates, the entire file would have been through the shredder by Tuesday afternoon."

Henderson picked up his tumbler.

He took a slow sip of the whisky, his throat working with a heavy, dry swallow that made the skin above his collar pull tight.

"You're sure it was the Gould woman?" Henderson asked.

"She's been taking them for years," Vane said, his voice flat with disgust. "The supervisor—that fat creature Noonan—admitted it when I questioned her this afternoon. Said Martha Gould has a 'thing' about old papers. Takes them home in her shopping bag to light her coal fire on the estate. She’s got half the nineteen-nineties in that hovel of hers."

"Did she take the November book?"

"The November ninety-seven night book was missing from the shelf when the Shred-Con team pulled Box 14," Vane said. "And the transfer duplicate from Casualty wasn't in the registered file. If she has both, she has the original entry before the red-line void, and she has Hallows's signature on the police custody receipt."

Henderson set his glass down.

The heavy crystal base made a sharp, dry *tock* against the oak desk.

"Clitheroe's dead," Henderson said softly. "He died in a nursing home in Southport three years ago. His mind was completely gone. And John Hallows died of a carcinoma in twenty-twenty. There’s nobody left from the division who was in the yard."

"There's the sister," Vane said.

Henderson looked up. "Brenda?"

"She was on Ward 7 until seven this morning," Vane said, his thumb flicking across his screen. "I’ve pulled her agency timesheets. She’s been working three nights a week here for six months. Her father had an off-duty pocket book, Peter. The regional police standards unit contacted our legal department three weeks ago. They’re running a historic review into the Railway Hotel licenses from eighty-five to ninety-eight. If Brenda Hallows has that pocket book and she hands it to the Gould woman, we don't have an administrative irregularity. We have a corporate manslaughter referral to the Crown Prosecution Service."

Henderson rubbed his temples with the heels of his hands.

Under the bright white LED downlights, his face looked old—suddenly and terribly old, the smooth, expensive skin around his eyes collapsing into deep, gray hollows that showed the shape of his eye sockets beneath.

"I was twenty-five, Nicholas," he whispered. "I was twenty-five years old. I’d been on duty for thirty-six hours without sleep. Clitheroe told me the lad had fallen off the weir. He said if I put him into the surgical theater, the registrar would lose the bypass patient who was on the table. He told me he’d take full responsibility for the custody log."

"Nobody cares about thirty-six hours, Peter," Vane said, his voice dropping into that cold, clinical cadence that Martha had heard him use on the shredder driver. "This is not nineteen-ninety-seven. The CQC doesn't care about young house officers with tired feet. They care about *corporate governance failures.* If this incident is reopened, the Trust's Foundation authorization is revoked on the spot. The nine-million-pound capital grant for the new cancer center is cancelled. And you and I will spend the next three years sitting in a hearing room at the Queen Elizabeth II Conference Centre in London, being interrogated by junior barristers from the public inquiry."

Vane leaned forward, both hands flat on the desk.

"Where is the old woman?"

"At Highfield," Henderson said. "I initiated the end-stage protocol this morning. Ten milligrams of midazolam. She won't survive the weekend, Nicholas. Her renal function was already down to fifteen percent. Once the sedation is established, the death is certified as multi-infarct dementia with hypostatic pneumonia. It’s entirely routine. The local authority will sign off on the public health funeral within forty-eight hours."

"And the daughter?"

"The daughter has no legal standing once the mother is deceased," Vane said. "She’s not the executor; there’s no will. Under the Law of Property Act, the tenancy on Moor Lane reverts to the council on the death of the primary tenant. The housing association has already issued a Category 2 remediation notice for unauthorized storage. Their contractors will clear the flat on Monday morning."

He stood up, sliding his iPad into its gray felt sleeve.

"Every scrap of paper in that flat goes into the council skip on Monday at eight o'clock," Vane said. "I’ve arranged with the site manager at Broadwater Homes to have the skip taken directly to the municipal incinerator at Whinmoor. It won't even touch a sorting belt."

Henderson looked at the window.

The reflection of his own face hung in the dark glass, pale and transparent against the lights of the city below.

"And if she goes to the police before Monday?"

"With what?" Vane said, his mouth curling into a small, thin line that was not quite a smile. "A forty-year-old hospital cleaner with two stolen ledgers and a notebook full of dying people’s ravings? Who’s going to listen to her, Peter? The Chief Constable? The local rag? The *Guardian* doesn't write about night domestics who steal scrap paper from the recycling bins. By Monday afternoon, Martha Gould won't have a job, she won't have a flat, and her mother will be in the cold-room at the municipal undertakers."

He picked up his briefcase from the floor.

"Go home, Peter. Get some sleep. Have your wife give you one of those little blue pills you prescribe for the private patients. By Tuesday morning, St. Jude’s will be paperless, the foundation assessment will be green across all four quadrants, and you can buy that boat you’ve been looking at in Dartmouth."

The two men moved toward the inner office door that led through to the executive lift lobby.

Martha did not move.

She stood in the shadow of the linen cupboard, her yellow duster held flat against her thigh, her clogs silent on the gray wool carpet.

The lights in Suite 402 clicked off with a soft, electronic *chirp* as the motion sensors detected that the room was empty.

Inside the dark office, on the oak desk, the crystal glass sat in the pale moonlight that came through the high double-glazed sashes, the water from the melted ice cube spreading in a thin, greasy ring across the varnish that had never seen a drop of wax in its life.

Martha looked at the door.

She reached into her tabard pocket, pulled out her narrow reporter's pad, and in the dim green light of the emergency exit sign, she wrote three lines:

*Henderson & Vane. 22:48. Suite 402.*  
*'Every scrap of paper goes into the council skip on Monday at eight o'clock.'*  
*Whinmoor incinerator.*

She closed the pad.

Then she walked to her Nilfisk vacuum cleaner, took the handle in both hands, clicked the red power switch with her right clog, and let the screaming, three-stone motor pull her down the long, gray corridor toward the fire stairs.

### Chapter 14

At three in the morning, the hospital had a second pulse.

It was not the pulse of the living—not the sharp, electric rhythm of the monitors in the intensive care unit or the frantic ringing of the bells in Casualty when the ambulances came in three deep from the motorway. It was the slow, sluggish movement of the waste: the gray linen sacks tumbling down the metal chutes from the fourth floor, the rattle of the macerators in twenty different sluice rooms grinding down cardboard and sputum, and the hum of the cold-compressors in the mortuary basement keeping forty-two stainless-steel trays at four degrees Celsius.

Martha had returned to the sub-basement.

She had an hour before the four o'clock tea break, when the six night domestics were supposed to sit in the locker room and drink their instant tea out of plastic cups while Mrs. Noonan watched the shopping channel on the little portable television.

The archive corridor in Three-B was forty yards of peeling whitewash and exposed cast-iron heating pipes that had been cold since the coal strike.

At the far end, outside the double doors of the old dispensary that had been closed in 1988, stood three large, gray plastic wheelie-bins. Each bin had a red plastic seal through the hasp, stamped with the words: *SHRED-CON SECURE DESTRUCTION — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY.*

Beside the bins, stacked four high against the damp brickwork of the light-well, were twelve heavy corrugated cardboard cartons.

Each carton was two feet long, eighteen inches wide, tied with heavy jute string that had rotted down to brown fluff where it touched the concrete floor. Across the front of the top box, stenciled in black spray paint that had run in the damp:

*CENTRAL ARCHIVE — CASUALTY & ADMISSIONS — 1996 TO 1999 (REDUNDANT).*

The shredder lorry had gone for the night; its empty bay on the loading dock was dark, the iron roller-shutter pulled down and padlocked from the inside. But the driver had left his hydraulic hand-truck parked against the wall, its yellow steel forks resting on the floor slabs like the teeth of a sleeping animal.

Martha walked to the stack of boxes.

Her rubber shoes made no sound on the wet concrete. The air smelled of damp cellar-mould, coal-dust, and the faint, sweet trace of the hydraulic fluid that had leaked from the hand-truck’s pump.

She knelt by the bottom carton.

Her knees gave their double-click—*tock-tock*—the sound hollow in the long, vaulted brick tunnel.

She didn't use her knife. The jute string on Box 14 was so rotten that when she hooked her index finger under the knot, it snapped with a dry, papery *pop* like a dead dandelion stem.

She pulled back the cardboard flaps.

Inside, laid in three neat rows with sheets of yellowed tissue paper between them, were forty-two black-bound books.

They were not the ward ledgers.

These were smaller, five inches by eight, bound in heavy black buckram with red morocco spines:

*ST. JUDE’S HOSPITAL — ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY — CASUALTY OFFICER’S LOG.*

Martha reached into the center row.

Her hand went straight to the volume marked with a small white paper label gummed to the spine:

*VOL. 84 — OCTOBER 1997 TO DECEMBER 1997.*

She opened the cover.

The first fifty pages were missing.

They hadn't been torn out with a ruler; they had been cut out with a scalpel, the sharp blade leaving forty-two clean, white paper stubs along the inner margin, right where the thread had been stitched into the cloth tape.

Across the stub of page forty-eight, written in the same red china marker that had defaced the Ward 4 night book:

*REMOVED UNDER LEGAL PRIVILEGE — 18/11/97. TRANSFERRED TO SAFE 2, TRUST SECRETARIAT. BY ORDER: J. FINCH, CHIEF EXECUTIVE.*

Martha touched the white stubs.

They had thought of everything.

Even then, forty-eight hours after Liam had died in the side room with his wrist strapped to the cot-side, Finch and the Trust solicitors had gone through the hospital with their scalpels, cutting the boy’s name out of the emergency intake, out of the porter’s book, out of the theatre registry, leaving only the ward night book that Sister Boyd had locked in her personal tea-cabinet behind the maternity pads.

And Boyd had never given it up.

Catherine Boyd had stayed at St. Jude’s until 2004, when she retired to a small convent of the Sisters of Mercy outside Sligo. She had never married; she had never had a bank account that paid interest; she had owned two black wool skirts, four white nylon blouses, and a leather suitcase that held her mother’s prayer book and her certificate of midwifery from the Coombe Hospital in Dublin.

Before she left, Boyd had walked into the basement, pulled the 1997 night book out of her canvas holdall, and dropped it into the blue recycling crate in the third-floor sluice room, right where she knew the night domestics would find it before the incinerator men arrived.

Boyd had known Martha Gould.

She had seen Martha’s mother bringing the clean laundry to the nurses' home in nineteen-sixty-eight; she had seen Liam when he was four years old, sitting on the wall outside the casualty entrance with a grazed knee and a sugar-bag in his fist.

She hadn't had the courage to go to the police—not with Clitheroe sitting in the station on Commercial Street and the Borough magistrates dining with the hospital consultants every second Tuesday at the Conservative Club.

So she had left the paper for the floor-cleaner.

Because she knew that in forty years, when the doctors had taken their pensions and the chief executives had gone to their graves with their CBEs in velvet boxes, the women with the mops would still be walking the corridors, checking the corners where the wax collected and keeping the things that the management had thrown into the bins.

Martha closed Box 14.

She did not take the mutilated volume. A book with fifty missing pages was only evidence of what someone had tried to hide; it wasn't proof of what had happened to the flesh and the bone.

She stood up, her joints cracking in the damp air.

She walked to the end of the tunnel, pushed open the heavy wooden door that led to the boiler house stairs, and began to climb the sixty-eight cast-iron steps that led up to the light-well behind Ward 7.

### Chapter 15

At five in the morning, the snow began.

It was not the soft, decorative snow that settled on the holly trees in the park; it was the thin, hard grit that blew off the Irish Sea across the Fylde, freezing as it hit the asphalt of the ambulance ramp and turning the steps of the casualty entrance into a slide of yellow ice.

Martha stood in the kitchen of Ward 7, waiting for the electric kettle to boil.

The kettle was a heavy aluminum swan-neck that had been donated by the League of Friends in nineteen-eighty-five; it took twelve minutes to bring four pints of water to the turn, and its element hummed with a fierce, angry rattle that made the tin mugs on the draining board dance against the porcelain.

The door behind her clicked open.

It was not Brenda Hallowes.

It was Nicholas Vane.

He had taken off his dark wool coat, but he still wore his charcoal suit, the trousers slightly creased now around the groin from six hours of sitting in the executive leather chair on the fourth floor. His tie was straight, his glasses clean, but the skin around his nostrils had that pinched, white look that comes from breathing air-conditioned air and drinking black coffee from a thermos flask since midnight.

He did not come into the kitchen. He stood in the doorway, his shoulder leaning against the jamb, his iPad held loose in his left hand by his hip.

"Gould," he said.

Martha did not turn around. She kept her eyes on the spout of the kettle, where the first thin feather of steam was beginning to curl into the cold air.

"Mr. Vane."

"You've had a long career at St. Jude's," he said.

### Chapter 18

The carriage works had been dead for thirty-two years.

If that notebook is produced in a court of law, Martha, the Trust’s barristers will destroy his daughter’s career before she can step out of the witness box. They will have her struck off the nursing register for concealing stolen evidence. They will take her house. They will leave those two children of hers on the parish within six weeks."

"And what will you have?" he asked. "A dead brother who’s been under the sod for twenty-six years. A mother who doesn't know what month it is. And an old cleaner who will spend her sixty-first year in the remand wing at Styal for stealing public documents."

### Chapter 20

The chapel of rest at St. Oswald’s was four feet below the street level, squeezed between the north wall of the church and the retaining wall of the railway cutting.

It had been built in the eighteen-seventies for the Irish laborers who died of the cholera while digging the tunnel under the ridge: a low, barrel-vaulted crypt of dressed millstone grit that had no windows, no heating, and three stone slabs set into the flags where the coffins sat on iron trestles while the priests said the De Profundis in the candle-light.

Father Michael was eighty-one.

He had lived in the presbytery behind the church since nineteen-sixty-nine, a small, dried-up walnut of a man who wore three woolen waistcoats under his black cassock and whose spectacles were tied to his ears with lengths of green garden twine.

He was sitting on a wooden bench by the iron stove, peeling an apple with a bone-handled pocket knife, when Martha came down the stone steps.

"Martha Gould," he said without looking up.

The voice was thin, ancient, smelling of pipe tobacco and peppermint drops.

"Father," she said.

"Your mother didn't come to the altar rail on Sunday," he said, the apple skin curling in a long, unbroken green ribbon from his blade. "Old Mrs. Preece told me Evelyn had been taken with the chest."

"She's at Highfield," Martha said. "Room 12."

The priest took a bite of the white apple flesh, his gums working with a slow, mechanical rhythm.

"Highfield," he muttered. "A terrible place. All that plastic flowers and the carpet that smells like a chemist's shop. A Christian should die in his own bed, Martha, with the window open an inch so the soul can get out over the chimney-pots."

Martha walked to the central stone slab.

The chapel was empty. On the slab lay an old oak bier, its leather straps dark with grease, and beside it a stack of folded purple velvet palls that had been used for the funerals of the railwaymen since the First World War.

She reached under her coat.

She pulled out the black rexine pocket-book.

The cold of the crypt seemed to rise from the flagstones to meet it, the dry smell of old incense and coal-dust wrapping around the worn cloth spine like an old coat.

"I need you to keep something, Father," she said.

Father Michael stopped his knife.

He looked at the small black book in her hand. His eyes were small, watery, but deep inside the wrinkled sockets was that sharp, peasant cunning that sixty years of hearing the confessions of drunken dockers and frightened thieves had beaten into his skull.

"Whose is it?" he asked.

"John Hallows," she said.

The priest’s jaw stopped working. He set the apple down on his knee, the bone-handled knife held upright in his fist like a little dagger.

"Hallows," he whispered. "The sergeant from Commercial Street."

"Aye."

"He was a bad man, Martha," the priest said softly, looking at the purple palls on the trestle. "A hard, bitter man with the drink in him. He came here once, in ninety-eight, after the boy died. He sat on that very bench by the stove for two hours on a Saturday night. He wouldn't go into the box. He said, 'Father, if I tell you what was in the yard behind the Railway, you’ll have to give me three thousand Hail Marys and tell the bishop to unfrock me.' I told him the Mercy of God had room for two hundred sergeants and all their inspectors. But he went out into the rain without the blessing."

"He wrote it down," Martha said.

She laid the black book on the oak bier, right between the two leather straps that had carried her father and her uncle to the municipal cemetery at Whinmoor.

"The doctors are coming for my flat on Monday morning," she said. "They have two constables and a paper from the judge. They’re going to search the scullery for the hospital books."

"Where are the hospital books?"

"In the boiler house," Martha said. "Behind the blue bricks."

Father Michael picked up the black notebook.

His fingers were yellowed with tobacco, the skin transparent as tracing paper over the blue veins. He didn't open it. He weighed it in his palm, testing the balance of thirty-year-old ink against the stone walls of the crypt.

"They'll destroy you, Martha," he said.

"They've already destroyed me, Father," she said, her voice flat, level, the voice of the buffer on the night shift. "They destroyed me on the fifteenth of November nineteen-ninety-seven, when they told my mother her boy had drowned in four feet of canal water with his shoes on."

The priest looked toward the low, arched door that led up to the street.

Above them, through the six feet of earth and gritstone, came the rumble of the afternoon coal train from the docks, the vibration traveling down through the foundations of St. Oswald’s until the brass candlesticks on the altar gave a small, shivering ring in the dark.

"There is an inquiry," Father Michael said.

Martha stopped with her hand on the iron rail of the stairs.

"What inquiry?"

"The Police Ombudsman," the old man said, slipping the black book into the deep, greasy pocket of his woolen waistcoat, beneath his black cassock. "A young woman came to see me three weeks ago. From Manchester. Miss Varma. She had a briefcase with the royal arms on the leather. She was asking about the burials from ninety-seven. She was asking why Liam Gould had been buried in unconsecrated ground at Whinmoor without an inquest verdict on the church register."

He touched the silver cross at his throat.

"I told her I didn't know," Father Michael said. "I told her Father Thomas had the parish then, and Father Thomas went to his reward in nineteen-ninety-nine. But I have her card, Martha. It’s in the sacristy, behind the bottle of altar wine."

Martha turned on the stone step.

"Give it to me," she said.

The priest stood up, his cassock rustling against the flags like dry reeds by the weir.

"God doesn't work through inspectors, Martha," he murmured, shuffling toward the iron curtain that led into the vestry. "He works through the women who have to scrub the flags after the men have done their murder."

"I know," Martha said. "That’s why I brought the book."

### Chapter 21

Sunday night came down over the Broadwater Estate like a wet wool blanket dropped over a dying fire.

Martha sat at her kitchen table.

She had her uniform on: the navy nylon tabard, the clean white cotton socks, the rubber-soled clogs that she had scrubbed with carbolic until the black rubber showed no trace of the hospital wax.

Her spiral reporter's pad, open to the page that bore the names of Albert Garside and Liam Gould.

### Chapter 22

At three o'clock on Monday morning, Ward 7 was as cold as a stone trough.

The heating engineers had shut down Boiler 3 for "scheduled maintenance" at midnight, which meant the hot water in the sluice room taps had dropped to a lukewarm dribble that wouldn't melt the tallow on a candle, and the radiators along the south wall were making a hollow, sucking sound like a horse trying to drink from an empty bucket.

"Martha, you can't go down there," Brenda said, reaching out to catch her arm, her fingers cold and wet through the nylon of the tabard. "Vane’s got two private security guards from the firm in Leeds. They’ve got dogs in the back of the van, Martha. They’re guarding the loading dock."

Martha looked down at Brenda’s hand.

"I know every service duct from the boiler house to the mortuary," she said. "I know the tunnel where the coal-cars used to run under the chapel before your father was born. There isn't a lock in that basement that hasn't had my oil on the wards since nineteen-ninety-three."

### Chapter 23

The tunnel beneath the chapel of rest had never been drawn on the hospital's modern fire evacuation plans.

On the architects' laminated schematics that hung beside the lifts, the space between the laundry basement and the mortuary was simply marked with gray diagonal hatchings and the notation: *UNEXCAVATED VOID / HISTORIC SUB-STRUCTURE.*

Martha knew the hatch.

It was hidden behind the condemned iron calorifier in the old wash-house, covered by a sheet of corrugated zinc held down by three heavy building bricks from the nineteen-thirties. When she lifted the zinc, the smell that came out was not the cold, chemical bleach of the modern wards; it was the heavy, sweet, mineral breath of the red sandstone ridge—seventy feet of damp rock sweating into five feet of unmortared brick cellar where the hospital’s original coal-cars had run on wooden rails from the canal wharf to the boilers in the days of Queen Victoria.

She didn't use an electric torch.

A torch was a yellow circle that moved on the walls, a warning that brought security guards with walkie-talkies down the service stairs.

She carried a four-inch piece of paraffin wax candle she had taken from the chapel sacristy three winters ago, its wick trimmed short with her scissors so it burned with a small, blue-centered flame that threw no more light than a glow-worm in a ditch. She had to walk with her shoulders rounded, her head tilted forward so that the coarse wool of her coat brushed the whitewashed barrel vault. She counted the brick piers.

*One. Two. Three.*

Above her, through three feet of stone flags and horsehair plaster, she could hear the dull, rhythmic thrumming of the mortuary compressors: *chug-hiss, chug-hiss.*

*Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.*

She stopped.

To her right, set into an arched alcove that had once been the inspection pit for the coal trucks, was a low opening three feet square. The opening had been covered with a piece of heavy expanded steel mesh, secured at the four corners with half-inch coach bolts driven into the mortar.

Martha set her candle stub on a projecting brick. She took her Stanley knife, reversed the tool in her fist, and used the steel butt of the casing to strike the remaining bolt two sharp, heavy blows.

The rusted iron gave way with a dry *ping* like a snapped piano string.

She pulled the mesh back. It groaned on its iron hinges, the rust flaking off onto her rubber shoes like dry cinnamon.

She climbed through on her hands and knees.

The space beyond was the dead-air cavity behind the back wall of the old dispensary.

It was three feet wide, twenty feet long, running between the eighteenth-century sandstone foundations of the original infirmary and the modern concrete breeze-block wall that had been built in nineteen-ninety-one to create the secure archive known as Three-B.

### Chapter 24

The locksmith from Manchester was kneeling on an upturned plastic milk crate in front of Safe 2.

### Chapter 30

The four books sat in the basement of the County Hall in Lancaster for seventeen days.

*1997.*

### Chapter 31

The funeral took place on a Wednesday in the second week of January.

*EVELYN GOULD*  
*1942 – 2026*

*One.*  
*Two.*  
*Three.*

### Chapter 32

### Chapter 33

### Chapter 36

"Take the book in your right hand," the usher said.

Martha took the book.

The leather was warm from the hand of the police detective who had sworn his oath ten minutes earlier.

"I swear by Almighty God," Martha said, her voice flat, clear, reaching every corner of the high, timbered ceiling without an echo, "that the evidence I shall give to the court shall be the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth."

She kissed the book.

She set it on the small wooden ledge beside the water carafe.

David Ross KC stood up at the inner bar.

He adjusted his wig with both hands, pulling the white horsehair forward until it sat two inches above his red, bushy eyebrows. He didn't look at his brief; he kept his hands tucked into the pockets of his waistcoat, his thumbs hooked over the silver watch-chain.

"Miss Gould," Ross said, his voice soft, melodic, the voice of an uncle asking about a school report. "How long have you been employed by the National Health Service?"

"Thirty-one years," Martha said. "Thirty-two come November."

"And what is your current post?"

"Domestic assistant, Grade 1," she said. "Ward 7. The night turn."

"Have you ever been employed in any other capacity at St. Jude’s Hospital?"

"No," she said. "Just the floors. And the brasses on the front entrance when the League of Friends have their annual meeting."

Ross turned half-an-inch toward the jury box.

The twelve jurors—eight men and four women from the cotton towns and the farming villages of the Fylde—were watching her with that intense, silent scrutiny that northern people give to a horse that has been brought to the fair without a halter.

"Miss Gould," Ross said, "I want to direct your attention to the four ledger volumes currently marked as Exhibit Twenty-Two."

He nodded to the court clerk.

The clerk lifted the four heavy, cloth-bound books from the table in the well of the court and laid them on the ledge of the witness box, right in front of Martha’s hands.

The bindings were worn, their corners showing the gray strawboard, the buckram on Volume 4 still bearing the faint, dark outline of the red china marker where Dr. Henderson had drawn his four-inch line across the name of Liam Gould.

"Do you recognize these books, Miss Gould?"

"Aye," Martha said.

"Where did you first see them?"

"In the blue crates," Martha said. "The recycling bins in the third-floor sluice room."

"And why did you remove them from those bins?"

Martha looked at the books.

She reached out with her right index finger—the short, blunt finger that had the yellow wax-stain behind the nail—and touched the corner of Volume 4.

"Because they had the names in them," she said.

The courtroom was so quiet that the sound of the rain against the high leaded sashes sounded like handfuls of rice thrown against a barn door.

"What names, Miss Gould?" Ross asked softly.

"The names of the people who died when the lights were low," she said. "The ones who didn't have nobody to sign the cheques. The ones who worked forty years in the carding rooms and the boiler sheds, and when their chests gave out, the doctors put them in the side rooms where the bells didn't ring."

She looked at the dock.

She did not look at Nicholas Vane in his wheelchair.

She looked straight into the pale blue, watery eyes of Dr. Peter Henderson.

"Liam was twenty-four," Martha said. "He had his apprentice time nearly done. He was going to buy our mother the toaster from the Co-op with the little bell on it. That man in the brass buttons told Sergeant Clitheroe that Liam had fallen in the weir. He told my mother that her lad had been taken by the cold water before he could get his shoes off."

She tapped the buckram cover of Volume 4 with her knuckle.

The sound was small, dry, like a beetle behind the wallpaper.

"He died forty feet from my cleaning cupboard," she said. "With his left wrist tied to the iron of the cot-side with a two-inch calico bandage, and the vomit in his windpipe because that doctor had let the registrar take the suction unit to Theatre 1 to save a man who had two hundred shares in the railway."

In the dock, Peter Henderson did not move.

His manicured hands stayed on the dark oak rail, his silver hair catching the white light from the high windows, but from the corner of his left eye, a single, clear drop of water ran down through the red furrows of his cheek, hung for a second on the gray skin of his jaw, and dropped onto the gold-shield crest of his Cambridge tie without making a sound.

### Chapter 37

The cross-examination lasted forty-eight minutes.

Sir Julian Preece KC, the leader of the Northern Circuit who had been retained by the Medical Defence Union for eighty thousand pounds to defend Dr. Henderson, was a tall, thin man of sixty-five who looked like an elderly heron that had spent forty years standing in a cold ditch waiting for a trout.

He had long, pale, aristocratic hands that he held together under the skirts of his black silk gown, and his voice was so soft, so cultured, so musical that it sounded like an expensive gramophone playing chamber music in an empty drawing-room.

"Miss Gould," Sir Julian said, stepping to the lectern with a small, graceful inclination of his head. "We all have the profoundest sympathy for your family's loss. A young man of twenty-four, at the threshold of life... a tragedy that must have cast a very dark shadow over your mother’s final years."

Martha did not answer.

She stood with her hands folded over the ledge of the box, her face set in the flat, gray lines of an Accrington brick.

"You have testified," Sir Julian said, "that you removed these volumes from the hospital premises between twenty-twelve and twenty-twenty."

"Aye," Martha said.

"And you were aware, were you not, that these documents were the property of the St. Jude’s Hospital NHS Trust?"

"They weren't in the hospital," Martha said. "They were in the rubbish."

Sir Julian smiled—a thin, delicate, papery smile that showed two long canine teeth that had been capped with platinum.

"The recycling bins, Miss Gould. A temporary staging location for confidential waste prior to professional destruction under the Trust’s information governance protocols."

"The bins were in the sluice room," Martha said. "Under the bedpan washer. The bedpan washer had a leak on the waste-seal that dripped three pints of gray water an hour into the bottom of the crate. If I hadn't taken them books out, the paper would have been pulp before the refuse lorry came from Skelmersdale on Thursday morning."

Sir Julian turned a page of his brief with a dry, rustling snap.

"You took them to your home on the Broadwater Estate?"

"Aye."

"And you kept them beneath your kitchen sink?"

"Behind the copper pipe," she said. "Where the air was dry."

"For twelve years?"

"For twelve years."

Sir Julian leaned forward, his black silk gown billowing around his shoulders like the wings of a bat.

"Miss Gould, during those twelve years, did you ever make an application to the Trust Secretariat under the Freedom of Information Act?"

"No," Martha said. "I don't have no typewriter."

A small, nervous ripple of laughter went through the public gallery, but it died instantly when the judge—Mr. Justice Holroyd, a sixty-year-old former coal-board arbitrator from Barnsley—turned his cold, gray eyes toward the benches.

"Did you ever consult a solicitor?" Sir Julian asked.

"A solicitor costs sixty pound an hour," Martha said. "My take-home was three hundred and forty-two pound a week. I had two hundred and eighty-four pound to pay to Mrs. Corfield every Thursday to keep my mother from the garden level at Highfield. If I’d spent sixty pound on a solicitor, my mother would have been on the plastic chairs with the game-shows four years ago."

Sir Julian paused.

He looked at the jury box.

He saw what David Ross had seen three days earlier: twelve working people from the north who knew to the penny what an hour with an attorney cost, who knew what happened to an old woman when her local authority grant was cut, and who knew the difference between an administrative protocol and an eight-ton shredder lorry running in a cellar at three in the morning.

He changed his footing.

"Miss Gould," he said, his voice dropping into that soft, dangerous whisper that barristers use when they are trying to lead a witness onto the ice. "You kept a notebook, did you not?"

Martha’s hand went to the pocket of her coat.

"Aye," she said. "I keep a notebook."

"A notebook in which you recorded the private, confidential statements of patients under your care?"

"They weren't under my care," Martha said. "They were under the doctors' care. The doctors gave them the medicine and went to their beds. I was the one who had to clean the floors."

"You wrote down what they said in their delirium?"

"I wrote down what they said when they were frightened," she said.

Her voice had that thirty-year cadence again: the cadence that didn't move for the high-court judges, didn't move for the silk gowns, and didn't move for the eighty-thousand-pound briefs from the Temple.

"Albert Garside talked about his rivets," she said, looking straight at Sir Julian’s long, pale nose. "Mrs. Cleary talked about her red scarf. And my brother Liam talked about his time at the works."

She stepped half-an-inch closer to the brass rail of the box.

"If a person lives seventy years on this earth, Sir Julian, and forty of them was spent in grease, somebody ought to write his name down before the bed is stripped for the next one. That’s not stealing. That’s just being a Christian."

Sir Julian stood with his brief half-open in his long, aristocratic fingers.

For five seconds, the only sound in Courtroom 1 was the ticking of the mahogany wall-clock above the judge’s bench: *tock-tock, tock-tock.*

Then he closed the file with a small, flat slap of calfskin on card.

"Thank you, Miss Gould," Sir Julian said, his voice barely audible above the clock. "I have no further questions."

### Chapter 38

The jury was out for three hours and twelve minutes.

They came back at four o'clock on Friday afternoon, while the winter sleet was turning the cobblestones of the market square outside into a gray slide of slush.

The foreman was a forty-year-old diesel mechanic from the bus garage at Chorley: a man with broad, flat thumbs, a blue nylon fleece that he hadn't unzipped all week, and a small gold ring in his left ear.

"On the first count of the indictment," the clerk of the court asked, "conspiracy to pervert the course of justice, do you find the defendant Peter Henderson guilty or not guilty?"

"Guilty," the mechanic said.

The word was flat, hard, with the short Lancashire *u* that sounded like an iron bolt being driven home with a copper mallet.

"And on that count, do you find the defendant Nicholas Vane guilty or not guilty?"

"Guilty."

"On the second count of the indictment, statutory theft of public records..."

"Guilty against both."

"On the third count, misconduct in public office..."

"Guilty against Peter Henderson."

In the dock, Nicholas Vane put his head down on the wooden rail of his wheelchair and began to weep: small, wet, rhythmic sobs that made the white plaster of his hip-spica creak against the oak timber.

Peter Henderson did not weep.

He stood up straight, his hands by his sides, his blazer buttoned across his narrow chest, looking at the royal arms above the judge’s bench with the cold, vacant dignity of an officer whose sword has just been broken on the parade ground.

Mr. Justice Holroyd did not postpone the sentencing.

He pulled a small square of black silk from the pocket of his scarlet robe—not the death cap, but the old, formal silk handkerchief that northern judges carried when they had to pass seven-year sentences on men who had been to public school.

"Peter Henderson," the judge said, his voice dry, harsh as gravel sliding down a chute. "You were a physician. You had sworn the ancient oath that binds your profession to the preservation of human life. You were presented with a young man of twenty-four, injured in a squalid brawl that arose from the criminal activities of corrupt police officers and a venal publican."

The judge leaned forward, both elbows on the bench.

"Instead of exercising your skill to preserve that life, or at the very least ensuring that his final hours were attended with the dignity that is the common inheritance of every subject of the Crown, you participated in a cold, calculating conspiracy to conceal his admission, to falsify the clinical records, and to permit him to die in an unmonitored room without the commonest instruments of medical relief."

The judge looked at the dock.

"You did so not from panic, but from ambition. You did so because you wished to preserve your own career, your own advancement, and your own comfort. For twenty-six years, you built an eminent reputation upon the foundation of that young man’s unrecorded agony. You became a director of the Trust; you sat upon the regional boards; you accumulated the honors and the emoluments of your calling, while his sister scrubbed the floors beneath your feet and his mother waited in vain for the sound of his boots upon her flags."

The black silk handkerchief twitched between his fingers.

"On the first count, you will go to prison for seven years. On the second count, three years, to run concurrently. On the third count, five years, to run consecutively. A total of twelve years' imprisonment."

He turned to the wheelchair.

"Nicholas Vane. You are a younger man, but your moral culpability is scarcely less grave. You brought into a great public institution the ruthless, amoral efficiency of the modern corporate balance-sheet. You regarded eighty years of human suffering as a 'square-footage liability.' You treated the memory of the dead as an administrative inconvenience to be eliminated by the teeth of an industrial shredder in the dark of the night."

The judge’s mouth tightened until his lips were white.

"You will go to prison for four years."

The two prison officers stepped forward.

They unlocked the rear gate of the dock.

Peter Henderson turned without a word, walked down the narrow stone stairs that led to the holding cells below, his polished shoes clicking on the iron treads with the same slow, measured cadence that Martha Gould had heard on the linoleum of Ward 7.

Nicholas Vane’s wheelchair was wheeled down the ramp by two burly officers from Manchester, the rubber tires leaving two wet tracks on the fumed oak floor that would take twenty minutes with a nylon pad to remove.

Martha Gould stood up from the public bench.

She picked up her Co-op bag from between her feet, buttoned her brown tweed coat to the throat, and walked out through the double doors into the gray Lancashire rain, her clogs clicking on the flagstones of the porch with that thirty-one-year rhythm that had outlasted eight chief executives, three ward reorganizations, and two wars that nobody remembered the names of anymore.

### Chapter 39

The spring came early to the Fylde in twenty-twenty-seven.

By the second week of March, the hawthorn along the railway cutting had put out its first pale green leaves—that delicate, watery green that northern people call the "bread-and-cheese"—and the coltsfoot had begun to push its yellow buttons through the cinders between the sleepers on the old siding.

On a Saturday morning when the sun was high and warm on the brickwork, seventy people gathered in the engine pit of the carriage works.

The roof was still gone, but the men from the union branch had spent three weekends clearing the brambles, laying down five tons of gray limestone gravel from the quarry at Clitheroe, and building a low stone parapet around the brick vault where Evelyn and Liam Gould lay side by side.

On the south wall of the boiler house, right above the hole where the five blue engineering bricks had lived for twenty-six years, sat a bronze plaque.

It had been cast by the retired molders from the ironworks, using eighty pounds of scrap copper split-pins that Billy Noonan had collected from forty different garden sheds on the estate.

The letters had been chased by hand with a three-cornered file:

*IN MEMORY OF*  
*LIAM GOULD (1973 – 1997)*  
*APPRENTICE BOILERMAKER — L&YR WORKS*  
*AND THE SEVENTY-FOUR UNRECORDED CITIZENS*  
*WHO DIED AT ST. JUDE’S HOSPITAL*  
*DURING THE SILENT YEARS*  
*1996 – 1999*  
*RESTORED TO THE ROLLS BY THE HAND OF MARTHA GOULD*  
*'THE NAME STAYS ON THE BRICK'*

Father Michael was there, wearing a clean white cotta over his cassock that had been bleached with chloride of lime by Mrs. Noonan, his green garden twine replaced by two silver-plated spectacle chains that had been donated by the League of Friends.

He sprinkled the bronze plaque with holy water from a small brass aspergillum, the drops hissing faintly as they hit the warm metal in the sun.

Beside him stood Brenda Hallowes.

She was wearing her dark blue sister’s tunic with the silver buckle, her hair cut short now, her face full and brown from the spring winds, holding her little girl by the hand.

"Look, Jack," Brenda whispered to the child, pointing to the bronze plate. "That's your granddad's town. That’s where the work was done."

Martha Gould stood by the corner of the wall.

She had her good coat on, her second-best shoes, and her hands were tucked into her pockets, her right fingers resting on the cracked face of Liam’s nickel watch.

The watch was ticking.

Billy Noonan had taken it down to an old watchmaker who lived in an attic behind the market, who had replaced the balance-staff with a piece of piano wire and cleaned forty years of boiler-grease from the brass gears with lighter fuel. It didn't keep modern time; it gained four minutes a day if the weather was damp, and lost three if the sun was on the glass, but the tick was loud, heavy, and regular, like the sound of an apprentice’s hammer on a cold boiler plate.

Miss Anjali Varma walked over to her.

She wore a gray wool coat that had no mud on the cuffs, and she was carrying a small, square parcel wrapped in brown butcher’s paper and tied with red cotton tape.

Martha did not reach out immediately.

"What is it?"

"It’s Volume 4," Miss Varma said. "The High Court made an order under the Coroners Rules. Martha took the parcel.

The paper was stiff, heavy, smelling of the archival paste and the beeswax they had used to seal the spine.

"Where will you keep it?" Miss Varma asked.

Martha looked at the boiler house chimney.

Above the eight-sided brick tower, two jackdaws were carrying twigs into the top flue, their black wings flashing like coal-shovels in the lemon sun.

"Under the sink," Martha said.

Miss Varma smiled.

"Behind the copper pipe?"

"Aye," Martha said. "Billy put the new washers in. The lead-faced ones from the wagon works. It’ll stay dry down there for forty years, and if the housing association sends another young man with an iPhone, I’ve got my father's footprint wrench in the scullery drawer, and I know how to turn the stopcock until the pressure blows."

### Chapter 40

At three o'clock on Monday morning, Ward 7 was quiet.

The heating had been balanced; the new condensing boilers in the sub-basement were humming with a smooth, continuous purr that had no knocking in the pipes, and the radiators along the south wall threw a steady, gentle warmth into the bays that smelled of clean wool and hot iron.

Martha Gould was working her buffer down the central aisle of Bay 2.

The machine was three weeks old—a new Numatic Hurricane with a twenty-inch drive-plate that had no pull to the left, but she had adjusted the handle with a strip of rubber tire-tubing so that it swung through her six-foot arcs with that old, familiar weight against her hip.

In Bed 4, where Albert Garside had died, sat an old man named Jack Ormerod.

He was seventy-two, a retired signalman from the box at the north junction, who had two new titanium knees and a chest that whistled like a curlew whenever he turned over in the bed.

"The three-forty from Hellifield," Jack murmured into the green light of the exit sign. "She's got two engines on the head. A Jubilee and an eight-F. Tell the stationmaster to clear the middle road. She’s got twenty-four wagons of limestone for the steelworks, and her brakes are running hot."

Martha clicked the switch on the handle.

The motor died down: *whine-hum-stop.*

The silence came in, clean, cold, and steady.

She reached into her tabard pocket.

Her red spiral pad had forty clean pages left.

She pulled out her two-inch stub of HB pencil, pressed the lead against the pale blue line, and by the light of the stars that were shining through the tall Victorian sashes over the railway cutting, she wrote:

*Ormerod, J. 03:14. Middle road. Limestone for the steelworks. Brakes running hot.*

She blew the gray graphite dust from the paper.

She looked down the long, green corridor toward the sister's office, where Brenda Hallowes was sitting under the desk lamp, her pen scratching rhythmically across the white clinical cards as she entered the four-hourly temperatures with a bottle of black Quink ink.

Brenda looked up through the glass.

She saw Martha.

She did not nod; she did not wave; she simply raised her left hand two inches from the blotter, her palm open, her fingers steady in the lamplight, the silver watch on her chest catching the reflection of the ward clock as the minute hand jumped to fifteen minutes past the hour.

Martha raised her hand in answer.

Then she clicked the red power switch on the Hurricane with her right clog, leaned her weight into the turn, and took the machine into the shadow of the fire escape doors, where the floor was dark, the wax was cold, and the names of the living and the dead were waiting to be gathered before the sun came up over the town.
### Chapter 41

By May, the lilacs behind the hospital laundry had bloomed and turned brown twice in the fluctuating heat of the valley.

The smell of the laundry changed with the weather; when the east wind blew off the peat mosses, the steam coming through the louvers of the drying house smelled of wet wool and the carbolic tallow they used for the operating theater blankets, but when the rain settled in from the Irish Sea, the air was clean, sharp with the ozone from the electric transformers behind the railway cutting.

Martha Gould was teaching young Shirley how to wax the cross-corridor.

Shirley was twenty-two, with two small children who had their noses permanently red from the damp in the bottom flats on Moor Lane, and she wore her hair in two tight cornrows pinned with pink plastic butterfly clips that rattled whenever she swung her mop.

"Don't fight the motor, Shirley," Martha said.

"Aye?" Martha said, blowing the steam from the surface of the tea.

"The Goulds didn't write the books," Martha said. "Sister Boyd wrote them. And Sister MacIntyre before her, and old Sister Clough who had the fever ward during the blitz. My brother only died in one of them."

"A book belongs to the person who saves it from the fire, Martha," Dr. MacIntyre said softly. "The people who wrote it were just doing their shifts. ### Chapter 42

On the second Saturday in June, the Broadwater Estate had its first street market since the lockout of nineteen-eighty-eight.

The council had closed Moor Lane from the bus turnaround down to the railway cutting, and forty tables had been set up on the broken tarmac between the front gardens: trestles of scrap timber covered with wallpaper remnants, selling old paperback westerns, hand-knitted cardigans with plastic pearl buttons, jars of rhubarb jam made from the canes behind the gasworks, and boxes of brass screws and iron washers that had been salvaged from the carriage sheds thirty years ago.

Billy Noonan was standing behind the table outside Number 14, wearing his good white shirt without a collar and his flat cap pushed to the back of his gray head.

On his table sat seventy-two miniature boilers.

He had made them in his garden shed from two-inch copper pipe he had salvaged from the hospital refit, with tiny brass fire-doors that opened on copper wire hinges and chimneys made of turned brass split-pins that had been polished with emery cloth until they caught the summer sun like little mirrors.

"She's quiet," Gemma said. "She has the butterscotch drops in her drawer, just like your mam. Every time I go in with the clean linen, she asks me if the bus from Clitheroe has come over the hill yet."

Gemma smiled, her freckled face bright in the summer light.

"I’ll tell her, Martha," she said.

She took her two boys by the hands and walked on down the lane toward the boiler house, the wooden horse clicking its red wheels against the tarmac with a dry, rhythmic sound that sounded like the points being changed on the middle road.

### Chapter 43

At seven o'clock that evening, Martha walked up the ridge toward Highfield.

She did not go to the home.

She stopped at the stone bridge where the road crossed the railway cutting, half a mile below the moorland wall.

Below her, forty feet down between the sheer limestone revetments that had been blasted out by the navvies in eighteen-forty-eight, the double track ran straight as a rifle shot toward the north. The rails were polished like silver knives by the passage of the evening trains, reflecting the pink and orange light of the sunset that was dying over the Irish Sea.

Beside the track, in the narrow strip of rough grass between the ballast and the drainage ditch, the wild raspberries had come into leaf, their pale, fuzzy shoots smelling of green water and stone-dust.

Martha leaned her elbows on the dressed freestone of the bridge parapet.

The stone was rough, warm from twelve hours of June sun, pitted with the small, gray lichens that northern people call "map-stone" because they look like the islands of a country that nobody has ever visited.

She reached into her coat pocket.

Her hand came out with the small black rexine notebook that Father Michael had given back to her after the trial: Sergeant John Hallows’s pocket-book.

The book was empty now.

The pages that had the times on them—the pages that had the names of Tommy Vane, Dr. Henderson, and the red china marker from the sister’s pantry—were in the permanent evidence vault of the Crown Court at Preston, mounted between sheets of archival mylar in a red buckram box that bore the royal arms in gold leaf.

Only the blank leaves remained: twenty-two pages of cream laid paper that had been ruled by hand with a steel nib in nineteen-ninety-seven.

Martha took her two-inch stub of HB pencil from her pocket.

She did not use the Stanley knife to sharpen it; the lead was rounded, smooth from six months of writing down the words that people said when the lights were low on Ward 7.

She opened the cover.

On the first blank page, beneath the stamped crest of the Borough Police, she wrote:

*14th June 2027.*  
*Ward 7. Day-shift hand-over.*  
*All beds clean.*  
*Linoleum waxed with two coats of Numatic standard.*  
*No unrecorded casualties.*

She looked at the five lines.

Her handwriting was not the copperplate of the old sergeants; it was not the loose, nervous slant of the junior doctors; it was the small, square, unshakeable hand of a woman who had spent thirty-one years signing her name on the key register in the sub-basement between seven-ten and seven-fifteen every morning of her life.

She turned the page.

On the next leaf, she wrote three more names:

*Albert Garside. Wagon-builder. The three-inch rivets.*  
*Agnes Clitheroe. Carder. The resin on the belt.*  
*Liam Gould. Boilermaker. The brown crepe-soled shoes.*

She closed the book.

The elastic band was still perished; it hung in its little gray loops from the spine like a piece of dry twine, but she tucked the book into the inside breast pocket of her tweed coat, right over her heart, where the stiff boards pressed against her ribs like an iron stay.

Below the bridge, a whistle blew.

It was the seven-fifteen freight from the docks: three locomotives on the head, their diesel exhausts blowing gray plumes of oily vapor against the limestone walls of the cutting, hauling forty wagons of imported iron ore for the blast furnaces at Scunthorpe.

The train did not stop.

It went through the bridge with a roar that shook the heavy gritstone parapet beneath Martha’s elbows, the wind from the wagons whipping her lace mantilla back from her ears and filling her nostrils with the sharp, familiar smell of burning brake-linings, hot axle-boxes, and wet steam coal.

Martha did not move.

She stood on the bridge until the red tail-lamp of the brake-van had vanished into the shadow of the tunnel under the moor, until the sound of the wheels had died away into the dry clicking of the crickets in the grass, and the first star of the summer night came out over the chimney of the carriage works like a small, white rivet driven into the dark.

Then she turned her back on the hills, picked up her plastic Co-op bag from the grass by the milestone, and started down the long road toward the hospital on the ridge, where the lights were already coming on in the high sashes of Ward 7 and the green floors were waiting for the buffer to begin its turn.
### Chapter 44

August in the valley was a heavy, yellow month that smelled of creosote and drying river mud.

The sun stayed behind a thin gauze of high, milky cloud that trapped the heat between the gritstone ridges like steam inside an iron pot, until the tar between the stone setts on Railway Road bubbled into black, sticky blisters that stuck to the soles of the porters’ boots. In the wards, the sash windows were propped open with two-inch wooden wedges that the joiners had made from scrap deal, letting in the smell of the diesel shunters from the siding and the dry, papery rustle of the dry grass on the railway cutting.

Martha Gould was sixty.

Her birthday had fallen on a Tuesday in the middle of the heatwave.

She had celebrated it by coming onto Ward 7 forty minutes early, before the day shift had finished their drug round, carrying a round tin of seed cake she had baked in her gas oven on Monday morning. She had set the tin on the sister’s desk beside the sterile water bottles, with a note written on a slip of blue ruled paper from her pad: *DOMESTIC STAFF & NURSES — TAKE A PIECE BEFORE THE SEVEN O'CLOCK BELL.*

By midnight, the tin was empty except for three crumbs and a curled piece of greaseproof paper.

In Bay 3, an old weaver named Tommy Hargreaves was sitting up in bed, his thin, white shins sticking out from under the cellular sheet like two birch poles.

He had spent fifty years behind the looms at the Jubilee Mill, his shoulders permanently twisted from throwing the heavy oak shuttles, and his fingers were working continuously in his lap, knotting and unknotting the hem of the sheet with the rapid, mechanical twitch of a man who had tied twenty thousand broken ends of warp in his time.

"The left selvedge," Tommy whispered to the darkness outside the window. "It’s dragging on the reed. Tell the overlooker to slack the weight on the back-beam. If the warp snaps now, the whole cut’s spoiled, and the master will dock fourpence off the ticket."

Martha stopped her buffer.

The machine was quiet tonight; she had oiled the brass wheel-bushings with two drops of sewing-machine oil from her basket before she started the cross-corridor, and the nylon pad moved over the green linoleum with the dry, whispering hush of autumn leaves being swept into a gutter.

She pulled her reporter's pad from her apron.

The pad was her third since the trial: a narrow black wire-bound notebook with eighty ruled leaves, the cardboard cover protected from the damp by a sleeve of clear plastic that Billy Noonan had cut from an old fertilizer sack.

*Hargreaves, T.,* she wrote with her pencil stub. *01:15. Left selvedge dragging. Slack the weight on the back-beam. Fourpence on the ticket.*

She closed the book.

She did not put it in her pocket. She held it against the flat of her palm, feeling the thick, fibrous texture of the paper through the plastic.

Across the bay, the night sister was making her round.

It was Brenda Hallowes.

She wore her dark navy tunic, her silver belt buckle catching the yellow sodium light that came through the sashes from the ambulance ramp below. She carried her small plastic drug-tray with two glass medicine measures containing orange liquid, her rubber shoes moving over the linoleum without a sound.

She stopped beside Martha’s buffer.

"He's quiet tonight," Brenda said, nodding toward Bed 3.

"He's thinking about his cut," Martha said. "He thinks the master’s going to dock him fourpence for the warp."

Brenda smiled—a slow, tired movement of her mouth that had no bitterness in it now, only the patient, permanent weariness of a woman who had worked fourteen hours on her feet and had two loads of washing waiting for the twin-tub when she got home to the terrace.

"My dad had an uncle who was a weaver," Brenda whispered, leaning her hip against the rubber bumper of the linen trolley. "He died in the old workhouse infirmary out at Whinmoor in nineteen-seventy-two. When he was going, he kept shouting for his loom-hook. He said the overlooker had stolen his hook out of his basket while he was at his dinner."

She looked down at Martha’s notebook.

"Are you still writing them down, Martha?"

"Aye," Martha said. "Somebody has to."

"Why?"

Martha looked down the long aisle between the beds.

In the dim light of the emergency tubes, the twenty-four iron bed-frames looked like the sleepers of an old railway track running through a tunnel, each one carrying its freight of worn-out bone, scarred skin, and tired lungs toward the daylight at the far end.

"Because the doctors only remember the numbers," Martha said. "The blood pressure, the sugar, the creatinine. When a man goes through that door with the canvas sheet over his face, the doctors write *Cardiac Arrest* or *Respiratory Failure* on the blue card, and then they wash their hands and look at the next chart."

She tapped the wire spiral of her pad with her fingernail.

"The numbers don't tell you who he was," she said. "The numbers don't tell you that he had fourpence docked off his ticket because the reed was dragging, or that he had a pair of brown shoes he bought for the dance that stayed under the clay for twenty-six years without rotting."

Brenda looked at the window.

Through the glass, the lights of the city were stretched out across the dark floor of the valley: the yellow dots of the sodium lamps on the bypass; the blue-white glare of the freight yard where the container trains were being marshaled for the night run to the ports; and the faint, red glow of the brickworks kiln on the ridge that never went out, winter or summer.

"You're a stubborn woman, Martha Gould," Brenda said softly.

"I’m an Accrington woman," Martha said. "My father was an Accrington man, and his father worked the puddling furnaces at the ironworks before the railway was built. We don't throw things away just because the council says there's no room on the lorry."

She turned the handle of the buffer.

The motor caught with a clean, low hum that settled into the concrete beneath their feet like the vibration of an engine room at sea.

"Go and give Tommy his mixture," Martha said. "The selvedge will wait till morning, but his cough won't."

Brenda picked up her drug tray.

"Aye, Sister Gould," she murmured, her eyes crinkling at the corners with that dry, ironical humor that nurses use when they have seen forty people die between two cups of tea.

"Don't call me Sister," Martha said, swinging the buffer three feet to the left. "A sister has to sign the incident reports. I’ve only got to keep the linoleum clean, and that’s plenty for a woman of my years."

### Chapter 45

In October, the frost came early.

On the twenty-seventh of the month—the exact anniversary of the day Nicholas Vane had first arrived at St. Jude’s with his iPad and his German trousers—the Trust held an opening in the old chapel behind the mortuary.

The chapel had been scrubbed from the floor-flags to the oak hammerbeams by eight women from the domestic team, led by Martha with two gallons of carbolic and four pounds of soda crystals. The three stone slabs where the cholera victims had sat in the nineteenth century had been covered with sheets of plate glass, and beneath the glass, laid out on beds of unbleached linen, were forty-two volumes from the *Gould Collection.*

There was the Men’s Surgical for nineteen-eighty-nine.

There was the Trauma Admissions Register for nineteen-ninety-four.

And in the center of the middle slab, illuminated by two small spotlights that hung from the iron tie-rods of the roof, lay Volume 4: *Ward 4 Night Report & Casualty Transfers — November 1997.*

The book was open at page forty-eight.

The red china marker had faded slightly under the archival conservation lights, but the thick, waxy stroke was still distinct across the name of Liam Gould, and beside it, in the margin, the signature of Dr. Peter Henderson stood out in its thin, nervous ballpoint: *LIFE EXTINCT — 04:15.*

Beside the book sat Liam’s silver watch, ticking with its heavy, irregular three-beat rhythm, and three three-inch copper split-pins that Billy Noonan had cleaned with metal polish until they looked like three drops of golden honey on the white cloth.

The room was full of people.

There were sixty doctors and nurses from the hospital, all of them wearing their everyday uniforms with their ID badges hanging from blue lanyards; thirty people from the Broadwater Estate; and the new Chief Executive of the Trust—a woman named Dr. Claire Henderson (no relation to Peter), who had spent twenty years running public health programs in the East End of London and wore flat wool skirts and brown leather boots that had the heels worn down from walking the wards.

Dr. Claire Henderson did not make a long speech.

She stood on the stone step below the altar, her hands folded in front of her, her face serious, pale in the cold daylight that came through the lancet windows.

"We are here today," she said, her voice clear, quiet, reaching every corner of the vaulted roof, "not to celebrate a reform, but to acknowledge an inheritance."

She looked at the glass case where Volume 4 lay.

"For thirty years, this institution practiced the art of administrative erasure. We believed that if a death was not entered on the computer, it had not occurred. We believed that if an inquiry was marked *legally privileged,* the truth could be buried in an iron safe behind an eighty-year-old wall. We treated the human beings who passed through our doors as operational data to be managed, minimized, and discarded when their care exceeded our financial metrics."

She turned her head toward the back of the chapel.

Martha Gould was standing by the iron radiator, wearing her brown tweed coat with her hands tucked into her pockets, her clogs hidden beneath the hem of her trousers.

"The person who prevented that erasure," Dr. Henderson said, "was not a director. She was not a consultant. She was not an officer of the Crown. She was a woman who carried an ash mop and a four-pound galvanized bucket down the corridors of Ward 7 for thirty-one years without an increment or a commendation."

The Chief Executive reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a small, flat box of dark red leather with a gold clasp.

"The Board of Governors has resolved," she said, "to create the *Martha Gould Fellowship for Nursing Integrity.* It provides two thousand pounds a year for training ward staff in clinical documentation and patient advocacy. And with it comes the Trust’s Gold Medal for Meritorious Service."

She held the red leather box out toward the back of the room.

The chapel was so quiet that the sound of the steam pipes clicking in the corners sounded like small pebbles falling into a dry well.

Every face in the room turned toward Martha.

Martha did not move her feet.

She looked at the red box. She looked at the gold clasp. She looked at the Chief Executive’s brown leather boots that were dusty from the gravel in the car park.

"Thank you, Doctor," Martha said.

Her voice had that thirty-year cadence: the cadence of the Accrington brick, the cadence of the carriage works siding, the cadence that didn't rise for gold medals and didn't fall for four-year sentences in the Crown Court.

"You can keep the medal in the case," she said. "I don't have no mantle for it. The clock takes up the middle of the shelf, and my father’s brass calipers have the left side, and Liam’s photograph has the right."

She took her hands from her pockets.

"And you don't need no fellowship to teach nurses to write the truth," she said. "You just need to give them a pen with black ink, twenty minutes at the end of the shift that isn't docked off their card, and the sense to listen when an old weaver is talking about his loom."

She turned toward the heavy oak door of the chapel.

"Martha," Dr. Henderson called after her, stepping down from the altar. "Will you not stay for the tea?"

Martha stopped with her hand on the iron latch.

"The tea can wait, Doctor," she said. "The day shift on Ward 7 has left three buckets of soapy water in the sluice room, and if the yarn sits in the soda for more than an hour, the cotton rots and then the mop drops its strings all over the wax when the buffer starts."

She pushed open the door, stepped out into the clean, cold autumn air that was blowing across the mortuary yard from the moors, and walked toward the fire stairs with her head down against the wind.

### Chapter 46

November came back to the estate like an old dog returning to its hearth.

On the fourteenth of the month—twenty-seven years to the day since Liam had gone out through the scullery door with eleven pounds in his pocket and his union card—the sleet came over the ridge at three in the afternoon, turning the cobbles by the locks into sheets of gray grease that mirrored the cold sky.

Martha Gould sat at her kitchen table at Number 14 Moor Lane.

The clock on the mantelpiece struck ten.

Its pendulum swung with a steady, brass *tock-tock*, the sound heavy and slow in the small kitchen that smelled of carbolic soap, dry tea-leaves, and the clean mineral water that came through Billy Noonan’s new copper pipe.

Under the sink, behind the cream-painted hardboard doors, sat the yellow Morrisons bag.

It was not empty.

Inside, wrapped in three layers of dry flannel from an old sheet, lay the four notebooks:

The first spiral pad, with Albert Garside’s rivets and Mrs. Cleary’s red scarf.

The second pad, with the notes from Suite 402 and the names of the doctors who had looked through the ceiling.

The third pad, with Agnes Clitheroe’s resin and Darren Finch’s fourteen pounds of petrol.

And Sergeant John Hallows’s small rexine pocket-book, with its twenty-two blank pages that had been filled with the names of forty different people who had died on Ward 7 since the winter of the reorganization.

Martha did not open the cupboard.

She knew they were there. They sat in the dark behind the lead-faced washers, nine inches above the floorboards, dry, safe, and silent, like the coal-cars that had slept under the chapel before the rails were pulled up for scrap.

She stood up.

She pulled her navy nylon tabard over her head, tied the tapes behind her back with a double knot, and put on her brown tweed coat.

She checked her pocket.

Her keys were there: sixty-eight brass and iron keys on an eight-inch ring, heavy as a horseshoe in her palm.

Her Stanley knife was there, with a new carbon blade fitted into the casing.

Her tin of butterscotch drops was there, with four sweets left in the cellophane wrappers.

And her red spiral pad was there: a fresh one, bought from the stationer’s behind the market on Saturday morning, its seventy-two ruled pages clean and white as a fresh sheet from the laundry press at Skelmersdale.

She turned the key in the bottom Yale lock of the front door.

She did not lock the top Chubb.

She hadn't locked the Chubb since the day the Lord Chief Justice had quashed the inquisition in Court 4; if someone came down Moor Lane in the night looking for a place to get out of the sleet, the latch would yield to a shoulder, and there was always two inches of tea in the caddy and a box of matches on the gas bracket by the scullery door.

She stepped out into the dark.

The street lamps on Moor Lane were yellow dots in the drifting mist, each one surrounded by a fuzzy ring of cold vapor that looked like the halo around a dying moon.

She walked down the hill toward the railway bridge.

The wind was coming off the canal basin, carrying the smell of the wet limestone from the quarry trains and the rich, greasy breath of the old erecting shops where the bronze plaque was sleeping in the weeds.

As she passed the gate of the carriage works, she stopped.

She reached into her coat pocket, pulled out the tin of butterscotch drops, and took out a single sweet.

She peeled the paper wrapper with her left thumb, careful not to let the cellophane blow away into the ditch, and slipped the golden square into her mouth.

The sugar was sweet, sharp with the taste of burnt butter and salt that her mother had loved for eighty years.

"There's your sweet, Mam," she whispered to the dark brickwork of the boiler house chimney. "And the kettle’s on the trivet, and the boy has got his shoes on, and the works is shut until the morning."

She walked on across the footbridge.

The water under the planks was moving toward the sea, deep, black, and silent, carrying the dead leaves and the soot from the chimneys down through the locks to the salt marshes where the gulls were sleeping on the sandbanks.

She climbed the long asphalt ramp that led up to the north elevation of St. Jude’s.

At the top of the slope, the lights of Ward 7 were shining through the tall Victorian sashes: four rows of high, rectangular windows that hung in the black sky like the ports of a great ship riding at anchor in a roadstead.

Behind the glass, the shadows of the nurses were moving between the bays, small, white figures carrying their trays of water and their aluminum clipboards through the quiet hours.

Martha Gould pushed open the heavy teak door of the service entrance.

The warmth of the hospital came out to meet her: the smell of boiled sheets, pine disinfectant, and seventy years of human life being gathered up and cleaned before the morning shift came up the hill.

She took off her coat, hung it on the wooden peg behind the domestic supervisor’s desk, and signed the register with her three jagged strokes:

*M. GOULD — DOMESTIC — 23:15.*

Then she took her three-pound cotton mop from the ringer, clicked the red power switch of the Numatic Hurricane with the toe of her rubber clog, and stepped out onto the green linoleum, her shoulders rounded, her hips swinging through their slow, eternal arcs, ready to listen to the things that the living and the dead would say when they thought that nobody in the world was listening.

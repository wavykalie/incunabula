# Chapter 14: Fire Escape

The heavy steel fire escape on the south wall of the warehouse overlooked an overgrown rail siding choked with wet blackberry vines and abandoned shipping containers. Mira pushed through the emergency push-bar door, stepping out into the cold river wind that blew off the tidal flats.

Teresa Cruz was leaning against the rusted railing on the second-floor landing, smoking a hand-rolled cigarette under the corrugated metal overhang. She wore her faded canvas work jacket, grease-smudged canvas trousers, and steel-toed boots caked with dried acoustic plaster.

"You look rough, Mira," Teresa said without turning around. She tapped an ash into the rusty downspout. "You have been in 4B since dawn. You got that gray skin engineers get when they sit in front of cathode ray tubes too long."

Mira leaned her elbows on the cold steel rail, watching a tugboat push a gravel barge down the gray river. The wind carried the salty, industrial tang of the estuary, cutting through the stale ozone smell that clung to her woolen sweater.

"Teresa," Mira said, her voice dry. "When you were running sound for the municipal theater in the nineties, did you ever run across an engineer named Arthur Pendelton?"

Teresa paused, her cigarette halfway to her mouth. She squinted through the rising tobacco smoke, her weathered face tightening around the eyes.

"Pendelton was Delta Labs," Teresa said slowly. "Old school broadcast technician. Built custom consoles for the university research teams. Disappeared back in seventy-four or seventy-five. Jumped off the bridge during the winter storms."

"Did anyone talk about what he was working on before he went off the span?"

Teresa took a slow, deep drag, letting the smoke curl from her nostrils before speaking.

"Every engineer who spends forty hours a week in an isolated acoustic box goes a little sideways eventually," Teresa said, turning to face her. "You stare at VU needles and listen to twenty-millisecond delay taps long enough, your brain starts building patterns out of the pink noise. Acoustic pareidolia. I knew a monitor tech in Seattle who spent three weeks convinced the cooling fans in his Crown amps were reciting Bible verses in Polish."

"This isn't pareidolia, Teresa," Mira said quietly. She stared at the murky river water churning around the bridge piers.

"What is it then?" Teresa asked, flicking an ash into the wind. "Some spooky frequency that vibrates your liver?"

"The tape is describing things in the studio that happen after the tape starts playing," Mira said. "It described my sweater. It described the flickering light in the hall. It is counting down paces toward my desk."

Teresa looked at her for a long, silent moment. The skepticism in her dark eyes didn't vanish, but her jaw tightened.

"You have a five-thousand-dollar contract with a corporate collector," Teresa said. "Corporate collectors buy junk from bankrupt estates and invent ghost stories to double the auction value. You let some spooky old reel get under your skin because your ear is ringing and you haven't eaten a hot meal in two days."

She dropped the cigarette butt into a muddy puddle beside the landing. The glowing tip died with a faint hiss.

"You're a technical engineer, Mira," Teresa added, leaning against the cold railing. "You know how circuits work. A tape recorder is a coil of wire, a piece of rusted plastic ribbon, and an electric motor. It doesn't have eyes. It doesn't have an intellect. If you hear someone talking about your clothes, that voice is coming out of your own temporal lobe."

Mira looked at her hands resting on the rusted railing. "Pendelton drew the paces in his notebook, Teresa. He measured the distance between his front porch and his workbench."

Teresa shook her head, pulling her canvas collar tight against the river draft.

"Whatever Vance is paying you, it isn't worth losing your grip," Teresa said, resting a calloused hand on Mira's shoulder. "Take your fee, hand over the digital copy, and walk away. Some tapes belong in the incinerator."


Teresa Cruz leaned her back against the rusted iron railing of the fire escape, looking down into the murky gray waters of the Duwamish River fifty feet below.

A container barge was moving slowly downstream toward Elliott Bay, its diesel engines rumbling with a heavy, low-frequency throb that Mira felt through the steel grating under her work boots.

"Back in ninety-eight," Teresa said, flicking an ash into the wind, "we had a guy working in Studio 1A. Older guy named Henderson. Had a master's degree in psychoacoustics from Stanford. He was doing spectral restoration on old Edison wax cylinders for a private collector in San Francisco."

Mira took a bite of her cold turkey sandwich, keeping her eyes on the industrial gulls circling above the mudflats.

"What happened to Henderson?" Mira asked.

"Worked thirty-hour shifts," Teresa said. "Refused to leave the console. Started claiming that the wax surface wasn't just holding the music of the brass band. Said the needle was picking up the acoustic reflections of the room where Edison had carved the master. Said he could hear people breathing behind the cylinder lathe."

Teresa took a slow, deep pull from her cigarette, the paper crackling in the cold river draft.

"One morning I came in with the mail," Teresa continued quietly. "Henderson had taken a pair of diagonal wire cutters and cut every single cable in the patch bay into three-inch segments. Over six hundred balanced lines. Smashed the monitor cones with a ball-peen hammer. He was sitting on the floor in the dark, rocking back and forth, holding his hands over his ears."

"Did they take him to Swedish?" Mira asked.

"Western State," Teresa replied flatly. "Psychiatric hold. Never touched a mixing console again. His daughter came by two weeks later to pack up his textbooks. She told me he couldn't stand the sound of running water from a kitchen faucet because he said the pipe noise had words in it."

Teresa drops her cigarette butt into a puddle and tells Mira to get some fresh air.

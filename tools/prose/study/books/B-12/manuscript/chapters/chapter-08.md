# Chapter 8: The Exit Drive

Mira withdrew her hand from the cold ceramic mug and wiped her wet finger on the leg of her jeans.

Her breath came in short, jagged gasps that rasped against the acoustic silence of the room. She hit the spacebar on the workstation keyboard, stopping the transport.

The voice fell silent.

The green VU meters settled back against their rubber stops. In the sudden quiet, her damaged left ear screamed with four kilohertz of white-hot tinnitus, a shrill whistling tone that felt like a hot needle driven into her temporal bone.

She took a clean paper towel from the dispenser, picked up the blue ceramic mug by its handle, and carried it to the small sink in the prep alcove.

She poured the dark liquid down the stainless steel drain.

The cold tea swirled around the brass strainer, leaving a thin film of brown tannin on the metal. She turned on the cold water tap, watching the liquid wash away until the basin was clean.

She stood over the sink with both palms pressed against the wet stainless steel rim, her head hanging down between her shoulders.

This was acoustic pareidolia. It had to be.

Teresa had told her: when an engineer spent forty hours a week trapped inside an isolated acoustic chamber with a damaged auditory cortex, the brain began constructing intelligible language out of ambient noise. The mind was an evolutionary pattern-matching engine; denied external sensory input, it turned inward, projecting its deepest anxieties into the random fluctuations of tape hiss and transformer hum.

She walked back to the console.

She opened her leather-bound technical workbook. Using her steel ruler to align the margin, she recorded the session parameters in blue ballpoint ink:

*Tuesday, 03:00. Session master 74-D. Channel 4 isolation pass complete. Signal exhibits severe psychoacoustic resonance and apparent contextual artifacts. Recommend immediate cessation of near-field monitoring. Shift to visual FFT analysis for remainder of contract.*

She closed the workbook, clicked the pen shut, and laid it neatly beside the keyboard.

She powered down the Genelec monitors, switching off the master breakers on the rack power supply. The green power LEDs died one by one, plunging the room into dim amber gloom illuminated only by the standby pilot lights of the Studer deck.

She stood in the center of the dark control room for five long minutes, listening to the building settle on its wooden pilings.

The warehouse was an old maritime cold-storage plant built in nineteen sixty-eight, framed with heavy Douglas fir timbers and clad in corrugated galvanized steel. When the tide rose in the shipping channel, the hydrostatic pressure lifted the river mud against the foundation, creating subtle structural stresses that traveled through the floor joists as deep, groaning creaks.

She knew every sound in this building: the sixty-cycle rattle of the vending machine in the hallway, the hum of Teresa's table saw in Unit 4A, the distant whistle of the rail switchers.

None of those sounds explained the voice on Track 4.

The voice had known the temperature of her room down to the tenth of a degree. It had known the sugar in her tea.

She looked at the empty blue mug sitting upside down in the stainless steel drying rack. A single drop of amber liquid clung to the glazed rim, glistening in the amber standby light of the Studer console.

She took her trench coat from the bentwood stand, buttoned it to her throat, and pulled her woolen scarf around her neck.

She stepped out into the hallway, pulling the heavy acoustic slab shut. She turned both brass deadbolts, testing the handle twice to ensure the latch had caught firmly in the strike plate.

The warehouse corridor was pitch black.

Mira walked quickly toward the southern exit, her flashlight beam dancing across the concrete floor, illuminating the rusted sprinkler pipes overhead. She unlocked the heavy steel fire door and stepped out into the freezing Tuesday morning rain.

The industrial park was deserted. Across the tidal river basin, the amber lights of the container cranes flickered through the mist like distant signal fires.

She reached her station wagon parked in the gravel turnaround. Unlocking the driver's door, she climbed inside, turned the ignition key, and listened to the starter motor grind twice before catching with a rough four-cylinder idle.

She switched on the heater and clicked the headlights to high beam.

Her car headlights sweep across the rain-blackened asphalt of the empty industrial park.

# Chapter 20: Unannounced Visit

Julian Vance arrived at Studio 4B at two o'clock on Wednesday afternoon without a phone call or courier warning.

Mira was at the workbench cleaning the pinch roller of her backup Revox deck when the heavy outer deadbolt turned from the outside. Vance stepped through the door, carrying a slim leather document folio under his left arm. He wore a dark wool overcoat tailored with sharp shoulders, spotless leather oxfords, and black leather driving gloves.

He closed the door behind him, turning the lower brass thumb-latch with casual familiarity.

"You left yellow chalk marks in the public corridor, Miss Lin," Vance said. His voice was quiet, cultured, and devoid of warmth. "Our logistics courier noted them during his midday perimeter drive. One might mistake your technical facility for a crime scene."

Mira laid the solvent swab on the glass workbench top. "I was verifying acoustic transit delays between the exterior gate and the mixing console."

Vance walked across the floating floor, his steps silent on the Sylomer-damped marine plywood. He stopped at the console, looking down at the Studer A80 with the appraising gaze of a diamond merchant inspecting an uncut gem. His gloved finger reached out and touched the edge of the aluminum reel flange, checking for oxide shedding.

"The tape pack is clean," Vance said. "You have adhered to the thermal stabilization protocol."

"The physical ribbon is stable," Mira said, stepping around the drafting table to face him. "The signal on Track 4 is not."

Vance turned his head slowly. His pale gray eyes settled on her face, sharp and dissecting behind his frameless spectacles. "The Oakhaven trustees did not retain you to interpret the signal, Miss Lin. We retained you to execute an uncorrupted digital transfer."

"I spoke with Clara Haze this morning," Mira said.

A subtle shift passed over Vance's expression--a momentary tightening of the skin across his cheekbones before his professional corporate mask snapped back into place.

"Mrs. Haze is eighty-one years old and resides in a memory care wing in Victoria," Vance said smoothly. "Her recollections of nineteen seventy-four are clouded by advanced cognitive decline. If you are contacting former session personnel, you are in direct violation of the non-disclosure covenants in Section Twelve of your retainer."

He unzipped the document folio and withdrew a crisp, three-page legal memorandum printed on twenty-four-pound linen bond.

"This is an affidavit of immediate compliance," Vance said, laying the papers flat on the console meter bridge beside her blue ceramic mug. "It certifies that you will deliver four discrete, unedited twenty-four-bit stems by Friday afternoon at five o'clock. If the transfer is incomplete or if the physical media suffers damage, our firm will execute the fifty-thousand-dollar indemnity judgment within thirty minutes of the deadline."

Vance walked to the tape rack along the western wall, his gloved fingers trailing along the steel uprights.

"Your facility is well engineered, Miss Lin," Vance noted in his quiet, conversational baritone. "Floating floor, sand-filled cinder blocks, dedicated isolation transformers. It provides an exceptional acoustic containment volume. In Basel, our laboratories require six feet of reinforced lead concrete to achieve ninety decibels of structural attenuation."

"Containment?" Mira asked, turning to face him. "You didn't bring me an audio restoration project. You brought me an acoustic biohazard."

Vance smiled--a faint, humorless curvature of his pale lips that vanished as quickly as it appeared.

"Every cultural artifact carries an energetic charge," Vance said smoothly. "In the nineteenth century, collectors preserved paintings and sculptures. In our century, we preserve kinetic and acoustic phenomena. Track 4 is a pristine specimen of non-local auditory manifestation. To destroy it would be an act of cultural vandalism that our trust cannot permit."

Mira looked at the signature line. "Arthur Pendelton recorded something that was not musical, Mr. Vance. You knew what was in that crypt."

"I know that historical artifacts require custody," Vance replied softly. He pulled a heavy gold fountain pen from his inside breast pocket, unscrewed the cap, and laid the instrument beside the affidavit. "Sign the compliance acknowledgment, Miss Lin. You have forty-nine hours remaining."

Mira did not touch the pen. Her hands remained flat at her sides, her nails pressing into the denim of her jeans.

Vance watched her for ten silent seconds. Then, without a word of anger or frustration, he picked up the pen, screwed the gold cap back onto the barrel, and slid it into his pocket.


Julian Vance had arrived at Studio 4B at three o'clock on Wednesday afternoon without phoning in advance.

He stood in the doorway of the control room wearing a bespoke charcoal wool overcoat, his polished oxfords spotless despite the mud in the warehouse alleyway. In his left hand he carried a slim leather document folio bearing the embossed gold seal of the Oakhaven Trust.

He did not step across the rubber floor mat. He stood on the threshold, his eyes scanning the control room with cold, clinical appraisal.

He looked at the Studer deck, the glowing amber VU meters, the open splicing blocks, and the yellow chalk lines drawn across the concrete floor outside.

"Your working environment appears somewhat chaotic, Ms. Lin," Vance remarked, his tone polite and frictionless. "The trustees expected an archival transfer conducted under cleanroom standards. I see oxide dust on the head assembly."

Mira did not rise from her swivel chair.

"The tape is fifty-two years old, Mr. Vance," she said evenly. "The binder is shedding despite the four-hour bake. If you wanted cleanroom transcription, you should have digitized this master in 1980 before the polyurethane degraded."

Vance stepped into the room, his leather soles clicking against the rubber matting.

He walked to the workbench, his gloved fingers touching the rim of the empty aluminum flight case. He ran a fingertip along the foam lining, inspecting the black surface for dust.

"The Oakhaven Trust holds thirty-four thousand audio recordings," Vance said softly. "Ninety-nine percent are standard ethnographic and musical field recordings: Navajo chants from 1932, Appalachian fiddle tunes, Gullah spirituals recorded on aluminum discs. But certain holdings represent specialized scientific research."

"Session 74-D," Mira said.

Vance turned his head toward her, his dark eyes impenetrable.

"Arthur Pendelton was a visionary," Vance said. "A man ahead of his era. Modern quantum electrodynamics is only now beginning to validate what Arthur demonstrated empirically in 1974: that spatial information can be encoded within coherent acoustic fields."

"Arthur drowned himself in Lake Union," Mira said.

Vance withdrew a gold Montblanc fountain pen from his interior breast pocket. He tapped the gold nib against the cover of his leather document folio.

"Arthur was an employee who breached his fiduciary duty," Vance stated coldly. "You are an independent contractor governed by a commercial agreement. I trust you have reviewed paragraph fourteen regarding breach of contract."

"The fifty-thousand-dollar liquidated damages clause," Mira said.

"Precisely," Vance murmured. "If the four-track digital stems are not delivered to our corporate headquarters by Friday at five o'clock, the trust will initiate immediate legal proceedings. We will file a prejudgment attachment against your personal assets, your studio equipment, and your accounts receivable."

He leaned toward her desk, the faint scent of expensive cologne masking the smell of ozone in the room.

Vance places his gold pen back into his breast pocket and reminds her that Friday 5:00 PM is final.

# Chapter 6: The Hallway Vending Machine

Midnight arrived with the sound of rain drumming against the corrugated tin of the warehouse loading canopy.

Mira stepped out of Studio 4B, pulling the heavy acoustic slab shut behind her. The cold draft in the unheated warehouse corridor hit her face like a damp towel, shocking her tired eyelids open. She had been sitting in the dark control room for four hours, listening to the first playback pass on her Beyerdynamic headphones, her ears ringing with the eerie, unresolved cello chords of Saint Jude's Priory.

She walked down the long, dimly lit hallway toward the employee break nook near the freight entrance.

The corridor was illuminated by a single incandescent bulb hanging from a frayed cloth cord. At the far end of the hall, the vintage cold-drink vending machine buzzed with an angry sixty-cycle vibration, its illuminated plastic sign casting a sickly green rectangle across the concrete floor.

Teresa Cruz was leaning against the machine, holding a lukewarm can of ginger ale.

She wore her gray mechanic's overalls, her work boots dusted with dry acoustic plaster from the soundstage renovation she was supervising in Unit 4A. A pair of wire-rimmed safety glasses hung from a lanyard around her neck, reflecting the pale neon light.

"You look rough, Mira," Teresa said, taking a sip from her can. "Like you have been breathing too much solder flux."

"Just ear fatigue," Mira said, leaning her shoulders against the cinder-block wall. The cold masonry chilled her spine through her thin cotton shirt. "I've been working on that nineteen seventy-four reel Vance brought in on Monday."

Teresa frowned, lowering her can. "Vance? Julian Vance? The guy in the black town car with the Zurich plates?"

"You know him?"

"I know the type," Teresa said, crossing her arms over her chest. "Suit cost more than my first mixing console. He bought out the old Delta Labs inventory back in the nineties when the company went into bankruptcy. My old boss at the municipal theater told me Vance was buying up every piece of mobile recording gear that came out of the St. Jude deconsecration."

Mira looked at the older engineer. Teresa had been wiring patch bays and running live sound since the late nineteen seventies; her knuckles were scarred from hot soldering irons, and she knew every industrial rumor along the riverfront.

"Did your boss ever mention Arthur Pendelton?" Mira asked quietly.

Teresa's face tightened. She set her soda can on the top of the vending machine with a deliberate, metallic clunk.

"Pendelton was chief engineer on Delta's mobile truck," Teresa said. Her voice dropped half an octave into a low, confidential register. "Brilliant guy. Built custom transformer preamps with Swedish nickel cores that every broadcast studio in town tried to copy. But he lost his mind during the St. Jude sessions. They said he started hearing voices in the empty channels. Two weeks after they wrapped the mobile recording, he drove his car out to the river suspension bridge and walked off the center span."

A cold draft whistled through the gap beneath the loading bay doors, carrying the smell of tidal mud and wet creosote.

"Vance didn't mention that," Mira said, her throat dry.

"Of course he didn't," Teresa replied dryly. "Vance doesn't pay people to ask about dead technicians. He pays them to digitize magnetic oxide before the binder turns into soup. What's on the tape, Mira?"

"An avant-garde chamber piece," Mira answered, keeping her eyes fixed on the cracked concrete between her boots. "Cello, prepared upright piano, soprano."

"And the fourth channel?" Teresa asked, her dark eyes sharp behind her safety glasses. "Those old Delta consoles were four-track machines. What was on Track 4?"

"Nothing cataloged," Mira said carefully. "Just room noise. Tape hiss."

Teresa studied her face for three long seconds. She reached into the pocket of her canvas overalls, pulled out a heavy handheld demagnetizer wand shaped like a red plastic pistol, and laid it in Mira's open palm.

"Take this," Teresa said. "Your playback heads are probably magnetized from dirty utility power. The river line has ground faults every time the tide rises. Demagnetize your deck before you play that tape again. And keep your wits about you, kid. Some old studios have bad grounding that'll make your fillings hum."

Mira leaned her head back against the painted cinder blocks, watching a brown moth flutter around the bare incandescent bulb. The insect's wings beat against the dusty glass with a faint, dry clicking sound, like paper clips dropped on linoleum.

"Teresa," Mira said softly. "When Arthur Pendelton had his breakdown, did anyone examine the master tapes he delivered to Vance?"

Teresa took another slow sip of ginger ale, her throat moving with a dry swallow.

"The police impounded the mobile truck for two weeks," Teresa said, looking down the dark corridor toward the freight elevator. "My old boss told me they brought in an acoustic specialist from the university physics department to review the four-track reels. The physicist spent three days in the truck with headphones on. On the fourth morning, he packed his briefcase, handed the keys back to the coroner, and resigned his university tenure. Moved his family to an off-grid orchard in the Okanagan."

"What did he hear?"

"He didn't say," Teresa answered, her eyes cold. "He just told the coroner that some magnetic patterns operate like optical illusions on the auditory nerve. Once the brain maps the frequency, the ear can't unhear it. It becomes an acoustic tinnitus that never stops."

Teresa hands over the heavy demagnetizer with a dry warning not to drop it near magnetic tape.

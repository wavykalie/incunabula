# Chapter 15: Forty-Eight to Thirty-Six

Mira returned to the control room at four-fifteen. She locked both deadbolts on the heavy acoustic door and lowered the steel drop-bolt into its brass floor socket.

She did not touch her headphones.

She routed Track 4 directly to the main Genelec monitors, bypassing the console equalizer and driving the discrete amplifier modules with raw line voltage. The green power LEDs on the front baffles shone bright and steady against the matte black finish of the cabinets.

She sat down in the center of the listening triangle, exactly six feet from each speaker axis, forming an equilateral acoustic geometry.

She pressed play.

The tape transport engaged with a solid clunk of solenoid actuators. The ten-inch reels spun into motion, the brown ribbon flowing across the head block like dark water over stones.

The convent strings filled the room, majestic and sorrowful, their minor-key chords decaying against the simulated chapel walls.

Then the strings paused on an unresolved suspension.

In the sudden acoustic hole, the voice on Track 4 spoke with clinical precision:

"Thirty-eight paces. Passing the fire extinguisher cabinet."

Mira's pulse hammered against her temples. She closed her eyes and counted her breaths, trying to anchor herself in the physical properties of the room: the three-quarter-inch marine plywood floor, the rockwool insulation panels, the cold smell of isopropyl alcohol evaporating from the head block.

"Thirty-seven paces," the voice said. "The left sole of the work boot drags slightly on the rough concrete."

Mira opened her eyes.

She stood up from her chair and walked slowly toward the studio door.

The sound isolation in Studio 4B was built on a floating room-within-a-room design: two layers of five-eighths-inch Type X gypsum board mounted on resilient steel channels, isolated from the outer warehouse concrete by four inches of dense mineral wool batting. The door itself weighed nearly one hundred pounds, constructed from solid wood core clad in steel sheet, sealed with double perimeter neoprene gaskets and an automatic drop-seal at the threshold.

Sound from the outer corridor should have been attenuated by at least forty-five decibels.

Yet the dragging sound of the boot sole had transmitted through the barrier with clear physical presence, as though the rubber were scraping against the floorboards on her side of the threshold.

Mira knelt by the bottom seal of the door.

She pressed her bare palm against the marine plywood floorboards.

A distinct vibration hummed through the wood beneath her fingers: a low-frequency oscillation centered at twenty-eight hertz, shivering through the tongue-and-groove decking with the mechanical power of a heavy industrial motor idling against the foundation pilings.

She looked at the gap between the door and the brass sill.

A thin wisp of cold white vapor was drifting beneath the neoprene gasket, curling across the black rubber runner like smoke from an unvented flue. The vapor smelled of wet river silt, coal dust, and that sweet almond scent from the aluminum flight case.

Outside in the warehouse corridor, three inches beyond the six-inch insulated door, came the distinct, heavy scrape of rubber against unfinished concrete.

The sound was faint, muffled by eighty pounds of acoustic mineral wool and double gypsum board, but it was physical. It carried mechanical energy that transmitted through the door frame, setting up a faint sympathetic resonance in the brass latch assembly.

Mira gripped the armrests of her chair, her knuckles turning bone-white.

She looked at the sound level meter on her desk. The needle kicked upward by two decibels, registering acoustic energy that was not originating inside Studio 4B.

"Thirty-six paces," the voice whispered from both speakers at once. The stereo image had collapsed from the left channel to dead phantom center, perfectly focused between the two drivers like a physical presence standing on the meter bridge.

The air in the room seemed to thicken, pressing against her eardrums with the weight of deep water. The phantom tone in her left ear dropped half an octave, shifting from four kilohertz to a dull, grinding low-frequency thrum.

She reached for the master fader, her fingers shaking as she gripped the ribbed plastic cap.

Outside, the physical drag of the boot sole stopped directly beside the door lintel.

The tape counter on the Studer transport clicked over to nine hundred and sixty feet.


Mira returned to the control room at two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, locking the heavy acoustic door behind her with both the brass mortise latch and the heavy steel deadbolt.

She sat at the console and engaged the solo monitor bus for track four.

The cello and piano disappeared from the mix.

The room filled with the pure, unadorned signal of channel four.

The tape hiss was minimal, reading minus seventy-four decibels on the digital peak meters. In the background of the channel, there was no hum, no mechanical flutter, no tape modulation noise.

Then, out of the silent carrier floor, the voice began to count.

The cadence was slow, deliberate, and mathematically steady, pulsing at intervals of precisely two point four seconds:

"Forty-eight paces to the chair," the voice stated, its uninflected monotone sounding with absolute clarity directly between Mira's monitor speakers. "The path begins at the exterior asphalt threshold of the warehouse loading bay. The heavy steel sliding door is propped open three inches with a wooden cedar wedge."

Mira sat upright in her chair.

She had propped that exterior door open herself twenty minutes ago, when she returned from the sandwich shop, using an old cedar wedge she had found near the dumpster.

"Crossing the threshold onto the unsealed concrete slab," the voice continued. "Passing the row of decommissioned marine battery racks. The air carries seventy-two percent relative humidity and the scent of sulfuric acid residue."

The description was anatomically precise.

The old battery racks stood thirty feet inside the warehouse entrance, remnants of the boat-repair business that had leased the building in the 1990s.

"Forty-six paces," the voice said. "Stepping over the black six-gauge conduit pipe bolted to the floor. The left shoe makes a dry, scuffing contact with the expansion joint."

Mira's pulse accelerated, pounding against her temples with the force of an acoustic bass transient.

The voice was not recounting a memory from 1974.

It was describing a physical progression taking place in real time across the architectural floor plan of Riverbank Industrial Warehouse 4B.

"Forty-four paces. Passing the pallet of five-gallon drywall compound buckets. Two buckets have cracked lids; the compound has dried into white chalk."

Mira knew those drywall buckets. They were stacked against the brick pillar twenty paces from the studio corridor entrance.

She reached for the master transport stop switch.

Her fingers hovered above the aluminum button.

If she stopped the transport, would the progression halt? Or would the entity continue forward through the dark warehouse in silence, arriving outside the studio door without the countdown to warn her?

She kept her hand still.

"Forty-two paces. Entering the central access corridor. The overhead light fixture contains a forty-watt fluorescent tube with a failing ballast. The lamp flickers at one hundred and twenty hertz."

Above the hallway ceiling outside her door, through the double-pane viewing window, Mira saw the faint, rhythmic flicker of the failing fluorescent lamp.

The flicker was erratic, jumping between dull violet and yellow-white.

On the tape, the voice matched every flicker with a subtle micro-voltage fluctuation across the channel four signal.


The Voice continued its relentless, chronological tally:

"Thirty-eight paces. Passing the rusted fire hose cabinet bolted to the southern brick wall. The red enamel paint is blistered from industrial heat. The brass nozzle is missing its rubber gasket."

Mira knew the fire hose cabinet. It sat forty feet down the hallway, directly opposite the heavy timber door to the paint locker. She had walked past it a thousand times during her four years at Riverbank Warehouses.

She stood up from her chair and walked to the studio sound lock.

She placed her ear against the cold painted wood of the inner door.

Outside in the hallway, through the four inches of solid timber and lead baffling, there was no physical sound. No boot heels striking concrete. No rustling fabric. No heavy breathing.

The warehouse was locked in mid-afternoon quiet, punctuated only by the distant clatter of freight trains shunting boxcars along the Duwamish river spur.

Yet on the tape, the countdown advanced without hesitation:

"Thirty-six paces. The floor drops three-quarters of an inch where the original 1920 foundation slab meets the 1968 loading bay addition. The sole scuffs the cold asphalt filler."

The precision was horrifying.

The Voice was not guessing. It was reading the physical topography of the floor with the tactile sensitivity of a blind traveler feeling the ground with an iron-tipped cane.

Mira stepped back to the console desk, her breath shallow and cold.

Her left ear—the ear with the sixty-five-decibel sensorineural loss—felt hot, throbbing with a dull, rhythmic ache that seemed to radiate from the mastoid bone into her jaw.

The tinnitus carrier tone was vibrating at four thousand one hundred and twenty hertz.

It matched the pitch of the unmetered sub-carrier on channel four.

The internal ringing in her skull and the external signal on the tape were locking into phase synchronization, pulling her auditory cortex into sympathetic resonance with the advancing observer.

"Forty paces. Turning toward the fire door."

The voice falls silent on the count of forty-eight.

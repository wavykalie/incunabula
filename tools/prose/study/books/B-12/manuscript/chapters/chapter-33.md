# Chapter 33: Ten Hours Remaining

Ten hours remained before the Friday five o'clock deadline.

At seven o'clock on Friday morning, the storm cleared into a freezing, razor-sharp dawn that cast pale horizontal shafts of sunlight through the wire-glass skylights. The warehouse around Studio 4B was locked in absolute, tomb-like silence. The rain had ceased, leaving the corrugated eaves dripping into frozen puddles on the gravel turnaround outside.

Mira Lin sat motionless in her swivel chair, wrapped in her woolen trench coat. Her breath rose in faint, shallow vapor, illuminated by the amber glow of the Studer VU meters.

In the deadened room, the sound of her own body had become loud, almost deafening.

Without external acoustic masking, the ear's internal gain cranks upward, searching for stimulus. She heard the wet, rhythmic click of her tongue against the roof of her mouth every time she swallowed. She heard the faint, high-pressure squeak of blood pumping through the carotid arteries in her neck. And beneath those physiological rhythms, her tinnitus carrier tone buzzed at four thousand one hundred and twenty hertz—a cold, mechanical sine wave that seemed to have established physical roots in her temporal bone.

On the mixing console, the Studer A80 transport was running in whisper mode.

The tape ribbon slid across the playback heads at fifteen inches per second, moving with the smooth, hypnotic fluidity of liquid dark honey. The heavy cast-aluminum supply reel, once thick with quarter-inch brown polyester ribbon, had emptied down to a narrow dark ring around the black center hub.

She glanced down at the mechanical footage counter.

The white numeral wheels were turning slowly: 2,394 feet... 2,395 feet... 2,396 feet.

A standard ten-and-a-half-inch reel of Ampex 456 held exactly two thousand five hundred feet of tape ribbon. At fifteen inches per second, two thousand five hundred feet represented thirty-three minutes and twenty seconds of total recorded time.

One hundred and four feet remained.

Less than ninety seconds of recorded audio before the trailer tape reached the photoelectric runout sensor and tripped the automatic stop relay.

Mira looked toward the barricaded studio door.

The stack of oak shipping pallets and steel flight cases filled the entrance alcove like an unyielding bunker wall. The four-inch deck screws she had driven into the oak frame gleamed dull silver in the gloom. The lead ingots stacked inside the top flight cases exerted downward pressure, anchoring the mass to the foundation slab.

Nothing stirred in the corridor outside.

Through the narrow cracks between the wooden pallet stringers, the yellow light from the hallway incandescent bulb showed no shadow. No dragging footsteps echoed against the concrete floor. No metallic clicks issued from the main three-phase electrical breaker panel.

The silence was heavier than the storm had been.

It was the pressure differential before a major acoustic blast—a sharp vacuum that occurs when a high-amplitude shock wave draws the surrounding atmosphere inward toward its epicenter.


The Studer A80 console was an instrument of uncompromising Swiss engineering. Built in Regensdorf in 1976, its cast-aluminum chassis weighed over two hundred pounds, housing three separate high-torque DC motors that maintained tape tension through optical servo controllers.

Mira reached out and placed her palm against the top transport deck plate.

The metal was freezing to the touch, chilling her skin through the calluses on her fingertips. Through the metal, she felt the low, rhythmic hum of the capstan flywheel spinning at nine hundred revolutions per minute. The brass flywheel was dynamically balanced with lead inserts, rotating on precision German ball bearings bathed in synthetic turbine oil.

She checked the head assembly under the gooseneck lamp.

The four-track head block was a marvel of miniaturized inductive engineering. Each track used a laminated permalloy core wrapped with thousands of turns of microscopic enameled copper wire, shielded in mu-metal casings to reject external hum. The gap between the pole pieces was exactly three microns wide—smaller than the diameter of a single red blood cell.

As the Ampex ribbon passed over that three-micron gap at fifteen inches per second, the microscopic magnetic domains in the ferric oxide induced a voltage across the copper coils measuring less than two millivolts.

That two-millivolt electrical flutter was the entire bridge between the physical universe and the recorded observer.

She picked up an analog multimeter and checked the bias voltage on channel four.

The needle hovered at seventy-five volts AC, oscillating at an ultrasonic bias frequency of one hundred and fifty kilohertz. The high-frequency bias was necessary to linearize the magnetic recording process, kicking the ferric oxide particles into a continuous state of agitation so they could respond to the audio signal.

Under normal conditions, bias current existed only during recording.

On the Studer deck this morning, the bias oscillator was active during playback.

The circuit board for channel four had developed a parasitic oscillation, drawing power from the main transformer rail to keep the erase and record coils continuously energized.

The deck was not merely reading the tape. It was continually re-biasing the ribbon as it passed through the heads, refreshing the magnetic orientation of the microscopic spirals before they could decay.

Arthur Pendelton had designed the system to be self-sustaining.

Once the collector initialized, the playback deck became an active induction furnace, feeding energy back into the magnetic medium with every revolution of the capstan.

She looked at the yellow chalk marks she had drawn across the concrete floor on Wednesday.

The line marked *EIGHTEEN PACES* ran directly in front of the barricaded doorway. The line marked *TWELVE PACES* crossed the center of the equipment rack. The line marked *FOUR PACES* crossed the corner of her desk.

And the chalk mark labeled *ZERO* was an open circle drawn directly around the caster wheels of her swivel chair.

She reached for her technical workbook.

Her fingers were stiff with cold, but her handwriting remained firm as she recorded the morning's baseline calibration figures:

*07:15 AM. Ambient room temperature: 48 degrees Fahrenheit. Barometric pressure: 29.84 inches of mercury and falling. Line voltage on isolated technical rail: 119.4 volts AC. Studer head azimuth verified with 10 kHz test tone. Channel four output level: plus four decibels balanced.*

She closed the canvas cover. The dry snap of the binding echoed sharply against the acoustic tiles.

She did not fear the deadline.

Julian Vance had thought the threat of financial ruined would compel her to deliver the digitized master like an obedient clerk. He had miscalculated the psychological reality of an audio engineer who had spent her entire adult life analyzing the physical mechanics of signal decay.

A recording represented a physical transaction of energy between the vibrating source, the storage medium, and the listener's nervous system rather than an abstract legal asset.

If the transaction was designed to terminate in this room, she would ensure the energy reached its thermodynamic conclusion.

She leaned forward over the transport deck.

The dark ribbon slipped across the ceramic capstan shaft, guided by the precision sapphire pins. The brown ferric oxide surface showed microscopic striations—hairline scratches where the particles were shedding under the intense mechanical friction of the playback heads.

The counter clicked again: 2,398 feet... 2,399 feet.

The aluminum supply reel wobbled faintly on its rubber hub as the ribbon approached the end of the wind.

The Studer deck counter passes the 2,400-foot mark on the reel.

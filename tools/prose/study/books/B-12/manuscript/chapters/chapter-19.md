# Chapter 19: Clara Haze

The telephone connection to the nursing home in Victoria was filled with the faint, watery hiss of undersea coaxial cable. Mira sat at her desk with her field log open, the black bakelite handset pressed tight against her good right ear.

On the third ring, an elderly woman's voice answered. The voice was thin, reedy, and accompanied by the soft mechanical wheeze of an oxygen concentrator in the background.

"Hello?"

"Mrs. Haze? Clara Haze?" Mira asked, keeping her tone calm and respectful. "My name is Mira Lin. I am an audio restoration technician working in Vancouver. I am currently transferring the master tapes from the Saint Jude Convent session recorded in November nineteen seventy-four."

Silence stretched across the maritime cable for five full seconds. The only sound from the other end was the rhythmic click-whir of the oxygen pump.

"Where did you get that reel?" Clara Haze whispered. Her voice carried the faint, elegant vibrato of a retired concert cellist, but the timbre was brittle with fear.

"The Oakhaven Collection acquired it in probate," Mira answered. "Julian Vance delivered the aluminum flight case to my studio on Monday."

"Julian Vance," the old woman repeated, spitting the syllables like bad medicine. "He was thirty years old back then. He wore black cashmere and stood in the back of the chapel with a leather briefcase while Arthur set up the microphones. We thought he was an arts patron from the university endowment."

Mira leaned forward, her ballpoint pen poised over the lined page of her notebook. "Mrs. Haze, the session sheets list an experimental transducer on Track 4. The notes say it was dropped into the sealed crypt beneath the chapel altar. What were they recording down there?"

The elderly cellist let out a long, trembling breath that rasped against the microphone capsule.

"We were hired to play a memorial requiem for an anonymous patron," Clara said softly. Her voice carried the distinct northern English vowels of someone who had trained at the Royal Northern College of Music fifty years ago. "Arthur Pendelton had three microphones on the floor. But during the second movement, he kept stopping the transport. He kept walking over to the altar and kneeling on the floor, listening to the stone flags."

"Did you hear anything from below?" Mira asked.

"The chapel was cold as a tomb," Clara replied. "My fingers were stiff on the ebony neck. After the third take, Arthur took an unshielded microphone capsule and lowered it down the coal chute into the crypt on fifty feet of heavy cable. When he came back to the mobile console, his face was white as lard."

"What did he tell you?"

"Arthur instructed the musicians not to listen to the monitoring headphones," Clara whispered. "Arthur urged the soprano to sing louder, to push her voice until the preamplifiers distorted. The engineer stated that the machine was pulling something out of the stones, and that the only way to keep it on the ribbon was to feed it sound."

"Mrs. Haze," Mira asked, her voice trembling slightly. "Why did Arthur drop the transducer into the crypt during the second movement?"

Clara's breathing rasped against the maritime connection, the oxygen pump clicking in the background.

"Arthur discovered a subterranean air shaft behind the altar," Clara whispered. "The priory was built over eighteenth-century burial vaults that were sealed during the smallpox outbreaks. Vance brought an archival document from Geneva showing that the diocese had experienced acoustic manifestations in that chapel for two hundred years: chanting without choir members, unexplainable pressure drops that extinguished sanctuary candles. Vance convinced Arthur that they could capture the phenomenon on magnetic emulsion."

"Did Vance stay in the chapel?"

"Vance never entered the sanctuary," Clara replied with bitter emphasis. "He stayed outside in his black sedan with a field monitor plugged into the truck's line feed. He let Arthur take all the acoustic exposure."

Mira gripped the desk edge. "Arthur died a week later, Mrs. Haze. He jumped from the river bridge."

"I know," Clara said. Her voice broke slightly. "He called me two days before he jumped. The engineer insisted that the tape had memorized his address. Arthur claimed it was measuring the distance between his front porch and his workbench. Miss Lin, listen to me carefully."

"I am listening," Mira said.

"Arthur kept saying that the transducer was not a recording microphone," Clara whispered, her words trembling against the background wheeze of the oxygen pump. "He said it was an inductive tap. A closed loop that used the chapel's limestone acoustics to trap an observer that had lived in the cholera crypt since eighteen forty-nine. He said the entity had no physical body, but that it possessed acoustic mass. Once you hear it speaking, it attaches to the cochlear nerve."

Mira touched her left ear, feeling the cold temporal bone beneath her skin. "Why did Vance want it recorded?"

"Vance didn't want the music," Clara said, her voice dropping into a rasping hiss. "Oakhaven collects physical vessels of acoustic entities. They buy them at probate auctions and store them in vault lockers across Europe. Miss Lin, do not let that tape run to the end of the spool."

"What happens at the end of the spool?"

"The circuit closes," Clara said. "The entity leaves the magnetic ribbon and grounds itself in the ear of the person operating the machine. Arthur couldn't bear the pressure in his skull. That's why he jumped. Take a razor to that ribbon before the counter hits zero."


The telephone on Mira's desk was an old Western Electric rotary model, connected to an analog copper landline that ran through the industrial park's private PBX switchboard.

She had spent three hours tracking down Clara Haze's current residence.

The surviving cellist from the 1974 Oakhaven session was now eighty-one years old, living in an assisted-living facility near Point Defiance in Tacoma. Her contact information had appeared in the alumni directory of the Cornish College of the Arts, where Clara had taught chamber music until her retirement in 2005.

Mira dialed the ten-digit number, listening to the mechanical pulses of the rotary wheel clicking back into place.

The line rang four times.

A frail, breathy voice answered the call:

"Hello?"

"Ms. Haze?" Mira said, keeping her tone calm and professional. "My name is Mira Lin. I am an audio restoration specialist in Seattle. I am currently working on the analog tape archives of the Oakhaven Foundation."

A sharp, audible intake of air sounded across the telephone line.

The background noise in Clara's room—the faint, tinny murmur of a daytime television broadcast—was suddenly cut off as the elderly woman muted the television with a remote control.

"Who authorized you to speak to me?" Clara asked. Her voice trembled, carrying a thin, brittle edge of alarm. "Julian Vance promised there would be no more inquiries. The settlement agreement included a perpetual non-disclosure covenant."

"Vance didn't send me," Mira said quickly. "I am a contractor. I have the master tape for Session 74-D on my workbench right now. The four-track recording from the convent chapel."

A long silence followed.

The copper telephone line hummed with sixty-cycle induction from passing utility lines.

"You should not have opened that canister," Clara whispered. Her voice had dropped to a conspiratorial murmur. "That was not music, Ms. Lin. We were not performing a chamber piece."

"Arthur Pendelton's session log called it *Passacaglia for the Unobserved*," Mira said.

"A label for the grant paperwork," Clara said with sudden, bitter clarity. "Arthur had constructed an acoustic trap in that cellar. He spent two weeks embedding microphones into the mortar between the foundation stones. He told us to play with maximum dynamic attack—to drive the cello gut strings until the instruments rattled against the fingerboards."

"What happened during take four, Ms. Haze?"

Across the line, Mira heard the elderly woman's trembling breath.

"Arthur stayed in the tracking alcove with the reel deck," Clara said. "He had a directional microphone pointed into the empty center of the chapel nave. Between the movements, when Evelyn and I rested our bows, Arthur began whispering into the tracking mic. He wasn't talking to us through the headphone foldback. He was speaking to the empty air in the room."

"What was he saying?" Mira asked, leaning over her notepad with her pen poised.

"He was counting," Clara whispered. Her voice shook with profound terror. "He was counting down the distance between the chapel door and his mixing desk. 'One hundred paces... eighty paces... sixty paces.' Evelyn Vance stood up and refused to touch the piano. She threw her felt wedges onto the floor and ran up the cellar stairs."

"And Arthur?"

"Arthur stayed until the tape ran out," Clara said. "When the harbor patrol found him in Lake Union two weeks later, the police came to interview me. They asked me if Arthur had any enemies. I told them Arthur had invited something into that cellar, and when it arrived, he couldn't close the door."


Clara Haze's voice across the telephone line took on the desperate, pleading tone of someone who had survived a shipwreck and watched another vessel steer straight toward the reef.

"Listen to me, Mira," Clara said, using her first name for the first time. "Arthur didn't jump off the Aurora Bridge because he was insane. He jumped because the sound was getting louder."

"The countdown?" Mira asked, gripping the plastic telephone receiver until her knuckles turned ivory.

"The countdown stopped at zero paces while Arthur was sitting at his console in the convent," Clara whispered. "When take four finished, the Voice said: 'Zero paces. The ear is reached.' And then it stopped speaking through the monitors."

"Where did it speak?"

"Inside his head," Clara said. "Arthur told me on the phone two days later. He said the sound had entered his auditory cortex through bone conduction. It wasn't speaking words anymore. It was breathing. He could hear it drawing breath between every thought, pacing the perimeter of his skull from the inside."

Mira felt the hair on the back of her arms stand rigid.

"Why didn't he destroy the tape?" Mira asked.

"Because the tape was holding part of him," Clara replied. "When an acoustic wave converges, it creates a reciprocal bridge. The entity enters the ear, and the auditor's consciousness is drawn backward into the magnetic oxide. Arthur believed that if he burned the tape, he would extinguish his own mind."


Clara Haze paused on the telephone, her breathing shallow and audible through the copper landline receiver.

"There is one more detail, Mira," Clara said softly, her words coming slowly. "A detail that Marcus Vance paid us an additional five thousand dollars never to mention to the coroner or the municipal police."

Mira pressed the telephone receiver tight against her right ear.

"What detail, Clara?"

"On the Friday afternoon before Arthur died," Clara murmured, "Arthur telephoned me at the conservatory. He was calling from a payphone at the marina on Lake Union. He told me he had tried to erase the four-track master using an industrial bulk demagnetizer at an electrical repair shop in Ballard."

"What happened when he tried to erase it?"

"The electromagnetic field didn't clear the tape," Clara whispered. "Arthur said the field caused the ribbon to heat up until the aluminum reel flanges were too hot to touch. And through the speaker of the demagnetizer's power supply, the Voice spoke to him in broad daylight. It said his name. It told him the temperature of the water beneath the Aurora Bridge."

Clara gave a faint, shuddering gasp.

"Arthur threw the demagnetizer into the canal and ran. Forty-eight hours later, they pulled his body from the water."

Mira looked at the Studer deck sitting on her workbench, the ten-inch aluminum reel resting peacefully under the inspection lamp.

The physical ribbon held fifty-two years of memory.

Clara gave a faint, wheezing sob.

"The settlement money bought my house in Tacoma," Clara said. "Vance gave each of us twenty thousand dollars to sign the confidentiality agreement. I took the money. Evelyn Vance took the money. We bought our silence, and we left Arthur in the dark."

Clara's breath grew ragged.

"Listen to me, Ms. Lin," the elderly cellist commanded, her words sharp and desperate. "Do not let the tape run to the end of the reel. When the trailer tape clears the heads, the transfer is complete. Cut the ribbon. Burn the canister. Walk out of that room while you can still hear the world."

The elderly woman hangs up without saying goodbye.

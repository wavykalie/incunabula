# Chapter 36: Vance at the Intercom

At two o'clock on Friday afternoon, three hours ahead of the contractual deadline, the outer security gate of the warehouse rolled open with a heavy, rumbling roar of steel chains.

Mira did not look up from her console.

She heard the heavy thud of multiple car doors closing in the gravel alleyway outside, followed by the firm, synchronized tread of heavy rubber-soled boots marching down the concrete central corridor.

A moment later, the ceiling speaker of the studio intercom crackled with high-voltage static.

The green pilot light on the intercom faceplate flared bright emerald in the dim room.

"Ms. Lin," Julian Vance's voice projected into the control room, flat, loud, and metallic through the small oval paper speaker cone. "I am standing in the corridor outside Studio 4B with two licensed security couriers from Northern Logistics. Our transport vehicle is idling in the alleyway. The time is precisely fourteen-fifteen hours."

Mira sat motionless, her right hand resting flat against the metal console armrest.

"We are aware that you have constructed an unauthorized structural obstruction against the interior face of the access door," Vance continued, his legalistic tone unhurried and completely devoid of emotion. "Section eight, paragraph four of your facility lease expressly prohibits the erection of unpermitted barricades or physical alterations that impede emergency egress."

Outside the heavy door, behind the splintering oak pallets, there came the sound of a heavy pry bar striking the steel door frame.

A crowbar levered into the gap between the door and the jamb, testing the wood.

"You have thirty seconds to remove the obstruction and open the access hatch voluntarily," Vance said through the intercom speaker. "If you refuse, my couriers are authorized under the terms of our salvage lien to apply hydraulic forced-entry equipment. Furthermore, your refusal will be logged as an intentional breach of contract, forfeiting your restoration fee and triggering immediate litigation for fifty thousand dollars in statutory damages."

Mira reached for the desk talkback microphone, depressed the momentary leaf switch, and leaned toward the capsule.

"The countdown is at eight paces, Julian," she said, her voice quiet and deadly calm. "It is touching the outer pallet. If your couriers breach that door, they will step directly into the spatial coordinates matrix."

A brief silence followed on the intercom line.

Through the speaker, Mira heard the faint rustle of Vance's cashmere overcoat as he adjusted his scarf, followed by the dry, muffled murmur of a brief consultation between Vance and his security personnel.

When Vance spoke again, his voice had lost its veneer of corporate courtesy. It was cold, sharp, and impatient.

"Arthur Pendelton spoke identical nonsense before he destroyed his career," Vance said through the speaker. "You have been sitting in an unventilated control room for four days breathing ozone and solvent fumes. What you perceive as an acoustic presence is middle-ear pareidolia induced by physical exhaustion and your pre-existing sensorineural deficit. The entity is not a physical threat to anyone in this building. It is an informational artifact that will ground itself the moment the digital transcription is completed."

"Then why didn't you stay in the convent chapel in 1974?" Mira asked.

The line went dead for three seconds.

In the corridor, the crowbar struck the door frame again, a violent metallic *crack* that rattled the lead core inside the door.

"Use the hydraulic spreader on the lock mortise," Vance instructed someone in the hallway, his voice leaking through the door wood rather than the intercom. "Pop the hinges from the exterior pins."

"You came three hours early because you knew the tape counter was almost at runout," Mira said into the talkback mic. "You wanted to be outside the room when the transfer hit zero paces, so you could collect the reel from a dead woman's hands without paying the contract fee."


Outside the barricaded door, the electric-hydraulic power unit continued its high-pressure cycle.

Through the heavy wood and lead core of the door, Mira heard the distinct, metallic groaning of the steel strike plate as the hydraulic jaws pushed against the jamb. The sound was like a freight train rounding a tight mountain curve—a continuous, screaming shriek of metal undergoing plastic deformation.

"Ms. Lin," Vance's voice crackled through the intercom speaker once more, his words clipped and cold. "The hydraulic ram exerts ten thousand pounds of lateral force. The timber framing of this partition wall was constructed in 1968 from standard non-load-bearing two-by-fours. The entire assembly will fail within forty seconds."

Mira did not answer. She stood with her arms crossed over her chest, watching the four-inch deck screws slowly pull free from the splintering oak pallets.

"If the master reel is damaged during dynamic entry," Vance said, "you will be arrested on felony property destruction charges before you leave this facility. I have the King County prosecutor's office on standby. Your professional license will be revoked. You will never touch an archival recording in this country again."

"You don't care about the tape, Julian," Mira said into the open talkback microphone. "You already have the original 1974 tracking notes. You have Arthur Pendelton's complete schematics. The only thing you need is a certified death certificate showing that the terminal listener absorbed the transfer without surviving."

On the other side of the intercom, Vance went completely silent.

In the corridor, the hydraulic motor gave a sudden, high-pressure cough.

"You think this is a corporate conspiracy," Vance said after a long, icy pause. "You think my family created this phenomenon out of malice. You have no conception of what my father encountered in that convent chapel."

"Your father was a coward," Mira said. "He sent Arthur into the room with eighteen microphones and locked the iron door from the outside."

"My father contained it," Vance whispered, his voice dropping into a harsh, venomous hiss. "For fifty-two years, my family has maintained the quarantine. We paid Arthur's pension. We paid your mother's medical bills in Ballard. We held the boundary. But the medium is degrading. The binder is hydrolyzing. If the tape turns to powder before the transfer completes, the standing wave will expand into the urban grid. It will ground itself in the telephone switches, the fiber-optic lines, the auditory nerves of tens of thousands of people along the Interstate corridor."

Mira stared at the intercom speaker.

"You're not saving the city, Julian," she said softly. "You're trying to patent it."

In the hallway, Vance gave a short, bitter laugh that sounded like dry sticks snapping in the cold.

"Everything has commercial value, Ms. Lin," Vance said. "Even an acoustic singularity."

He turned away from the door. Mira heard him barking an order to his couriers:

"Double the hydraulic line pressure. Take the door down now."

"Break the door down," Vance commanded.

Outside, the high-pitched whine of an electric-hydraulic pump started up, its high-pressure fluid lines humming against the concrete.

Mira released the talkback switch.

She stood up from the console chair and walked to the wall-mounted electrical panel beside the doorway.

She located the dedicated twenty-amp toggle switch that supplied power to the studio intercom system and the hallway monitoring cameras.

She gripped the heavy plastic lever between her thumb and forefinger.

She pulled the lever downward with a sharp, decisive snap.

The circuit breaker tripped open. The green pilot light on the intercom died instantly.

The small paper cone fell silent, severing all contact with the corridor outside.

Mira switches off the intercom power supply, plunging the control room into isolation.

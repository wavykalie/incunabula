# Chapter 38: Observation Glass

Through the heavy double-glazed observation window set into the studio's interior wall, a pale face suddenly appeared.

Teresa Cruz pressed herself against the outer glass pane, her eyes wide with unvarnished panic.

She held an eighteen-inch steel pipe wrench in her gloved right hand. She brought the heavy forged head of the tool down against the outer glass with all her strength.

The impact was deafening—a sharp, dull *thud* that barely resonated through the three-inch acoustic dead-air gap between the laminated panes. The glass did not shatter; it was reinforced with fine wire mesh designed to withstand industrial explosions.

Teresa struck the window again, her knuckles bleeding through her leather work gloves.

She shouted through the glass, her voice muffled into a faint, desperate squeak:

"Mira! Get out! The whole building is being cleared! Vance brought harbor police with a federal warrant! They're cutting the main transformer feed from the street!"

Mira did not move from her station behind the mixing desk.

She looked at Teresa through the double glazing. She saw the raw terror in the studio manager's face—the genuine, blue-collar concern of a woman who had spent fifteen years looking out for the young technicians who drifted through the industrial park.

Teresa had warned her. Teresa had known from the very beginning that the warehouse held old, hungry things that fed on isolated minds and late-night frequencies.

Mira shook her head slowly.

She raised her left hand to the glass, her palm open in a gesture of finality and peace.

She mouthed three words through the acoustic barrier:

*It's too late.*

Teresa stopped hammering with the wrench. Her mouth opened in horror.

She was not looking at Mira's face anymore.

Her eyes had drifted upward and backward, focusing on the empty volume of space directly behind Mira's swivel chair.

In the reflected glare of the sodium security lights filtering through the warehouse clerestory, the air behind Mira's head was no longer transparent.

A towering, vertical column of absolute darkness had gathered behind the chair. It rose from the rubber floor mat to the ceiling acoustic baffles, six and a half feet of dense, light-absorbing spatial distortion that twisted the reflection of the equipment racks into jagged, broken shards.

Within the black column, faint geometric outlines pulsed in rhythmic synchronization with the spinning tape reels.

Concentric logarithmic spirals.

Thousands of microscopic ferric needles suspended in the air, aligning themselves along invisible magnetic flux lines that reached down toward Mira's left ear like delicate, reaching fingers.

Teresa stepped backward from the window, dropping the heavy steel pipe wrench.

The tool hit the concrete corridor floor with a ringing clatter that Mira felt through the foundation slab.


Through the double-glazed observation glass, Teresa Cruz's face was bathed in the flickering amber light of the warehouse emergency beacons.

Teresa had backed away toward the central pillar, but she had not fled.

She stood twenty feet down the corridor, her hands gripping the steel safety railing of the loading dock stairs. Her eyes were fixed on the studio door with an expression of paralyzed, helpless grief.

Teresa knew what happened to engineers who went into the dark with archival tapes.

Ten years ago, a young mastering technician named David had spent three weeks in Studio 2B restoring old radio broadcast transcriptions from the Aleutian Islands. He had started talking about radio voices in the ventilation ducts. One morning Teresa had arrived to find the studio door locked from the inside and David sitting in his chair with both wrists slashed, his blood soaking into the acoustic carpets while the reel-to-reel deck played static at thirty inches per second.

Teresa had sworn she would never let another engineer die in this building.

She looked toward the warehouse exit, where two harbor police officers in high-visibility yellow jackets were unrolling a heavy yellow police barrier across the gravel driveway.

Julian Vance stood beside his Mercedes sedan, speaking into a cellular phone with rapid, angry gestures. His cashmere overcoat was pulled tight against the freezing wind, his black leather gloves gesturing toward the warehouse roofline.

He had called the municipal fire marshal. He had claimed an active chemical spill—a solvent explosion in Studio 4B—to give the city authorities legal justification to cut the main electrical feed from the utility pole.

On the utility pole beside the railroad tracks, two city linemen in bucket trucks were preparing the high-voltage disconnect poles.

Within two minutes, the entire industrial park would lose power.

If the power died before the Studer transport cleared the runout sensor, the playback would freeze mid-cycle. The standing wave would remain trapped in the building forever, an immortal acoustic predator roaming the empty concrete bays.

Teresa looked back at the observation window.

She saw Mira standing behind the mixing desk, her silhouette illuminated by the amber glow of the Genelec VU meters.

Mira looked small against the towering equipment racks, but her stance was completely immovable.

Teresa raised her hands to her mouth and shouted through the cold air with all the power of her industrial studio lungs:

"Mira! Hit the master! Blow the circuit!"

The sound did not penetrate the lead-lined glass.

Inside the control room, Mira did not turn her head.

She did not need to hear Teresa's shout.

She already knew what had to be done.

Teresa backed away down the dark hallway, crossing herself with a trembling hand, her gaze locked on the window until the darkness of the warehouse swallowed her.

Teresa's face turns white as she sees the physical distortion standing directly behind Mira's chair.

# Chapter 28: Eighteen Paces

At two o'clock on Thursday afternoon, the rain settled into a steady, freezing drizzle that veiled the industrial park in gray mist.

Inside Studio 4B, the ambient temperature had dropped to fifty-four degrees. The heating element in the wall-mounted radiator had failed an hour earlier, its thermal fuse tripping with a dry pop that Mira had chosen not to investigate.

She sat at the console with her woolen trench coat buttoned to her throat, her numb fingers wrapped around a steaming mug of boiled tap water.

On the desk, the Studer transport stood idle. The mechanical tape counter read 1,842 feet. Exactly six hundred feet remained on the ten-inch aluminum reel before the trailer tape reached the runout sensor.

At fifteen inches per second, six hundred feet represented eight minutes of continuous playback.

Eight minutes between the current position of the observer and the end of the reel.

Mira looked up at the frosted glass panel set into the heavy acoustic door of the control room.

The corridor outside was fifty feet long, flanked on one side by the raw brick wall of the warehouse shell and on the other by the heavy timber framing of the storage bays. Every ten feet, a bare incandescent bulb hung from an unshielded junction box, casting dim pools of yellow light onto the uneven concrete floor.

Directly outside the studio door, mounted at shoulder height on the brick wall, was the building's main three-phase electrical breaker panel—a massive steel enclosure painted industrial gray, its door latched with a brass hasp.

From the breaker box to the center of Mira's chair was exactly eighteen feet.

She set down the mug of hot water. The ceramic base clicked against the laminate console desk with an unnatural sharpness that made her flinch.

Her rational mind—the disciplined training of an audio engineer who had spent fifteen years analyzing signal-to-noise ratios, acoustic dispersion patterns, and room modes—screamed at her to stop. She could unthread the ribbon, coil it back into its aluminum canister, padlock the flight case, and carry it out to her car. She could drive across the bridge into Seattle, walk into Julian Vance's corporate office on Fourth Avenue, drop the case on his mahogany desk, and forfeit the restoration fee.

She could walk away.

Her hands moved toward the aluminum reel flanges.

Her fingers touched the cold metal rim.

A deep, bone-level certainty held her in place. The realization from Pendelton's blueprint was absolute: the circuit was already closed. The observer was not inside the tape; the observer was inside the physical corridor of the building, navigated by the spatial coordinates the tape had already broadcast into the structure.

If she severed the ribbon or stopped the playback, the spatial coordinates would freeze. The observer would remain trapped in the corridor, locked between the physical world and the magnetic domains, unable to complete the transfer or dissipate back into the null state.

She would be hunted in the dark by an entity that possessed no acoustic reflection.

The only way to end the event was to drive the system to its physical termination point.

She reached for the Studer transport controls. She pressed the play button.

The solenoid clunked into position. The pinch roller slammed upward against the capstan, trapping the tape ribbon in a vice of turning neoprene.

The monitor speakers hissed.

The cello resumed its mourning melody, the low notes vibrating through the wood of the mixing console.

Mira did not put on her headphones. She kept the master monitor faders set at conversational volume—sixty-five decibels SPL—allowing the direct sound from the Genelec nearfields to fill the small room.

The prepared piano sounded its damp chord: E-flat, G-natural, B-flat, with a felt-muted brass bolt vibrating against the lowest copper-wound bass string.

Then the silence fell.

The background hiss on channel four dropped into the familiar, terrifying vacuum.

The voice spoke through both monitor speakers simultaneously, its phantom center image perfectly focused midway between the two black cabinets:

"Eighteen paces."

The voice did not elevate its volume. It maintained the same dry, flat delivery, devoid of breath or inflection.

"Pausing by the fuse box," the voice stated. "Left hand resting flat against the cold enamel of the steel cabinet door. The metal is cooler than the surrounding air by four degrees."

Through the frosted glass panel of the studio door, forty inches above the floor, the dim yellow light from the corridor suddenly dimmed.

A dense, opaque shadow fell across the glass.

The shadow was not the silhouette of a human being. It possessed no distinct head, no shoulders, no recognizable limbs. It was a vertical column of total blackness, six feet in height and two feet in width, so dense that it completely obliterated the light from the hallway incandescent bulb behind it.

Mira stopped breathing.

The hair on her forearms rose, standing stiffly against the wool of her sweater sleeves. A static electrical charge swept across the room, crackling softly through the console wiring.

On the mixing desk, the needle of the channel four VU meter swung upward, pinning itself hard against the red plus-three decibel stop pin.

The needle did not fluctuate with speech dynamics. It remained locked against the mechanical bumper, trembling under an unbroken surge of direct-current voltage.

Outside the door, through two inches of solid core wood and lead-lined acoustic baffles, there came a distinct, metallic sound.

The latch of the electrical breaker panel clicked open.

The sound was not an acoustic vibration traveling through the air. Mira felt it through the soles of her boots, transmitted directly through the concrete foundation slab into the steel frame of her chair.


The black column on the frosted glass did not flicker when the hallway light wavered.

Mira reached into the drawer of the console desk and withdrew an optical lux meter—a handheld digital light meter she used for calibrating monitor video screens. She stood up slowly, keeping her boots on the rubber matting to avoid making a sound, and extended the white diffuser dome toward the door.

The ambient illumination in the control room read twelve lux. When she aimed the sensor directly at the frosted glass panel where the shadow pressed, the digital display dropped to zero point zero lux.

Total optical extinction.

The shadow was not merely blocking the illumination from the corridor lamp. It was absorbing the ambient photons in the room, pulling light inward across the glass surface like a cold heat sink drawing warmth from an engine block.

She lowered the meter. The digital readout returned to twelve lux as soon as the sensor moved three inches away from the perimeter of the dark column.

A sharp, electric static tick sounded inside the ceiling speaker of the studio intercom. The intercom was an analog tube-driven unit mounted above the doorway, wired to a brass talkback station in the outer corridor. The power switch sat in the off position.

The small green pilot light on the intercom faceplate glowed with a faint, ghostly luminescence.

The voice spoke through the intercom speaker, bypassing the audio console entirely:

"Seventeen paces. The floorboards beneath the gray vinyl tile are saturated with dry rot. Weight transferred to the right foot."

The voice on the monitors spoke again:

"Counting the fuses. Sixty-amp main disconnect. Thirty-amp branch circuit for Studio 4B. The copper bus bar carries sixty cycles per second."

The shadow on the frosted glass remained perfectly still.

It did not sway. It did not breathe. It lingered against the frosted pane like a stain of black ink pressed between two sheets of plate glass.

Mira sat in her chair with her hands clenched in her lap, her fingernails digging deep into the palms of her hands until the pain broke through the numbness.

She kept her eyes fixed on the glass, watching the black outline.

The tape counter advanced: 1,843 feet... 1,844 feet... 1,845 feet.

The shadow lingers on the glass, motionless, waiting for the tape counter to advance.

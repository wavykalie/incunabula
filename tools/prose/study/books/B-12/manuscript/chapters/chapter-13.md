# Chapter 13: The Cardigan

At two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, the cold river fog pressed flat against the wire-glass skylights, turning the studio ceiling into a dim, pearlescent sheet.

Mira had not eaten since morning. She brewed a fresh mug of green tea using the electric kettle on the workbench, listening to the small heating element hiss as the water came to a boil. Her left ear throbbed with a persistent, low-frequency pressure, like the suction created inside an elevator cab descending too rapidly.

She set the blue ceramic mug on the corner of the mixing desk.

The control room was warmer now, heated by the vacuum tubes in her outboard compressor rack and the idling power supplies of the Studer deck. Feeling a bead of sweat prickle beneath the collar of her wool cardigan, Mira unbuttoned the gray knit fabric, slipped her arms from the sleeves, and hung the garment on the bentwood coat stand near the door.

She sat down in her swivel chair and engaged the transport.

The ten-inch metal reels turned smoothly on their splines. The Ampex tape slipped past the playback head, whispering against the polished steel guides.

Through the Genelec monitors, the slow cello line from the convent chapel unfolded, its dark vibrato drifting through the treated room. Beneath the strings, the tape hiss rose and fell with the rhythmic breathing of Track 4.

Three seconds passed.

"Gray cardigan on the brass hook," the voice said from the left speaker. The delivery was flat, conversational, and devoid of theatrical emphasis. "The left cuff has three threads unraveled near the horn button."

Mira froze in her chair.

She did not breathe. She did not turn her head.

Slowly, deliberately, she looked down at the empty sleeve of her trench coat hanging on the rack. Behind it hung the gray cardigan. The frayed threads on the left cuff were visible even in the dim halogens, dangling three inches below the seam where she had snagged the wool on a flight case latch two weeks ago.

Her heart began to hammer with heavy thuds against her ribs. She had not touched the cardigan while the tape was playing yesterday. She had taken it off less than ninety seconds ago, while the tape was paused on the transport.

The voice spoke again, its tone neither hurried nor delayed:

"Forty-two paces. The second light fixture in the outer hallway flickers at one hundred and twenty cycles."

She stood in the center of the control room, her eyes fixed on the gray cardigan.

The garment hung motionless from the brass peg. The wool was five-gauge ribbed lambswool, dyed charcoal gray, with horn buttons sewn along the right placket. On the left cuff, three strands of wool thread dangled three inches below the seam, exactly as the voice had spoken.

She had snagged that cuff two weeks ago on the corner latch of her portable acoustic analyzer while loading gear into her station wagon after a field recording session in Abbotsford. No one in this building had ever seen that frayed thread. She had never mentioned it to Teresa Cruz. She had never mentioned it to anyone.

A cold draft crept beneath her shirt, raising goosebumps along her forearms.

She stepped to the mixing desk, her hand reaching for the solo fader of Channel 4.

The fader was an analog Penny and Giles conductive-plastic slide potentiometer, calibrated in steps of two decibels. She pushed the slider up to unity gain.

The tape hiss grew louder, filling the quiet room with the soft pink-noise murmur of magnetic particles passing the head gap. Behind the hiss, the breathing on the track continued: slow, calm, rhythmic, drawing air at fourteen cycles per minute.

It was the resting respiration rate of an adult human.

Mira sprang from her chair, crossed the room in two strides, and threw open the soundproofed door.

The outer warehouse corridor stretched sixty feet into the gloom, cold and damp. High overhead, mounted to the rusty steel roof trusses, the second fluorescent ballast buzzed with an angry, oscillating hum, its twin tubes strobing with a pale, stuttering violet glow.

She stood in the doorway, the draft biting through her thin cotton shirt.

The hallway was deserted. At the far end, forty paces away, the heavy corrugated security gate was padlocked shut against the rain. The concrete floor was slick with condensation, reflecting the stuttering violet light of the failing ballast.

She listened for footsteps, holding her breath until her ribs ached.

Nothing moved in the drafty corridor except a torn scrap of cardboard tumbling across the expansion joints. But the air smelled different out here. It smelled of cold river water, wet clay, and the faint, sweet trace of crushed almond meal that she had noticed when Vance first opened the aluminum flight case.

She walked forward three paces into the corridor, her sneakers squeaking on the wet concrete.

The flickering fluorescent tube buzzed louder as she approached, casting strobing shadows that made the cinder blocks seem to vibrate. She looked up at the ballast casing. It was an old industrial ballast from nineteen seventy-two, its transformer tar oozing from a thermal crack in the housing.

The frequency of the flicker was exactly one hundred and twenty cycles: the rectified line frequency of utility alternating current.

How could an analog magnetic tape recorded fifty years ago in a convent chapel know the electrical failure rate of a light fixture installed in an industrial warehouse in nineteen ninety-four?

She backed into Studio 4B and dragged the heavy door shut, twisting the double deadbolts until the brass cylinders clicked into their sockets.

Across the quiet floor of the control room, the Studer reels continued to spin, feeding ribbon into the take-up spool with mechanical indifference.


The control room had grown uncomfortably warm by mid-afternoon.

The vacuum tubes inside the vintage Pultec equalizers and the heavy power transformers of the Studer console vented continuous dry heat into the sealed enclosure, raising the ambient temperature to seventy-six degrees.

Mira felt sweat prickling beneath the collar of her charcoal wool cardigan.

She paused the Studer transport, unbuttoned the cardigan, and stood up from the console. She walked to the small coat stand in the corner of the room—a brass Victorian pole with four curved hanging hooks that Teresa Cruz had salvaged from an estate sale in Queen Anne.

She hung the cardigan on the north-facing hook.

The brass hook dipped slightly under the weight of the wool, oscillating back and forth with a faint, metallic pendulum swing that lasted five seconds before coming to rest against the acoustic wall panel.

She returned to the console, took a sip of lukewarm tap water from her blue mug, and pressed the green play button.

The reels spun forward.

The cello resumed its mournful passacaglia.

During the fourth bar of the movement, the cello melody rested on an open D-string sustain.

The voice on channel four spoke:

"The gray wool cardigan hangs on the brass hook. The third hook from the door. The fabric carries the scent of cedar shavings and laundry starch."

Mira froze with her hand hovering over the monitor volume knob.

She had purchased the cardigan two months ago at an estate clothing shop on Capitol Hill. She had kept it in a cedar chest in her apartment bedroom until this morning.

She turned her head slowly toward the corner of the room.

The brass hook was no longer stationary.

In the dead, unventilated air of the soundproofed studio, the heavy brass hook was swinging gently from side to side in a narrow, rhythmic arc, casting a moving shadow against the beige acoustic fabric of the wall.

Her gaze fixes on the swinging brass hook in the corner of the room.

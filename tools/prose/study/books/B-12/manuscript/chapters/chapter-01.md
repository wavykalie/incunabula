# Chapter 1: The Flight Case

The rain on the wire-glass skylight sounded like dried beans dropped into a zinc washbasin. Mira Lin counted seven seconds between the heavier gusts, her right thumb pressed against the aluminum edge of the Studer A80 console. The machine had been idling since dawn, its capstan spinning at a whisper, heating the room by four degrees. A faint smell rose from the ventilation slats behind the meter bridge: aged transformer varnish, warmed phenolic circuit board, and the sharp, mineral trace of dust settling on wirewound power resistors.

Outside, the industrial park along the river lay submerged in late November gloom. The tide was running high, pushing dark gray water against the creosoted pilings of the abandoned cannery piers three hundred yards away. A diesel switcher locomotive rumbled on the spur line behind the warehouse, its low-frequency vibration vibrating through the concrete slab and setting up a faint sympathetic buzz in the spring-reverb tank mounted in rack three.

Mira adjusted the bias trimmer on channel one with a ceramic alignment tool. In electroacoustics, stability was an illusion maintained by constant vigilance. The Studer's discrete audio cards, hand-soldered in Zurich thirty years ago, drifted with every two-degree shift in ambient temperature. If the bias current dropped by half a milliampere, high-frequency distortion crept into the top octave; if it climbed, the tape saturation ceiling collapsed by three decibels.

She loved the machine for its unforgiving precision. Unlike modern digital converters that hid errors behind floating-point rounding, the Studer laid bare every physical law: friction, inductance, magnetic hysteresis, and the slow thermodynamic decay of plastic tape ribbon.

Mira glanced at the wall behind the console, where her acoustic calibration certificates hung in simple black frames. Below them sat the patch bay: ninety-six balanced bantam jacks wired to Mogami multi-pair snake cables running through copper conduits beneath the floating floor. Every connection in Studio 4B was star-grounded to a dedicated eight-foot copper rod driven into the river silt beneath the warehouse slab, ensuring zero ground potential difference between chassis.

In high-end preservation, cleanliness was not a preference; it was an absolute physical law. A single particle of coal dust from the railway spur outside, lodged between a tape head gap and a running ribbon, could plow a permanent microscopic furrow through fifty years of recorded magnetic domains in a hundredth of a second.

She kept the room under positive air pressure, forcing filtered air outward through the door seams to prevent industrial pollutants from entering. But the smell of Julian Vance's wet overcoat brought the damp river air straight into her cleanroom.

Julian Vance did not shake the rain from his coat. He stood two paces inside the heavy acoustic outer door of Studio 4B, his charcoal overcoat dark with wet down the shoulders, holding a brushed aluminum flight case by its recessed spring handle. The case measured eighteen inches square, fitted with butterfly latches and ball corners stamped from galvanized steel. Water dripped from the hem of his trousers onto the black vulcanized rubber mat by the sill, forming small, separate beads that refused to soak into the non-porous material.

You keep the hallway cold, Miss Lin, Vance said. His voice was soft, pitched just below the forty-decibel ambient noise floor of the room. He spoke with the flat, scrubbed cadence of someone whose corporate position depended entirely on never having his accent placed by strangers.

The air handling unit is balanced for the humidity cabinets, Mira said. She did not leave her swivel stool. Her hands remained flat on the armrests, fingers curling slightly around the cracked vinyl padding. If the relative humidity climbs over forty-five percent, thirty-year-old binder turns into gum on the tape lifters. You said on the telephone you had a ten-inch reel.

A ten-inch aluminum spool, Vance said. He stepped across the sealed neoprene boundary of the floating floor. The floorboards gave off no creak; two inches of dense Sylomer damping pads beneath the tongue-and-groove decking took his weight without a sound. Session master 74-D. Recorded in October nineteen seventy-four at Saint Jude's Priory before the diocese deconsecrated the chapel. The Oakhaven Collection purchased the physical archive in probate last November.

He laid the flight case flat on the drafting table beside the soldering station. His hands were pale, the nails trimmed square and buffed. Dull gunmetal studs secured the cuffs of his white shirt, protruding half an inch beyond the heavy woolen sleeves of his coat.

The contract is straightforward, Vance said. He withdrew a black calfskin folder from his inside breast pocket and set it beside the case. Physical restoration, cleaning, baking if the oxide has destabilized, and an uncompressed twenty-four-bit, one-hundred-and-ninety-two-kilohertz archival digitization. You have until Friday afternoon at five o'clock. At five-fifteen, our courier will collect the physical reel and our digital storage drives from your loading dock.

Four days is tight for Ampex tape from seventy-four, Mira said. She touched her left mastoid bone with two knuckles, tapping twice in quick succession. A dull knock sounded through the temporal bone, deadened and hollow, like tapping through damp cardboard. If the polyurethane binder has undergone hydrolysis, the tape has to bake at fifty-five degrees Celsius for twelve hours minimum. Then it requires twenty-four hours in the cooling cabinet to stabilize the tension before it can touch a transport.

Then you will begin this morning, Vance said. He did not blink. His eyes were pale gray, rimmed with red veins that suggested he had spent the night in an airport lounge or an overnight sleeper train. The Oakhaven trustees do not offer extensions on provenance materials. The consignment includes a fifty-thousand-dollar indemnity clause for non-performance or media loss. You reviewed the schedule before signing the engagement letter.

I signed the engagement letter on the assumption that the reel was in readable condition, Mira said. Archival recovery is not a printing press. If the oxide flakes off during the first wind, fifty thousand dollars won't put the magnetic particles back on the polyester base.

The reel is intact, Vance replied, his voice dropping another decibel into a quiet whisper. It has remained inside this flight case since nineteen seventy-nine. The previous custodian kept the case in a climate-controlled vault in Zurich. There is no physical mold. There is no vinegar syndrome.

He reached out and flipped the two butterfly latches on the flight case. The heavy spring-loaded wings rotated ninety degrees with a sharp metallic clack, the cams pulling the lid free from its rubber extrusion seal.

A puff of cold, dry air escaped from the dark interior foam.

Mira smelled it across three feet of open space: iron oxide, whale-oil lubricant from an early batch of Ampex magnetic emulsion, and a peculiar sweet odor like crushed almond meal.

Inside the die-cut ester foam lay a ten-and-a-half-inch precision aluminum reel with three NAB hub cutouts. The dark brown magnetic ribbon was wound smooth across the hub, but the outer wraps showed a dull, grayish cast along the edges.

Sticky-shed, Mira said, leaning forward to examine the dull bloom without touching it. The polyester base is fine, but the binder has taken on atmospheric moisture over fifty years. If I hit play on this deck right now, the oxide would peel off the backing like wet paint and clog the playback gap in three seconds.

That is why you are paid forty-five hundred dollars to bake it, Vance said. He did not close the case. He turned his wrist and checked his watch--a vintage mechanical chronometer with an enamel dial and a sweep-second hand that ticked six times between each numeral. Forty-eight hours for stabilization leaves thirty-six hours for transfer and quality control. We expect clean multi-track stems. Four discrete channels.

Mira frowned. She picked up the carbon copy of the purchase agreement from her desk. The session sheets in your email listed an acoustic trio. Cello, prepared upright piano, and soprano.

There is a fourth channel, Vance said.

What is on Track 4?

Vance adjusted the gold cufflink on his left wrist. The gray fabric of his overcoat rustled softly in the quiet room. The recording engineer at Saint Jude kept a reference microphone open in the ambulatory to track low-frequency structural rumble from the river traffic. We require that channel captured with identical fidelity. Do not filter it. Do not apply dynamic gating or high-pass equalization.

If it's ambient low-frequency bleed below forty hertz, it will eat up digital headroom, Mira said. Sub-bass rumble from industrial barges can push the converters into inter-sample clipping if the analog preamps aren't high-passed.

Leave the headroom to our mastering technicians in Basel, Vance replied, his voice firm and final. Your contract specifies flat, bit-accurate reproduction from the reproduction head to the digital master file. No equalization curve other than the NAB standard alignment. Clean it, equalize it to the NAB curve, and output the four stems as broadcast WAV files with embedded timecode.

Mira looked from the open flight case to Vance's pale, unblinking face. Through the wire-glass skylight, the sound of the rain slackened for three beats, leaving only the quiet sixty-cycle hum of the Studer power supply and the faint, rhythmic ticking of the chronometer on Vance's wrist.

She reached toward the flight case, her fingers stopping half an inch above the aluminum flange.

The metal of the reel was freezing cold, colder than the room air, as though the spool had been stored in an uninsulated ice locker rather than a climate-controlled vault.

I will log the tape intake in the preservation ledger, Mira said. You will receive the verification hash by email when the first pass completes.

Vance gave a single, curt nod. 

The Studio 4B control room had been built in 1972 inside the former cold-storage locker of a Pacific salmon cannery.

The walls were fourteen inches thick, constructed from heavy cedar timbers clad in lead-sheet soundproofing and faced with two-inch acoustic rockwool panels covered in coarse beige burlap. Because the room possessed no parallel surfaces—the ceiling sloped downward at an eight-degree angle and the side walls splayed outward toward the rear bass traps—it possessed an acoustic decay time of less than one tenth of a second across the speech band.

In that dry, deadened environment, human speech felt startlingly close, stripped of the comforting acoustic bloom that rooms normally impart to the human voice.

Mira sat at the center of the listening triangle, exactly six feet from the dual Genelec nearfields.

She opened the calibration binder and laid out her precision alignment tools: a stainless steel azimuth adjustment screwdriver with an insulated ceramic handle, a magnetic flux loop probe, a mirror-polished head inspection prism, and an unrecorded spool of virgin leader tape.

Before she touched the vintage Oakhaven master, every guide pin, flutter roller, and capstan shaft on the Studer transport had to be demagnetized. A magnetized guide post could erase high-frequency harmonics from a fifty-year-old tape ribbon on contact, leaving permanent bias flutter across the audio tracks.

She plugged in the heavy handheld demagnetizer wand, waiting for its internal sixty-cycle coil to reach operational temperature.

The flight case sat on the heavy maple workbench like an unexploded naval shell.

Mira inspected the exterior aluminum extrusions. The corners were capped with three-rivet nickel-plated steel ball caps, scratched and pitted from decades of rough transit in cargo holds and equipment vans. A stenciled white serial number—*OAK-74-004-D*—ran along the upper valance, partially obscured by yellowed residue from peeling masking tape labels.

Julian Vance stood by the doorway, his polished umbrella dripping water onto the corrugated rubber runner.

"The terms of the transfer are explicit, Ms. Lin," Vance said, his voice quiet against the drumming of the rain. "The audio on that reel represents the only master in existence. There are no safety copies, no lacquer reference discs, and no cassette dubs. You are receiving custody of the physical primary source."

Mira pulled on a pair of white cotton inspection gloves before touching the case.

"A fifty-two-year-old Ampex master requires controlled environmental stabilization," she told him, her tone firm and matter-of-fact. "If the polyurethane binder has undergone hydrolysis, the oxide layer will strip off against the capstan in thirty seconds. I need to bake the reel at fifty-four degrees Celsius for four hours before threading it."

Vance did not blink. His pale gray eyes remained fixed on her face.

"You have four days," Vance replied smoothly. "The digitized stems must be delivered to our legal representatives by Friday at five o'clock. If the physical ribbon is damaged or if the transcription is incomplete, our agreement specifies liquidated damages of fifty thousand dollars."

He did not wait for her answer.

He turned on the rubber mat, opened the heavy acoustic door, and paused on the threshold.

Five o'clock on Friday, Miss Lin, he said without looking back. The courier does not wait for second passes.

Sharp click of the heavy steel flight case latches snapping open on the workbench.

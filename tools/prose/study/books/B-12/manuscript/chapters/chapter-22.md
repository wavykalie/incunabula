# Chapter 22: The Coffee Test

At seven o'clock on Wednesday evening, a sharp knock rattled the outer door of Studio 4B.

Mira jumped from her chair, her heart slamming against her ribs. She grabbed the steel splicing block from the workbench, weighing the two-pound slab of metal in her right fist like a bludgeon.

"Mira? Open up, it's Teresa."

The voice through the heavy insulated door was muffled but unmistakably human.

Mira exhaled a ragged breath, set the splicing block down on the bench, and unlocked the two heavy deadbolts. She pulled the door open three inches.

Teresa Cruz stood in the dim corridor holding two cardboard takeout cups from the diner on the highway. She wore her grease-stained canvas jacket and a wool beanie pulled down over her ears. Her breath misted in the cold hallway air.

"You look like you're about to swing a bat at my head," Teresa said, eyeing Mira's pale face and bloodshot eyes. "I brought black coffee. Real coffee, not that instant sawdust you brew on the hot plate."

"Come in," Mira whispered, stepping back and pulling the door wide. "Close it behind you. Lock both bolts."

Teresa stepped across the threshold, kicking the door shut with the heel of her work boot. She twisted the two deadbolt cylinders until the brass tongues clicked home.

"Jesus, Mira," Teresa said, looking around the control room. "What is with the yellow chalk on the floor out there? You got pace numbers drawn all the way to the loading dock like a murder investigation."

She handed Mira one of the paper cups. The cardboard was hot against Mira's cold fingers, smelling of burnt chicory and dark roast.

"I measured the hallway," Mira said, leaning against the drafting table. "Arthur Pendelton's tape is counting down the exact distance from the exterior rolling gate to this console."

Teresa sighed, blowing across the surface of her coffee before taking a slow sip. "Look at yourself, Mira. You're trembling. Your eyes are bloodshot. You've been locked in an isolated acoustic chamber for three days listening to a forty-year-old tape of a dead man. You're suffering from sensory deprivation psychosis."

"It's not psychosis, Teresa," Mira said, her voice shaking with quiet intensity. "The voice spoke thirty-six paces when you walked up. It described the weather-stripping. It described the coat on the hook yesterday."

Teresa took off her canvas jacket and threw it over the back of the drafting stool. Beneath the jacket, her flannel work shirt was stained with dark half-moons of sweat beneath the armpits.

"Listen to me, Mira," Teresa said, resting her calloused hands on the console armrest. "Thirty years ago, when I was working as a junior tech at the CBC studios on Georgia Street, we had an overnight editor who worked on historical radio dramas. He spent eighty hours a week cutting magnetic tape with razor blades, breathing Freon fumes and listening to actor dialogue through Sennheiser headphones."

Mira watched her. "What happened to him?"

"He started hearing coded messages in the applause tracks," Teresa said, her voice dropping. "He claimed the audience members were coughing in Morse code, warning him that the station transmitters were cooking his bone marrow. One night he took an axe to the master tape vaults, smashed twenty thousand dollars worth of two-inch multi-tracks, and ran down Burrard Street naked in the snow."

"That was chemical toxicity from solvent fumes, Teresa," Mira said.

"That was isolation, Mira," Teresa countered, leaning close. "When you take human hearing and strip away room reflections, the brain builds its own monsters out of the noise floor."

Teresa set her cup down on the meter bridge, took off her work gloves, and looked at the Studer deck.

"All right," Teresa said, rolling up the sleeves of her flannel shirt. "You want to prove to me that I'm wrong and you're sane? Put the headphones on me. Play the track."

Mira hesitated. "Teresa, Clara Haze warned me--"

"Put the cans on my head, Mira," Teresa insisted, her dark eyes steady and unyielding. "I've been mixing live sound for thirty years. If there's an entity talking on that magnetic ribbon, my ears will hear it."


The three sharp knocks on the heavy acoustic door sounded like gunshots in the dead quiet of the room.

Mira leaped from her chair, her elbow striking the edge of the console and sending a box of cotton swabs spilling across the rubber floor mat.

"Mira? It's Teresa. Open up."

Mira drew a ragged breath, unlocked the steel deadbolt, and pulled the heavy door open four inches.

Teresa Cruz stood in the corridor holding two paper cups from the Greek diner on Fourth Avenue, the steam rising through the plastic sip lids into the freezing hallway air.

Teresa wore her heavy canvas Carhartt work jacket with the fleece collar turned up against the cold. She took one look at Mira's face—at the dark, bruised circles under her eyes and the pale, trembling tension around her mouth—and pushed her way into the control room with her shoulder.

She set the two coffee cups on the workbench beside the splicing block.

"You look like death, Lin," Teresa said bluntly, crossing her arms over her chest. "When was the last time you slept?"

"I slept on the cot on Tuesday," Mira said, leaning against the door frame.

Teresa looked down at the concrete floor outside the door, where Mira's yellow railroad chalk marks ran down the center of the hallway.

"What's with the crime-scene chalk?" Teresa asked, pointing a blunt thumb toward the corridor. "And why is the whole building smelling like hot solder?"

"It's the tape, Teresa," Mira said quietly. "Channel four has an acoustic coordinate matrix encoded into the oxide. It's tracking the distance from the loading dock to this chair."

Teresa stared at Mira for five long seconds.

Her expression shifted from irritation into deep, professional concern.

"You've been in this box for seventy-two hours listening to high-level test tones on headphones," Teresa said gently. "Your left ear is already compromised. Acoustic fatigue does things to the brain, Mira. Auditory pareidolia is real. Your auditory cortex is trying to extract speech patterns out of tape hiss and sixty-cycle hum."

"It's not pareidolia," Mira insisted. "It described my mother's maiden name. It described the cardigan on the brass hook."

Teresa walked over to the console desk.

She picked up the Beyerdynamic DT 990 Pro headphones from the armrest, inspecting the velvet ear pads.

"All right," Teresa said firmly, stepping between Mira and the console. "We settle this right now. If there's an entity talking on channel four, I have two healthy ears that have been mastering audio since nineteen ninety-two. Let's see what's actually on the ribbon."

Teresa puts on the monitoring headphones and presses play on the Studer transport.

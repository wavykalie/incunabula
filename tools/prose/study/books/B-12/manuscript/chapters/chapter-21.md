# Chapter 21: Thirty-Six Paces

Dusk fell over the industrial river basin at four-forty on Wednesday afternoon, turning the warehouse skylights into sheets of bruised purple glass.

Mira sat in her listening chair with the room lights extinguished. The only illumination came from the amber dials of the Studer VU meters and the green power LEDs on the front baffles of the Genelec monitors. In the dim control room, the analog meters looked like two glowing eyes staring out from the rack gear.

She had spent the last two hours reviewing the digital stems on her workstation.

Channels 1, 2, and 3 were glorious: the cello vibrato, the prepared upright piano with brass bolts wedged between the strings, the soprano's soaring Latin lament echoing off the chapel limestone. The stereo imaging on the music tracks was wide and three-dimensional, capturing the natural acoustic boundary of the deconsecrated sanctuary with breathtaking clarity.

Then came Channel 4.

Mira engaged the solo button on Track 4 and set the Studer speed selector to fifteen inches per second.

The transport relays clicked. The heavy flywheel spun up to speed, and the tape began to flow across the reproduction head.

At first, there was only the steady hiss of Ampex 456 tape stock--a soft, velvety pink noise centered around two kilohertz. Then came the breathing: slow, regular, unhurried, like a deep sleeper resting in a quiet bedroom.

"Thirty-six paces," the voice said from the phantom center between the monitors. The tone was calm, flat, and chillingly intimate. "Hand touching the acoustic weather-stripping."

She looked at the digital correlation meter on her secondary monitor.

The Lissajous display was expanding from a hair-thin diagonal line into a wide, rotating ellipse. The phase relationship between the left and right Genelec monitors was shifting continuously, revolving at seven cycles per second.

In acoustic engineering, a rotating phase ellipse indicated that the sound source was physically orbiting the listening position.

Yet the room was sealed.

Through the double-glazed acoustic observation window, the outer hallway was pitch black except for the flickering violet tube at pace forty-two.

She felt a heavy pressure building behind her eyeballs, like the intracranial hypertension caused by carbon dioxide inhalation. Her left ear throbbed with a rhythmic, pounding ache that matched the thirty-six-pace countdown on the tape.

Outside in the corridor, thirty-six paces from where Mira sat, a floorboard in the hallway creaked.

The sound was distinct, physical, and unmistakable: the sharp, wooden groan of a tongue-and-groove plank bending under eighty kilograms of distributed weight.

Mira's hands locked around the armrests of her chair.

The warehouse corridor floor was poured concrete, not wood. The only wooden flooring in this entire wing was the tongue-and-groove marine plywood of Studio 4B's floating room.

The creak had not come from the hallway.

It had come from the perimeter sill of her own studio door.

She sat motionless, her breath trapped in her throat, listening to the silence. In her left ear, the four-kilohertz tinnitus screamed like a dentist's drill, but her right ear strained into the dark room.

Beneath the steady hiss of the tape monitors, she heard the faint, dry rustle of fabric brushing against the neoprene weather-stripping along the bottom of the heavy acoustic slab.

Someone was standing on Point Zero.

"Thirty-five paces," the voice murmured from the speakers. "The brass latch is cold."

Mira reached slowly for the desk lamp switch, her fingers trembling so violently that she knocked her ballpoint pen off the blotter. The plastic barrel rolled across the desk and dropped onto the plywood floor with a loud snap that sounded like a gunshot in the silent room.

She froze, staring at the brass handle of the deadbolt.

Through the six-inch acoustic door, she felt a sudden drop in air pressure. Her eardrums popped inward, exactly as they did when descending rapidly in an unpressurized aircraft. The air in the room grew noticeably colder, dropping three degrees in thirty seconds.

On the console, the Track 4 VU meter twitched upward to minus six decibels, pulsing in sync with the sound of slow, heavy breathing outside the threshold.


Dusk arrived at five-fifteen on Wednesday, casting long, bruised shadows across the warehouse clerestory windows.

Mira sat alone in Studio 4B with the overhead fluorescent tubes switched off. The only illumination came from the amber backlit dials of the Studer VU meters and the green phosphor glow of her digital workstation monitor.

She reached for the Studer transport controls and pressed play.

The solenoid engaged with its heavy, familiar clunk.

The quarter-inch ribbon began its journey across the head block at fifteen inches per second.

The cello sounded through the Genelec nearfield monitors, playing the sixth variation of the passacaglia. The melody was somber, constructed around ascending minor thirds that resolved onto an unresolved dominant ninth chord.

The prepared piano struck its damp notes, the felt wedges muting the upper harmonics into a dull, wooden knock.

Mira kept her hands flat on the console armrest.

The room tone on channel four deepened.

The subtle acoustic flutter that accompanied the Voice shifted downward in frequency, dropping from sixty hertz into the sub-acoustic register at eighteen hertz.

At eighteen hertz, the sound was not heard by the ears.

It was felt by the rib cage—a rhythmic, physical vibration that made the air inside Mira's lungs tremble with every oscillation.

The Voice emerged from the center channel:

"Thirty-six paces."

The monotone was identical to Tuesday's delivery, completely devoid of dynamic inflection or human emotion.

"Hand touching the acoustic weather-stripping of the sound lock door," the Voice stated. "The neoprene rubber is aged, cracked along the top corner where the door frame has settled."

Mira looked toward the sound lock door twenty feet away.

The door was fitted with black closed-cell neoprene weather-stripping that she had installed herself three years ago to isolate the control room from corridor noise. The top corner of the stripping was indeed cracked, split along the diagonal seam where the heavy timber frame had shifted during the winter rains.

No one else knew that weather-stripping was split.

It was invisible from the outside, hidden behind the lead-lined door stop.

From the hallway outside the sound lock, a distinct sound traveled through the heavy double door.

A heavy wooden floorboard creaked.

The sound was not an electrical artifact on the tape.

It was an acoustic event occurring in the physical hallway thirty feet from where she sat.

The floorboard groaned under the deliberate, concentrated weight of an adult human foot shifting forward onto the ball of the sole.

Mira stopped breathing.

Her right hand moved toward the monitor faders, but her fingers froze before touching the plastic knobs.

On the tape, the Voice continued:

"Left foot stepping onto the rubber matting. Thirty-four paces. The corridor air temperature is fifty-two degrees. Relative humidity seventy-eight percent."

In the hallway, a second floorboard groaned.

The sound was five feet closer.

Mira sat rigid in her swivel chair, her fingernails pressing into the vinyl upholstery of the armrests.

The Studer deck hummed quietly, its aluminum reels spinning in perfect balance, drawing the magnetic ribbon through the three-micron playback gap at fifteen inches per second.

Outside the sound lock, the heavy footsteps paused.


The silence in the hallway stretched for four minutes.

Mira remained frozen behind the mixing console, her eyes locked on the double acoustic doors of the sound lock.

The Studer A80 transport hummed softly, its capstan motor drawing the quarter-inch Ampex ribbon through the playback guides with uninterrupted precision. The supply reel had emptied down to the one-thousand-foot mark, the white mechanical counter clicking past one thousand two hundred feet.

On the mixing desk, the needle of the channel four VU meter was rising slowly.

It was no longer twitching on transient spikes.

It was climbing in a smooth, continuous direct-current ramp: minus ten decibels... minus seven decibels... minus three decibels... zero decibels.

The electrical power on channel four was increasing exponentially as the physical distance closed.

Under normal electroacoustic conditions, sound pressure decreases with the square of the distance from the source—the inverse-square law that governs all spherical radiation in three-dimensional space.

On channel four, the law was inverted.

The signal was gaining amplitude as it approached, drawing kinetic energy from the ambient air of the warehouse corridor to build its acoustic wavefront.

Then, from the outer sound lock vestibule, there came a sound that made Mira's teeth ache with cold.

A fingernail—or something hard, keratinous, and dry—scratched slowly down the painted steel face of the outer door.

The sound was deliberate.

It traveled down the metal panel for three feet, producing a high-pitched, metallic shriek that vibrated through the lead core into the control room.

On the tape, the Voice spoke with immediate, simultaneous synchronization:

"Thirty-two paces. Testing the exterior barrier with the fingernail. The steel is cold. The gray industrial enamel is chipped along the lower hinge."

Silence settled over the warehouse, thick, freezing, and absolute.

Mira sits frozen in her chair, holding her breath to listen to the silence.

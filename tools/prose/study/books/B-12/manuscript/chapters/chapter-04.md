# Chapter 4: The Calibration Loop

The convection oven had completed its cooling cycle at dawn on Tuesday. Mira Lin unlocked Studio 4B at seven-thirty, her sneakers making a soft, rhythmic squeak on the damp asphalt outside before crossing the rubber threshold mat.

The prep alcove was cool again. The digital thermometer on the dehydrator read nineteen point two degrees Celsius.

Mira opened the oven door, reached inside with clean cotton gloves, and lifted the Delrin baking hub. The tape pack felt firm and consolidated, cool to the touch, with none of the greasy tackiness that had coated the emulsion yesterday morning. The outer wraps had drawn tight against the hub core, forming a glassy, chocolate-brown cylinder that showed no stepped edges or popped winds.

She carried the hub to the inspection bench.

Using the brass hub adapter, she remounted the tape onto its original ten-inch aluminum NAB spool, torquing the three Allen screws to eight inch-pounds with a torque driver.

Now came the machine alignment.

The Studer A80 was a masterpiece of Swiss mechanical engineering, weighing nearly two hundred pounds, its die-cast chassis machined to tolerances measured in ten-thousandths of an inch. But after thirty years in service, even a Studer drifted if the ambient temperature shifted by five degrees.

Mira took her reference alignment tape from the steel cabinet. The reel was an authentic Magnetic Reference Laboratory calibration standard, recorded at fifteen inches per second with precision test tones conforming to the NAB equalization curve.

She mounted the MRL reel on the supply spindle.

She threaded the pink leader through the optical end-of-tape sensors, past the left tension arm, across the sapphire guides, and over the three magnetic heads: erase, record, and playback. She wrapped the tail around the empty take-up hub, giving the spool three manual revolutions to seat the friction clamp.

She powered up the transport.

The massive hysteresis synchronous capstan motor began to spin, its quiet whisper blending with the low hum of the console power supply.

She cleaned the reproduction head once more with ninety-nine percent isopropyl alcohol, waiting ninety seconds for the liquid to flash off into the room.

She hit play.

The MRL tape flowed past the heads at fifteen inches per second. From the Genelec monitor speakers, the one-kilohertz reference tone burst into the room--a pure, uninflected sine wave pitched at exactly one thousand cycles per second, registering zero VU across all four console meters.

Mira checked the meter bridge.

Channels 1, 2, and 3 were within zero point two decibels of unity gain. Channel 4 was down by nearly three decibels.

She reached for her ceramic alignment screwdriver.

Locating the reproduction gain trimpot on the discrete Channel 4 amplifier card beneath the transport deck, she turned the tiny brass screw clockwise by an eighth of a turn. The green needle on the fourth meter crept upward, steadying itself exactly on the zero VU mark.

Next came the ten-kilohertz high-frequency azimuth alignment.

She switched the test signal to the ten-kilohertz azimuth tone. A high, pure whistle filled the control room, ringing in her good right ear while her damaged left ear registered only a faint, buzzing distortion.

She adjusted the azimuth locking screw on the reproduction head until the Lissajous pattern on the Tektronix oscilloscope formed a tight, unmoving diagonal line.

She picked up the ceramic alignment tool and adjusted the high-frequency reproduction equalization trimpot on Channel 1.

The Studer A80 used individual plug-in channel cards housed in a shielded card cage beneath the transport deck. Each card contained discrete silicon bipolar transistors, precision metal-film resistors, and multi-turn cermet potentiometers. To adjust the ten-kilohertz reproduction curve, Mira had to reach beneath the chassis with a non-conductive ceramic blade, turning the tiny brass screw while monitoring the console VU meter.

A metallic screwdriver would have distorted the magnetic flux around the card, throwing the alignment off by two decibels the moment the tool was withdrawn.

She monitored the Lissajous pattern on the Tektronix oscilloscope.

In a perfectly aligned stereo pair, an identical mono signal fed to both channels produced a straight diagonal line inclined at forty-five degrees. If one channel lagged by even five microseconds--the time it took a sound wave to travel one-sixteenth of an inch through air--the diagonal line opened up into an ellipse. At ninety degrees of phase error, the ellipse became a circle; at one hundred and eighty degrees, the line flipped into a backward diagonal, signifying complete phase cancellation.

Mira adjusted the azimuth locking collar until the green line narrowed to a razor-thin diagonal slash.

Phase alignment was complete.

The transport was calibrated, the tape heads were clean, and the reproduction electronics were aligned to within half a decibel across the entire audible spectrum.

She stopped the MRL tape, spooled the calibration standard back onto its storage reel, and packed it away in its velvet-lined wooden box.

She was ready for the master.

Mira took the Saint Jude reel from the drafting table, holding the aluminum flanges between her palms. She seated the spool onto the left spindle, pushed down the NAB hub adapter until the spring-loaded rubber ring locked with a solid click, and threaded the dark brown Ampex ribbon through the guide rollers.

She lowered the tape tension arms.

The green VU meter needles spring to life as the playback reels begin to spin.

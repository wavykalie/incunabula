# Chapter 9: The Tuesday Call

The morning air inside Studio 4B smelled of ozone, transformer lacquer, and damp concrete. Rain had seeped beneath the corrugated eaves along the loading bay overnight, running down the exterior cinder blocks before pooling against the rear insulation wall in dark, shallow sheets. Mira Lin unlocked the three-point deadbolt at eight-fifteen, her work boots squeaking against the wet asphalt before catching the ribbed rubber runner inside the vestibule.

Cold draft pushed past her ankles as she swung the heavy insulated slab shut. The brass latch clicked into its strike plate with the solid, muffled thud of a bank vault.

The Studer A80 console stood in the dim center of the control room, its dark gray chassis silent beneath the suspended acoustic baffles. She had left the main power rail energized overnight to prevent thermal drift in the discrete transistor cards. Across the meter bridge, the analog VU dials glowed a steady, pale amber, their needles resting against the bottom neoprene stops like small curved scythes.

She shook out her wet trench coat, listening to the heavy water droplets patter against the linoleum. She hung the coat on the bentwood coat stand by the door, making sure the hem cleared the rubber mat.

The environmental gauge on the wall read forty-two percent relative humidity and sixty-six degrees Fahrenheit. The desiccant chambers in the drying cabinet had held the line against the storm outside. Mira knelt beside the steel cabinet, opened the glass door, and inspected the digital hygrometer sensor. The silica gel crystals in the bottom perforated tray were still cobalt blue, showing no moisture absorption from the coastal river air.

She rose, wiping her palms on the rough denim of her work jeans. On the drafting table beneath the gooseneck lamp, the brushed aluminum flight case sat with its heavy butterfly latches thrown wide.

Inside the die-cut foam, the ten-and-a-half-inch Ampex master reel lay exactly as she had left it after the midnight bake. The dark brown magnetic ribbon looked dry and uniform, its outer pack tightened by the slow tension wind she had executed at four o'clock yesterday afternoon. There was no fresh white oxide powder along the reel flanges, and no curling along the slit edges of the polyester ribbon. The thermal crosslinking cycle had done its mechanical job: the hydrolyzed urethane binder had fused back onto the base film, locking the microscopic gamma ferric oxide needles into their physical bed.

Before she could reach the wall bank to switch on the overhead halogen track lights, the desk phone began to ring.

The instrument was an old black Western Electric model 500 with a brass dual-bell ringer inside its heavy bakelite housing. The clapper struck the internal bells at twenty cycles per second, producing a harsh metallic rattle that bounced between the treated gypsum walls like dropped scrap metal.

Mira paused with her hand hovering over the power strip for the near-field monitors.

Four rings. Five rings.

Mira stood beside the Western Electric telephone, listening to the harsh rattle of the brass bells reverberate between the gypsum wall panels. The telephone was an analog instrument, completely separate from the studio's digital audio network. Two insulated twenty-four-gauge copper wires ran from the back of the molded housing down the baseboard, through a conduit pipe in the concrete floor, and out to the municipal terminal pole in the alleyway.

She hesitated, her hand hovering above the handset.

Before lifting the receiver, she pulled her small steel inspection mirror and a penlight from her pocket. She checked the terminal block mounted beneath the desk. The four brass binding posts were clean, showing no unauthorized taps, no inductive pickup coils, and no RF splitters. The phone line was plain telephone copper, carrying standard forty-eight-volt direct current from the central exchange.

Yet Vance had known her exact arrival time down to the minute.

She took a slow breath, centering her diaphragm against the rising adrenaline. Her left ear gave off a shrill four-kilohertz spike that made her teeth ache. She reached down, lifted the heavy handset from its cradle, and brought the carbon earpiece to her good right ear.

Only three people had this private landline: her landlord in Vancouver who received the monthly wire transfer for the warehouse lease, Teresa Cruz working next door in Unit 4A, and Julian Vance.

She stepped across the rubber runner and lifted the heavy handset to her good right ear.

"Studio 4B," she said, keeping her breath shallow and even.

"Good morning, Miss Lin," Julian Vance said. His voice arrived through the carbon transmitter with startling, immaculate clarity, stripped clean of the usual digital jitter or cellular phase wash. "You arrived twelve minutes later than yesterday. The drainage ditch near the river rail spur has overflowed across the tarmac again."

Mira tightened her grip on the textured plastic barrel. "How do you know when I arrived?"

"We maintain a modest logistics presence outside the warehouse park," Vance said smoothly. His cadence possessed that polished corporate legalism where every veiled surveillance notice was framed as routine administrative care. "Our trustees take a keen interest in chain-of-custody protocols for artifacts of this caliber. Tell me about the tape."

Mira looked toward the workbench where the brushed aluminum flight case rested beside the splicing block. The case sat open under the bench lamp, its dark foam cutouts cradling the ten-and-a-half-inch aluminum reel.

"I finished the thermal stabilization yesterday afternoon," she said, leaning her hip against the edge of the steel desk. "The hydrolysis was grade three, but the urethane binder crosslinked cleanly after eight hours in the convection oven at fifty-four Celsius. I ran azimuth alignment this morning using an MRL calibration loop. Frequency response on the reproduction head is flat within one decibel out to sixteen kilohertz."

"That covers the transport physics," Vance said. Through the earpiece came the dry, rasping scrape of heavy ledger paper turning on a mahogany desk. "I asked about Track 4."

Mira watched a speck of airborne lint drift past the warm glow of the right channel VU meter. Her left ear throbbed with its familiar four-kilohertz phantom tone, a thin, needle-like whistle that seemed to emanate from the temporal bone behind her earlobe.

"Track 4 carries an uncataloged spoken recording," she said evenly. "The gain trim on Pendelton's original track sheet was inked at zero, but the signal was patched directly to the record head via an unattenuated transformer tap. A man is speaking in the present tense."

Silence fell over the connection for three long seconds. In the background of Vance's line, she could hear no street noise, no office murmur, no air conditioning hiss.

"Did you run noise suppression?" Vance asked. The silken courtesy had thinned, revealing the rigid framework beneath.

"I examined the spectral envelope," Mira answered. "I inserted an eighth-order Butterworth high-pass at one hundred and twenty hertz and notched out the sixty-cycle line hum to check the noise floor against the tape hiss."

"Remove every filter," Vance commanded. His tone dropped half an octave into flat bureaucratic authority. "You were retained to perform physical transfer and exact archival duplication, not creative restoration. The Oakhaven Collection did not contract you to clean the recording. We contracted you to generate an uncorrupted digital mirror of the magnetic emulsion."

"The voice on the reel is describing this room, Mr. Vance," Mira said, her fingers digging into the coiled cord until her nails pressed into her palm. "It noted the temperature of the air. It identified the blue ceramic mug sitting on my console meter bridge. It stated that forty-eight paces remained between the outer loading dock and my chair."

Another silence lengthened across the line. Through the carbon receiver, Mira heard the precise, rhythmic ticking of a mechanical pocket watch: five crisp beats per second, steady and indifferent.

"Independent sound engineers are famously susceptible to sensory fatigue, Miss Lin," Vance said softly. "Especially practitioners coping with progressive unilateral deficits. Your contract allows seventy-eight hours before Friday afternoon at five o'clock. If the full multi-track archive is not transferred and verified by that hour, our legal department will initiate immediate breach proceedings and seize all collateral."

"You already knew what was on this ribbon," Mira whispered.

"I know that magnetic tape requires an attentive listener to complete its circuit," Vance said. "Do not alter the equalization. Do not disconnect the transport."

"What happens when the count reaches zero?" Mira asked. Her voice cracked slightly on the final syllable.

"The consignment requires complete delivery by Friday at five o'clock," Vance replied, ignoring the question with the smooth precision of a train sliding over greased rails. "Our courier will arrive with the certified bank draft for your fee. Have the digitized files packaged in the pelican case alongside the master reel. Good morning, Miss Lin."

The line disconnected with a dry, mechanical relay click.

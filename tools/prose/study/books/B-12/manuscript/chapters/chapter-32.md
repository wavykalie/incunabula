# Chapter 32: The Barricade

Mira worked until one o'clock in the morning without turning on the main studio lights.

She operated by the pale yellow beam of a battery-powered inspection flashlight held between her teeth, moving between the storage bay and the control room with silent, deliberate steps.

The heavy acoustic door of Studio 4B was constructed from two sheets of five-eighths-inch marine plywood sandwiching a core of sheet lead and compressed mineral wool. It weighed over two hundred pounds, hung on three heavy ball-bearing steel butt hinges.

The deadbolt engaged into a solid oak jamb, but against an entity that exerted mechanical displacement through acoustic resonance, the lock was meaningless.

She needed mass.

In the rear of the storage bay, fifty feet down the dark corridor, stood a stack of heavy oak shipping pallets used for transporting vintage console transformers. Each pallet was constructed from four-by-four timber stringers, weighing sixty pounds of solid, dense hardwood.

Mira dragged the pallets into the studio one by one, hauling them across the concrete threshold on their edges to minimize the noise of dragging wood.

She stacked the first four pallets vertically against the interior face of the door, wedging their cross-members directly beneath the heavy brass lever handle. She drove four-inch steel deck screws through the pallet frames into the solid oak door frame using an eighteen-volt cordless impact driver.

The sharp, hammering clatter of the impact driver echoed violently inside the small control room, but she did not care. The entity already knew where she was.

Behind the wooden pallets, she built a secondary wall of steel flight cases.

She used the heavy ATA road cases that normally housed her portable multitrack decks, power amplifiers, and vintage tube equalizers. Each case was constructed from three-quarter-inch birch plywood clad in vulcanized fiber and bound with heavy aluminum extrusions and steel ball corners.

She filled the cases with discarded iron transformer cores, lead counterweights from boom microphone stands, and six twenty-pound ingots of scrap lead she kept for casting custom acoustic damping weights.

The total mass of the barricade exceeded one thousand two hundred pounds.

She stacked the cases three high, wedging the final flight case between the oak pallets and the steel pedestal base of the Studer console desk. The barricade was now an integrated structural brace, transferring any inward force on the door directly into the concrete floor slab through the bolted steel legs of the console.

Mira stepped back to inspect her work.

Her hands were blistered, covered in black grease, rust flakes, and yellow splinter dust. Her woolen coat was torn at the elbow where it had snagged on a pallet nail, exposing the gray thermal lining.

She tested the barricade.

She threw her entire body weight against the top flight case, driving her shoulder into the aluminum trim.

The barricade did not budge. It felt as solid as a poured concrete bunker wall, rigid and unyielding.

She took the inspection flashlight from her mouth and swept the beam across the control room.

The space was reduced to a narrow ten-by-twelve-foot pocket. In the center sat the mixing desk, the Studer A80, and her swivel chair. Behind her, the galvanized washbasins still caught the slow, rhythmic drips from the clerestory roof.

To her right, the Genelec monitor speakers sat perched on their sand-filled stands like silent sentinels.

To her left, the patch bay and the outboard equipment racks glowed with faint amber pilot lamps.

She checked the power supply. The Studer deck was plugged into an isolated line-conditioning transformer, protected from line surges and voltage sags.

She laid her tools out on the console armrest: the wire cutters, the roll of heavy gaffer tape, the bottle of isopropyl alcohol, and her ceramic splicing blade.

Beside them lay Teresa Cruz's heavy tape head demagnetizer—a red plastic wand housing a massive AC electromagnet, capable of generating an intense sixty-cycle alternating field strong enough to erase magnetic tape at a distance of three inches.

Everything was prepared.

Tomorrow morning, the final countdown would begin.

She walked to the swivel chair, her legs trembling with physical exhaustion. She did not bother to unlace her boots or brush the sawdust from her coat.

She wedges the last steel flight case against the door and collapses onto her swivel chair.

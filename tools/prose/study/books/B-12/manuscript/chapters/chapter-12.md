# Chapter 12: Room Impulse Response

Back in Studio 4B, Mira Lin booted her primary workstation into electroacoustic diagnostic mode to measure the transfer function of the vocal track.

Every physical enclosure possessed a distinct acoustic signature known as an impulse response. When a sound wave was generated inside an enclosed room, it traveled outward in all directions at eleven hundred and twenty-eight feet per second, striking the floor, ceiling, and walls before returning to the microphone as a complex pattern of early reflections and diffuse reverberant decay. By firing a transient test burst into a room and recording the decay tail, an engineer could map the physical volume, surface absorption, and boundary geometry of the space with mathematical precision.

If Track 4 was recorded in the convent chapel alongside the musicians, its impulse response would show the reflective geometry of stone walls, high vaulted timber ceilings, and plaster alcoves.

She pulled a high-resolution twenty-four-bit WAV capture of the vocal stem into the spectral convolution tool on her primary display.

Outside, the wind gusted against the warehouse skylights, making the wire glass rattle in its lead frames. Mira ignored the weather, her attention fixed on the high-definition FFT analyzer. The room around her was silent, insulated by heavy rockwool baffles that kept the river traffic muffled to a dull low-frequency vibration.

She hit the analyze key on the console keyboard.

The software began calculating the energy-time curve across one hundred and twenty-eight frequency bands.

A standard vocal recorded in a live room produced an energy-time curve that bloomed outward over several hundred milliseconds, decaying exponentially into the ambient noise floor. The chapel recording on Tracks 1, 2, and 3 exhibited an RT60 reverberation time of two-point-four seconds, with clear acoustic reflections bouncing from the limestone columns every eighteen milliseconds.

Track 4 generated no energy-time curve.

Mira stared at the graphic display on her high-resolution monitor. The graphic pane was empty.

The vocal waveform rose instantaneously from absolute digital black to full amplitude, then dropped back to zero without a single millisecond of reverberant decay. There were no room modes. There was no boundary reflection from a floor, a ceiling, or a rear wall. There was not even the microscopic acoustic shadow cast by a microphone stand or a human head.

The acoustic signature was that of an infinite anechoic vacuum.

Direct transmission, she thought, resting her chin on her palm. It had bypassed the physics of air altogether.

She opened the phase-coherence window to examine the inter-aural time differences. In any physical recording, the acoustic wave struck the two ears or stereo capsules at slightly different angles, creating microsecond phase offsets that the brain decoded as directional space.

On Track 4, the inter-aural time difference was zero point zero zero milliseconds. The signal was purely monophonic and devoid of spatial dispersion.

She checked the frequency spectrum. A natural human voice recorded through an analog microphone was limited by diaphragm inertia and transformer inductance, rolling off sharply above sixteen kilohertz.

Track 4 did not roll off.

The spectral plot showed flat, unattenuated high-frequency energy extending past twenty kilohertz, straight into the ultrasonic spectrum until it slammed into the Nyquist cutoff of her digital converters.

It had not been recorded through a microphone diaphragm. The magnetic oxide had been magnetized directly at the atomic level, molecule by molecule, without the physical intermediary of sound waves moving through air.

The voice was not a recording of someone who had spoken fifty years ago.

Mira leaned back in her swivel chair, her hand resting on the polished aluminum frame of the Studer deck. The capstan motor hummed with a quiet thirty-cycle vibration, steady as a heartbeat.

If the signal was direct magnetic induction, then Pendelton had not captured sound waves bouncing through the crypt. He had captured the electromagnetic imprint of something altering the tape ribbon itself while the spool revolved across the head block.

She stood up and paced the length of the control room.

Her boots made a faint, rhythmic scuff on the black rubber runner. She looked at the patch bay, the analog compressors, the vintage oscilloscope with its green electron beam tracing a flat horizontal line across the graticule. Everything in this room operated according to the established laws of Maxwell and Faraday: current in a coil induced a magnetic field; magnetic flux in a core induced a voltage.

Track 4 violated the second law of thermodynamics. It introduced information into a closed system without expending thermal energy or acoustic work.

She unmuted the track for five seconds to verify the waveform visually against the playback head. The ribbon slipped past the lifters with a faint dry rustle, the green meters jumping in crisp unison with the syllables.

The voice on the monitor spoke with cold clarity: "Forty-four paces. The copper drainpipe outside the rear fire door has a split seam."

Mira stopped the transport. She stepped to the rear of the studio, opened the metal emergency door three inches, and looked out into the gravel alleyway. Water was gushing from a longitudinal crack in the green copper downspout, spraying against the brick foundation.

Cold river wind blew through the opening, carrying the smell of wet soot and decaying cedar.

She pulled the fire door shut and threw the deadbolt.


An acoustic impulse response is the sonic fingerprint of a physical enclosure.

When a transient sound—like an electrical spark, a starter pistol, or a popped balloon—occurs inside a room, the sound waves travel outward in spherical wavefronts. The primary wavefront strikes the listener's ear as direct sound. Milliseconds later, the secondary wavefronts arrive, having bounced off the floor, the ceiling, and the surrounding walls.

By deconvolving the recorded signal against a known mathematical excitation signal, an engineer can calculate the exact dimensions, wall materials, and volume of the enclosure.

Mira initiated an impulse response deconvolution routine on track four using a software plugin calibrated to the acoustic properties of St. Jude Chapel.

The algorithm ran for forty seconds, processing the silence preceding the first spoken word.

When the calculation completed, the impulse response display presented an empty, horizontal line.

No direct spike. No early reflections. No diffuse reverberant decay tail.

The room impulse response was zero.

For an acoustic signal to possess zero room impulse response, one of two conditions must be met: either the recording took place inside an absolute anechoic chamber lined with four-foot fiberglass wedges in a deep underground mine, or the microphone was positioned inside an acoustic vacuum.

Yet St. Jude Chapel was a stone enclosure with four-second natural reverberation. The cello and prepared piano on tracks one and two showed hundreds of distinct early reflections bouncing off the limestone arches.

Track four occupied the exact same physical coordinates on the tape ribbon, recorded by the same transport deck at the same instant in 1974.

The voice was not in the room.

It was an electrical voltage that existed only within the magnetic domain of the tape head gap.

The frequency plot shows a flat, razor-straight response extending beyond 20 kHz.

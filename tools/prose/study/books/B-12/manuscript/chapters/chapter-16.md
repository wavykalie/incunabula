# Chapter 16: The Hallway Grid

Midnight settled over the industrial park in complete, freezing stillness. Inside Studio 4B, the analog clock on the wall above the tape cabinet clicked with a heavy, dry cadence, its second hand jumping six degrees between each silent interval.

Mira Lin sat at the console with her elbows propped on the meter bridge. The Studer A80 had been powered down to its standby state, the cooling fans idling with a faint, thirty-cycle murmur that barely stirred the air beneath the acoustic baffles. Across the room, the ten-inch Ampex master reel rested in its aluminum tray, its dark brown ribbon motionless against the polished lifters.

She had not slept since five o'clock yesterday morning. Her eyes burned with the gritty sting of eyestrain, and the four-kilohertz phantom whistle in her damaged left ear had widened into a rough, sibilant hiss that mirrored the ambient noise floor of the room.

She reached for the canvas tool bag beneath the workbench.

From the bottom compartment, she pulled out a fifty-foot Stanley steel tape measure with a yellow nylon-coated blade. She added a thick stick of yellow lumber crayon from the carpentry shelf, a pair of cotton work gloves, and a heavy three-cell Maglite machined from black anodized aluminum.

She stood up, shaking the stiffness from her knees. She slipped into her woolen trench coat, buttoning it up to the throat against the damp chill of the unheated warehouse.

She unlocked the deadbolts on the heavy acoustic door, eased the brass latch back, and pulled the slab inward.

The warehouse corridor was pitch black. The air smelled of stagnant river mud, diesel exhaust from the rail spurs, and the cold iron tang of wet structural steel. No heat reached this wing of the complex; the concrete floor drew the freezing winter damp straight up from the riverbank pilings.

Mira stepped across the neoprene sill plate and clicked on the Maglite.

The bright halogen beam cut through the floating haze that drifted beneath the corrugated ceiling. Dust motes swirled in the white column of light, illuminated against the peeling gray paint of the hallway walls.

She knelt at the threshold of Studio 4B.

Hooking the brass claw of the Stanley tape over the outer lip of the metal sill, she locked the blade brake and stood up. She laid the yellow lumber crayon against the concrete floor directly in line with the door jamb, pressing down hard until the soft wax left a thick, bright yellow mark.

Zero paces: the threshold of the listening room.

She began to walk down the center of the corridor toward the distant loading dock, keeping her stride natural and measured, her boots striking the concrete with a dull, rhythmic thud.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

At five paces, she passed the electrical breaker box that fed the branch circuits for Unit 4B. The galvanized steel door was closed, but the forty-amp main breaker hummed with a quiet sixty-cycle vibration that she felt through the soles of her shoes.

She marked the concrete with a yellow slash: 5.

Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

At ten paces, she passed the padlocked wooden door of Unit 4A. Through the weather-stripped seam came the faint, dry smell of pine sawdust, contact cement, and the bitter trace of stale tobacco from Teresa Cruz's workbench. A hand-lettered cardboard sign hung from the brass doorknob: Closed for the Night. Back at 7:00 AM.

Mira paused, shining the flashlight beam across the locked door. The warehouse around her was silent except for the distant drip of condensation into an empty metal drum near the boiler room.

She made another mark on the floor: 10.

She continued walking, counting each stride under her breath.

Fifteen paces. Twenty paces. Twenty-five paces.

At twenty-eight paces, the corridor widened near the abandoned freight elevator. The heavy wire-mesh cage hung suspended between two greasy steel guide rails, its counterweight cables dripping black lubricant into the dark pit below. The cold air rising from the shaft smelled of ancient coal dust and flooded silt.

She drew another yellow line across the concrete: 28.

Thirty paces. Thirty-five. Thirty-six.

At thirty-six paces, her boot toe came to a stop directly beneath the red metal housing of the wall-mounted fire extinguisher.

Mira aimed the Maglite at the red steel cylinder. A heavy layer of industrial dust coated the curved top of the tank, undisturbed except for a faint, oval smudge where a bare palm had rested against the metal bracket. She knelt down and marked the floor: 36.

This was the number the voice on Track 4 had spoken at four o'clock yesterday afternoon: Thirty-six paces. Passing the fire extinguisher cabinet.

Her hands began to tremble inside her cotton work gloves.

Thirty-seven paces. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two.

At forty-two paces, she looked up. Directly above her head, suspended from two rusty steel chains hooked to the roof truss, hung the second fluorescent lighting fixture. The twin forty-watt tubes were dark now, but the transformer casing bore the burned black scar of a shorted capacitor.

She marked the floor: 42.

Forty-two paces. The second light fixture in the outer hallway flickers at one hundred and twenty cycles.

She walked the final six paces in complete silence, her flashlight beam fixed on the floor.

Forty-three. Forty-four. Forty-five. Forty-six. Forty-seven. Forty-eight.

At forty-eight paces, the steel toe of her boot struck the galvanized floor channel of the exterior rolling security gate.

Mira stood motionless in the freezing draft that whistled beneath the corrugated steel curtain. The gap between the rubber weather-seal and the cracked concrete was half an inch wide. Cold river wind blew through the opening, carrying the smell of wet creosote and rotting river reeds from the tidal mudflats outside.

She shone the flashlight beam downward between her boots.

The numbers were physical facts, not psychoacoustic hallucinations born of sleep deprivation. Pendelton's tape had recorded the spatial geometry of this specific warehouse, stride by stride, thirty-five years before she had signed the lease on Studio 4B.

She turned around and looked back down the sixty-foot corridor.

In the far distance, a single thin rectangle of warm amber light framed the bottom edge of the closed door to Studio 4B, glowing like an open furnace door in the pitch-black warehouse.


Mira stepped out into the dark hallway with a steel measuring wheel she had borrowed from the warehouse maintenance locker.

The wheel had a rubber tire, an aluminum telescoping handle, and a mechanical five-digit counter calibrated in feet and tenths.

She walked to the exterior entrance of the building, where the sliding security door met the wet asphalt turnaround.

She placed the rubber wheel on the concrete threshold, clicked the reset button until the mechanical digits displayed zero zero zero point zero, and began to walk toward Studio 4B at her normal, natural walking pace.

Her boots clicked against the concrete.

The mechanical counter clicked in rhythm with the wheel rotation.

Past the battery racks: fifteen feet.

Past the cracked drywall compound buckets: thirty-two feet.

Across the black electrical conduit pipe: forty-eight feet.

She turned the corner into the studio corridor, her flashlight beam sweeping across the brick wall and the metal electrical conduit runs.

Past the failing fluorescent fixture: sixty-four feet.

She reached the threshold of Studio 4B, pushed through the heavy acoustic door, and stopped the measuring wheel directly beside the caster wheels of her swivel chair.

She looked down at the mechanical display on the wheel.

It read: one hundred and twenty point zero feet.

One hundred and twenty feet divided by her standard stride length of two and a half feet equaled exactly forty-eight normal paces.

The tape did not merely understand the architecture of the building.

It understood her physical stride.

The metallic clang of the warehouse security gate rolling shut behind her.

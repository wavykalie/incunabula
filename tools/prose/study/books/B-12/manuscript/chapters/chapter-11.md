# Chapter 11: Arthur Pendelton

The central branch of the municipal library on Commercial Street smelled of damp umbrella nylon, petroleum floor wax, and crumbling newspaper pulp. Mira Lin sat in the microfilm carrel on the fourth floor, her trench coat draped across the back of the molded fiberglass chair, turning the knurled brass advance wheel on the viewing machine.

The reading room was quiet except for the low hum of the ventilation registers and the occasional scrape of wooden chair legs on linoleum. Outside the high arched windows, the rain fell in gray sheets across the brick chimneys and rusted gantries of the lower harbor. A cargo freighter moved slowly down the shipping channel, its diesel engines rumbling at twelve hertz, vibrating through the sandstone masonry of the library building.

Mira rubbed her eyes, feeling the gritty drag of fatigue beneath her eyelids. Her left ear whistled with a thin four-kilohertz phantom carrier tone, competing with the mechanical whine of the microfilm cooling fan. She had spent the last two hours spooling through reels of provincial probate filings, trying to trace the provenance of the Saint Jude master tape before Julian Vance had purchased the collection.

The microfiche spool was cataloged under regional corporate acquisitions: The Oakhaven Trust - Fine Arts Acquisitions, 1970-1979.

She scrolled past hundreds of handwritten acquisition vouchers, insurance receipts, and gallery bills of lading. The bright light of the projection lamp threw the scratched emulsion of the film against the translucent green screen, making her pupils ache. On every invoice, Vance's signature appeared in sharp, slanted black ink, always notarized by the same private legal firm in Bern.

On frame eighty-four, she found the session sheet.

The document was dated November fourteenth, nineteen seventy-four. The letterhead read St. Jude Convent Memorial Hall, Studio Mobile Three.

The recording engineer of record was Arthur Pendelton, thirty-six years old, chief technician for Delta Sound Laboratories. Beneath his name, the technical notes were recorded in neat, cramped engineering print using blue ballpoint ink:

Four-track Ampex 456 tape stock. Master reel recorded at fifteen inches per second, NAB equalization. Ambient temperature fifty-eight degrees Fahrenheit. Relative humidity sixty-two percent. Microphones deployed: Neumann U87 pair on strings, AKG C12 on prepared upright piano.

Then came the entry for Track 4:

Channel 4 patched direct to experimental transducer in sealed crypt. Gain uncalibrated. Do not monitor during performance. Deliver master directly to Mr. J. Vance upon completion of reel.

Mira leaned her forehead against the cool plastic hood of the viewer. St. Jude had been an active convent until nineteen seventy-four, built over an eighteenth-century brick crypt used for quarantine burials during the river cholera outbreaks. Pendelton had dropped an unshielded transducer through the stone floorboards into the sealed void below the chapel altar.

In audio engineering, an unshielded transducer acted as a massive inductive antenna, picking up stray electromagnetic fields, radio frequencies, and electrostatic discharges. Yet Pendelton had specified a sealed subterranean vault surrounded by six feet of damp masonry.

She spun the knurled advance knob to frame eighty-five.

Frame eighty-five was an insurance claim for ruined acoustic test equipment: two blown transducer coils, a scorched tube preamplifier, and four crushed microphone diaphragms. The attached adjuster note stated that the physical damage was caused by severe high-voltage inductive surge, though the mobile generator truck had experienced no power fluctuations.

Frame eighty-six was a police report.

On November twenty-first, nineteen seventy-four--exactly seven days after the tape was boxed and sealed into its aluminum flight case--Arthur Pendelton parked his Morris Minor on the center span of the suspension bridge over the industrial river channel. He left the ignition keys on the steering column, his wallet on the passenger seat, and his technical field log open on the dashboard.

The coroner's inquest summary was brief: Death by drowning following fall from height. No suicide note recovered. Decedent's colleagues reported severe acoustic agitation and auditory delusions during previous forty-eight hours.

Attached to the police report was a photocopied clipping from the morning gazette. The headline was small, buried on page eight beneath harbor shipping reports: Technician Missing After River Bridge Fall.

The grainy newsprint photograph showed Pendelton's basement laboratory in the days following his disappearance. The room was lined with acoustic foam wedges and heavy military-surplus tape recorders. On the wooden workbench sat an empty tape spool and three notebooks filled with dense columns of numbers.

Mira adjusted the focus barrel until the type snapped sharp against the green screen. The numbers in Pendelton's notebook were distance measurements recorded in feet and paces. Across forty pages of engineering ledger, he had tracked the gradual shrinkage of an acoustic boundary.

Every page listed a timestamp, a foot count, and a physical observation of his house. The entries were written in ballpoint ink, growing heavier and more jagged as the numbers decreased.

Forty paces: scratch at the basement bulkhead door. Thirty paces: floorboard groan above the coal chute. Twenty paces: cold air draft through the floor register.

The final line at the bottom of the page was written in thick grease pencil: The observer does not walk. The observer is pulled across the gap by the playback.

She turned the microfilm advance knob to frame eighty-seven, scanning the witness statements attached to the coroner's inquest.

A night watchman on the railway bridge had observed Pendelton's car stopping on the center span at two-fifteen on the morning of November twenty-first. The watchman reported that the driver had climbed out of the vehicle and walked to the pedestrian rail, moving with an unsteady, stumbling gait as though disoriented by severe dizziness.

*The decedent was holding both hands pressed tight against his ears,* the watchman's handwritten statement read. *He was shouting at the empty air, telling someone to stop counting. Before I could reach the center span with my lantern, he stepped over the safety stanchion and dropped into the river.*

Mira leaned closer to the viewing screen, her heart beating with a slow, heavy rhythm.

The police inventory of Pendelton's Morris Minor listed several items recovered from the front passenger seat: an empty tape carton labeled *Master Reel 74-D*, a set of brass feeler gauges, a pocket acoustic tuning fork, and a portable cassette recorder containing an unspooled six-minute cassette tape.

The cassette tape had been shredded with a razor blade.

Beside the cassette player lay a single sheet of paper torn from Pendelton's technical notebook. The ink had smeared from rain blowing through the car's cracked quarter-vent, but the final sentence was still legible: *The sound has entered the foundation walls. It is forty paces from my bed.*

Mira sat back in the fiberglass chair, her fingers numb against the cold metal casing of the machine. The cooling fan wound down as she flipped the main power toggle, plunging the carrel into gray afternoon gloom.

Outside, the rain beat against the glass with renewed fury. She could see the rusted steel arches of the suspension bridge three miles downriver, half-swallowed by the harbor fog.

She closed the microfilm cabinet door, took her coat from the chair, and walked toward the elevator banks.

Her boots made no sound on the heavy wool runner of the landing. In the quiet elevator cab descending toward the street, she tapped her left ear twice. The dull hollow thud reminded her that whatever was happening in Studio 4B was not confined to her damaged hearing.

Pendelton had possessed perfect bilateral pitch. He had calibrated broadcast networks for twenty years. And he had heard the exact same footsteps counting down the paces.

She stepped out through the brass revolving doors of the library into the raw coastal wind, pulling her collar high against the driving rain.



Pendelton's doctoral dissertation was cataloged in the University of Washington's digital archives under the title *Non-Reciprocal Spatial Distortion in High-Flux Magnetic Recording Systems (1969).*

Mira downloaded the two-hundred-page PDF document onto her workstation.

The dissertation was dense with differential equations, tensors, and boundary-value problems in classical electrodynamics. In chapter four, Pendelton had investigated the behavior of magnetic hysteresis loops when subjected to relativistic acoustic shock waves.

He had proposed that if an acoustic standing wave achieves sufficient power density within a non-linear dielectric medium, the phase velocity of the wave can exceed the local acoustic propagation limit.

When that occurs, the wave does not propagate forward in time.

It folds back upon itself, creating a closed timelike curve within the magnetic domains of the recording tape.

Pendelton had written:

*The recording tape ceases to be a passive storage archive. It becomes an active acoustic boundary condition that constrains the propagation of sound across both space and time. An auditor who listens to the transcription does not observe an historical performance; the auditor provides the necessary terminal boundary condition that allows the wave to complete its trajectory.*

Mira read the paragraph three times, the cold air from the warehouse clerestory chilling her neck.

Arthur Pendelton had understood the trap he was building.

He had constructed a physical loop, and fifty-two years later, Julian Vance had handed the keys to her.

The Seattle Public Library's digital microfilm repository held three newspaper clippings regarding the death of Arthur Pendelton.

The first article appeared on page fourteen of the *Seattle Post-Intelligencer* on Tuesday, October 22, 1974. The headline was brief, relegated to the bottom of the regional news section: *Harbor Patrol Recovers Body from Lake Union.*

The report stated that commercial fishermen had spotted a body floating near the western pilings of the George Washington Memorial Bridge, known locally as the Aurora Bridge. The coroner had identified the deceased as Arthur Vance Pendelton, age forty-four, an electrical engineer and acoustic consultant residing in the Laurelhurst neighborhood.

A subsequent investigative piece, published two weeks later in a local underground arts weekly, provided the details that the daily press had omitted.

Pendelton had not merely climbed the bridge pedestrian railing on Sunday evening.

According to the King County medical examiner's supplemental autopsy report, the engineer had sustained severe bilateral puncture wounds to both temporal bones prior to entering the water. Two three-inch steel masonry nails had been driven through the external auditory canals, penetrating the middle ear cavities and shattering the ossicular chains.

The coroner had noted that the trauma was self-inflicted, executed with a magnetic tack hammer found in the pocket of Pendelton's wool overcoat.

Beside the coroner's summary, the newspaper had reproduced a photograph of Arthur Pendelton's personal basement laboratory on 45th Street, taken by municipal police during the initial missing-person investigation.

The photograph was grainy, taken with a wide-angle flash bulb that threw harsh, black shadows across the stone foundations.

In the center of the room sat a massive wooden workbench cluttered with disassembled vacuum-tube reel decks, custom delay coils, and hundreds of hand-drawn acoustic schematics pinned to the floor joists.

On the floor beneath the workbench lay dozens of ruined master tape reels, their quarter-inch magnetic ribbons sliced into confetti with razor blades or melted into twisted black slag by intense localized heat.

Across the white concrete wall behind the workbench, written in thick black transformer varnish with a two-inch paintbrush, were six words:

*THE DISTANCE CANNOT BE RESET.*

Mira sat in the silent control room of Studio 4B, staring at the scanned photograph on her screen.

The handwriting on the wall was identical to the spidery script on the tape canister labels resting three feet away on her workbench.

She closes the PDF viewer on the grainy black-and-white photograph of Pendelton's empty studio.

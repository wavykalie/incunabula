# Chapter 2: The Convection Oven

The studio prep alcove smelled of clean cotton gloves, denatured alcohol, and warm sheet metal. Mira Lin set the ten-inch aluminum reel onto the rubberized turntable of the inspection jig, keeping both hands on the flanges to avoid touching the exposed ribbon edges.

The tape was Ampex 456, manufactured in early nineteen seventy-four. The brown oxide coating had that greasy, matte sheen that every preservationist dreaded. Under forty-watt inspection lamps, the outer wraps showed the telltale banding of uneven tension: slight ridges where the tape pack had slipped against itself during some long-forgotten rewind cycle in a Zurich vault.

She picked up a wooden toothpick and touched the outer tail of the paper leader tape.

The paper leader came away with a faint tearing sound, leaving tiny specks of brown pigment clinging to the yellow paper.

"Sticky-shed," she whispered to the empty room.

The chemistry was simple and unforgiving. In the early nineteen seventies, chemical suppliers switched from long-chain polyester urethanes to short-chain polymers in their tape binders. Over twenty years, the ester bonds absorbed water molecules from ambient air, breaking apart into carboxylic acids and alcohols. The binder softened into an adhesive sludge that could rip the oxide clean off the polyester base if pulled across a stationary tape guide.

The only remedy was heat. Dry, controlled, slow heat to drive the water molecules back out of the urethane lattice and re-crosslink the polymers.

She turned to the modified commercial food dehydrator mounted on the steel prep table. The unit had been gutted of its plastic trays and fitted with three perforated aluminum shelves and a digital PID temperature controller wired to two platinum resistance probes. A small centrifugal fan circulated air through an internal HEPA filter, keeping the air velocity uniform across the interior chamber.

She checked the digital display on the controller: twenty-one point four degrees Celsius. Thirty-eight percent relative humidity.

She took a clean lint-free wipe, moistened it with three drops of distilled water, and cleaned the silicone gasket around the oven door.

She opened the bottom drawer of the prep bench, retrieving her glass bottle of chemical-grade trichlorotrifluoroethane substitute and a box of lint-free polyester swabs. In nineteen seventy-four, Ampex technicians had cleaned tape guides with pure Freon, watching the solvent evaporate instantaneously without leaving a single molecule of residue. Today, environmental regulations had banned chlorofluorocarbons, forcing preservationists to rely on ninety-nine percent anhydrous isopropyl alcohol and pure mechanical cleaning.

Alcohol was safe on metal and glass, but dangerous near vintage rubber. If a single drop touched the neoprene pinch roller of the Studer deck, the solvent would leach the plasticizers from the rubber matrix, turning the resilient roller into hard, glazed vulcanite that slipped against the capstan shaft.

Mira inspected the Delrin baking hub under the halogen lamp. The hub had been precision-turned on a lathe from a solid billet of acetal homopolymer, chosen for its zero thermal expansion coefficient and complete chemical inertness. When the Ampex tape expanded under heat, the Delrin core would yield by two microns, absorbing the radial pressure without pinching the ribbon edges.

Next, she prepared the reel. She could not bake the tape on its original aluminum flanges; differential thermal expansion between the metal spool and the polyester ribbon could pinch the edges and produce permanent cupping.

Using a brass hub clamp, she transferred the tape pack to an inert Delrin baking hub. Her fingers moved with practiced efficiency: steadying the tape pack with her left palm while loosening the three Allen screws on the NAB adapter with her right. When the front flange lifted clear, the naked cylinder of tape sat on the hub like a giant spool of dark silk.

She checked the pack for popped strands. If a single wrap stuck out by half a millimeter, the hot air would curl the exposed edge, ruining high-frequency phase coherence forever.

Two strands projected near the five-inch mark.

Mira took her Teflon burnishing tool and pressed the errant wraps gently back into the pack, working under an illuminated magnifying visor. Her breath came slow and shallow through her nose, fogging the edge of the lens.

She placed the hub onto the center rack of the oven.

She closed the glass door. The magnetic latch caught with a soft thump.

On the control panel, she keyed in the parameters: target temperature, fifty-four point five degrees Celsius; ramp rate, zero point five degrees per minute; soak duration, eight hours; cool-down ramp, one point zero degree per minute back to room ambient.

She pressed the green start button.

The heating elements behind the rear baffle began to hum with sixty-cycle current. The centrifugal fan spun up, circulating a gentle, warm draft across the Delrin hub.

Mira pulled off her white cotton gloves, dropping them into the laundry bin beside the sink. Her hands felt clammy despite the chill in the room.

Through the double-glazed observation glass of the oven door, she watched the dark brown cylinder of tape. A faint, sweet odor began to escape through the HEPA exhaust port: the warm scent of whale oil lubricant, synthetic ester plasticizer, and aged polyurethane breaking down under the initial heat.

In nineteen seventy-four, Ampex had believed their new Grand Master formulation would preserve acoustic waveforms for a century. Instead, the chemistry had begun degrading within fifteen years, turning millions of master recordings into brittle, squealing scrolls of magnetic tar.

Every analog archive was a slow-motion fire.

She washed her hands at the stainless steel sink, using unperfumed laboratory soap to strip any residual skin oils from her fingertips. Drying her hands on a clean paper towel, she stepped to the wall calendar pinned above the desk.

Today was Monday. If the thermal cycle concluded at ten o'clock tonight, the reel would require twelve hours of passive cooling at room temperature to normalize the polyester tension. That meant the first playback pass could not occur before ten o'clock on Tuesday morning.

That left seventy-nine hours until Friday at five o'clock.

Seventy-nine hours to calibrate the Studer deck, transfer four discrete channels at ninety-six kilohertz, clean the high-frequency dropouts, generate the delivery checksums, and package the archive for Julian Vance's courier.

Mira reached up and set the mechanical timer dial on the wall above the oven. The brass spring tightened inside the casing, clicking against the escapement gear.

The oven timer dial clicks into its mechanical four-hour countdown.

# Chapter 13: The Locked Cabin

The smell of boiled cabbage and canned salt beef filled the saloon at nineteen-thirty.

Outside, the sea had flattened slightly into long, rounded mounds as the yacht crossed the edge of the shallow shelf into deep ocean water. But inside the cabin, the atmosphere was dense with suspicion.

Tom Nowak served the stew into four stainless bowls, sliding them into the wooden table fiddles. Nobody spoke. The only sound was the clinking of spoons against metal and the steady, hydraulic hum of the autopilot ram working behind the aft cabin bulkhead.

Julian Kross ate quickly, keeping his head down, his eyes fixed on his bowl.

"I need the spare staysail halyard from the forward sail locker," Artie said, setting his spoon down on the mahogany table. "The chafe guard on the masthead sheave is worn through. I want to swap it before the night watch."

Julian stopped chewing. "The locker is organized, skipper. I can get it for you."

"I'll get it myself," Artie said, standing up. "I want to inspect the forward collision bulkhead while I'm up there."

He walked down the narrow corridor past the two midship heads. At the forward end of the passage, where the master cabin gave way to the sail storage compartment in the bows, a heavy watertight door of painted composite opened into the sail locker.

Artie reached for the aluminum dogs that sealed the door.

On the upper latch, hanging through the welded steel hasp, was a heavy brass padlock.

Artie stopped. He reached out and touched the padlock. It was cold, clean, and entirely non-standard. The ship's lockers were keyed to a single master ring kept in the chart desk. This was a private four-pin padlock, bought off the shelf at a hardware store, and it was locked tight from the outside.

Artie walked back down the corridor and stepped into the saloon.

The three crew members looked up at him.

"Marcus," Artie said, his voice quiet and level. "Who put the padlock on the forward sail locker?"

Julian set his spoon down. His hands were shaking slightly. "I did, skipper."

"Why?"

"My personal gear is in there," Julian said, the words coming out in a defensive rush. "The forward bunks were wet from the deck hatch leaking. I moved my duffel bag into the sail bins to keep it dry."

"A crew member does not padlock a watertight compartment on a commercial vessel," Artie said. "If the bow takes a container or a submerged log in the night, Tom has to be able to reach the collision bulkhead valves within fifteen seconds. A locked door on an ocean passage is a safety violation that can sink the ship."

"There is nothing in there that affects the pumps," Julian stammered, his face turning the color of chalk. "It's just my clothing and my papers."

"Take the lock off," Artie said.

"Skipper, please, "

"I said take the lock off, Mr. Ward. Or I take a hacksaw to the hasp right now."

Julian looked at Clara, then at Tom. Neither of them moved. Tom chewed slowly on a piece of dark bread, his expression carved from gray granite. Clara watched with cold, analytical detachment, her arms folded across her chest.

Julian pushed himself back from the table. He reached into his trouser pocket, pulled out a small brass key on a split ring, and walked down the forward corridor.

Artie followed him.

Julian inserted the key into the padlock, turned it until the cylinder popped open with a dull click, and pulled the lock from the hasp. He opened the composite door, revealing the dark sail bin filled with rolled bags of Kevlar reachers and nylon spinnakers.

In the corner of the bin, wedged securely between two sail bags and strapped down with a ratchet lashing, sat a black Pelican hard-shell equipment case, the size of a large briefcase, sealed with dual combination latches.

"Open it," Artie said.

"No," Julian said, turning around, his back pressed against the sail bags. His voice cracked with genuine, desperate terror. "No, Captain. You don't know what is in there. If you open that case, you become an accessory under European banking law. Delacroix will have his lawyers destroy you before you ever set foot on the dock in Lisbon."

Artie looked at the black plastic case, then looked into the younger man's wild, terrified eyes.

"Lock the hasp with the ship's safety pin," Artie said calmly. "Keep your padlock in your pocket. If I ever find a locked door on this boat again, the case goes over the side into three thousand fathoms of water. Do you understand me?"

Julian nodded, his chest heaving, his fingers clutching the brass lock like a lifeline. He followed Artie back into the saloon, moving with the slow, broken step of a man who had narrowly escaped an execution.

The silence in the saloon was thick, broken only by the rhythmic creak of the interior joinery as forty tons of displacement rolled through the dark swells.

Artie sat on the settee, watching Julian's face across the table. He could see the pulse beating rapidly in the hollow of the younger man's throat, a frantic, trapped cadence that betrayed the terror beneath his calm exterior.

"You think Delacroix will have police waiting in Horta?" Artie asked, his voice low and conversational.

Julian looked up, his fingers twisting a paper napkin into a tight, shredded cord. "He will have lawyers. He will have local maritime agents with a court injunction. If he knows I am on this boat, he will file a fraudulent crew petition with the maritime tribunal in Ponta Delgada, claiming that Marcus Ward is an impostor who stole proprietary company equipment."

"He would be right about the impostor part," Clara pointed out from the chart table, her voice icy and detached.

"He would," Julian admitted, without looking at her. "Marcus Ward is dead. I took his book. But the equipment is not company property; it is the evidence of systemic fraud that spans five European jurisdictions. Delacroix has laundered four hundred million euros through shell tanker companies registered in Limassol and Panama."

"And you were his compliance officer," Artie said.

"I was," Julian said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I signed the internal audit memos for four years. I told myself that offshore finance was just paper, that nobody was getting hurt. Then, six months ago, an uninspected chemical tanker Delacroix owned caught fire in the Red Sea. Three Filipino deckhands died in the pump room because the emergency halon system had been disconnected to save money on certification inspections."

Julian looked directly into Artie's eyes.

"That was the day I copied the drives," Julian said. "Three men burned to death in an engine room that cost four million euros less than this luxury yacht. I am not running to save my money, Captain. I am running to make sure someone answers for those men."

Artie Vance looked at him for five long seconds. He slowly reached across the table, picked up Julian's cold cup, and filled it with hot tea from the thermos.

"Drink your tea, Julian," Artie said. "Tomorrow we have forty-foot seas to steer. The dead will wait for Horta."

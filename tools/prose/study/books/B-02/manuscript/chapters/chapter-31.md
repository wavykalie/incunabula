# Chapter 31: The Back of the Gale

The veer of the wind came at noon on the eleventh day.

Arthur Vance sat on the saloon settee, his back wedged between two canvas lee cloths, watching the compass card through the companionway opening. Over the space of forty minutes, the wind shifted sixty degrees to the right, backing out of the southwest and settling into the north-northwest. The velocity dropped from fifty knots to thirty-four knots, the violent shrieking in the rod rigging subsiding to a low, hollow drone.

The change in wind direction brought no immediate relief to the sea state.

The ocean remained a monstrous, disorganized wilderness. The old, thirty-foot southwesterly swell was now crossed at right angles by a steep, twenty-foot northwesterly sea kicked up by the new breeze. Where the two wave trains intersected, the water erupted into chaotic, pyramid-shaped crests that leaped eight meters into the air, collapsing with the sudden, unpredictable violence of exploding rock.

*Aura VII* corkscrewed through the turbulence, her twin carbon rudders working continuously as Tom Nowak and Julian Kross manned the cockpit winch lines.

"Wind thirty-two knots from three-two-zero," Clara Lindholm reported from the companionway ladder. "The barometer is up to one thousand two hectopascals. It rose six millibars since dawn."

Artie nodded, feeling the dull ache in his taped ribs with every roll. "The cold front has cleared to the east. The depression is moving toward the British Isles. We have the Azores High building behind it."

"Can we set canvas?" Clara asked.

"Not yet," Artie said. "In this cross-sea, the boom would flog itself to pieces. If a breaking crest hits the mainsail while the boat is in the trough, the shock load will tear the gooseneck right off the mast. We run under the storm staysail until the sea lays down."

Tom Nowak climbed down into the saloon, his oilskins dripping salt water onto the floorboards. His face was gray with exhaustion, the skin around his eyes encrusted with white brine.

"The batteries are down to twenty-two point four volts, Artie," Tom said, his gravelly voice tight with concern. "The lithium monitor is beeping. If we do not start the Volvo to charge the house bank, the bilge pumps will die by nightfall, and we will lose the fluxgate compass."

Artie reached for the engine key on the chart table. "Start her up, Tom. Run at twelve hundred RPM in neutral to charge the bank."

Tom nodded, kneeling by the engine hatch. He turned the main battery isolator switch, pushed the glow-plug preheat button for ten seconds, and hit the starter switch.

The electric starter motor engaged with a sharp clatter. It spun the flywheel twice, wheezed, and stopped.

Tom frowned, waiting thirty seconds before hitting the starter again.

The engine cranked with a slow, grinding groan, coughed once, spat a cloud of gray, unburnt fuel vapor into the engine bay, and died completely.

Silence returned to the cabin, heavy and suffocating.

Tom pulled his head out of the hatch, looking at Artie with a grim, hollow expression.

"She won't fire," Tom said. "The fuel line is bone dry. The primary suction pickup in the main tank is completely blocked with sludge."

The morning sun broke through the remaining scud at seven knots, painting the underside of the cumulus clouds with pale amber and gold. The air had warmed to sixteen degrees, and the cold bite of the storm was gone, replaced by the clean, soft smell of land.

Artie stood at the bow, his arm wrapped around the forestay, looking eastward across the sea. He knew the smell before he saw the peaks: the scent of damp volcanic earth, wild cedar, and cow pastures carried forty miles out to sea on the morning offshore breeze.

"There," Noah called from the cockpit, pointing toward the eastern horizon.

A dark, sharp tooth rose above the haze. It was not a cloud; its edges were too sharp, its slopes rising at thirty degrees straight out of the Atlantic swell. It was the volcanic cone of Mount Pico, seven thousand seven hundred feet high, its summit crowned with a narrow ring of white vapor.

"That is Pico," Artie said, stepping aft into the cockpit. "And to the north of it is Faial."

Clara came on deck holding the ship's logbook and the international courtesy flags. She looked at the blue pyramid on the horizon for a long moment without speaking. Her face was pale, lined with the strain of seven days of continuous vigilance, but her jaw was set firm.

"We made it across," she said.

"We are close," Artie replied with his habitual caution. "The delivery is not finished until the lines are doubled on the dock, the customs clearance is stamped, and the broker has signed the acceptance sheet. More boats are lost on the final ten miles of an ocean passage than in the middle of the Atlantic. Keep your attention on the helm."

He went below to prepare the auxiliary engine for starting, checking the raw-water intake seacock beneath the galley sole to ensure it was open and free of marine growth.

# Chapter 36: The Paperwork in the Lazarette

The private conversation took place in the forward sail locker at seventeen-hundred hours, while the island of Faial was growing large and green off the starboard bow.

Arthur Vance walked down the narrow corridor to the forward cabin. The composite watertight door stood open, the brass safety pin dangling from the latch.

Inside the sail bin, Julian Kross was kneeling on a rolled Dacron reacher, his hands resting on the black Pelican hard-shell equipment case. The dual combination latches were unlocked, the heavy rubber gasket popped open half an inch.

Artie stopped in the doorway, bracing his shoulder against the composite ring frame to spare his fractured ribs.

"Packing your bags, Julian?" Artie asked calmly.

Julian looked up. His eyes were shadowed with the exhaustion of the crossing, but the panic that had haunted him since St. John's was gone.

He reached into the Pelican case, lifted out three small, ruggedized solid-state storage drives sealed in antistatic silver envelopes, and set them on the lid of the case.

"These are the master backups," Julian said, his voice flat and steady. "The Rotterdam ledgers. The shell company asset transfers. The forged safety certificates for Delacroix's entire offshore tanker fleet."

He looked at the three silver packets, then looked up at Artie.

"They are worth ten million euros in civil penalties to the European maritime authorities," Julian said. "And they contain the evidence that Delacroix ordered this yacht to sail into a Force Ten storm knowing her safety drogue was missing and her satellite link was cut."

He pushed the three silver envelopes across the black plastic lid toward Artie's boots.

"Take them, Captain," Julian urged softly. "Give them to your maritime lawyers in London. When Delacroix tries to withhold your escrow or sue you for deviation, you can use these drives to destroy him. You can take his company apart piece by piece."

Artie Vance looked down at the three silver packets.

He thought of Henri Delacroix sitting in his air-conditioned office in Monte Carlo, looking at satellite tracking maps, threatening him with contract breaches while four people fought thirty-foot seas with Spectra ropes on a broken steering quadrant. He thought of the auction gavel in Camden ten years ago, and the ten years of bitter, grinding delivery crossings that had stolen the strength from his knees and the warmth from his bones.

It would be easy. A phone call to an admiralty solicitor in London, a deposition at the maritime arbitration board, and Delacroix would be tied up in international litigation for five years.

Then Artie reached out, took the three silver packets, and dropped them back into the foam cutouts inside Julian's Pelican case.

He slammed the plastic lid shut and snapped the two heavy latches down with a loud, mechanical click.

"I am a delivery skipper, Julian," Artie said, his voice hard, quiet, and completely immovable. "I am not a blackmailer, and I am not an auditor."

Julian stared at him in disbelief. "Artie, he tried to kill us!"

"He tried to save his corporate skin," Artie said. "Which is what cowards do on land. But out here, I don't settle scores with stolen computer drives. I settled my score with Henri Delacroix when I kept his four-million-euro racing toy off the bottom of the Atlantic and brought every member of my crew to landfall alive."

He pointed his finger at the black case.

"Those drives belong to you," Artie said. "If you want to take them to the fraud prosecutors in Hamburg, that is your business. But when we tie up at the fuel dock in Horta, my logbook will record that *Aura VII* arrived four days ahead of schedule, with her hull sound, her machinery operational, and her delivery terms fulfilled."

He looked into Julian's eyes with an iron intensity that left no room for doubt.

"You sail on my ship, you leave clean," Artie told him. "Put your case in your duffel bag. Get on deck with the mooring warps. We have a harbor to enter."

The marina office was cool, shaded by thick stone walls and louvered wooden shutters that looked out over the anchorage. Behind the wooden counter sat the harbor master, a gray-haired man in a crisp white maritime uniform with gold anchor pins on his collar.

Artie laid the ship's document folder on the polished wood: the vessel's registration papers, the Bermuda clearance certificate, the crew manifest, and their three passports.

The harbor master examined each document with unhurried, bureaucratic precision, checking the hull identification number against the international registry. He looked up at Artie's salt-streaked face, his weathered eyes taking in the bloodshot sclera and the grease beneath the captain's fingernails.

"You came through the gale south of the bank?" the officer asked in fluent, accented English.

"We had fifty knots from the south, shifting to northwest," Artie replied. "Nine-hundred-seventy-eight millibars at the low point."

The harbor master nodded slowly, reaching for his brass seal. "A French sloop arrived yesterday with a broken boom. A British ketch lost their liferaft off Flores. You were fortunate."

"We prepared the ship," Artie said simply.

The harbor master pressed his steel stamp into an inkpad and brought it down on their clearance form with three solid thumps. The purple ink marked the paper with the official seal of the Republic of Portugal, Port of Horta, Azores.

"You are cleared into the Azores, Captain Vance," the officer said, handing back the passports with a slight smile. "Welcome to Faial. Go to Peter's for your beer."

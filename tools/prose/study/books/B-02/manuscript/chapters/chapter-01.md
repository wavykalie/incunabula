# Chapter 01: The Delivery Sheet

The delivery sheet was printed on eighty-gram copier paper, creased twice across the middle and damp around the margins where sea fog had crept past the companionway washboards.

Arthur Vance smoothed the paper against the varnished mahogany of the chart table. The figures had not changed since the courier handed him the envelope on the fuel dock at St. John's. Forty-two thousand Canadian dollars gross. Fourteen thousand advanced to the provisioning account and the bonded diesel locker. Twenty-eight thousand held in third-party escrow in Saint Helier, Jersey, payable upon execution of a clean hand-over certificate at Horta Marina in the Azores.

Beneath the payment schedule, printed in nine-point Helvetica, ran the penalty clause. A deduction of one thousand two hundred dollars for every twenty-four-hour period beyond sixteen sea days from departure, acts of God and verified mechanical breakdown excepted, subject to immediate telex validation by the owner's representative in Monaco.

Artie set his brass Weems & Plath dividers on the top edge of the sheet to stop the heater draft from curling it. Fifty-eight years old, with two titanium screws in his left ankle and eighteen thousand ocean miles logged in the last four years alone. When the bank took his coastal charter schooner ten years back, there had been no ceremony. Just an auctioneer with a chipped wooden gavel on the wharf in Camden, Maine, selling twenty-five years of equity in thirty-five minutes to pay off a balloon mortgage on an engine repower that had arrived six months late. Since then, deep-water delivery was the only trade left that paid cash on arrival without asking for a credit rating. Deliver the hull intact, keep the engine oil clear of water, sign the customs docket, collect the wire. This crossing was the escrow balance that would clear his remaining commercial debt and leave four years of rent in a savings account.

He stepped up into the companionway. Outside, the Southside Hills of St. John's rose steep and black into low drizzle. The cold Atlantic air smelled of cod meal, diesel exhaust, and wet granite.

*Aura VII* lay snug against four heavy pneumatic fenders at the commercial slip. She was a fifty-four-foot custom performance cutter, laid up in pre-preg carbon fiber at a boutique yard in Kiel two years earlier. Narrow beam, plumb stem, twin spade rudders machined from high-modulus carbon stocks, and a hydraulic furler for the staysail that hummed like a dentist's drill whenever someone pressed the cockpit pedestal button. A fast, expensive machine designed for weekend regattas in the Mediterranean, now tasked with crossing two thousand miles of northern ocean in late October.

Tomasz Nowak was standing on the aft deck, his yellow foul-weather jacket open despite the five-degree air. He was forty-five, broad-shouldered, with grease ground into the skin around his cuticles and two missing fingertips on his left hand where a merchant turbocharger had taken them off twelve years ago in Gdansk.

"Bunkers full, skipper," Tom said. His English was gravelly and unhurried. "Main tank three hundred liters. Forward reserve one hundred fifty. I dipped both with the wooden stick. Fuel is clean, no red dye, no water. Oil level in the Volvo is right at the high notch. I put half a liter of 15W-40 in the reserve can."

"What about the coolant?" Artie asked.

"Green and clean. Expansion tank half full cold. I replaced the raw water impellor this morning. The old one had two cracked vanes. I left the spare in the plastic tub under the galley floor." Tom spat a shred of nicotine gum into a scrap of paper and folded it into his pocket. "The cook stove is primed. Four bottles of propane in the lazarette. All sealed with factory caps."

"Good," Artie said. "Get your gear below. We leave on the ebb."

He turned back toward the saloon. At the navigation desk, Clara Lindholm had already set up her workstation. She was twenty-nine, her blonde hair pulled tight into a seaman's knot, her laptop connected to a portable USB barograph sensor sitting beside the main electrical panel. Two years ago she had been one of the rising navigators on the European offshore racing circuit until an offshore trimaran collision off Fastnet Rock ended with an inquiry, a suspended ticket, and a skipper who swore under oath that Lindholm had misread the AIS radar vector while he was asleep in his bunk. The commercial delivery circuit was full of people whose careers had cracked against hard rock or legal paper.

"The satellite terminal is locked, Artie," Clara said without looking up from her screen. Her voice was flat, Scandinavian, precise.

"Locked how?"

"Locked to receive-only. Look at the Furuno console." She tapped the gray casing of the Inmarsat unit mounted above the chart table. "The broker in Monaco configured the account profile. We can receive weather bulletins, fleet advisories, and direct incoming telexes from the owner's office. But the transmit buffer is disabled. No outgoing email, no voice calls, no manual position reports unless we hit the dedicated GMDSS distress button."

Artie leaned over her shoulder, smelling her wet wool sweater and the faint tang of battery acid. The green LCD screen on the Furuno displayed the system status in block letters: `RX ACTIVE // TX RESTRICTED BY SHORE DISPATCHER // CODE 04`.

"Did you talk to the agent in St. John's?" Artie asked.

"The agent said the owner pays the satellite bill and the owner sets the permissions," Clara replied, her fingers tracing the digital isobar lines on her weather map. "He said if we need to talk, we talk to God or wait until Faial. He also said the boat has an automated tracking beacon sending five-minute pings back to Monaco, so they know where we are whether we write to them or not."

Artie rubbed his chin. The stubble was gray and stiff under his palm. A delivery boat with a receive-only wire was not illegal, but it was cheap. It meant Monaco could change routing instructions whenever their private meteorological subscription found a faster lane, while Artie had no way to explain why twenty-foot head seas made thirty knots of wind untradable.

"We have the single-sideband receiver for Canadian Coast Guard weather," Artie said. "And the paper charts for the North Atlantic approaches. We don't need to chat with Monaco."

"I am not interested in chatting," Clara said. "I am interested in knowing why the GRIB model run from Halifax shows an explosive cyclonic low deepening south of Cape Race in seventy-two hours, while the delivery routing sheet from the owner tells us to maintain fifty-two degrees north on the great circle."

Artie looked down at the navigation desk. Clara had overlaid the owner's rhumb line against the Canadian meteorological fax. The owner's track ran straight northeast through the cold core of the Labrador Current, cutting across the tail of the Grand Banks into sixty-knot wind barbs.

"We adjust when we get off soundings," Artie said calmly. "Nobody sails into a bomb low to save forty miles of diesel."

"The contract has the sixteen-day clause, Artie. I saw your sheet."

"The contract is between me and the broker. Your day rate is guaranteed regardless of arrival date."

"My license is not," she said, her voice dropping an octave, cold and level. "I am not putting another maritime court inquiry into my jacket because a Monaco landlord wants his boat tied up in Cannes before the slip fees double."

Before Artie could answer, the companionway hatch slid back with a sharp clatter. Cold drizzle swept into the cabin, followed by a dark canvas duffel bag and a man wearing brand-new yellow foul-weather gear with the retail fold creases still visible across the chest.

Julian Kross lowered himself down the wooden steps, holding the handrails with both hands as if stepping onto an icy roof. He was thirty-four, thin, with sharp cheekbones and dark circles under his eyes that looked like bruises. In the crew paperwork filed with the harbor master that morning, he was listed as Marcus Ward, British national, commercial Yachtmaster Offshore, endorsed for commercial delivery.

"The slip line is cast off forward," Kross said. He glanced quickly around the saloon, his gaze lingering on the receive-only Furuno terminal before settling on Artie. "The fuel dock attendant said the surge is building in the Narrows. If we don't drop our lines now, the tide turns against us."

Artie took the duffel bag and tossed it into the starboard pilot berth. "You have your discharge book, Marcus?"

"I gave the copy to the port agent," the younger man said. His voice was slightly too fast, the rhythm stiff, like a student reciting a memorized passage.

"I need the original in the ship's safe. Customs regulation for high-seas departure. If we get boarded by the Canadian fisheries patrol off the Banks, they want originals, not photocopies."

Ward hesitated. For half a second, his fingers tightened around the zipper of his jacket. Then he reached into his inner pocket and drew out a dark blue leatherette booklet with gold lettering embossed on the cover: *United Kingdom Seaman's Discharge Book*.

Artie took it, feeling the dampness of the cover. "Bunk four is yours, forward to port. Tom is on the engine watch. You and Clara take the first dogwatch once we clear the fairway buoy. Stow your bag and get on deck with the winch handles. We're letting go."

Ward nodded once, turned, and disappeared down the narrow corridor leading toward the forward cabin.

Artie sat down on the chart bench, opened the ship's steel lockbox mounted beneath the radio shelf, and placed the blue discharge booklet inside beside his own weathered master's ticket. As he dropped the booklet into the tray, the corner of an expired inspection stamp caught his eye through the glassine page protector. He did not examine it then. Outside, the Volvo auxiliary engine thrummed through the hull framing, sending a steady fifty-cycle vibration through the soles of his sea boots.

He closed the steel lid, snapped the brass padlock shut, and pulled his wool watch cap down over his ears.

Ten minutes later, *Aura VII* slipped past the Battery under the towering granite walls of the Narrows. The raw Atlantic swell caught the bow the moment they crossed the line between the harbor lights, lifting the fifty-four-foot carbon hull and dropping her into the first deep trough with a shudder that resonated through the deck fittings. To the east, the ocean stretched away into black drizzle, two thousand miles of cold salt water between their bowsprit and the volcanic cliffs of Faial. Artie throttled the Volvo to eighteen hundred RPM, trimmed the small staysail against the rising easterly draft, and set his course on zero-eight-two magnetic.

The night run down the channel required three separate checks of the sea chest. Artie did not rely on the digital depth sounder alone. He kept the lead line coiled on the starboard deck box, thirty fathoms of braided hemp marked with scraps of red bunting and white cotton cord at two-fathom intervals.

He stepped to the weather rail, swung the seven-pound lead weight in two easy pendulum arcs beneath the shrouds, and cast it forward into the black chop.

The lead struck the water twenty feet ahead of the bow. Artie took up the slack with rapid hand-over-hand motions until the line came plumb under his shoulder. His thumb felt the mark: four fathoms and a foot. Twenty-five feet of salt water under the keel. The bottom tallow cavity brought up a smear of grey harbor silt mixed with broken shell.

"Depth confirms the paper chart," he told Noah at the helm. "Stay thirty yards west of the green flasher."

Noah adjusted the wheel three spokes to port. The steering cables creaked inside their bronze conduits beneath the cockpit sole.

Artie climbed down into the main salon to conduct the hourly bilgewater inspection. In a newly refitted hull, small leaks were routine, but routine leaks killed ships if neglected. He took his wooden sounding rod, calibrated in quarter-inches, and lowered it through the inspection hole in the center floorboard.

The rod came up damp for one inch.

"Three gallons in the keel sump," Artie recorded on the maintenance slate hanging by the companionway. "Rule bilge pump float switch is dry. Seepage from the mast boot collar."

He dried the sounding rod with an oily rag, hung it back on its brass hooks, and checked the secondary battery isolator switch. The house bank was taking a solid twenty-eight amperes from the Balmar alternator, bringing the twelve-volt system up to thirteen point six volts. Everything hummed with mechanical order.

He climbed back into the cockpit, closed the sliding hatch halfway to keep the spray off the instrument console, and sat on the windward cockpit bench with his back against the cabin bulkhead. The crossing had begun.

The deck log was an eighty-page canvas ledger bound in blue sailcloth, stamped with the insignia of the Royal Bermuda Yacht Club. Artie opened it to page one, writing in block capital letters with an indelible drafting pen.

He recorded the vessel's registration number, her gross tonnage of twenty-four metric tons, the diesel fuel capacity of one hundred and twenty gallons across two aluminum saddle tanks, and the potable water capacity of ninety gallons in the polyethylene keel tank. Beneath the particulars, he listed the crew watch rotation: four hours on, eight hours off, with Artie taking the dog watches from sixteen hundred to twenty hundred to supervise the change of sails.

"Every entry requires three data points," Artie told Clara, who was watching from the galley counter. "Barometric pressure to the nearest tenth of a millibar, true wind speed and direction, and vessel position by GPS and dead reckoning. If the electrical system takes a lightning strike mid-Atlantic, the dead reckoning track is the only line keeping us off the rocks."

Clara took the pen, testing the ink on the margin of the navigation log.

"What is our leeway allowance for the Gulf Stream crossing?" she asked.

"Two degrees of leeway for every fifteen knots of wind on the beam," Artie said. "With the Gulf Stream setting north-northeast at two point five knots, we steer ten degrees south of the rhumb line to compensate. The current will sweep us right onto the track by tomorrow evening."

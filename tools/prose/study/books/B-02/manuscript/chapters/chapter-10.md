# Chapter 10: The Split Decision

By fourteen-hundred hours, the color of the ocean had ceased to be blue or green. It was the dull, leaden gray of industrial cement, streaked with broad ribbons of yellow-white foam that trailed across the water like the tracks of heavy machinery.

Arthur Vance stood at the companionway, clipped to the cockpit jackline by his six-foot nylon safety tether.

The wind was blowing a sustained thirty-six knots from two-eight-five degrees, gusting forty-four in the squalls. Aloft, the mainsail had been lowered completely, flaked down onto the carbon boom and secured with five heavy webbing straps. Under only the orange Dacron storm staysail, a tiny triangle of bulletproof fabric sheeted hard to the inboard track, Aura VII was surging downwind at nine point two knots.

The motion was violent, abrupt, and completely unforgiving.

Unlike a heavy displacement steel or wood vessel that sliced through the water with steady momentum, the light carbon cutter danced across the surface. When an overtaking swell lifted the broad, flat stern, the yacht surfed down the face with a burst of speed, reaching twelve or thirteen knots before burying her plumb bow into the boiling white water of the trough with a deceleration that threw everyone against the bulkheads.

Artie climbed fully into the cockpit and took the wheel from Clara Lindholm.

Her knuckles were white through her rubberized offshore gloves. Her face was encrusted with dried salt crystals, her eyes narrow and squinting against the stinging spray.

"Heading zero-nine-seven," she shouted above the shriek of the rigging. "Speed nine point four. The barometer is down to nine-nine-six. It fell eight millibars since noon."

"Get below," Artie told her. "Get some hot soup into you. Tell Tom to wedge the floorboards in the galley."

Clara hesitated, looking back over the stern.

Behind them, a massive swell, at least eighteen feet from trough to crest, was rolling out of the gray squall. The face of the wave was steep, almost vertical, topped with a curling lip of frothing white foam that hissed as it collapsed down the slope.

Aura VII rose to it with a sharp, heavy roll to starboard, her twin rudders biting deep into the turbulent water as Artie applied a quarter-turn of port helm to keep the transom square to the crest. The wave passed beneath them with a roar like a freight train passing through a tunnel, throwing forty gallons of icy brine across the cockpit coaming before rolling away into the mist ahead.

"She handles it," Clara murmured, almost to herself.

"She handles it if you steer the hull," Artie said. "Go below, Clara."

She unclipped her safety line and dropped through the companionway hatch, sliding the washboard shut behind her.

Artie stood alone at the binnacle.

He looked at the small grease-pencil marks he had drawn on the gelcoat of the cockpit coaming. Heading zero-nine-seven.

It was a split decision, a calculated compromise between two impossible masters.

To the north, along the great circle track demanded by Henri Delacroix and the Monaco owners, lay sixty-knot headwinds, freezing temperatures, and the violent cyclonic core of the developing low. To obey Monaco meant risking the structural integrity of the carbon rig and the lives of four people to satisfy a computer algorithm that prioritized a charter date in Cannes.

To the south, along the track recommended by Southampton weather routing, lay five hundred miles of diversion, four days of calms, empty fuel tanks, and the total forfeiture of his twenty-eight-thousand-dollar delivery fee under the contractual penalty clause. To obey Southampton meant arriving in Horta as an unemployed, penniless fifty-eight-year-old skipper with a defaulted mortgage and no future in the commercial trade.

So he had chosen the path between them.

Heading zero-nine-seven kept the yacht on the southern edge of the gale, riding the fast winds of the rear quadrant while steering clear of the storm center. It was a course that demanded non-stop, flawless hand-steering for forty-eight hours through thirty-foot seas. If anyone on the helm missed a wave, if the boat broached and turned broadside to an eighteen-foot breaking crest, the force of the water would roll the fifty-four-foot hull upside down before the autopilot could react.

Artie felt the carbon wheel vibrate beneath his palms.

Down in the aft cabin, in the safe under the navigation desk, lay Julian Kross's forged discharge book and the encrypted memory drive that the younger man guarded like his life. In the forward locker lay sixty gallons of contaminated diesel taking on salt with every roll. Behind the companionway steps sat an exhausted Polish engineer nursing an old injury, and at the chart table sat a disgraced young navigator trying to salvage her career from the wreckage of a collision inquiry.

Four strangers, an untested racing machine, and an ocean that cared nothing for contracts, court inquiries, or human pensions.

Artie took a deep breath of the freezing Atlantic air. He planted his boots firm against the teak foot-brace, gripped the wheel with both hands, and steered the bow down the face of the next dark wave. The salt burned cold in the corners of his eyes, but his grip remained steady on the rim.

The afternoon wore on with the slow, crushing inevitability of deep-water ocean travel.

Every four hours, the watch changed with the precision of a naval chronometer. Two hours on the helm, two hours on standby, four hours of fitful, exhausted sleep in a wet fleece blanket on a rolling settee.

Artie Vance inspected the mast boot beneath the companionway floorboards. The heavy neoprene collar that sealed the mast where it passed through the coachroof was weeping slightly, a thin trickle of salt water running down the black carbon spar into the keel sump. He dried the fitting with a sponge, wrapped three turns of rubber rigging tape around the clamp, and tightened the stainless hose clamp with a stubby screwdriver.

In the galley, Tom was preparing the evening meal: a large pot of dried navy beans soaked in fresh water from the manual pump, cooked with canned ham, onions, and black pepper. The thick, savory steam filled the saloon, providing a brief, comforting illusion of domestic security against the howling gale outside.

Julian Kross sat in the corner of the settee, his waterproof notebook open on his knees, writing with a short pencil stub.

Artie glanced at the page as he passed.

The columns of numbers had ceased. In their place, Julian was writing a simple, daily journal of the crossing: wind speed, wave height, sail trim, the color of the water at dawn, and the taste of Tom's sourdough bread.

The corporate fugitive was learning to see the world as a sailor.

Artie climbed the companionway ladder into the cockpit. The sun was setting behind the western cloud bank, throwing long, bloody rays of crimson light across the tumbling gray peaks of the Atlantic swells.

*Aura VII* surged down the face of an eighteen-foot wave, her twin rudders carving twin tracks of emerald foam into the dark water. Heading zero-nine-seven. The compass needle held true, pointing directly toward the Azores, five days and eight hundred miles across the deep blue abyss.

# Chapter 32: Bleeding the Lines

The dissection of a marine diesel fuel system at sea requires patience, clean rags, and complete indifference to petroleum nausea.

Tomasz Nowak sat on the rubber floor matting in front of the open engine compartment, a set of metric flare-nut wrenches laid out across a dry towel. The air in the saloon was thick with the suffocating fumes of raw diesel oil, warm transmission fluid, and the sharp chemical odor of brake cleaner.

Arthur Vance knelt beside him, holding a halogen inspection lantern aimed into the narrow void behind the companionway stairs. Every breath sent a sharp needle of pain through Artie's taped ribs, but he did not move away.

"Show me the pickup pipe," Artie said.

Tom held up a twenty-inch copper suction tube he had unbolted from the top inspection plate of the main fuel tank.

The lower end of the pipe, which sat half an inch above the bottom of the fuel tank, was completely packed with a thick, tar-like plug of black asphaltic sludge. It had the consistency of cold axle grease, mixed with dead microbial matter and granular red rust scales.

"Diesel bug," Tom said, scraping a glob of the black sludge onto a paper towel. "Cladosporium resinae. The fungus lives in the water layer at the bottom of the tank and feeds on the hydrocarbons. When the gale rolled the boat on her beam-ends, the motion stirred the sludge off the floor and sucked it straight into the pipe."

He tapped the copper tube against the mahogany step. A solid plug of black gunk dropped onto the towel.

"The tank is poisoned," Tom said. "Two hundred liters of fuel in the main bunker, and every liter is full of suspended black slime. If I reconnect the suction pipe, it will choke the Racor filter in ninety seconds."

"What about the secondary engine filter?" Artie asked.

"Clean," Tom said. "I took off the spin-on canister. The paper element is white. But the high-pressure injection pump has air in the gallery. Even if we give it clean fuel, we have to bleed every high-pressure injector line at the cylinder head before the cylinders will fire."

Clara Lindholm appeared at the companionway opening, holding a clipboard with the hourly dead-reckoning plot.

"Our speed has dropped to four point two knots," Clara reported. "The wind is easing to twenty-five knots, but the swell is rolling us thirty degrees every ten seconds. The batteries are down to twenty-one point eight volts. The radar display just shut down from low-voltage cutoff."

Artie looked at the battery monitor. The digital screen was flickering, displaying a yellow warning icon: `CRITICAL LOW VOLTAGE // 15% CAPACITY REMAINING`.

Without electric power, the automatic Rule bilge pumps would cease to function. If a shaft seal leaked or a thru-hull fitting wept under the strain of the swells, the bilge water would rise unnoticed beneath the floorboards.

"We need that engine running, Tom," Artie said. "Not for propulsion. For the alternator."

"I cannot clean two hundred liters of fuel in a rolling boat, Artie," Tom replied, his scarred hands wiping a smear of black sludge from his forehead. "I would need a centrifuge and six spare filter elements. We don't have them."

Julian Kross stepped into the saloon from the forward passage. He was carrying a twenty-liter yellow plastic jerry-can in each hand, his breath coming hard under the weight.

"The deck reserves," Julian said, setting the plastic cans down on the floorboards with a dull thud. "We have four twenty-liter cans lashed against the liferaft cradle on the coachroof. St. John's commercial diesel. Pure, clean marine gas oil."

Tom looked at the yellow canisters, then looked at Julian. A slow spark of mechanical inspiration ignited in the Polish engineer's tired eyes.

"Eighty liters," Tom murmured. "Eighty liters of clean fuel. If we bypass the main tank entirely, we can run a direct feed straight into the injection pump."

Artie turned the engine ignition key to the run position. The glow plug indicator light glowed orange for seven seconds on the instrument panel, then clicked off with a sharp relay pulse.

"Clear the propeller!" Artie called up the companionway.

Noah glanced over the starboard quarter to verify that no mooring lines, fishing gear, or sheets were trailing in the water. "Prop is clear!"

Artie pushed the starter button.

The heavy twelve-volt starter motor engaged with a sharp clunk, spinning the Volvo diesel against its compression strokes. For two seconds the engine wheezed, blowing a puff of white unburnt fuel out the transom exhaust, and then the four cylinders fired with a deep, rhythmic thrum that resonated through the fiberglass hull.

Artie kept his eyes glued to the oil pressure gauge. The needle jumped instantly from zero to forty, then climbed steadily to fifty-five pounds per square inch as the new copper line held tight. Under the engine hatch, the mechanical lift pump stroked rhythmically, without a single drop of fuel weeping from the flare fittings.

"Oil pressure normal," Artie called. "Raw water exhaust is spitting."

Noah leaned over the stern rail and watched the transom discharge. Every three seconds a gallon of warm saltwater surged from the exhaust elbow, churning the blue water with a cloud of white foam.

"Cooling water is strong," Noah reported. "Temperature gauge is coming up to sixty."

Artie let the diesel idle at nine hundred revolutions per minute for five minutes, listening with a practiced ear to the valve train, the alternator belt tension, and the smooth hum of the raw water pump. Everything ran true. The mechanical debt incurred during the gale had been paid down in full with cold grease, spare copper, and disciplined labor.

"Engage forward gear," Artie said, climbing into the cockpit. "One thousand two hundred revs. Let's make our approach."

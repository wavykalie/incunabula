# Chapter 14: Scent of Ozone

The satellite receiver died at twenty-two-hundred hours.

Clara Lindholm was sitting at the navigation station, running her fingers across the touchpad of her laptop. She was downloading the latest high-resolution GRIB weather model from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, watching the progress bar crawl across the screen.

Suddenly, the screen froze.

The progress bar stopped at forty-two percent. A bright red dialogue box popped onto the display: CONNECTION TERMINATED BY REMOTE HOST // ERROR 403: SERVICE SUSPENDED.

Clara frowned, clicking the refresh icon. The screen flashed white, then displayed a blank network status page.

She turned to the Furuno Inmarsat terminal mounted above the desk. The green LCD screen was dark. The power LED was lit, but the status line displayed three flashing asterisks followed by a single line of text: ACCOUNT DEACTIVATED BY SYSTEM ADMINISTRATOR // CONTACT SHORE DISPATCH.

"Artie," she called out, her voice sharp with sudden alarm.

Artie Vance came out of the aft head, drying his face with a gray towel. "What is it?"

"The satellite terminal is dead."

"Did the breaker trip?"

"No," Clara said, flicking the main communications toggle on the electrical switchboard. "The unit has twelve volts DC. The receiver is powered. But the provider has terminated our account. The shore dispatcher in Monaco cut the uplink."

Artie leaned over her shoulder, examining the screen. He pressed the manual sync button on the Furuno casing. The machine beeped twice, attempted an automated handshake with the geostationary satellite overhead, and displayed: ACCESS DENIED // SIM CARD BLOCKED.

"They shut us off," Clara whispered, staring at the dead terminal. "Delacroix shut off our satellite link."

"Why would he shut off the weather feed?" Artie asked, his mind working through the commercial possibilities. "Even if he's angry about my routing, he has fifty-four feet of carbon yacht out here. Turning off our weather radar in a late-October Atlantic depression is reckless endangerment."

"Unless he doesn't want us to know where the storm is," Clara said.

From the forward doorway, Julian Kross stepped into the red cabin light. He was wearing his dark wool sweater, his hands thrust deep into his pockets.

"It's not about the storm," Julian said calmly.

Artie and Clara both turned to look at him.

"What did you say?" Artie asked.

Julian looked down at the teak floorboards, his shoulders slumping as if an invisible weight had finally crushed his spine. "Delacroix didn't cut the satellite to blind you from the weather. He cut the satellite because he knows I am on board."

The saloon was silent for five long seconds. The only sound was the rush of water outside the hull and the faint, hot scent of electrical insulation cooling inside the dead receiver.

"Explain," Artie said, his voice dangerously soft.

Julian looked up. In the red light, his face was hollow, haunted, and completely stripped of pretense.

"Delacroix monitors the incoming telex traffic to all his fleet vessels through an automated server in Rotterdam," Julian said. "Three hours ago, when the Canadian Coast Guard broadcast that Pan Pan alert with my real name, the server flagged the keyword match. Delacroix knows that Julian Kross is signed on Aura VII under Marcus Ward's credentials. And he knows what is inside my Pelican case."

"What is inside the case, Julian?" Clara asked, using his real name for the first time.

"His bank ledgers," Julian said. "Seven years of fraudulent vessel financing, forged safety certificates, and illegal asset transfers for shell companies in Cyprus. If those drives reach the maritime fraud prosecutor in Hamburg, Delacroix goes to prison for twenty years, and every asset in his management fleet is seized by the European courts."

Julian looked at Artie with a bleak, desperate intensity.

"He cut the satellite so we cannot transmit our position to the authorities," Julian said. "And so we cannot receive any warnings. He wants this boat to disappear in the gale. If Aura VII sinks in forty-foot seas off the Flemish Cap, his problems are buried under three thousand meters of Atlantic salt water, and the underwriter pays him four million euros for a total hull loss."

Artie turned to the dead Furuno screen. He reached out and clicked the main breaker to OFF, isolating the circuit.

"We sail by barometer and eye," Artie said, his voice hard as iron. "We don't need Monaco to tell us the wind is blowing."

Artie stepped over to the main electrical panel, pulling the circuit breaker cover open to inspect the bus bar wiring behind the dead Furuno terminal.

The terminal's power supply was fed directly from the twenty-four-volt service bank through a Victron DC-to-DC converter. The converter's green LED was steady, humming with a high-pitched forty-cycle whistle. The wiring was pristine, clean marine tinned copper, showing no signs of overheat, chafe, or melted insulation.

"The unit is healthy," Artie said, snapping the cover shut. "The hardware is intact. It is a remote account lock, exactly as Julian said."

Clara stood beside the chart table, her laptop screen reflecting pale blue against her cheekbones. She had disconnected the USB satellite modem, opening the local meteorological analysis software that operated without an external uplink.

"We have our last GRIB model from eighteen hours ago," Clara said, tapping the trackpad. "The model shows the cyclonic depression deepening to nine hundred sixty-eight millibars over the Mid-Atlantic Trench. If the storm follows the historical track of late-autumn Atlantic lows, the center will pass sixty miles to the north of our rhumb line."

"Which puts us in the dangerous semicircle," Artie said.

"Yes," Clara said. "The southern quadrant. The winds will be southwest, veering to northwest as the cold front sweeps through. The fetch across the ocean is fourteen hundred nautical miles. That gives the waves time to build to fifteen meters."

Tom Nowak came forward from the galley, wiping his hands on a clean tea towel. "Fifteen-meter waves. Forty-five feet. In shallow water, that would break a hull in half."

"This is not shallow water," Artie told him. "We are over the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The depth sounder shows three thousand four hundred meters. The waves will be long, rolling ridges, not shelf breakers. As long as we keep the hull moving at eight knots and don't allow the stern to broach, the boat will lift over the crests."

"And if the steering gear fails?" Julian asked from the corridor doorway.

"Then we find out how well Tom Nowak can weld in a gale," Artie said flatly. "Get your foul-weather gear on, Julian. It's time to check the deck lashings."

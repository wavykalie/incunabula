# Chapter 33: The Gravity Feed

The surgery on the fuel system began with a sharp box cutter and six feet of clear reinforced vinyl tubing from the emergency plumbing kit.

Tomasz Nowak worked with the feverish, methodical focus of a surgeon in a field hospital. He unbolted the primary Racor filter assembly from the forward bulkhead, bypassing the ship's contaminated fuel lines completely.

He took the first twenty-liter yellow jerry-can, unscrewed the plastic cap, and inserted a clean three-quarter-inch siphon hose into the amber fuel.

"We need height," Tom said, looking around the cabin. "Gravity feed. The lift pump on the Volvo is mechanical, but if we give it two feet of positive hydrostatic head pressure, the fuel will flow down into the injector pump without pulling air through the fittings."

"Use the saloon handrail," Artie suggested, pointing to the heavy stainless steel grab bar bolted to the cabin deckhead above the galley.

Julian Kross immediately grabbed two heavy webbing ratchet straps from the lazarette bin. He hoisted the twenty-liter fuel can, which weighed nearly forty pounds, and lashed it tight against the overhead grab rail, securing the container with three crisscrossed wraps that held it rock-solid against the rolling motion of the hull.

The clear vinyl hose ran straight down from the inverted spout, dropping four feet into the secondary fuel filter mounted on the side of the engine block.

Tom opened the brass valve on the siphon hose.

A clear stream of pale amber diesel filled the vinyl tube, bubbles rising to the top as the fuel displaced the trapped air.

"Gravity is working," Tom said. "Now, Artie, hold the wrench on the number one injector nut."

Artie knelt beside the engine block, his left arm pressed tight against his taped ribs to brace against the pain. In his right hand, he held a seventeen-millimeter flare-nut wrench, fitting the jaws over the hex nut that connected the high-pressure steel fuel line to the first cylinder injector.

"Julian," Tom ordered. "Take the engine key. When I say crank, you turn the starter for five seconds. Not four, not six. Five seconds."

"Ready," Julian said, his fingers poised over the ignition switch.

"Crank!" Tom barked.

The starter motor engaged with a heavy, rhythmic chug: chug-chug-chug-chug-chug.

At the number one injector, a mixture of frothy white air bubbles and diesel fuel sputtered past the loosened threads of the nut.

"Hold!" Tom yelled.

Julian released the key.

Artie quickly tightened the seventeen-millimeter nut with a sharp twist of his wrist, locking the seal before air could enter the line.

"Number two injector," Tom said, moving his wrench to the second cylinder. "Crank!"

Again the starter churned: chug-chug-chug-chug.

A solid jet of clean, amber diesel squirted past the nut, hitting the engine box insulation with a sharp hiss.

"Tighten!" Tom shouted.

Artie spun the wrench, locking the fitting.

They repeated the process on cylinder three and cylinder four, bleeding every bubble of air from the high-pressure gallery until all four lines were filled with solid, incompressible fuel.

Tom wiped his hands on a rag, stood up, and took a deep breath of the petroleum-soaked air.

"Give her half throttle, Julian," Tom said, his voice dropping into a quiet, solemn cadence. "Say a prayer to Saint Christopher. Hit the starter."

Julian pushed the glow-plug button for eight seconds, then turned the key to the start position.

The starter motor gave two slow revolutions.

Then, with an explosive, rhythmic roar that shook the floorboards, the seventy-five-horsepower Volvo D2-75 caught fire.

The engine coughed once, blew a short puff of dark exhaust smoke out through the transom water-lift muffler, and settled into a steady, beautiful, eighteen-hundred-RPM purr.

Inside the cabin, the high-frequency vibration was like the heartbeat of a revived giant.

On the main switchboard, the red warning light on the lithium battery monitor flickered and turned to solid green. The digital amperage display jumped immediately: `CHARGING // +65.4 AMPS // 27.8 VOLTS`.

Tom Nowak rested his forehead against the companionway bulkhead, closed his eyes, and let out a long, shuddering sigh of pure relief.

Up on deck, Clara Lindholm looked down through the companionway hatch, her face splitting into a wide, brilliant smile that erased three years of inquiry cynicism in a single instant.

"The alternator is charging!" she shouted. "The radar is back on! We have power, Artie!"

Artie stood at the base of the companionway, his hand resting on the warm cast-iron valve cover of the engine. Beneath his palm, the machine thrummed with life, burning clean fuel from an overhead canister, driving forty tons of carbon toward safe harbor.

The island grew larger with every hour, transforming from a blue silhouette into a landscape of incredible detail. Dark green patchwork fields of pasture were divided by dark basalt stone walls, climbing steeply from black sea cliffs toward the cloud-draped caldeira. Along the shoreline, white cottages with terracotta tile roofs clustered around small stone churches.

"Channel current is setting us north," Clara observed, taking a compass bearing on the lighthouse at Ponta da Espalamaca. "Two knots of drift toward the rocks."

"Steer one-hundred-twenty degrees to compensate," Artie commanded. "Keep the lighthouse two points on the port bow until we clear the southern reef."

Noah was on the foredeck, unlashing the primary anchor from the bow roller. He pulled twenty feet of galvanized chain out of the locker, laying it in neat bights on the deck so it would run free without snarling if they had to anchor in an emergency.

"Anchor is clear to drop," Noah signaled aft.

Artie throttled the diesel back to one thousand revolutions. The wind had dropped to a light eight-knot breeze under the shelter of the high island ridge, and the water flattened into a glassy, turquoise sheet that reflected the volcanic slopes above. The smell of land was overwhelming now: wet grass, woodsmoke, eucalyptus, and the rich, mineral scent of crushed volcanic stone.

"Take down the staysail," Artie ordered. "We enter harbor under bare poles and auxiliary power."

Clara eased the staysail halyard, and Noah caught the cloth as it slid down the stay, bundling it neatly on the foredeck and securing it with two sail ties. The yacht was stripped for harbor, her deck clean, lines coiled, and halyards stopped off to the mast rails to prevent them from slapping in the breeze.

The profile of Mount Pico dominated the entire eastern sky as the morning mist burned away under the midday sun. The great volcano was an imposing landmark, its black basalt peak rising serenely above a belt of white trade-wind clouds that girdled its waist at four thousand feet.

Artie sat on the teak cockpit bench, his marine binoculars pressed to his eyes. He scanned the rocky headlands of Faial, picking out the white tower of the Ponta da Espalamaca light, the green pasture slopes of the Monte da Guia peninsula, and the red channel buoys that marked the entrance channel.

"We have two knots of favorable current through the channel now," Clara reported, checking the Portuguese tide tables she had found in the nav desk. "Slack water is at fourteen hundred. If we maintain six knots over the ground, we will arrive at the breakwater right at slack."

"Timing is everything in pilotage," Artie said, lowering his binoculars. "Going through the Faial channel with three knots of foul current against a southwesterly swell creates standing waves four feet high in the entrance. A boat under auxiliary power can lose her steering and be swept onto the Espalamaca reef."

Noah was standing by the mast, coiling the spare halyards and hanging them on the teak pin rails. The young man looked transformed from the exhausted deckhand who had struggled through the Gulf Stream two weeks before. His skin was tanned a dark walnut brown, his movements were sure and balanced, and his hands had the tough, calloused grip of a working seaman.

"Noah," Artie called. "Rig the harbor dock lines. Double bow lines, two stern lines, and two long spring lines with chafe gear on the eyes."

"All eight-strand nylon, Captain?"

"Eight-strand nylon," Artie confirmed. "It has thirty percent stretch, which absorbs the surge inside the harbor when the outer swell penetrates the mole."

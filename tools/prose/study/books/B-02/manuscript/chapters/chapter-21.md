# Chapter 21: Rigging the Storm Jib

The strike of the gale was not a gradual increase of wind. It was an atmospheric detonation.

At fifteen-twelve, the oily calm of the low-pressure center was torn open by a shrieking wall of northwest wind that hit Aura VII broadside with the impact of an iron plate. In less than forty seconds, the wind speed leapt from eight knots to fifty-four knots, blowing the tops clean off the eight-meter swells and turning the air into a blinding fog of flying brine and pulverized foam.

The cutter was laid flat on her starboard beam-ends, her carbon spreaders plunging toward the boiling water before Arthur Vance could throw his full weight against the leeward wheel spokes.

"All hands on deck!" Artie's voice roared through the companionway hatch, vibrating with the raw, instinctive authority of survival.

Below, Tomasz Nowak and Julian Kross were already struggling into their heavy oilskin smocks. The cabin sole was tilted at an impossible forty-degree angle, water from the bilge wells splashing against the mahogany table. Clara was wedged against the chart table, her boots braced against the battery box, clutching the companionway sill with both hands.

"The staysail is backing!" Clara yelled, water cascading down her yellow hood as she peered out through the hatchway slider. "The sheet is caught on the inner shroud turnbuckle!"

Artie dragged himself along the high side of the cockpit, his safety tether clipped short to the bridge deck padeye. The force of the wind was deafening, a high-pitched scream that drowned out human speech, vibrating through his teeth and vibrating through the fiberglass coaming.

"We need the storm jib hoisted on the inner forestay!" Artie shouted into Tom's ear as the engineer climbed into the cockpit. "The staysail will tear into ribbons if we leave it flogging! Tom, take the halyard winch! Julian, you and I go to the foredeck!"

Julian looked at the foredeck. Thirty feet of narrow carbon deck, wet as greased slate, swept by green Atlantic water every eight seconds as the bow buried into the steep wave faces. His face was white, his eyes wide and bloodshot with terror, but he did not hesitate. He hooked his secondary tether to the heavy stainless jackline running along the cabin top and unlatched the heavy sail bag.

They crawled on hands and knees across the coachroof, keeping their bodies flat against the deck nonskid.

The temperature had plunged to two degrees Celsius. The spray froze against their oilskins, forming a crackling crust of salt rime across their shoulders. Beneath them, the carbon hull groaned with a sharp, resonant ping each time a wave struck the stem, the composite fibers flexing under thousands of foot-pounds of hydraulic torque.

At the base of the mast, Julian braced his boots against the halyard organizer, gripping the luff tape of the fluorescent orange storm jib. His fingers were stiff with cold, but his rigging instincts took over with rapid precision. He fed the heavy Dacron bolt-rope into the aluminum feeder groove, snapping the bronze hanks onto the wire stay one by one.

"Hoist!" Artie bellowed toward the cockpit, waving his arm in a wide, circular arc.

Back at the companionway, Tom Nowak threw three wraps of the Spectra halyard around the heavy Lewmar 65 winch, braced his hip against the bulkhead, and ground the handle with furious, rhythmic power.

The small orange triangle rose into the screaming air, stiff as sheet steel, snapping with a sound like pistol shots until Julian sheeted the clew home through the inboard snatch block.

Just as the sail took the load, pulling the yacht's bow away from the wind and easing the dangerous heel, a rogue wave rose out of the dark water on the port beam.

It was twelve feet higher than the surrounding swells, a sheer, breaking cliff of gray water crowned with three tons of tumbling white foam. The wave hit the port midships with the sound of a cannon shot, washing completely across the deck.

The water knocked Tom Nowak off his feet, sweeping him across the cockpit sole toward the open transom. His primary safety tether snapped against the lifeline stanchion with a sickening crack as the bronze snap-hook bent open under the immense load.

"Tom!" Artie screamed from the mast base.

Tom was sliding over the aft coaming, his boots dangling in the white foam of the quarter wave, his hands clawing at the slick carbon edge.

Julian Kross did not think. He did not look at the wave. He launched himself down the sloping coachroof, throwing his entire body across the cockpit bench, his right arm shooting out and catching the collar of Tom's yellow oilskin jacket with an iron grip.

The force of the water nearly dragged Julian over the stern alongside the engineer. Julian's left hand caught the stainless steel pedestal guard, his fingers locking around the two-inch tube with desperate, bone-crushing strength.

Artie reached the cockpit three seconds later. He grabbed Tom's secondary safety line, threw a bight around the leeward sheet winch, and hauled the gasping Polish engineer back onto the cockpit floorboards.

Tom lay on the nonskid, coughing salt water, his chest heaving under his sodden fleece. He looked up at Julian, who was still clutching the stainless pedestal, his knuckles bleeding where the gelcoat had scraped the skin away.

"You caught me," Tom gasped, spitting a mouthful of Atlantic brine onto the deck. "You caught me, boy."

Julian did not answer. He collapsed against the carbon wheel pedestal, his chest heaving in short, ragged sobs of shock and relief, his blood mixing with the cold salt water that drained through the cockpit scuppers.

Artie knelt beside them both, checking their tethers, his hand resting on Julian's shoulder with a firm, silent weight.

"The sail is set," Artie said, his voice cutting through the roar of the gale like a steel blade. "She is tracking. Get below, both of you. Dry off. The real blow is only just beginning."

The glass did not pause at nine-ninety-four. By midday the needle rested on nine-ninety-one, and the sea lost its chop, flattening into long, oily hills that slid under the hull without breaking.

Artie went forward to inspect the bow roller. The anchor was lashed down with three turns of six-millimeter chain and a safety line around the windlass gypsy. He checked the bow shackle, tightened the seizing wire with his pliers, and checked the chain locker seal. Saltwater here was heavy. A loose hatch cover could take thirty gallons into the forepeak with every plunge, soaking the spare sails and adding half a ton of deadweight where the boat could least afford it.

"Deck is tight," he called aft.

Noah nodded from the helm, both hands resting on the stainless spokes. The steering felt heavy now, loaded by the growing quarterly swell. "Vane is holding four knots on the log. Should I pull the daggerboard up three inches?"

"Leave it down," Artie replied. "We need the lateral resistance when the front hits. If we drift to leeward in forty knots, we will lose twenty miles before nightfall."

Clara emerged from the companionway holding a plastic bowl of cold rice and tinned sardines. She handed a spoon to Noah and set Artie's share on the cabin top. Her wet weather jacket was zipped to her chin, the neoprene neck gasket tight against her skin.

"Barometer is down three millibars in the last ninety minutes," she reported. "I pulled the latest weatherfax from Boston on four megahertz. The isobar lines are packed together like fence posts south of forty-two north. The low is deepening as it moves over the warmer shelf water."

Artie scooped a mouthful of oily fish, chewing methodically. At sea, calories were maintenance gear. You did not eat for comfort; you ate so your hands did not tremble when you had to pull forty pounds of dacron off a foredeck in freezing rain.

"We take the second reef now," Artie said, wiping his spoon on an oily rag. "While we still have daylight and a dry deck. Once the wind shifts to the southwest, we will not want to be standing on the cabin trunk."

They moved together without haste, following the routine they had built over ten days. Clara took the topping lift and the halyard, while Artie worked the reefing lines at the mast base. The mainsail spilled its wind with a dry flutter, and Artie hauled the second cringle down to the gooseneck, securing it with the stainless horn. He winched the tack tight, tied off the bunt with three lengths of shock cord, and signaled Clara to sweat the halyard home. When the cloth filled again, the boat stood taller, shoulder to the sea, cutting through the swells at five steady knots.

The cloud ceiling lowered steadily throughout the afternoon, dropping from five thousand feet of altostratus to a ragged blanket of dark fractocumulus that skimmed two hundred feet above the masthead. The light turned a sullen sulfur yellow, reflecting off the rising gray swells with an unwholesome sheen.

Artie checked the Barigo aneroid barometer on the forward bulkhead. The brass needle had dropped another two millibars, pointing to nine-eighty-nine.

"Noah, bring the storm jib on deck," he commanded. "We will lash it to the base of the mast in its bag so we do not have to haul it out of the forepeak when the wind is screaming."

Noah hauled the heavy orange bag up the companionway ladder. The storm jib was made of nine-ounce bright fluorescent orange Dacron, with heavy roped luffs and stainless steel pressed eyelets. It was built to endure sixty knots of wind without stretching out of shape.

They lashed the bag securely to the mast pulpits with three turns of quarter-inch Dacron braid, finishing with two round turns and two half-hitches.

"Next, the sea berths," Artie said, dropping below.

He set the lee cloths on both salon settees, hauling the heavy canvas panels taut with their nylon lanyards and tying them off to the overhead teak handrails. In a heavy seaway, an unconstrained sleeper could be thrown across the cabin, breaking ribs or dislocating a shoulder against the opposite joinery.

"Sleep in your sea boots and fleece trousers tonight," Artie instructed Clara. "When the call comes for all hands on deck, you do not want to be looking for socks in the dark."

Clara nodded, stowing her personal gear in the overhead netting. Her movements were calm, deliberate, and without wasted motion. Over ten days at sea, she had shed every trace of shore hesitation. She was now a seaman, hardened to the pitch and yaw of the hull, attuned to every change in the rigging's song.

The preparation of the vessel for storm conditions was carried out with methodical, quiet precision. Nothing on deck was left to chance. Every line, fender, and spare sail had its dedicated lashings, tightened with trucker's hitches and secured with double half-hitches in braided Dacron.

Artie went below to inspect the battery bank beneath the main salon sole. In a severe knockdown or rollover, four eight-D lead-acid batteries weighing one hundred and forty pounds apiece could rip free of inadequate brackets and smash through the cabin joinery, destroying the hull from the inside.

He took a nine-sixteenths socket wrench and checked the steel hold-down rods. Each nut was cinched down over a heavy oak compression block that clamped the battery casings securely into their fiberglass box. He tried to shake the assembly with all his strength. It did not shift a millimeter.

"Batteries are locked down," Artie said, replacing the floorboard and turning the bronze quarter-turn latches.

Noah was working at the nav station, double-checking the emergency grab bag. Inside the waterproof yellow valise were the 406-megahertz EPIRB, the handheld Icom VHF radio with spare lithium batteries, six red parachute flares, a handheld GPS receiver, three thermal protective aids, two pints of emergency water, and the vessel's registration papers sealed in a zip-lock pouch.

"EPIRB battery test passed," Noah reported, pointing to the green flashing LED on the emergency beacon. "Strobe light is operational."

"Lash the grab bag to the companionway ladder," Artie instructed. "If we have to abandon ship in sixty seconds, that bag must be within reach of the first person out of the hatch."

Clara finished securing the galley. The stainless pots were locked into their fiddles with adjustable brass clamps. The salt shaker, oil cruets, and knives were stowed in their custom foam cutouts inside the galley drawers. The shutoff valve on the propane cylinder in the aft deck locker was turned completely off, isolating the gas line at the tank.

The ship was stripped, battened down, and ready to meet the Atlantic gale.

The final check before nightfall was the storm trysail track. The trysail had its own dedicated stainless track bolted alongside the mainmast groove, allowing the heavy orange storm sail to be hoisted without removing the mainsail from the boom.

Artie clipped his safety line to the mast pulpit, working with both hands in the gathering gloom.

He inspected the stainless gate at the base of the track. The knurled brass thumbscrew that held the gate closed was safety-wired to the track extrusion. He untwisted the wire, slid the heavy bronze trysail slides into the track one by one, and ran the gate shut, re-wiring the screw with fresh safety wire.

"Trysail halyard is shackled to the headboard," Noah called from the deck collar.

"Sheet is led through the aft snatch block," Clara confirmed from the cockpit rail. "Both sheet tails are secured to the secondary cockpit winches."

Artie slapped the heavy dacron cloth with his open palm. The sail was small, only one hundred and ten square feet, but the cloth was double-weight orange dacron with triple-stitched seams and leather-reinforced corners. It was a sail designed for survival, built to keep the bow from falling off into the trough when forty knots of wind ripped the tops off the swells.

"The rig is secure," Artie said, uncoupling his tether from the mast rail and making his way aft along the windward deck. "Let the gale come."

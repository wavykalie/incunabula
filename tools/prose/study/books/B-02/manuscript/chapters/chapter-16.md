# Chapter 16: The Confession in the Companionway

The confrontation happened at zero-two-fifteen on the steep companionway steps.

Arthur Vance was coming off his watch, dripping with icy salt brine, when he found Julian Kross sitting on the third step from the top, his knees pulled up to his chin, staring at the darkened saloon below.

Artie did not move past him. He stood on the companionway threshold, towering over the younger man, his yellow oilskins shedding water onto Julian's shoulders.

"Move," Artie said.

Julian did not move. He raised his head, looking up at Artie with eyes that were hollow, sunken, and bloodshot from three days of sleepless terror.

"My real name is Julian Kross," he said. The voice was barely louder than the hum of the engine beneath the floor.

Artie paused. He unclipped his safety line from the bridge deck padeye, stepped down onto the second step, and pulled the washboard shut behind him, sealing the wind outside.

"I know your name," Artie said. "The Coast Guard telex gave it to us yesterday."

"Then why haven't you locked me up?" Julian asked. "Why haven't you turned the boat around?"

"Turn around to where?" Artie said, his voice harsh and dry. "We are seven hundred miles east of Newfoundland. The wind is blowing forty knots from the northwest. If I turn around, I am beating into fifty-foot head seas against a gale. There is no turning around on the North Atlantic, Julian. There is only going forward."

Julian swallowed hard, burying his face in his hands. "Delacroix's cousin died in that offshore supply boat off Shetland. Marcus Ward. He was my second cousin. When Marcus drowned, his family never returned his commercial seaman's discharge book to the MCA in Southampton. It sat in a desk drawer in his mother's house in Portsmouth."

Julian took a ragged, trembling breath.

"I was the senior compliance auditor for Delacroix Maritime Management in Rotterdam," he continued, speaking into his palms. "For four years, I signed off on the internal audit reports. I knew the companies were shell entities. I knew the yacht ownership was routed through nominee trusts in Panama to hide money from international sanctions. I looked the other way because they paid me two hundred thousand euros a year and gave me an apartment on the Maas."

He dropped his hands, looking up at Artie.

"Six months ago, they asked me to sign a falsified valuation for three gas tankers being sold to a shadow fleet operator in Dubai. I refused. Two nights later, someone tried to force the lock on my apartment door while I was asleep. The next morning, my company car was broken into. I knew what was coming. I downloaded the entire server ledger onto three encrypted solid-state drives, took Marcus's old discharge book from Portsmouth, and fled across the Channel to Cherbourg."

"Why this boat?" Artie demanded, his voice cold as ice. "Why Aura VII?"

"Because Delacroix owned it!" Julian cried, his voice breaking with suppressed panic. "I knew the boat was scheduled for a transatlantic delivery to Cannes. I knew the delivery skipper would be hired through an independent agency in London who wouldn't check the credentials beyond the maritime database. I thought... I thought if I was in the middle of the Atlantic on one of his own yachts, his investigators would never look for me here."

He reached out, grabbing the wet fabric of Artie's sea sleeve.

"Captain, you don't understand what he does," Julian pleaded. "He doesn't care about this boat. He doesn't care about the delivery fee. The moment he realized I was on board, he cut our communications to make sure we die out here. He will let all four of us drown in this storm to keep those ledgers from reaching a court!"

Artie looked down at the younger man's trembling hands.

For ten long seconds, the only sound in the cabin was the heavy, rhythmic thud of the Volvo diesel and the groan of the carbon hull as a giant swell passed beneath the keel.

Artie reached down, pried Julian's fingers from his sleeve with calm, unyielding strength, and stepped past him into the saloon.

"I don't care about your European court cases, Julian," Artie said, turning around at the foot of the steps. "And I don't care about Delacroix's shell companies. My job is delivering fifty-four feet of carbon yacht to Horta Marina without losing a spar or a human life."

He unzipped his wet oilskin jacket, hanging it on the brass hook by the engine box.

"You took a watch on this ship under a dead man's name," Artie continued, his gray eyes fixing on Julian like steel rivets. "That makes you a fraud under maritime law. But out here, you are my deckhand. You eat the bread Tom bakes, you stand your four hours at the wheel, and when the gale hits at midnight, you help me rig the storm staysail on the foredeck. If we make landfall in Horta alive, you can take your black case and run wherever you want. But until my lines are tied to the fuel pier in the Azores, you answer to me. Do we understand each other?"

Julian looked at him, tears welling in the corners of his eyes, and nodded once. "Yes, Captain. We understand each other."

"Then get some sleep," Artie said. "You have the helm at zero-four-hundred."

Artie sat on the companionway step, his elbows resting on his knees, watching the younger man's breathing slowly return to a regular cadence.

The saloon below them was dimly lit by the red emergency lamps, casting deep shadows across the teak cabinetry and the charts unrolled on the table. Outside, the wind shrieked through the spreader roots with a rising, predatory whine, the sound of an Atlantic gale gathering its strength for the final assault.

"Why did you think you could cross an ocean, Julian?" Artie asked, his voice softening slightly in the dark. "You are an accountant. You have never stood a night watch in forty knots of wind in your life."

Julian gave a short, bitter laugh that ended in a cough. "I was desperate, Artie. When I left Rotterdam, I knew Delacroix had private security consultants watching the airport in Schiphol and the Eurostar terminal in Brussels. I had six hours before the company realized the server ledgers had been purged. Cherbourg was the only port within driving distance where private delivery yachts cleared for international departures without biometric passport scans."

He rubbed his eyes with his sleeve, leaving dark streaks of grease and salt on his forehead.

"I found the delivery listing on a crew agency board in the marina office," Julian continued. "Aura VII, fifty-four-foot cutter, transatlantic delivery to Cannes, master Arthur Vance. I looked you up on the maritime database from an internet café in town. The records showed twenty-five years of commercial deliveries, zero hull losses, zero safety inquiries."

He looked up at Artie through the dim red light.

"I thought... if I can get on board with a skipper who knows how to cross an ocean without asking questions, I can make landfall in the Azores, take a commuter ferry to Lisbon, and walk into the German embassy before Delacroix even knows which ocean I crossed."

"You made a mistake," Artie said calmly.

"I know," Julian whispered.

"Your mistake wasn't taking the boat," Artie said, his voice hard and firm. "Your mistake was thinking that an ocean is a hiding place. The North Atlantic doesn't care about your ledgers, Julian. It doesn't care about Delacroix or the European courts. Out here, you are either a sailor who pulls his weight, or you are ballast. And ballast gets thrown over the side when the ship takes water."

He stood up, stepping down onto the saloon floor. "Get some sleep, Mr. Ward. Your watch starts at four."

The second bilge pump was a Henderson Mark V diaphragm pump mounted beneath the salon sole. It was operated by a detachable stainless steel handle that slotted into a bronze socket flush with the floorboards.

Artie knelt on the damp teak veneer, fitting the handle into the socket until the detent ball clicked home.

"Stroke it ten times every change of watch," he told Noah. "The automatic float switch in the sump handles the routine seepage, but the Henderson draws from the lowest depression in the garboard strake. It pulls the heavy sediment that clogs the electric impeller."

Noah took the handle, bracing his shoulder against the galley counter. Each pull produced a solid, heavy suction stroke, the neoprene flapper valves slapping against their vulcanized seats with a rhythmic thrum. On the fifth stroke, the discharge hose burped a dark stream of bilgewater mixed with grease and rust flakes through the transom skin fitting.

"Clean water on eight," Noah reported.

"Good," Artie said. "Leave the handle clipped in its spring brackets behind the companionway ladder. In a knockdown, you do not want a twenty-inch steel rod rolling loose across the cabin sole."

He reached into the navigation desk drawer and pulled out the logbook of spare equipment. Every pump had a rebuild kit: spare flapper valves, nitrile diaphragms, and silicone grease in sealed foil envelopes. He checked the dates stamped on the rubber. Three years old, but pliable to the touch.

"Seawater eats nitrile if it sits dry," Artie noted. "Keep a coat of silicone on the spares, or they will crack the first time you put fifty pounds of suction on the lever."

# Chapter 29: Duty to Render Aid

The argument in the saloon lasted less than ninety seconds.

"It is maritime law," Clara said, her voice ringing with the clarity of a ship's officer standing on the bridge. "Title Forty-Six, International Convention on Maritime Search and Rescue. The master of every vessel at sea which is in a position to be able to render assistance on receiving information from any source that persons are in distress at sea is bound to proceed with all speed to their assistance."

"We are not in a position to render assistance!" Julian shouted, his eyes wild, stepping into the center of the cabin. "Look at us! Our fresh water is gone! We are steering with winches! Our skipper has broken ribs! If we turn broadside to fifty-foot seas to run twenty miles across the wind, we will roll the boat and drown ourselves! For what? For people who are already dead?"

"They are not dead," Tom Nowak said, stepping between Julian and the chart table. His gravelly voice was low, heavy, and dangerous. "They are seamen in the water. When a sailor is in the water, you go. You don't ask what the contract says. You don't ask who pays for the diesel. You turn the ship."

Julian spun toward Artie, his hands outstretched in desperate appeal.

"Captain, please! Delacroix's auditors will use this to break your contract! If you deviate twenty miles off your course without Coast Guard orders, the underwriter will declare an unlawful deviation! You will lose your twenty-eight thousand dollars! You will lose your pension!"

Arthur Vance stood with his back braced against the chart table fiddles.

Every breath sent a dull, stabbing tooth of fire through his left side where the broken ribs grated against the adhesive tape. His mouth was dry, tasting of salt and stale codeine.

He looked at Julian Kross.

He saw a young man who had spent four years navigating the legal loopholes of corporate banking, hiding behind offshore trusts, shell entities, and forged discharge books to protect his own skin. A man who believed that survival was a transaction, a calculation of personal advantage weighed against legal risk.

Then Artie looked at his own weathered master's ticket, mounted in its brass frame above the navigation desk: *Arthur Vance, Master of Steam and Motor Vessels, Unlimited Oceans*.

"A pension is just money, Julian," Artie said. His voice was soft, barely rising above the whistle of the gale outside, but it carried an authority that silenced the cabin instantly. "Money is something shore people invent to keep score. But out here, on this water, there is only one law that matters. You don't leave people to die in the cold."

He turned to Clara.

"Calculate the intercept vector," Artie ordered. "Bearing one-six-five true. What course do we steer to take the seas on our quarter?"

Clara's fingers flew across the paper chart with her parallel rule. "If we steer one-four-zero magnetic, the wind is forty degrees on our port beam. The swell will be on the forward quarter. It will be brutal, Artie. The hull will slam with every wave."

"We can carry it," Artie said.

He looked at Tom Nowak.

"Tom, get on deck. Relieve the port winch. Julian, you take the starboard winch. Clara, you take the lookout in the companionway with the night-vision glasses. When I give the command, we gybe the staysail and bring her head around sixty degrees to starboard."

Julian looked at Artie for three seconds, his chest rising and falling in rapid, shallow breaths. The terror was still in his eyes, but something else had entered his expression: a profound, humbling realization that the world did not revolve around his stolen ledgers or his European arrest warrants.

"Yes, Captain," Julian whispered.

He pulled his hood over his head, grabbed his heavy gloves from the heater rack, and climbed the companionway ladder into the screaming black night.

Five minutes later, *Aura VII* executed the most dangerous maneuver of her crossing.

Under Artie's quiet, iron commands, the four crew members coordinated their actions with flawless mechanical synchronization. As a giant wave lifted the stern, Tom ground the port winch, Clara eased the starboard line, and Artie backed the tiny orange staysail across the foredeck.

The yacht surged through thirty-five-foot seas, turning her bow into the cross-swell, water exploding across the deck in blinding white geysers as she headed south-southeast toward the distress datum.

Daylight returned as a pale, slate-gray smear along the eastern horizon. The cloud deck remained solid, but the rain had stopped, and the wind had settled into a steady thirty-five-knot blow, Force eight, clean and brisk from the north.

Artie hauled the drogue aboard by hand with the primary sheet winch. It took Noah forty-five minutes of steady cranking to retrieve the three hundred feet of line, winding the heavy wet braid around the aluminum drum while Artie flaked the small fabric cones into an empty plastic crate. Each cone came out of the water dripping brine, its seams tight, its nylon cords undamaged.

"That gear saved our rudder," Noah said, wiping his brow with the back of his hand. His shoulders were trembling from the effort of the winch.

"It kept our stern to the sea," Artie agreed. "A boat that broaches in forty-five knots rolls her spreaders in the water. Once you roll forty-five degrees, the rig comes down, and then you are just a raft waiting for a freighter to pick you up."

Clara brought up a loaf of brown bread from the galley and cut three thick slices on the cockpit seat, spreading each with salted butter and sharp cheddar. The bread was dry, but the butter tasted rich and clean.

"I checked the water tanks," she said between bites. "The forward tank has eighty gallons left. The port tank is empty, probably vented overboard when we were heeled forty degrees yesterday. That gives us ten days of drinking water at half a gallon per person per day."

"We will not need ten days," Artie said, pulling his charting points from his breast pocket and laying them across the plastic chart case. "We are two hundred and forty miles west of Faial. At six knots under the staysail and reefed main, we will raise the island in forty hours."

He laid his pencil on the chart, drawing a straight pencil line from their dead reckoning position to the safe water buoy at the entrance to Horta harbor. The line cut clean across the Azores bank, clear of the offshore banks and banks of shoals south of Flores.

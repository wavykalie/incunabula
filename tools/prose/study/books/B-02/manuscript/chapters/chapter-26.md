# Chapter 26: The Broken Rib

The second knockdown occurred at ten-fifteen, while they were changing the steering watch.

Julian Kross had just climbed through the companionway hatch to relieve Clara at the starboard winch. He had his safety tether clipped to the bridge deck padeye, his hands reaching for the winch handle, when a rogue crest rose out of the white foam directly on the starboard beam.

It was an unseen sea, a freak pyramid of water kicked up by the interference of two wave trains colliding over the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

The wave did not roll under the hull; it broke directly over the coachroof with the weight of forty tons of tumbling water.

The yacht was rolled past seventy degrees in a split second.

The force of the impact ripped Arthur Vance's hands from the port winch drum. He was thrown violently across the eight-foot width of the cockpit, his body hurtling through the air like a rag doll before slamming hard against the heavy aluminum binnacle coaming on the starboard side.

A sickening, audible crunch echoed through Artie's chest, accompanied by a blinding flash of white agony that drove all the air from his lungs.

He collapsed onto the flooded cockpit floorboards, curled on his right side, unable to draw breath, his vision swimming with dark gray spots.

"Artie!" Clara screamed from the companionway threshold.

The yacht was wallowing on her beam-ends, the starboard rudder completely out of the water, the bare spars shuddering violently as thirty tons of green water poured across the deck.

Without a second of hesitation, Clara Lindholm launched herself across the cockpit sole.

She did not stop to check on Artie. Her racing navigator training took over with cold, ruthless, life-saving clarity. If the yacht remained broadside to the seas for another five seconds, the next wave would roll them completely upside down, turning forty tons of carbon into an inverted tomb.

She grabbed the starboard steering line, threw three turns around the self-tailing winch, and yelled at Julian: "Grind port! Now! Grind it to the stop!"

Julian threw his weight onto the port winch handle, his bleeding hands working the aluminum crank with desperate, manic fury.

Clara heaved on the starboard line, hauling the emergency tiller across the quadrant with all the strength in her back and shoulders.

The rudder bit deep into the green water.

Aura VII pivoted on her keel, her plumb bow slicing through the wave crest, turning her transom square to the following seas once more. The hull popped upright, the water draining through the open aft cockpit with a deep, gurgling roar.

Clara stood at the helm station, her boots planted wide, her hands holding the steering lines with absolute command.

"Julian!" she barked, her voice cutting through the gale like a siren. "Get Artie below! Now!"

Julian knelt beside the fallen skipper.

Artie was gasping in short, shallow wheezes, his face the color of wet parchment, his left hand clutched tight against his lower left ribcage. A dark line of blood was trickling from his nose where his face had struck the fiberglass.

"Don't... don't let her round up," Artie whispered, the words bubbling through his teeth in agonizing fragments.

"She's on course, Artie," Julian said gently, unhooking Artie's safety tether and looping his own arm beneath the older man's shoulders. "Clara has the helm. She has the boat. Come on, Captain. Let me take your weight."

Tomasz Nowak appeared in the companionway opening, his scarred hands reaching up to take Artie by the armpits.

Between the two of them, moving with infinite care against the violent rolling of the yacht, they lowered Artie down the companionway steps and laid him flat on the saloon settee.

Tom cut away Artie's yellow oilskin jacket with his rigging shears, peeling back the sodden thermal fleece beneath.

Over the ninth and tenth ribs on Artie's left side, the skin was already swollen into a dark, purplish-black ridge the size of an orange. When Tom touched the edge of the bruise with his fingertips, the broken ends of the bone shifted with a distinct, grinding crunch beneath the skin.

Artie groaned, his head falling back against the cushion, his fists clenching into the upholstery.

"Two ribs broken clean," Tom said, looking at Julian. "The ninth and tenth. The lung is not punctured; the breath sounds are clear on both sides. But if he moves, the bone can tear the pleura."

Tom reached into the ship's medical kit, pulling out a six-inch roll of heavy elastic adhesive strapping and a sterile roll of cotton padding.

"Breathe out, Artie," Tom commanded, his voice gentle and gravelly. "Breathe all the air out of your lungs and hold it."

Artie exhaled in a long, shuddering hiss.

Tom wrapped the heavy adhesive strapping tight around Artie's chest, binding the broken ribs firmly against his ribcage with three overlapping layers of tape that restricted his breathing to shallow, measured movements.

Artie lay on the settee, his eyes closed, his chest rising and falling in tight, shallow beats. The pain was an iron band clamped around his torso, but his mind remained clear and cold.

Through the companionway hatch, he could hear the steady, rhythmic grind of the cockpit winches and Clara's voice shouting steering commands to Julian above the roar of the gale.

The ship was in good hands.

Inside the main salon, the roar of the gale was muted to a low, relentless rumble, like an iron train crossing an endless trestle. The motion had changed from a violent roll to a slow, deliberate heave. Each wave lifted the stern eight, ten, twelve feet, held the vessel for three seconds on the boiling crest, and then passed beneath her keel, setting her down gently in the flat slick behind.

Artie wiped the salt from his eyelashes with a dry towel. The cabin was damp, every surface carrying a thin film of condensation that condensed from their breath against the cold fiberglass.

"Bilge pump counter says twelve cycles in the last hour," Clara said, checking the digital readout above the navigation table. "That is sixty gallons. Mostly through the rudder shaft seal."

Artie took his flashlight and opened the aft cabin inspection hatch. He shone the beam onto the rudder tube. With every surge, a small jet of seawater pulsed past the lip seal where the stainless stock entered the bronze housing. It was not a catastrophic breach, but a persistent leak under thirty tons of hydraulic pressure.

"The packing nut needs half a turn," Artie said.

He retrieved the large bronze spanner from the tool locker, knelt in the cramped space beside the quarter berth, and set the jaws over the hex nut. He braced his foot against the steering quadrant and leaned his weight into the wrench. The bronze turned a quarter of an inch with a dry squeak. The water jet dwindled to a steady trickle, pooling harmlessly in the aft bilge sump where the secondary pump could reach it.

"That will hold," he muttered, crawling back into the main salon.

Noah had boiled a kettle of water on the gimbaled stove, his hand holding the stainless pot level as the stove swung thirty degrees through its arc. He poured three mugs of instant coffee, stirring in generous spoons of sweetened condensed milk.

"Good coffee," Artie said, taking the mug. The hot ceramic warmed his stiff palms. "We have seventy miles of sea room before we reach the western shoals. We hold this drift until the wind backs into the north."

Water entered the cabin not in torrents, but in cold, creeping drips that penetrated every uncaulked fastener and deck fitting under the continuous assault of the gale.

Artie made the rounds of the forward cabin with a roll of paper towels and a bucket. The forward Lewmar hatch had taken a direct hit from a breaking crest ten minutes earlier. A tablespoon of seawater had forced its way past the neoprene gasket, dripping onto the forward v-berth cushion.

Artie stripped the cushion cover, wrapped the foam core in a heavy plastic trash liner, and zipped the cover back in place.

"Keep the forward cabin doors latched," he said, stepping into the head compartment.

He checked the marine toilet seacocks. Both the three-quarter-inch intake and the inch-and-a-half discharge ball valves were closed tight, their bronze handles locked in the perpendicular position. An open head seacock in a knockdown could siphon twenty tons of water into the hull in less than an hour.

In the salon, Noah was sitting on the leeward settee, his boots braced against the centerline drop-leaf table. The motion was deep and relentless, lifting them toward the cabin roof and dropping them down with a stomach-hollowing elevator plunge.

"Barometer has stopped falling," Noah called out, shining his headlamp at the dial. "It has been stuck on nine-seventy-eight for forty minutes."

"The trough is passing over us right now," Artie replied. "In an hour, the wind will cut out, and then it will hit us from the northwest with double the velocity. Get twenty minutes of sleep now, Noah. When the shift comes, I will need you on the mainsheet."

Noah leaned his head back against the vinyl cushion and closed his eyes. Within thirty seconds, his breathing slowed to a deep, rhythmic cadence, sleeping as sailors sleep, resting between waves.

The motion of the hull under the series drogue was a testament to the design of the Swedish builders. While the wind howled outside with terrifying ferocity, the boat did not pitch violently or slam her flat sections into the sea. She was held by her stern, her fine double-ended run splitting the water cleanly and allowing the monstrous swells to pass beneath her keel with a slow, powerful rise and fall.

Artie stood at the base of the companionway, holding the grab rail with both hands. He checked the digital log repeater mounted on the bulkhead.

"Drift speed is two point two knots," he observed. "Course over ground is one-hundred-twenty degrees true. The drogue is doing its work."

Noah sat on the floorboards with his back against the salon table leg, his headlamp shining on a copy of the Volvo Penta engine manual. He was studying the fuel delivery schematic, tracing the flow from the primary fuel tank through the mechanical lift pump and into the high-pressure injector lines.

"The secondary filter has a micron rating of two," Noah said, pointing to the diagram. "If the fuel in the main tank was stirred up by this rolling, the asphaltites and sediment from the tank bottom will load the filter element within ten engine hours."

"That is why we change filters the moment we restart," Artie replied. "We let the sediment settle while we drift. When the gale blows out, we drain the bottom sump of the fuel tank through the water separator valve before we crank the starter."

Clara emerged from the aft cabin, holding a thermos of hot bouillon she had made on the gimbaled stove before the worst of the crests hit. She poured three plastic cups, the salty, savory broth filling the damp cabin with a comforting aroma.

"Drink," she urged them. "It replaces the salt you lose in sweat."

They drank in silence, the steam rising around their tired faces. Outside, another monster swell lifted the transom, held them suspended thirty feet above the dark troughs, and then gently lowered them into the smooth, hissing slick behind the crest. The boat shuddered, shed the water from her scuppers, and held her head straight.

The second hour of the storm center brought the heaviest water of the passage. A breaking wave broke squarely over the starboard quarter, slamming against the acrylic dodger with the force of a hydraulic ram. The canvas frame groaned under the impact, but the stainless tubing held firm.

Inside the cabin, the sound was like being struck by a falling log.

Artie was at the bilge inspection hatch before the foam had cleared from the deck. He pulled the center hatch board up and shone his light into the dark keel cavity.

The primary Rule four-thousand pump was running at full capacity, its twelve-volt motor humming with high-pitched intensity as it threw forty gallons a minute out the hull side.

"Float switch triggered," Artie shouted up to Noah. "Water is dropping in the sump. The limber holes are clear."

Noah looked down through the companionway hatch, his face dripping with salt spray. "Did the rudder seal fail?"

"No," Artie called back, checking the stuffing box with his hand. The flax packing around the bronze shaft was warm and moist, with only a small trickle running down the bronze casting. "The dodger took fifty gallons of green water down the companionway louver when the crest broke over the coaming. The scuppers cleared the rest within four seconds."

He dried the salon floor with a heavy natural sponge, wringing the salt water into a plastic galley bucket.

"Good boat," Artie muttered to himself, checking the structural tabbing where the main bulkhead joined the fiberglass hull. The resin fillets were smooth and intact, with not a hairline crack visible in the white gelcoat. "Built for heavy water."

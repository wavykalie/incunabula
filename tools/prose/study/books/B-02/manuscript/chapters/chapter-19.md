# Chapter 19: Rations and Rationing

By daylight on the eighth morning, the saloon of Aura VII had been converted into a survival station.

Arthur Vance sat at the table with the vessel's official provisioning manifest laid out beside his logbook. Across from him, Tomasz Nowak was inventorying every edible item remaining on board, arranging the cans and dry packets in neat, measured rows along the settee cushions.

The loss of the freshwater tanks had changed the arithmetic of the crossing from a commercial delivery into a strict endurance calculation.

"Forty cans of tuna and sardines," Tom reported, his gravelly voice methodically listing the calories. "Twelve cans of chopped tomatoes. Five kilograms of dried navy beans, but we cannot cook beans without fresh water to boil them. Three kilograms of rolled oats. Four packages of dry pilot biscuits. Six jars of peanut butter."

"What about diesel for the stove?" Artie asked.

"The stove runs on propane," Tom said. "Two full bottles left in the lazarette. We have gas for twenty days. But the water is the wall, Artie. Julian pumped for two hours this morning. His shoulders are cramping, and he produced eight liters of drinking water. It tastes like rubber and plastic from the hoses, but the salinity tester shows zero point zero two percent. It is drinkable."

"Eight liters a day is enough," Artie said, writing the numbers in the log. "Two liters per person per day. One liter for drinking, one liter for Tom to make oatmeal and hot tea for the watch. Nobody washes with fresh water. Nobody brushes their teeth with fresh water. We conserve every drop."

Clara came down the steps, wearing her heavy wool watch cap. Her lips were cracked from the salt air, her eyes shadowed with dark rings.

"The wind is building again," she reported, leaning against the companionway post. "Thirty-four knots from two-nine-zero. The barometer is holding at nine-nine-eight, but the swell from the west has increased to four meters."

Artie looked down at the paper chart.

Their position was approximately forty-five degrees north, forty-two degrees west. They had crossed the halfway mark between Newfoundland and the Azores, with nine hundred twenty nautical miles remaining to the harbor entrance at Horta.

"At eight knots, nine hundred miles is one hundred twelve hours," Artie said. "Four and a half days of sailing. If the wind stays behind us, we can make Horta by Thursday evening."

"If the wind stays behind us," Clara echoed calmly. "Look at the western horizon through the hatch, Artie. The clouds are not standard maritime stratus. They are altocumulus castellanus, building in vertical towers. That means severe atmospheric instability in the upper levels."

Julian Kross emerged from the forward passage, his face drenched in sweat despite the cold cabin air. His forearms were shaking with muscle fatigue from two hours on the manual desalinator pump. He held a three-liter plastic jug filled with cloudy, lukewarm water.

He set the jug on the table before Artie like a man placing gold on an altar.

"Three liters, skipper," Julian said, his breath coming in ragged gasps. "The membrane... the membrane is stiff. It takes sixty pounds of pressure on the lever for every stroke."

Artie took the jug, uncapped it, poured half a cup into a tin mug, and drank. The water was lukewarm and tasted heavily of chemical plasticizer and stale rubber, but it was pure, sweet, and free of salt.

He handed the mug back to Julian. "Drink half of it. You earned it."

Julian took the mug with trembling hands, drinking the water in three greedy swallows. He wiped his chin with his sleeve, looking at Artie with a quiet, exhausted dignity that had not been there in St. John's.

"I can do another two hours after my watch," Julian said.

"One hour at a time," Artie told him. "We rotate the pump between all four of us. Two hours per person per day. Nobody burns their arms out. We have four days of heavy sailing ahead, and I need every hand capable of grinding a winch on deck."

Tom sliced a loaf of dense, heavy rye bread into four equal pieces, placing a tin of sardines beside each portion. "Breakfast is served. Eat every crumb. In two days, when the front passes, you won't be able to keep a tin on the table."

They sat together on the saloon settees, four strangers bound by sixty feet of carbon fiber and eighteen liters of water, eating their measured rations in silence as the North Atlantic surged beneath their feet.

The rationing schedule was written in indelible black marker on the white plastic laminate of the galley bulkhead, directly above the gas stove.

Tom Nowak had divided their remaining supplies into sixteen numbered columns, representing the maximum number of days remaining before landfall in Horta under the worst-case sailing scenario.

"Two liters of fresh water per man, per twenty-four hours," Tom stated, pointing to the column with his wooden spoon. "One liter for drinking, distributed in individual marked bottles at dawn. One liter pooled for the galley: half a liter for morning porridge, half a liter for evening soup or tea."

Julian Kross stood beside the stove, holding his assigned plastic water bottle. He had drawn a line with his pencil halfway down the label: *MARCUS - 1000 ML*.

"What about food?" Julian asked.

"Food is calories, not luxury," Tom replied, his gravelly voice practical and calm. "Morning: forty grams of rolled oats cooked in water, one spoon of peanut butter for fat. Noon: two dry pilot biscuits with half a tin of sardines. Evening: boiled navy beans with canned tomatoes and salt beef broth. Total calories: approximately eighteen hundred per day."

"Eighteen hundred calories is enough to sustain a man at sea," Artie Vance said, stepping down into the saloon from his morning deck inspection. "You will feel hungry, your stomach will rumble, but your muscles will work and your brain will stay clear. The enemy out here is not hunger; it is fatigue and carelessness."

He sat down at the table, checking the logbook entries against Clara's navigation calculations.

"We have made two hundred forty miles in the last thirty hours," Artie reported. "Our average speed is eight knots. If the wind holds in the west, we will be within three hundred miles of Faial by Wednesday evening."

"And if the wind dies?" Clara asked from the chart desk.

"If the wind dies, we have eighty liters of clean diesel in the deck jerry-cans," Artie said. "At twelve hundred RPM, the Volvo burns one point eight liters an hour and gives us five point five knots of boat speed. Eighty liters gives us forty-four hours of motoring, or two hundred forty nautical miles. We have enough fuel to reach the island under power from seventy miles out."

Tom nodded, sliding a tin of sardines across the table to Julian. "Eat your fish, boy. Protein for the winches. In forty-eight hours, you will need every muscle in your back."

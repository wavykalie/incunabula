# Chapter 05: The Southampton Telex

At zero-six-hundred, the sky was the color of a wet slate shingle.

Arthur Vance sat at the navigation desk with a tin mug of cold tea resting between his palms. His sea boots were wet inside the ankles where an icy wave had curled over the cockpit coaming during his watch, soaking through the neoprene seals. The leather of the soles squeaked against the varnished floorboards whenever he shifted his weight against the steep heel of the hull.

Above the desk, the Furuno satellite receiver gave a sharp, mechanical chime.

The dot-matrix impact printer mounted into the teak cabinetry gave a short, stuttering whirr, followed by the dry screech of tractor-feed paper advancing two inches through the platen. The inked ribbon had begun to dry out along its lower edge, leaving the bottom halves of the capital letters faint and gray against the cheap roll paper.

Artie leaned forward, bracing his forearms on the chart fiddles. Clara Lindholm, who had just come off the helm, stood behind his shoulder, water dripping from the brim of her oilskin hood onto the green linoleum floor. Her breath came in short, tired puffs of white vapor in the unheated saloon.

The printout was brief, formatted in international maritime telegraphic code:

MSG REF: 2026/10/24-0612Z
FROM: OCEAN ROUTING SERVICES SOUTHAMPTON
TO: S/Y AURA VII / CALLSIGN V7QR2
ATTN: MASTER

WEATHER ADVISORY 03 // NORTH ATLANTIC SECTOR FOUR
EXPLOSIVE CYCLOGENESIS CONFIRMED VICINITY 46N 48W.
CENTRAL PRESSURE ESTIMATED 972 HPA AND DEEPENING RAPIDLY.
SYSTEM MOVING EAST-NORTHEAST AT 32 KNOTS.
WINDS SUSTAINED 50-55 KNOTS, GUSTS EXCEEDING 70 KNOTS.
SIGNIFICANT WAVE HEIGHTS 9-11 METRES DEVELOPING OVER GREAT CIRCLE ROUTE.

ADVICE: DIVERT IMMEDIATELY. ALTER HEADING TO 120 DEGREES TRUE.
RUN SOUTH TO LATITUDE 41N BEFORE TURNING EAST TO BYPASS
DANGEROUS SEMICIRCLE. ACKNOWLEDGE VIA SHORE AGENT.

The printer gave a final mechanical click and stopped.

Clara reached past Artie's shoulder, tore the strip of paper from the perforations, and smoothed it flat on the desk. Her fingers were red and stiff from the morning wind, the skin peeling slightly around her thumb nails.

"Southampton is professional," she said, her voice tight with controlled urgency. "They don't use the word 'immediate' unless their satellite scatterometer data confirms the fetch. A nine-meter significant wave height means the maximum crests will hit fifteen meters. In shallow shelf water, that is breaking crests, Artie. Not rolling swells. Vertical walls of water."

Artie picked up his brass dividers. He swung the legs across the Admiralty routing chart, measuring the distance from their current position to forty-one degrees north. The brass points tapped against the paper with a dull, hollow rhythm.

"If we alter course to one-two-zero," Artie said, "we head directly across the shipping lanes toward the Gulf Stream. It adds four hundred eighty nautical miles to the crossing."

"It keeps us out of the dangerous semicircle," Clara replied sharply. "A fifty-four-foot carbon yacht cannot survive forty-five-foot breaking seas on a broad reach without a parachute drogue. If we get rolled, the rig comes down. Carbon masts don't bend like aluminum; they shatter at the goose-neck."

"And the fuel?" Artie asked, tapping the pencil against the chart. "We have three hundred liters left in the main tank, and Tom says the fuel is taking on salt through the vent line. If we divert five hundred miles south, we enter the light air zone behind the depression. We will have to motor for four days through rolling swells. When the tanks run dry, we are drifting five hundred miles off the Azores with no auxiliary power."

"Then we sail," she said.

"Sailing a racing cutter in light air with a dirty bottom and four crew members on four-hour watches takes twelve days," Artie said. "Look at the calendar, Clara. If we arrive in Horta past the sixteen-day contract deadline, the penalty clause eats the delivery fee at twelve hundred dollars a day. By day nineteen, I owe the broker money for delivering his boat."

Clara stepped back, her mouth setting into a thin, bitter line. "So that's the calculation. Your pension against forty-foot seas."

"My calculation is the whole ship," Artie said, his voice dropping into the flat, dangerous calm he used when harbor pilots tried to bully him into bad berths. "A skipper who runs from every gale ends up in Bermuda with no fuel, no money, and a crew that wants to sue him for lost wages. We have forty tons of structural pre-preg carbon under us. We have three reefs in the mainsail and a heavy storm staysail. We don't run five hundred miles out of our way until we see what the barometer does at noon."

Before Clara could answer, Julian Kross stepped down the companionway steps. He had removed his foul-weather jacket, wearing a thick navy wool sweater that was slightly too large across his narrow shoulders. He held a small waterproof notebook in his hand, a spiral-bound pad with waterproof synthetic paper.

He looked at the torn telex strip in Clara's hand. His eyes widened slightly, shifting between the printed paper and the dark companionway hatch.

"Is that from Monaco?" he asked.

"It is from the weather router in Southampton," Clara said, turning toward him with cold hostility. "They advise us to turn south because there is a storm system coming that will tear the sails off this boat."

Ward looked from Clara to Artie. His fingers nervously turned the brass ring of his wristwatch, twisting the bezel back and forth with a faint click-click-click. "Can the router tell who is on board? Does the message name the crew?"

Artie looked up at the younger man. The question was unnatural for a deckhand. A sailor asks how hard the wind will blow and whether the hatches are dogged down; he does not ask if a weather advisory lists the names on the ship's articles.

"It is an open broadcast," Artie said. "Addressed to the vessel callsign. Nobody in Southampton cares who is sleeping in the bunks."

Ward let out a slow, shallow breath, nodding to himself as if confirming an internal equation. "Right. Of course. Just the weather."

He sat down on the settee, opened his notebook, and carefully wrote down the coordinates from the Furuno screen: forty-seven degrees eight minutes north, fifty degrees forty-four minutes west. Beside the numbers, he noted the barometric pressure and the time in UTC.

Artie watched him through the corner of his eye. The handwriting in the notebook was not the loose, sprawling script of an offshore mariner who writes while balancing against a bulkhead. It was the precise, compact printing of an auditor, the numbers aligned in vertical columns with ruler-straight margins.

The yacht slammed into another steep crest. A shower of cold salt water rattled against the saloon deckhouse like gravel thrown against a wooden fence. From the galley came the sound of Tom sliding an iron pot across the gas ring, humming a melancholy Polish hymn that rose and fell with the vibration of the propeller shaft.

Artie turned back to the paper chart. He drew a small pencil square around their estimated noon position.

Outside, the wind gusted to thirty-four knots, the staysail sheet humming like a cello string against the deck block. To the east lay twelve hundred miles of empty ocean, and every mile was going to cost sweat, fuel, and nerve.

Artie picked up his grease pencil, walking across to the companionway coaming to update the deck fix. The numbers read clearly under the glow of the red binnacle light: heading zero-eight-two, speed eight point four, barometer ten-twelve.

In the forward cabin, Julian Kross was lying in his bunk, staring at the white vinyl deckhead. His duffel bag was wedged between his legs, the hard corners of the black Pelican case pressing through the canvas against his shins.

Every time the hull slammed into a wave trough, the impact vibrated through Julian's ribs. He could hear the rush of water along the outside of the hull, six inches from his ear, a terrifying torrent of cold salt ocean separated from his body by only twenty millimeters of cured epoxy resin and carbon weave.

He thought of the clean, carpeted corridors of Delacroix Maritime in Rotterdam. The quiet hum of the air conditioning, the polished glass partitions, the espresso machines in the break rooms, and the soft-spoken executives who signed offshore charter contracts that sent old, uninspected tankers into pirate-infested waters without a second thought.

Out here, there were no carpets, no glass partitions, and no executive bonuses. There was only thirty feet of cold ocean water and an exhausted delivery skipper who checked his fuel filters with a glass jar every two hours.

Julian reached into his pocket, his fingers finding the cold brass shape of Marcus Ward's discharge book.

He had never met Marcus. He knew only that Marcus had died in the freezing water off Shetland, swept off the deck of an offshore supply boat while trying to secure a loose anchor chain in fifty-knot gales. Marcus had died for two thousand pounds a month, and now his name was signed on a Canadian customs manifest, carrying a fugitive auditor across the Atlantic.

Julian turned his face to the cabin wall, pulled his damp blanket over his ears, and listened to the endless, rhythmic hiss of the sea.

# Chapter 03: The First Siphon

The change of the midnight watch brought the cold inside the boat like water pouring through an open port.

Arthur Vance woke ten minutes before zero-hundred to the sound of the galley alarm clock buzzing against the teak shelf above his head. He swung his feet down onto the floorboards, planting them wide against the steep twenty-degree heel of the hull. The cabin smelled of damp fleece, diesel oil, and the sharp mineral tang of salt drying on Gore-Tex.

He pulled on his heavy wool socks, his mid-layer thermal trousers, and his sea boots. His left ankle throbbed with a dull, familiar ache where the metal pins joined the fibula. In cold seawater, the metal seemed to hold the chill longer than the bone around it.

He stepped into the main saloon. The red night-vision LED strips cast a dim copper wash across the navigation station and the galley counter. Tomasz Nowak was asleep on the leeward settee, strapped in with a canvas lee cloth, his heavy breathing steady beneath a wool blanket.

At the chart table, Clara Lindholm was already dressed in her offshore salopettes, her safety line clipped across her chest. She had a vacuum flask of hot water wedged between two wooden fiddles, slowly pouring it over a mug of black tea.

"Wind is up," she said, her voice quiet in the darkened cabin. "Twenty-seven knots from three-one-zero. Barometer ten-twelve. It fell two millibars since twenty-one hundred."

Artie glanced at the digital barograph readout above the desk. The trend line had a gentle downward curve, nothing alarming yet, but the steady descent of a typical North Atlantic autumn depression sweeping out of the Gulf of St. Lawrence.

"Engine running clean?" he asked.

"Tom checked the primary Racor filter at twenty-two hundred," Clara said, taking a sip from her stainless mug. "No water in the glass bowl. Temperature holding at eighty-two Celsius. Speed over ground eight point six knots." She reached out and touched the glass screen of her laptop. "I checked the delivery contract while you were off watch, Artie."

Artie paused, his hand halfway into the sleeve of his heavy foul-weather smock. "The contract is locked in the drawer, Clara."

"The paper copy is in the drawer. The broker emailed a PDF duplicate to my racing team account three weeks ago when he was scouting for a navigator with Category One offshore credentials." She turned her head toward him, her pale eyes catching the red glow of the instrument panel. "He didn't just offer the day rate. He included the performance schedule."

Artie zipped the heavy plastic zipper up to his chin. "Performance schedules are standard broker boilerplate."

"Not this one," Clara said evenly. "There is a twenty-thousand-euro bonus if Aura VII arrives at Horta within fourteen days of clearing St. John's. That requires an average speed of eight point eight knots over the entire rhumb line."

"Eight point eight knots is racing speed," Artie said, pulling his hood over his head. "This is a delivery, not the Fastnet. We don't carry a ten-man racing crew to grind winches and stack sail bags on the windward rail every four hours."

"The broker told me the owner wants the boat in the Mediterranean before the winter slip rates lock in at Monaco," Clara continued. "He said the skipper who takes the northern great circle track can easily make fourteen days if he keeps the big reacher up in the gales."

Artie stepped closer to the chart table, keeping his voice low so as not to wake the sleeping engineer. "The northern great circle runs through fifty-three north. In late October, that is the iceberg limit south of Cape Farewell. It is sixty-knot gales with forty-foot seas. If we blow out a mainsail or split a rudder stock up there, there is no Coast Guard helicopter coming out of St. John's to lift us off. The Canadian rescue squadrons don't have the fuel range past three hundred miles."

Clara looked at him steadily. "I know the range of the Cormorant helicopters, Artie. I sat through two weeks of inquiry hearings after the Vanguard went down off Fastnet. The lawyers spent forty hours debating whether six minutes of delay in launching the liferaft constituted gross negligence on the part of the watch officer."

She stood up, buckling her safety knife to her webbed belt.

"The inquiry cleared me on the paper, but every racing syndicate in France canceled my contract," she said, her voice hard as carbon rod. "They don't care that the skipper was drunk on prescription sleeping pills in the aft cabin. They only care that my signature was on the deck log at the moment of impact. I need sixteen clean ocean days, Artie. I need a clean endorsement from an accredited delivery master saying Clara Lindholm navigated an offshore passage without an incident or a delay."

"You'll get your endorsement," Artie said. "If we deliver the hull in one piece."

"Then don't run south into the horse latitudes to avoid a breeze," she said. "If you divert south of the Azores high, you add five hundred miles to the crossing. The fuel won't last, and we'll be beating against easterlies for three weeks."

"I don't run from weather that the boat can carry," Artie said. "I run from stupidity. There is a difference."

He unclipped the companionway washboard and slid the hatch open.

A blast of cold, wet air hit them both. The sea was running high and black, streaked with broad ribbons of white foam that glowed faintly under the overcast sky. Aura VII was surging down the face of a nine-foot swell, the spray from her plumb bow exploding into a broad white fan that swept across the foredeck.

Julian Kross was sitting behind the twin carbon wheels, tethered to the cockpit padeye with a heavy nylon jackline. His shoulders were hunched against the spray, his face pale under the sprayhood.

Artie climbed into the cockpit, clipped his own safety tether to the centerline jackstay, and sat on the windward bench.

"Report," Artie said.

Ward swallowed, wiping salt crust from his eyelashes. "Wind three-zero-five, gusting thirty-two. Speed eight point nine. Course zero-eight-five magnetic. The helm is heavy when the quartering swell lifts the stern. I have to apply half a turn of opposite rudder to keep her from broaching."

Artie reached out and took the spokes of the carbon wheel from Ward's hands. He felt the hydraulic resistance of the twin spades beneath the hull. The water pressure was immense, vibrating through the carbon linkage with the taut, springy tension of a loaded bowstring.

"Feel the wave with your knees before it hits the transom," Artie told him. "When the stern lifts, don't wait for the compass to turn. Head off two degrees into the trough, then bring her back as the crest passes underneath. You are steering the hull, not chasing the digital readout."

Ward stepped back, his breath coming in short, visible puffs of vapor. "She feels like she wants to spin."

"She is a racing boat," Clara said from the companionway opening. "She wants to plane. If you pinch her, she trips over her own keel."

Ward did not answer her. He unclipped his safety line from the padeye, his fingers trembling slightly with cold or exhaustion, and moved toward the companionway hatch.

"Get below," Artie told him. "Tom left hot tea in the thermos. Strip your wet gear in the shower stall, not on the floorboards. Dry bunks are the only thing that keeps a crew alive on this ocean."

Ward nodded once, his face tight and defensive, and vanished belowdecks.

Artie stood at the wheel, bracing his boots against the cockpit foot-chocks. The wind blew steady and cold off the port quarter, thirty knots of Arctic air rolling off the Labrador ice shelf. Above them, the small Dacron staysail pulled with the rigid curve of sheet iron, driving the fifty-four-foot carbon knife through the dark North Atlantic night. He watched the white crests rise up behind the quarter, studying their shape and rhythm. Out here, on the open water, there was no court of appeals, no insurance broker, and no second chance. The wheel moved four inches in his hands, answering the sea with quiet, practiced authority.

Artie stepped forward to the companionway binnacle, checking the magnetic steering compass against the digital mast readout. The liquid inside the glass bowl was crystal clear, the white compass card floating steady on its sapphire jewel.

In the galley, Tom Nowak had left a pot of salted cabbage soup simmering on the low flame of the gas burner. The aroma of boiled pork bone, bay leaves, and black peppercorns warmed the damp interior of the saloon. Artie ladled half a bowl into a stainless cup, eating the hot broth with a short pewter spoon as he watched the digital anemometer.

Clara came down the ladder, brushing salt rime from her eyebrows with the back of her glove. She unclipped her safety line from the companionway ring, hanging the wet nylon tether on the brass drying hook beside the engine box.

"Wind is steady at twenty-eight knots," Clara said, checking the barograph trend line on her screen. "The barometer is ten-ten now. It fell another two millibars over the last four hours. The center of the low is deepening faster than the Halifax model predicted."

"We hold our heading," Artie said, wiping the spoon clean. "The boat is balanced. The staysail is drawing clean. There is no reason to alter course until the wind shifts into the west."

"The wind will shift at dawn," Clara said, leaning over the chart table with a pair of brass parallel rules. "When the cold front passes, the wind will jump forty degrees to the right in ten minutes. If we don't have the third reef tied in the mainsail before the shift hits, the shock load will lay the mast in the water."

"We will tie the reef at zero-four-hundred," Artie told her. "When Tom comes on watch. We do it together, with two hands at the mast winch and two hands in the cockpit. Nobody works on the boom alone in the dark."

Clara looked at him, her pale eyes reflecting the red glow of the instrument lights. For the first time since leaving Newfoundland, her expression softened from professional hostility into cautious, respectful agreement.

"Agreed, Captain," she said softly. "At zero-four-hundred."

The deck routine resumed at four bells in the morning watch. Artie walked the perimeter of the cockpit, checking the tension on the lifelines and the lashing on the six-man Plastimo liferaft mounted in its stainless cradle forward of the companionway.

The hydrostatic release unit was armed, its green inspection indicator visible through the clear plastic housing. In a sinking, the water pressure at twelve feet would snap the release pin, allowing the canister to float free and inflate automatically.

"Noah, inspect the companionway washboards," Artie said.

Noah checked the heavy half-inch acrylic washboards slotted into the companionway channels. The stainless barrel bolts were greased and engaged into the teak trim, locking the lower two boards securely in place against a following sea. Even with the hatch open for ventilation, the lower washboards kept five hundred gallons of green water from flooding the cabin if a wave broke over the cockpit coaming.

Down below, Clara was heating a pan of oatmeal on the gimbaled two-burner stove. She used sea water for half the cooking liquid, an old delivery trick that conserved fresh tank water while providing the salt the body sweated out during heavy deck work.

She brought up two hot bowls, balancing them across the cabin sill.

"Eat it while it is hot," she said, handing a spoon to Noah. "The wind is freshening. The Barigo needle has dropped one millibar since four o'clock."

Artie took his bowl, leaning his shoulders against the companionway hood. The hot porridge warmed his chest through his wool sweater.

"The low pressure system over Cape Hatteras is moving northeast at twenty knots," Artie said, chewing thoughtfully. "By tomorrow night, we will have thirty-five knots on the beam. We will inspect the mast hounds and check the turnbuckles before the wind rises. A loose cotter pin in the rigging today is a dismasting tomorrow."

The cold front was preceded by a line of low, bruised clouds that marched across the western sky like an advancing wall of slate. The barometer on the bulkhead dropped another millibar, the brass needle trembling with the vibration of the hull.

Artie brought the emergency tiller from the lazarette locker and dry-fitted it over the top of the rudder stock.

The bronze head of the rudder shaft projected four inches through the cockpit sole beneath a screw-in deck plate. Artie seated the heavy stainless steel arm over the square bronze fitting, sliding the stainless retaining pin through the collar to lock it home.

"Test the sweep," Artie told Noah.

Noah gripped the long tubular handle, pushing it five degrees to port. Below the floor, the twin carbon rudder blades swung smoothly through their bearings, with no binding or play in the linkage.

"Smooth," Noah reported. "Zero resistance in the lower needle bearings."

"Good," Artie said, pulling the retaining pin and stowing the emergency tiller back in its leather brackets beneath the port cockpit bench. "If the steering cables part under the strain of a sixty-knot broach, you do not want to be fumbling with an emergency tiller that has seized from salt corrosion. Every piece of emergency gear on this ship must be exercised once every forty-eight hours."

He wiped his hands on a clean cotton rag, checked the wind instruments one final time, and stepped down the ladder to rest before his watch.

# Chapter 06: The Owner's Cable

The second transmission arrived at eleven-fifteen, while Artie was checking the tension on the hydraulic backstay ram in the cockpit.

The Furuno printer did not chime once. It emitted three rapid, high-pitched beeps, the signal reserved for high-priority commercial dispatch.

Artie came below, shedding his wet gloves into the plastic bin by the engine box. Clara was already standing by the terminal, her arms crossed over her chest, watching the black ink print out across the narrow white roll.

The header did not originate from Southampton. It came from a commercial telex gateway in Monte Carlo:

PRIORITY DISPATCH // MONACO MARITIME MANAGEMENT
DATE: 2026/10/24-1110Z
TO: CAPTAIN A. VANCE // S/Y AURA VII
COPIED: LLOYDS SYNDICATE 441 (UNDERWRITING)

INSTRUCTION CODE 01: ROUTING ADHERENCE
WE HAVE RECEIVED COPY OF SOUTHAMPTON ROUTER ADVISORY.
WE NOTE PROPOSED SOUTHERN DIVERSION TO 41 DEGREES NORTH.

THIS DIVERSION IS CATEGORICALLY REJECTED BY OWNER.
THE VESSEL IS BUILT TO GERMANISCHER LLOYD SPECIAL SERVICE CRAFT SPECIFICATION
AND IS FULLY CERTIFIED FOR SUSTAINED FORCE 10 SEA STATES.

THE CHARTER CONTRACT COMMENCES AT CANNES ON NOVEMBER 15.
ARRIVAL AT HORTA BEYOND 16 SEA DAYS CONSTITUTES MATERIAL BREACH OF ESCROW.
INSURANCE CLAUSE 14-B REMAINS CONDITIONAL UPON MAINTAINING DIRECT
GREAT CIRCLE ROUTING UNLESS ABANDONMENT IS DECLARED BY RESCUE COORDINATION.

MAINTAIN HEADING ZERO-EIGHT-FOUR TRUE. DO NOT ALTER COURSE SOUTH.
REPORT COMPLIANCE UPON LANDFALL.

The printer fed an extra three lines of blank paper and fell silent.

The cabin seemed suddenly very quiet, filled only with the low hum of the refrigeration compressor and the distant hiss of foam sliding along the carbon topsides.

Clara let out a short, humorless laugh. "Instruction Code Zero-One. The man is sitting in an air-conditioned office in Monte Carlo looking at a satellite map on a fifty-inch monitor, telling us that carbon fiber doesn't break."

Artie took the paper between his fingers. The text was signed by Henri Delacroix, the fleet manager whose name appeared on the bottom of his delivery contract. The tone was not maritime; it was legal. A carefully constructed liability document designed to place the financial burden of any delay entirely upon the delivery skipper.

"He copied the underwriter," Artie observed calmly.

"Of course he copied the underwriter," Clara said, her voice rising with sharp Nordic irritation. "He wants to make sure that if you turn south to save our lives, the underwriter voids the policy for unauthorized deviation. He is boxing you into a corner, Artie. He is betting this fifty-four-foot hull against your retirement money."

Julian Kross had come out of the forward passage, holding a tin cup of Tom's chicory coffee. He stood by the mast compression post, his knuckles whitening as he gripped the brushed aluminum sleeve.

"Can he cancel the contract while we are at sea?" Ward asked.

"He can't cancel the boat," Artie said without looking up from the paper. "The boat is out here. But he can freeze the escrow in Jersey. He can instruct the bank to withhold the balance pending arbitration in London."

"Which takes eighteen months," Clara added coldly. "And five thousand pounds in legal fees before a solicitor even opens the file."

Artie folded the Monaco cable twice and tucked it into the pocket of his sea smock, right beside Ward's expired discharge book. The two pieces of paper felt identical against his ribs: thin, dry cellulose carrying legal threats that meant nothing to the three-thousand-meter trench of ocean rolling beneath their keel.

He walked over to the chart table and picked up his brass dividers.

"We are at forty-seven degrees north," Artie said, pointing the sharp brass tip at the penciled track. "The center of the low is tracking east-northeast at thirty-two knots. If we maintain our present heading of zero-eight-four, we don't cross behind the storm. We intersect the southern quadrant of the depression in approximately thirty hours."

"Which means forty-five knots of wind from the southwest, shifting to northwest as the cold front passes," Clara said, leaning over the table.

"Can the boat carry forty-five knots?" Ward asked. His voice had the stiff, controlled pitch of a man trying not to betray terror.

"The boat can carry forty-five knots on a broad reach if we keep the third reef tied in the main and run the storm staysail," Artie answered. "The hull is strong enough. The question is the steering gear. Carbon rudder stocks have very high tensile strength, but they don't deform under impact. An aluminum stock bends when a breaking crest hits the blade; carbon either holds or it snaps clean at the lower bearing."

"And if it snaps?" Ward asked.

"Then you learn how to steer with a bucket on a warp," Tom Nowak called out from the galley, his head appearing above the half-bulkhead. He held an oily iron wrench in his hand. "Or you call your mother on the radio. Except the radio only hears what Monaco wants to say."

Artie turned to the Polish engineer. "How is the fuel bowl, Tom?"

"Another twenty milliliters of water drained at ten-hundred," Tom said, his face deadpan. "I opened the forward reserve tank. Clean fuel. But we only have one hundred fifty liters in that tank. If we run the engine continuously at eighteen hundred RPM, the forward tank is empty in forty hours."

Artie looked down at the paper chart. The black pencil line ran straight across the blank blue expanse of the North Atlantic, cutting through the Flemish Cap and heading toward the Azores.

"We don't divert five hundred miles south," Artie said, his voice flat and authoritative. "And we don't sail blindly into sixty knots. We hold heading zero-eight-four until eighteen-hundred. If the glass drops faster than two millibars an hour, we drop the mainsail, hoist the storm staysail, and run off thirty degrees to the south to take the sea on the quarter."

Clara looked at him, her eyes hard. "That is a compromise, Artie. The weather router told you to turn south now."

"The weather router doesn't pay my crew's wages," Artie said. "And he doesn't pay off my mortgage in Camden. We sail the boat."

He pulled his wool gloves back on, checked the battery voltage on the digital monitor, and climbed back up the companionway steps into the cold drizzle.

Artie walked to the forward locker, shining his flashlight beam into the chain pipe. The galvanized anchor chain was lashed securely with three turns of half-inch nylon rope to prevent fifty fathoms of heavy links from shifting in a knockdown and punching through the hull.

He checked the bilge cycle counter on the main switchboard. In the last four hours, the secondary electric pump had cycled twice, pumping approximately fifteen gallons of condensation and deck runoff out through the transom port. A normal rate for a performance hull driving into steep head seas.

Clara Lindholm was sitting at the chart table, eating a dry pilot biscuit spread with peanut butter.

"The Monaco telex was signed by Delacroix himself," Clara said, looking up from her plate. "I looked at the signature block. He doesn't usually sign routing cables. Usually it's a junior operations assistant in the fleet department."

"He signed it because of the insurance clause," Artie said, pouring himself half a mug of warm water from the galley kettle. "If the underwriter sees the fleet manager's personal signature ordering the skipper to maintain the great circle, the liability for any loss remains on the underwriter's books, not the management company's."

"He doesn't care about the underwriter," Clara replied, her voice cynical and quiet. "He cares about the charter date. Aura VII is chartered for the Cannes Film Festival support fleet. The charterer paid two hundred fifty thousand euros in advance. If the boat is not at the dock by November fifteenth, the contract has a full refund penalty."

"Two hundred fifty thousand euros," Artie repeated, shaking his head. "More money than my charter schooner earned in five years of coastal trade."

"Rich men don't like losing money, Artie."

"Rich men don't sail across the Grand Banks in late October," Artie replied. "They sit in Monaco and send cables."

He checked the barometer needle once more, noted the pressure in the canvas logbook, and stepped up into the cockpit to take the midnight helm.

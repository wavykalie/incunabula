# Chapter 02: The Expired Stamp

The sea had risen into long, dark ridges by mid-afternoon. Aura VII drove through them with the rigid, unyielding bounce of high-tensile carbon, the hull vibrating whenever the plumb bow sheared through a gray Atlantic crest.

Arthur Vance sat at the navigation station in the forward starboard corner of the saloon. He pulled the chart table drawer open until the brass slides clicked against their stops. Inside, nestled between rolled Admiralty paper charts and the vessel's registration papers, lay the ship's steel document safe.

Artie unlocked the small brass padlock with the key from his pocket ring. He lifted out the dark blue booklet he had taken from the younger man two hours earlier. The gilt lettering on the pebbled cover read: United Kingdom Seaman's Discharge Book. Below the royal crest, embossed numerals designated the record: R-984421.

He flipped through the initial pages. The plastic laminate over the photograph had slight hairline scratches near the lower edge, the sort left by grit in an envelope. The photograph showed a man with narrow shoulders, dark hair trimmed high around the ears, and a neutral, unblinking expression. It was Julian Kross's face, though three or four years younger, without the sunken hollows beneath the cheekbones.

The details matched the crew declaration sheet Artie had stamped at the customs desk in St. John's. Marcus Edward Ward. Date of birth: 14 March 1990. Place of birth: Southampton. Capacity: Deckhand / Yachtmaster Offshore.

Artie turned the heavy paper pages slowly. The entries were standard commercial delivery passages. A fifty-foot Swan from Palma to Cowes in June 2018. A Moody 45 from Gibraltar to Plymouth in September 2019. Each entry bore the oval rubber stamp of the shipping master, the purple ink slightly faded along the edges, signed by masters whose names Artie knew from the solent trade.

Then he reached page nine.

The entry was dated 18 October 2021. The vessel name was listed as M/V Northern Tern, an offshore platform support vessel registered in Douglas, Isle of Man. The stamp was clear: Discharge on Account of Decease. Across the entry line, written in black indelible ink with an official steel nib, ran the notation: Lost overboard in severe weather, North Sea sector 4, body recovered and certified at Lerwick, Shetland.

Artie rested his thumb against the paper. The ink was original. The paper fibers showed the slight indentation of the steel pen. But on the following page, dated May 2024, ran a fresh renewal endorsement for commercial yacht delivery, stamped with a Southampton MCA office number and an inspector's signature.

The dead mariner had been renewed three years after drowning off Shetland.

Artie closed the booklet and set it on the green blotting pad. His fingers smelled of salt spray and the light mineral oil he had used to lubricate the chart drawers that morning. He leaned back against the bulkhead cushion. The hull groaned softly as forty tons of displacement settled into the trough of a six-foot swell.

Through the forward saloon door, he heard the metallic rattle of a pot on the gimballed gas stove. Tomasz Nowak was in the galley, bracing his hips against the stainless crash bar as he cleaned an aluminum kettle. The sharp scent of roasted chicory and chicory-coffee grounds drifted into the passage.

"Tom," Artie called, keeping his voice below the thrum of the auxiliary engine.

The Polish engineer appeared in the doorway, wiping a pair of slotted pliers on a yellow rag. "Skipper?"

"Ward's watch habits. You were on deck when he rigged the preventer line."

Tom shrugged, shifting his weight with the roll of the vessel. "He knows how to tie a bowline. He knows which side of the winch drum the sheet turns. But he watches his boots too much."

"What does that mean?"

"A sailor looks at the horizon, Artie. He looks at the luff of the genoa, he looks at the wave coming off the quarter. This boy looks down. He watches where his feet go on the nonskid like he expects the deck to fall away under him. And his hands." Tom held up his own scarred, stub-fingered palms. "His hands are clean. No calluses on the rope fingers. He has the soft thumbs of an accountant or a man who types on keys all day."

"He has a Yachtmaster ticket," Artie said, nodding toward the safe.

"Lots of boys have paper from Southampton," Tom replied flatly. "Schools sell tickets every weekend. You pay five hundred pounds, you steer around a buoy in the Solent, you eat sandwiches, you get a stamp. Out here, paper does not keep water out of the bilge."

"Keep an eye on him on the helm," Artie said. "If the wind comes ahead of seventy degrees, don't let him pinch the cutter into the swell. The carbon rig has no tolerance for shock loading."

"I watch him," Tom said. "I also watch the stove. The gas regulator on the forward bottle is leaking slightly at the brass union. A tiny smell, like garlic. I tightened it with the adjustable wrench, but the neoprene gasket is hard. Probably five years old. These French brokers, they buy Italian champagne for the owner's locker and leave four-euro rubber washers on the propane line."

Tom turned back to the galley, muttering a brief Polish curse under his breath.

Artie picked up the discharge book and slipped it into his inner fleece pocket rather than locking it back in the steel chest. The weight of the booklet pressed against his ribs through the wool.

He looked at the chart table. The GPS repeater showed their position: forty-seven degrees twelve minutes north, fifty-one degrees thirty-six minutes west. Speed over ground was eight point four knots under staysail and engine. Course zero-eight-four true. The depth sounder showed three hundred forty meters, shelving rapidly as the Grand Banks dropped off into the deep oceanic basin of the Flemish Pass.

He picked up his brass dividers. The points were sharp, unmarred by rust. He measured twelve nautical miles along their projected track, marking the hourly dead reckoning fix with a small pencil dot.

A dead man's paperwork on an offshore delivery was not a crime Artie was paid to investigate. The admiralty courts in London had established centuries ago that the master of a vessel at sea answers for the ship's safety and the seaworthiness of her company, not the biographical pedigree of the deckhands recruited by shore brokers. If the man stood his four-hour watch, steered the compass heading, and handled a halyard without dropping the mainsail into the brine, his real name was irrelevant until the customs manifest in Horta.

Yet twenty-eight thousand dollars of Artie's own retirement sat in escrow in Jersey. A fraudulent crew declaration could void the vessel's P&I insurance policy the moment an underwriter's lawyer opened the file. If Aura VII lost her rig or sheared a rudder stock in deep water, the insurance surveyor would demand the original discharge books before approving a single pound of salvage.

Artie stood up, climbed the companionway steps, and pushed back the hatch.

Outside, the late afternoon light was gray and thin. Julian Kross was standing at the port helm pedestal, his gloved hands resting lightly on the carbon wheel rim. He was steering with small, nervous corrections, his eyes darting between the digital compass heading on the mast display and the tumbling gray crests advancing from the northwest.

"Heading holding?" Artie asked, leaning against the cockpit coaming.

Ward started slightly, his shoulders stiffening inside his yellow jacket. "Holding zero-eight-two, skipper. She hunts a little in the quartering swell. Two or three degrees either side."

"Let her hunt," Artie said calmly. "If you fight the wheel on every wave, you wear out the hydraulic ram and you tire your wrists before the dogwatch ends. Let the hull track."

"Understood," Ward said. He did not look at Artie. His eyes remained fixed on the green illuminated digits of the mast display.

Artie watched the younger man's profile against the darkening ocean. The wind was building, humming with a low, dry whistle through the rod rigging aloft. To the east, the horizon had dissolved into a solid wall of cold fog.

He stepped down to the lower step, checking the secondary bilge pump breaker on the main switchboard. The green pilot light was steady. The voltage was holding at twenty-six point four volts across the lithium house bank. Everything on the panel appeared functional, clean, and modern. Yet out here on the shelf edge, two hundred miles from the nearest Newfoundland coastguard cutter, modern electronics offered only the appearance of security. A loose ground wire or a drowned alternator could reduce forty tons of carbon technology to a dark, drifting raft within forty minutes.

Artie tapped the barometer glass beside the companionway. The needle settled at one thousand sixteen millibars. A steady reading, but the gray swell from the south carried a long, oily period that spoke of a gale already churning five hundred miles over the horizon.

He checked the secondary fuel valve beneath the navigation settee. The brass valve handle was stiff, covered with a thin crust of green verdigris from salt condensation. When he turned it forty-five degrees, the valve stem resisted with a dry squeak before opening to the forward reserve line.

Outside the saloon windows, the afternoon light faded into a dense, wet dusk. Artie walked the length of the accommodation corridor, testing the bronze latch on each overhead locker. In a heavy seaway, an unlatched door could swing with the momentum of a guillotine, splitting a man's forehead or crushing his collarbone. Every latch clicked shut with a solid, reassuring snap.

At the mast step, where the sixty-foot carbon spar passed through the deck to rest on the keel shoe, he inspected the hydraulic jacking cylinder. The pressure gauge read three thousand four hundred pounds per square inch. The rod rigging was singing in the damp air, humming at forty cycles a second, a pitch that vibrated through the composite bulkheads.

He climbed the companionway stairs again, carrying a thermos of hot chicory coffee in his jacket pocket. Ward was still at the wheel, his posture rigid, his shoulders hunched against the freezing drizzle that blew across the sprayhood.

"Take a swallow of this," Artie said, offering the plastic cup.

Ward looked down at the cup, hesitated, and accepted it with a gloved hand. The steam rose into his face, carrying the bitter, roasted smell into the wind.

"Thank you, skipper," Ward said.

"How does the wheel feel?"

"She has twenty pounds of weather helm when the swell lifts the quarter," Ward answered, speaking with careful precision. "I have to lead the wave with five degrees of rudder to stop the bow from searching."

"That is the carbon rudders," Artie told him. "They don't flex. When the water bites, it bites instantly. Don't fight her. Let the stern rise, let the water slide along the run, then bring her back as she levels."

Ward nodded, sipping the hot liquid. For a moment, the tension in the younger man's jaw seemed to soften, replaced by the quiet, shared exhaustion of two men standing on a deck in a rising sea.

The change of watch came at midnight under a clearing sky. The wind held steady from the north-northwest at twenty-two knots, kicking up a steep, four-foot chop that slapped against the forward quarters.

Noah handed the helm over to Clara, stepping back into the cockpit well to shake the cramps from his hands. His fingers were stiff from gripping the wheel against the pulse of the quarterly sea.

"Log shows thirty-two miles on the mechanical impeller," Noah reported to her. "Average speed six point four knots. Vessel is tracking true on zero-eight-five magnetic. The barometer on the bulkhead reads ten-fifteen and steady."

Clara took the wheel, testing the balance of the rudder with small movements. She felt the tug of the mainsail through the steering quadrant, the responsive lift of the bow as each swell passed under the bilge.

"She carries a light helm now," she said. "The second reef took the weight out of her."

Artie came on deck with a canvas bucket containing fresh sea water and a mercury thermometer. He dipped the brass-cased thermometer into the bucket, waited two minutes for the reading to settle, and held the glass tube up to the red glow of the binnacle lamp.

"Nineteen degrees Celsius," Artie noted. "We have crossed the northern boundary of the Gulf Stream. The cold inshore shelf water is behind us."

The surface of the sea began to glow with green phosphorescence. Every wave that broke against the bow threw off showers of cold emerald sparks that tumbled down the length of the topsides, lighting the dark water with eerie, fleeting brilliance.

Noah watched the glowing foam with silent fascination.

"The algae produces light under mechanical disturbance," Artie explained, watching the wake trail behind them like a burning green road across the black ocean. "Sailors call it sea fire. When you see it this bright, you know you are in the core of the warm current. We pick up two knots of free eastward push until we clear the eastern eddies."

By zero-two-hundred, the sea had smoothed into long, rhythmic hills of black water that lifted the stern without a jar. The masthead wind instrument registered nineteen knots of true wind from three-hundred-twenty degrees magnetic.

Artie came on deck with the canvas sail repair kit. He inspected the mainsail foot where the dacron cloth met the aluminum boom extrusion.

The outhaul line was half-inch low-stretch polyester braid, led through an internal sheave at the boom end to a small Lewmar winch on the cabin trunk. Artie took two wraps off the winch, eased the outhaul four inches to give the mainsail a deeper draft for downwind reaching, and re-cleated the line with a locking hitch.

"Look at the luff telltales," Noah noted, pointing his dim red flashlight at the lower batten pockets.

The green yarn on the starboard side was streaming straight aft, parallel to the sail seam, while the red yarn on the port side fluttered slightly.

"Trim the mainsheet in three inches," Artie instructed. "A reaching mainsail should never stall along the leech. If the telltales drop, you are dragging two hundred square feet of dead canvas through the sky."

Noah pulled the braided mainsheet through the cockpit traveler block, taking up the slack until both yarns flew straight and level. The boat responded with an immediate half-knot increase on the digital speed display, accelerating cleanly to seven point two knots across the water.

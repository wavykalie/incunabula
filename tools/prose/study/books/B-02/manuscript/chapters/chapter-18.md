# Chapter 18: The Bilge Alarm

The high-water alarm sounded at zero-two-forty in the morning.

It was not a gentle chime. It was a piercing, ninety-decibel piezoelectric screamer mounted directly into the saloon deckhead, designed to wake a dead crew through three bulkheads of soundproofing.

Artie Vance was out of his pilot berth before his feet touched the floorboards.

He dropped down the companionway into the saloon, his flashlight beam slicing through the red darkness. The floorboards were already floating. Two inches of clear water were sloshing back and forth across the teak cabin sole with every roll of the yacht, swoshing into the galley bins and washing empty tin cans across the linoleum.

Tomasz Nowak was already kneeling by the central bilge access hatch, his bare arms soaked to the elbows in water.

"Is it salt?" Artie shouted above the screech of the alarm.

Tom dipped his finger into the sloshing water and touched it to his tongue. He spat immediately, shaking his head. "Fresh! It is fresh water, Artie! The main domestic freshwater line has burst!"

Artie reached up to the electrical panel and killed the breaker for the automatic pressure pump. The electric pump, mounted in the forward engine space, had been cycling continuously for two hours, pumping six hundred liters of potable drinking water from the main forward tank directly into the bilge through a ruptured plastic plumbing fitting beneath the galley sink.

"Cut the bilge pump manual override!" Artie ordered.

Tom reached into the bilge well and engaged the heavy Rule 3700 electric submersible pump. The pump kicked on with a deep, throbbing whine, sending a solid three-inch column of water out through the transom discharge port into the sea.

Within eight minutes, the water level on the floorboards receded, leaving a slick film of wet dust and floating labels across the teak.

Artie knelt beside the galley cabinet, shining his light into the plumbing manifold.

The failure was total. A cheap plastic quick-connect elbow, manufactured from brittle white nylon and installed behind the hot-water heater, had cracked clean across the mold line, blown apart by the pressure of the domestic pump.

Tom pulled the water tank inspection gauge from the forward bulkhead.

The needle pointed flatly to zero.

"Both tanks empty," Tom said, his voice flat with grim exhaustion. "The main tank and the secondary tank were cross-connected through the manifold. Six hundred liters of fresh drinking water, gone into the sea."

Clara and Julian stood in the corridor doorway, their faces pale under the beam of the flashlight.

"How much water do we have left?" Clara asked, her voice trembling.

Tom walked over to the galley pantry, opening the lower lockers. He pulled out six plastic two-liter bottles of commercial mineral water and two half-empty gallon jugs from the refrigerator.

"Eighteen liters," Tom said, setting the plastic bottles on the counter. "Four people. Eighteen liters of water in the middle of the Atlantic."

"Eighteen liters is four days at minimal hydration," Clara said, her analytical mind immediately calculating the survival equation. "If we are four days from Horta, that is one point one liters per person per day. If we face head winds and the crossing takes eight days, we are dead from dehydration before we sight Faial."

Artie stood up, wiping water from his knees with a towel.

"Nobody is dying of dehydration on my ship," Artie said, his voice carrying the calm, absolute authority that had held his crews together through thirty years of offshore emergencies.

He turned toward Tom.

"Tom, where is the emergency ditch kit from the forward locker?"

"In the lazarette," Tom answered. "Beside the life raft."

"Go get it," Artie said. "And bring the yellow plastic Pelican case from the shelf behind the steering quadrant."

Tom nodded, turning immediately toward the aft cabin.

Three minutes later, he returned carrying a heavy yellow plastic case marked with red reflective tape: REVERSE OSMOSIS MANUAL DESALINATOR // SURVIVOR-35.

Artie opened the heavy plastic latches. Inside lay a rugged, hand-operated manual desalinator pump, designed for survival life rafts, equipped with a stainless steel pumping handle and two clear intake hoses.

"A manual watermaker," Clara said, stepping forward.

"Thirty-five strokes a minute produces four point five liters of fresh drinking water an hour from raw seawater," Artie said, lifting the heavy hand pump from its foam bed. "It takes physical labor. Two hours of pumping per watch, every day, until we tie up in Horta."

He looked at Julian Kross.

"Mr. Ward," Artie said. "You wanted to earn your keep on this boat. Your watch begins now. Take the intake hose, drop it through the galley sea cock, and start pumping."

Julian took the stainless steel handle without a word. He knelt on the floorboards, braced his knees against the galley fiddles, and began the steady, rhythmic stroke of the pump, sending the first clear trickle of fresh, life-saving water into a clean glass jar.

Tom Nowak spent two hours kneeling in the flooded galley bilge, tracing the fractured domestic plumbing line with his fingers beneath the floor framing.

He pulled out the broken white nylon quick-connect elbow that had caused the disaster. The plastic had shattered along the injection molding seam, weakened by months of engine vibration and water pressure.

"Cheap plastic," Tom spat, tossing the shattered fitting onto the counter with a metallic clatter. "A ninety-cent fitting on a four-million-euro boat. If they had used bronze or stainless steel, the water would still be in the tanks."

He reached into his plumbing kit, pulling out a solid brass hose barb, two stainless steel constant-torque hose clamps, and a roll of heavy rubber self-vulcanizing tape.

Working in the confined space beneath the galley sink, Tom cut away the damaged plastic hose, pushed the brass barb into the two severed ends of the reinforced vinyl tubing, and tightened the hose clamps with an eight-millimeter socket wrench until the rubber squeezed through the band perforations.

"The line is repaired," Tom said, climbing out of the bilge and wiping his knees with a wet rag. "The plumbing is tight. But the tanks are empty. We have eighteen liters of bottled water in the pantry, and whatever this manual pump can pull from the sea."

Artie walked over to the table where the yellow Survivor-35 manual desalinator sat in its foam cradle.

The hand-operated reverse osmosis pump was a masterpiece of military engineering, built for naval aviators and liferaft survival. Inside the heavy plastic cylinder sat a semi-permeable membrane that allowed fresh water molecules to pass through while rejecting ninety-eight percent of dissolved sodium chloride under eight hundred pounds of hydraulic pressure.

"The pumping lever takes thirty pounds of effort on every downstroke," Artie said, showing Julian how to position his body over the handle. "You don't pump with your arms. You pump with your back and your body weight. Down on the stroke, up on the return. Thirty-five strokes a minute."

Julian took the handle, bracing his knees against the saloon fiddles.

He pushed down. The lever resisted with a stiff, hydraulic push, the internal check valves clicking softly.

On the tenth stroke, a clear, crystalline bead of pure water appeared at the tip of the silicone discharge tube, dropped into the glass sample jar, and was followed by a steady, rhythmic trickle.

Tom dipped his finger into the jar and tasted it.

A slow smile broke across his scarred face. "Pure water, Artie. Better than the tap in St. John's. We won't die of thirst today."

The deck gear was subjected to continuous inspection every morning at first light. Noah walked the side decks with a roll of rigging tape and a grease gun loaded with waterproof lithium grease.

He checked the lifeline stanchions first. Each stainless base was secured through the deck with four five-sixteenths bolts backed by aluminum backing plates. He gave each stanchion a sharp kick with his boot heel. None budged. The lifelines were seven-by-seven stainless wire covered in white vinyl, tensioned at the stern pulpit by bronze pelican hooks.

"Look at the upper wire by the gate," Noah called aft.

Artie came forward along the weather deck, his tether clipped to the jackline that ran from cockpit to bow.

Noah peeled back an inch of split white vinyl. Beneath it, three strands of the stainless core had parted, their broken wire ends sticking out like stiff bristles.

"Salt got under the plastic covering," Artie said, examining the fracture under his thumb. "Moisture sits trapped between the vinyl and the steel, starves the metal of oxygen, and causes crevice corrosion. Never trust plastic-coated lifelines on an offshore passage."

He fetched the rigging bag from the foredeck locker. In five minutes, he had cut away the damaged section with heavy wire cutters, swaged a new stainless eye terminal onto the bare standing wire with the hand crimping tool, and seized the thimble to the pelican hook with four passes of waxed sailmaker's twine.

"That will hold five thousand pounds," Artie said, pulling on the wire with both hands. "Now check the jib furling drum. If the furling line overrides on the spool in forty knots of wind, you cannot roll the sail up without going out on the bowsprit."

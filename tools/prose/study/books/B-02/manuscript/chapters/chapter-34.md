# Chapter 34: First Light

Dawn broke on the twelfth morning with the sudden, startling magnificence of an opening theater curtain.

Arthur Vance climbed the companionway steps at zero-six-hundred, his movements slow and guarded against the dull ache in his ribs. For three days, the world had been a monochrome nightmare of charcoal water, white foam, and low, suffocating gray overcast that hid the sun and the stars.

Now, as he stepped into the cockpit, he stopped and stared east.

Along the horizon, stretching across fifty miles of open ocean, ran a narrow, incandescent ribbon of bruised violet and burning gold. Above the color, the sky arched away into a deep, crystalline cobalt, completely free of storm clouds, swept clean by the dry westerly winds of the advancing Azores High.

The sea state had transformed.

The violent, crumbling walls of the gale had dissolved into long, magnificent ocean swells, sixteen feet high, spaced three hundred yards apart. They rolled beneath the hull with a slow, majestic cadence, their dark blue slopes reflecting the morning light like polished obsidian.

*Aura VII* was sailing under her small staysail and the steady thrum of the auxiliary engine, tracking cleanly on heading zero-nine-eight true at eight point six knots.

Julian Kross stood at the port helm pedestal, his hands resting lightly on the carbon wheel rim.

The transformation in the younger man was total. The hunched, defensive paranoia that had marked his posture in St. John's had disappeared. His yellow foul-weather jacket was unzipped at the throat, revealing a clean wool collar. His face was weathered, tan, and lined with salt, his eyes looking straight at the rising sun with the steady, calm gaze of a man who had faced the worst the North Atlantic could offer and survived.

"Good morning, Captain," Julian said, his voice clear and untroubled.

"Good morning, Julian," Artie replied, stepping onto the cockpit bench. "How does she steer?"

"Like a dream, sir," Julian said, smiling faintly. "The swell is long and easy. One spoke to port on the crest, one spoke to starboard in the trough. She tracks on a rail."

Tom Nowak emerged from the companionway, carrying a tray with four steaming mugs of freshly brewed tea and a plate of warm, toasted sourdough bread spread with black currant jam.

"Breakfast for the watch," Tom announced, his gravelly voice filled with genuine good humor. "Real tea, made with fresh water pumped by Mr. Kross at dawn. No salt, no plastic flavor. Eat before the butter melts."

Clara Lindholm came up behind him, wearing a dry navy fleece and clean thermal trousers. She had brushed her blonde hair out of its tight knot, letting it fall loose around her shoulders. In her hand, she carried the paper chart and her brass parallel rule.

She laid the chart across the teak cockpit table, weighting the corners with the mugs of hot tea.

"Position fix at zero-six-hundred," Clara said, her voice bright with professional satisfaction. "Latitude thirty-nine degrees twelve minutes north. Longitude thirty-two degrees forty-eight minutes west."

Artie looked down at the penciled cross on the blue paper.

The pencil mark was less than one hundred twenty nautical miles from the western tip of Faial.

"One hundred twenty miles," Clara said, looking at Artie. "At eight point five knots, that is fourteen hours of sailing. We will make landfall before midnight."

"Landfall before midnight," Artie repeated, tasting the hot, sweet tea on his tongue.

The delivery contract had allowed sixteen sea days from St. John's. Today was day twelve.

Despite the gale, despite the shattered steering quadrant, despite the contaminated main fuel tank and the broken ribs, they were four days ahead of the contract deadline. The escrow in Jersey was secure. The penalty clause was void.

Artie looked out across the glittering expanse of the North Atlantic. The sun broke the horizon in a blaze of gold, warming the cold carbon deck and turning the spray from the bow into a shower of sparkling diamonds.

They rounded the massive stone breakwater of Horta at two in the afternoon. The outer mole was constructed of giant five-ton basalt blocks, scarred by winter storms and encrusted with black barnacles up to the high-tide mark. Inside the harbor, the water was as calm as an inland pond, sheltered completely from the Atlantic swell.

"Look at the quay," Clara whispered, stepping to the cockpit rail.

The concrete sea wall of the marina was covered from end to end with thousands of colorful paintings, dates, and ship names left by generations of transatlantic sailors. Every square foot of concrete bore the testimony of a voyage: sloops from Brest, ketches from Falmouth, cutters from Newport, catamarans from Cape Town, all painted with marine enamel by crews who had crossed the ocean and made safe landfall.

"It is maritime tradition," Artie told Noah. "If you cross the Atlantic and do not paint your ship's name on the wall, the old sailors say you will have no luck on the voyage home."

Noah stared at the wall with wide, quiet eyes. His fingers touched the salt-crusted fabric of his foul-weather jacket. "We will paint her name tomorrow."

"First we tie up," Artie said, shifting the engine into neutral. "Clara, get the fenders over the port side. Noah, take the spring lines and the bow line to the rail."

Four blue cylindrical fenders were dropped over the port topsides, their heights adjusted so they hung two inches above the waterline. The heavy braided mooring lines were laid out along the side deck, their spliced eyes ready to drop over harbor bollards.

Artie picked up the VHF microphone from the companionway and selected channel ten.

"Horta Marina, Horta Marina," he spoke in a clear, measured voice. "This is sailing vessel Cormorant, arriving from Bermuda, forty-two foot cutter, three souls on board. Requesting berth for delivery handover."

# Chapter 30: The Empty Raft

They reached the search coordinates at zero-six-fifteen, just as the first gray light of dawn filtered through the driving squalls.

The storm had eased slightly, the wind dropping from fifty-five knots to forty-two knots, but the sea remained a chaotic, towering wilderness of confused, twenty-five-foot swells that leaped into vertical pyramids of foam where the two wave trains crossed.

Clara Lindholm stood in the open companionway hatch, the night-vision binoculars pressed tight against her eyes. Her boots were braced against the companionway ladder, her body absorbing the violent corkscrew motion of the cutter.

"Anything?" Artie called up from the settee, his chest burning with every breath.

"Debris field!" Clara shouted suddenly, pointing off the starboard bow. "Three hundred yards, eleven o'clock! I see wooden pallets and floating plastic drums!"

Tom Nowak immediately eased the starboard steering line, bringing the yacht's head five degrees to the west, steering down the lane between two giant rolling crests.

The debris swept past them: broken wooden shipping pallets, two crushed white fiberglass hatch covers, and an empty six-man chemical survival suit floating like a drowned man in the foam.

Then Julian Kross screamed from the foredeck.

"Raft! Raft dead ahead in the trough!"

Artie dragged himself up the companionway steps, his face tight with pain, peering out past the sprayhood.

Eighty yards ahead, wallowing in the gray trough between two steep swells, sat a circular twenty-person commercial liferaft.

The canopy was fluorescent orange, fitted with retro-reflective tape that flared brightly in the morning light. The automated strobe light mounted on the apex of the canopy was still flashing, sending a rhythmic blue-white pulse through the flying spray.

The raft was upside down.

Its black neoprene ballast bags were turned toward the sky, trailing four heavy webbing bridles through the water.

"Bring us alongside to windward!" Artie ordered. "Tom, keep our speed at four knots! Don't run the raft down with the bow!"

Tom ground the starboard winch with surgical precision, sliding the fifty-four-foot carbon hull within ten feet of the capsized orange dome.

Julian stood at the forward lifeline, a heavy aluminum boat hook held in both hands, his safety tether locked short to the toe-rail. As the raft surged alongside on the crest of a swell, Julian lunged with the hook, catching the heavy black lifeline of the raft and hauling it tight against the hull.

Artie leaned over the cockpit coaming, looking directly into the flooded under-structure of the overturned raft.

The boarding ladder was dangling loose in the water. The sea anchor line was trailing aft, its parachute torn away.

Artie reached down with a gaff hook, snagging the heavy nylon painter line that connected the raft to its hydrostatic release unit.

He hauled the thick rope out of the water.

The bitter end of the painter was not frayed. It was not snapped by wave force.

The three-quarter-inch nylon rope had been severed with a single, clean diagonal cut, sliced through by the serrated blade of a professional maritime survival knife.

Artie looked into the dark void beneath the capsized canopy.

There were no bodies. There were no emergency ration tins floating in the water. The survival gear pockets were empty, their Velcro flaps torn open from the inside.

"The painter was cut," Artie said, his voice carrying clearly over the hiss of the foam. "The crew deployed the raft, cut the line to clear the sinking cargo ship, and evacuated."

"Are they in the water?" Clara asked, her voice trembling as she stared at the empty neoprene tubes.

"No," Tom Nowak said, pointing his finger toward the southern horizon.

Five miles to the south, breaking through the low stratus ceiling at five hundred feet, the black silhouette of a twin-engine maritime search-and-rescue aircraft was banking in a wide, slow orbit, its red and white navigation lights flashing through the rain.

Beneath the aircraft, barely visible through the sea smoke, the dark gray superstructure of a Portuguese navy frigate was driving through the swells at twenty-five knots, throwing fifty-foot bow waves from her sharp naval stem.

"The frigate picked them up," Tom said, his scarred hands resting on the winch handle. "The distress relay went out three hours ago. The navy was already on patrol off the Azores shelf."

Julian Kross slowly lowered the boat hook, letting the empty orange raft drift away into the tumbling wake of the cutter.

He stood on the foredeck, his face turned toward the distant gray warship, the morning wind whipping his yellow jacket against his ribs.

They had sailed twenty-two miles through a Force Ten gale with a broken steering quadrant and fractured ribs. They had risked forty tons of carbon hull, twenty-eight thousand dollars of retirement escrow, and four human lives on the razor edge of survival.

They had found only an empty piece of rubber floating in the Atlantic.

Yet as Julian looked back toward the cockpit, his eyes met Artie Vance's.

There was no regret in the older man's weathered face. There was only the quiet, immovable peace of a seaman who had answered the call of his trade and paid what he owed to the ocean.

"Set course zero-nine-five," Artie said, turning back toward the companionway. "We have three hundred miles to Horta. Let's go home."

The wind held true through the afternoon, easing gradually to twenty-five knots as the depression moved away toward the Norwegian Sea. The swell remained large, eight to ten feet, but the tops no longer broke, sliding smoothly under the hull in long, rhythmic rollers that carried the yacht forward on long, gliding surfs.

Artie stood at the companionway, watching the solar panel on the aft arch. The needle on the charge controller showed six amps flowing into the house bank. The batteries had survived the night, their voltage holding at twelve point four volts.

"We need the engine for the final approach," Artie told them. "The pilot book says the current through the channel between Faial and Pico runs at three knots on the ebb. If the wind goes light under the lee of the mountain, we will be swept south into the open Atlantic before we can round the breakwater."

Noah picked up his toolbox and headed below. "I have the new copper flare ready for the oil line, and the Racor filter is drained. Let's finish the mechanical work while the boat is running level."

They spent two hours in the engine compartment. Noah fitted the copper line to the oil pressure sender, tightening the compression nut with measured force. Artie filled the crankcase with fresh fifteen-forty mineral oil from the spare containers, checking the dipstick three times until the level reached the exact upper mark.

"Now we bleed the high-pressure injector pump," Artie said.

He cracked the ten-millimeter bleeder screw on top of the secondary fuel filter, worked the manual priming lever on the mechanical lift pump thirty times until pure, bubble-free diesel welled up through the threads, and tightened the screw down.

"She is primed," Noah said, his eyes bright in the flashlight beam. "Ready for trial."

"Not yet," Artie cautioned. "Let the oil settle into the galleries. We will fire her when we sight land."

Retrieving three hundred feet of drogue line in a declining sea required steady, coordinated teamwork. The wind had dropped to twenty knots, but the Atlantic swell was still eight feet high, lifting the hull and pulling hard on the remaining cones.

Artie led the retrieval line through a heavy snatch block shackled to the toe rail, then onto the large two-speed leeward primary winch.

"Take five wraps on the drum, Noah," Artie directed. "Grind only when the boat falls into the wave trough. When the stern lifts on a crest, hold the line firm on the self-tailer and let the winch brake take the strain."

Noah gripped the winch handles, spinning them in high gear as the boat slid down a swell, taking in three feet of line. When the stern rose, the line pulled taut, and Noah braced his thighs against the cockpit coaming, holding the ground he had gained.

"Good rhythm," Artie said, standing by the open crate to flake the cones as they reached the deck. "Do not rush. If you burn your shoulders out in the first hundred feet, we will be here until noon."

Inch by inch, the heavy braided line came home. Cone by cone, the orange fabric was caught, shaken free of water, and folded into the plastic bin. Clara stood beside the helm, using the engine in forward gear at idle speed to relieve the tension whenever a particularly steep swell approached.

By eleven o'clock, the terminal thirty feet of galvanized chain clattered over the stern roller. Noah dropped the winch handle with a groan of relief, his hands trembling with muscle fatigue.

"Drogue is on board," Artie declared, lashing the storage crate to the stern arch. "Deck is clear. Unfurl thirty percent of the genoa, Clara. Let's get Cormorant sailing toward Horta."

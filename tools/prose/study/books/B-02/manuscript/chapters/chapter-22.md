# Chapter 22: The Jackstay Run

The storm staysail had jammed in the roller-furling foil at twenty-three-hundred hours.

The wind had settled into a steady fifty knots from the northwest, with gusts screaming past sixty-two knots across the masthead anemometer. *Aura VII* was running before the gale under bare poles, trailing two heavy three-strand mooring warps from her stern cleats to keep her speed down to seven knots.

In the darkness forward, the heavy aluminum foil that housed the staysail had warped under the immense twisting load of the headstay. Ten feet of torn orange Dacron had escaped from the furler, whipping against the inner forestay like a steel flail. Every time the yacht plunged into a wave trough, the flogging canvas threatened to wrap itself around the masthead halyards and rip the furling drum completely out of the deck laminate.

Arthur Vance stood at the companionway, studying the bow through night-vision binoculars.

"We have to cut it away," Artie said, turning to Clara. "If that foil twists another five degrees, it tears the forestay chainplate out of the stem. If the forestay goes, the mast comes down backwards into the cockpit."

"Nobody can work on the foredeck in this sea," Clara shouted back, her face illuminated by the green glow of the radar screen. "The waves are breaking over the pulpit every fifteen seconds. A man on the bow will be washed into the sea before he can open a knife!"

"I will go," Julian Kross said, stepping up onto the companionway ladder.

He had wrapped his hands in heavy black self-amalgamating rigging tape to protect his torn palms. His foul-weather jacket was cinched tight with three webbing straps around his waist and chest, his safety knife sheathed in a plastic scabbard lashed to his forearm.

"You don't go alone, Julian," Artie said.

"I am the lightest," Julian replied, his voice calm, clear, and steady for the first time since St. John's. "I am seventy kilograms. You and Tom are over ninety. If you go on the bow, your weight will bury the stem into the troughs. I can move along the jackstay fast. I cut the luff tape, Tom eases the halyard from the cockpit, and the sail blows clear into the leeward sea."

Artie looked into Julian's eyes. The fear was still there, but beneath the fear was a hard, unbreakable focus. The fugitive had vanished; in his place stood an ocean deckhand who understood that survival was a matter of immediate mechanical action.

"Two safety tethers," Artie said, clipping a second heavy six-foot nylon line to Julian's chest ring. "You hook onto the centerline jackstay. You never unclip both hooks at the same time. If a wave breaks over the bow, you drop flat onto your stomach, wrap both arms around the deck rail, and hold your breath until the water clears. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you, Captain," Julian said.

He stepped out onto the cockpit sole, hooked his first tether to the cabin top jackstay, and began the perilous crawl forward.

Artie followed him as far as the mast base, holding the halyard line ready in his hands.

Julian moved with the careful, deliberate rhythm of a rock climber. When the bow rose toward the sky, he scrambled forward four paces, sliding his carabiners along the stainless wire. When the stern lifted and the bow plunged into the dark abyss of the trough, he dropped flat to the nonskid, his fingers locking into the perforated aluminum toe-rail as tons of white water swept over his body in a deafening torrent.

He reached the inner forestay.

Bracing his boots against the base of the anchor windlass, Julian drew his stainless serrated rigging knife. He waited for the wave to pass, stood up into the fifty-knot gale, and slashed at the heavy Dacron luff tape.

The knife bit deep into the fabric. The torn sail gave a loud, explosive rip as the wind caught the cut edge.

"Ease the halyard!" Julian screamed back through the roaring darkness.

At the mast, Artie threw the halyard off the winch drum, letting thirty meters of Spectra line scream through his gloved hands.

The ruined sail tore free from the aluminum foil, flapping once like a wounded bird, and was whipped away into the black Atlantic night, vanishing into the foam four hundred yards to leeward in less than two seconds.

Julian dropped back onto his stomach, secured his safety line, and began his retreat toward the mast.

When he reached the safety of the coachroof, Artie grabbed him by the scruff of his jacket, dragging him into the shelter of the companionway hood.

Julian was drenched, shivering violently from head to foot, his lips blue with hypothermia. But as he looked up at Artie through his salt-encrusted eyelashes, he gave a faint, exhausted grin.

"The foil is clear, skipper," he whispered. "The chainplate is intact."

Artie gripped the young man's arm, his fingers digging into the wet wool. "Good man, Julian. Get below to the heater. You saved the rig."

The shift came at three in the afternoon, sharp and cold as a blade drawn across the water. The wind swung forty degrees in four minutes, backing into the south-southwest. The surface water broke into white teeth, crossing the older northwest swell and sending green water across the starboard gunwale.

Artie stood behind the steering pedestal, watching the windex at the masthead. The small plastic arrow vibrated violently against its stops.

"Furl the genoa," he shouted through the roar of spray. "Get the staysail up on the inner forestay."

Noah leapt to the leeward winch, releasing the genoa sheet from the self-tailer. Clara hauled on the furling line from the cockpit, her shoulders working in rhythm as forty feet of heavy dacron rolled smoothly around the foil. The sail vanished into a tight white column, leaving the foretriangle bare.

Artie hooked the staysail hanks onto the inner wire. The steel snaps bit into the wire with sharp clicks. He had greased the piston hanks in Bermuda, and they slid easily up the stay without jamming.

"Halyard on winch two," Artie called. "Take it up until the luff bar is rigid."

Noah cranked the two-speed winch, his forearms tight under his fleece sleeves. The small, heavy triangle of cloth rose between the mast and the foredeck, stiff as sheet iron. When it caught the gale, the boat heeled ten degrees, groaned through its bulkheads, and leaned into its stride. The balance returned to the helm. The rudder no longer fought the water; it sliced through it, carving a straight furrow across the foam.

Down below, the cabin had become a dark, tilted vault. Canned goods rattled in their mahogany bins. In the bilge, the float switch tripped, and the Rule pump ran with a dull whir for twenty seconds, pumping five gallons of gray bilge water out the transom thru-hull. Artie climbed down the ladder, checked the battery monitor on the nav station panel, and recorded the hourly run in the log.

"Course one-hundred-ten magnetic," he wrote with a ballpoint pen. "Wind south-southwest Force seven. Vessel handling well under staysail and double-reefed main. Pressure nine-eighty-seven. Sea temperature thirteen Celsius."

He capped the pen, wedged it into the groove behind the barometer, and sat on the lower bunk to rest his boots.

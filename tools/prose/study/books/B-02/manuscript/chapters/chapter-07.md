# Chapter 07: The Great Circle

The geometry of the North Atlantic does not conform to the flat logic of a desktop map.

Arthur Vance sat at the navigation table with Admiralty Chart 4011 spread out beneath the brass chart weights. The projection was a gnomonic chart, designed specifically for great circle navigation. On a standard Mercator chart, a line of constant compass heading, a rhumb line, appears as a straight path, but on the curved sphere of the earth, that straight line is an illusion that adds hundreds of unnecessary miles to an ocean crossing.

On a gnomonic projection, the great circle, the shortest distance between two points on a globe, is a straight pencil line, while the compass course changes continuously as the vessel crosses the converging meridians of longitude.

From the Narrows of St. John's at forty-seven degrees north to the volcanic channel between Faial and Pico at thirty-eight degrees north, the true great circle track arched northward, reaching its highest latitude near fifty degrees north before curving southeast toward the Portuguese archipelago.

Artie placed his plastic parallel rule against the track.

The distance along the great circle was one thousand one hundred ninety nautical miles. The rhumb line, holding a steady compass heading of one hundred twelve degrees true across the parallels, measured one thousand two hundred eighty miles. A saving of ninety miles. In flat water under engine power, ninety miles was ten hours of steaming and eighty liters of diesel fuel.

But geography in the North Atlantic is governed by water temperature, not geometry.

To the north of their track lay the cold core of the Labrador Current, sweeping south out of Baffin Bay at two knots, carrying water that hovered within two degrees of freezing. Where that Arctic current collided with the warm, salty waters of the North Atlantic Drift over the shallow shoals of the Grand Banks, the ocean produced two permanent hazards: impenetrable sea fog and the southern drift of glacial ice.

Artie picked up his pencil and drew a light hatching across the northern quadrant of the chart, between forty-eight and fifty-two degrees north.

The International Ice Patrol boundary for late October.

Even in autumn, when the primary calving season of the Greenland glaciers had passed, growlers, low-lying blocks of dense glacial ice the size of a shipping container, washed flush with the ocean surface, drifted south through the Flemish Pass. A high-speed carbon fiber yacht striking a growler at nine knots would not dent her bow; the carbon laminate would crush like dry cardboard, tearing the watertight bulkhead away from the ring frames and sinking the vessel in less than four minutes.

"You are looking at the ice limit," Clara said, stepping into the navigation station. She had changed into dry thermal socks, her bare feet silent on the teak cabin sole.

"I am looking at the water temperature," Artie said, pointing his pencil at the digital sea-surface temperature gauge mounted beside the GPS display.

The screen read: SST: 4.8°C.

"Three degrees colder than yesterday," Clara observed. "We are in the Labrador water. If the fog comes down tonight, radar won't see a growler until it is sixty yards off the bowsprit."

"Radar doesn't see growlers at all in a three-meter sea," Artie said. "The wave clutter on the display drowns the return from a piece of smooth ice that only sticks eighteen inches above the water."

"Then why did the owner order us to stay on the great circle?"

"Because the computer program that manages his yacht fleet in Monaco uses an algorithm that optimizes for distance and charter delivery dates," Artie answered, setting his pencil down. "The algorithm knows the cost of a charter cancellation in Cannes. It does not know the specific gravity of Greenland pack ice."

He leaned back, rubbing the stiffness from his neck.

This was the professional reality he had lived with for twenty years. The modern marine industry had replaced the weathered knowledge of deep-water skippers with automated routing software, satellite telemetry, and corporate insurance schedules. The men who owned these yachts viewed ocean crossings as logistical transfer intervals, like shipping a Mercedes in a container from Hamburg to Genoa. They expected twenty-knot averages and guaranteed arrival dates, forgetting that forty feet of saltwater moving at twenty-five knots carries enough kinetic energy to flatten a concrete pier.

"We have to watch the sea temperature every hour," Artie said. "If the water drops below three degrees, we alter course ten degrees to the south, regardless of what Delacroix prints on the Furuno."

"And if the storm low catches us before we clear the cold water?" Clara asked.

"Then we have forty-foot seas with freezing spray," Artie replied calmly. "And you will find out how fast your racing reflexes work in the dark. We keep the watch rotation strict, four hours on, four hours off. Nobody goes on the foredeck alone without a tether clipped to the centerline jackstay."

He folded the chart edge down, recorded the noon sea temperature in the deck log, and checked the secondary bilge pump switch on the main electrical panel. The green indicator light burned steady in the dim cabin.

The geometry of great circle routing was first documented in detail by the Portuguese navigators of the fifteenth century, who understood that sailing a curved arc across the meridians allowed a vessel to take advantage of the prevailing westerly wind systems of the higher latitudes.

Artie picked up his navigation dividers, walking them along the fiftieth parallel.

Between forty-eight north and fifty-one north, the prevailing winds blew from the west and southwest seventy-two percent of the time in autumn. A vessel holding the northern arc could carry fair winds on her quarter for days at a time, surfing down the long ocean swells without tacking.

The cost of that geometry was the cold.

When the wind veered into the northwest, it drew freezing air directly off the glaciers of Greenland and the frozen tundra of Baffin Island. The temperature dropped five degrees in an hour, turning the flying spray into slush on the deck and threatening to coat the rigging with clear glaze ice.

A yacht carrying five tons of top-hamper ice loses her stability curve rapidly. As the weight aloft increases, the vessel's center of gravity rises, reducing her ability to recover from a knockdown. If the mast becomes coated with two inches of rime, a sixty-degree roll can become a permanent capsize.

"We keep the de-icing mallet by the companionway," Artie told Clara, pointing to a heavy rubber-headed mallet clipped to the tool rack. "If the rigging starts to ice up tonight, we take turns going along the deck and striking the shrouds to shatter the rime before it builds."

Clara touched the heavy rubber head of the mallet. "Did you ever have to de-ice a rig at sea, Artie?"

"Once," Artie said, his eyes distant and gray. "Off the coast of Labrador, twenty years ago, on an eighty-foot wooden schooner. We spent eighteen hours beating the halyards with baseball bats while forty-knot winds threw green water into our faces. Every time a shroud cleared, a sheet of ice fell onto the deck like a pane of plate glass. You don't forget that sound."

He unrolled the pilot chart for the North Atlantic, studying the historical gale frequencies. In late October, sector four showed a thirty-five percent probability of sustained Force Eight winds or higher.

"The odds are what they are," Artie said, rolling the chart back into its cylinder. "We sail the boat."

# Chapter 11: False Horizon

The morning brought no division between sea and sky.

Arthur Vance stood in the companionway hatchway with his arms braced against the teak frame. Ahead of the bowsprit, the North Atlantic was a uniform expanse of dark charcoal, the horizon swallowed by a low stratus ceiling that hung three hundred feet above the swells. The wind had backed into the west-northwest, blowing thirty-two knots with the sustained, heavy density of polar air that had rolled down across the ice fields of Davis Strait.

Aura VII was driving east under staysail alone. The knotmeter hovered between eight point eight and nine point four knots, the hull shivering with a high-frequency vibration each time the spade rudders sliced through the foam.

At zero-eight-twenty, the Furuno impact printer started with a harsh metallic scrape.

Artie swung down into the saloon, his sea boots sliding an inch on the damp cabin sole before catching the nonskid rubber mat. Clara was already at the chart table, coffee mug held tight against her chest, watching the paper advance past the platen. Her oilskin jacket was unzipped, revealing the salt-stained thermal fleece she had worn for four days without changing.

The message bore the header of the Canadian Coast Guard Maritime Rescue Sub-Centre in St. John's:

SECURITE // PAN PAN CITATION 04
BROADCAST TO ALL VESSELS NORTH ATLANTIC WESTERN SECTOR
ORIGIN: CROSS JOBOURG / FRENCH MARITIME GENDARMERIE

ATTENTION: MASTERS OF TRANSATLANTIC COMMERCIAL AND PLEASURE CRAFT.
AUTHORITIES SEEK INFORMATION REGARDING JULIAN KROSS, AGE 34, GERMAN NATIONAL.
SUBJECT FLED JURISDICTION AT CHERBOURG ON OCTOBER 19.
SUBJECT MAY BE TRAVELING ABOARD COMMERCIAL YACHT UNDER ASSUMED MARITIME CREDENTIALS.
BELIEVED TO POSSESS PROPRIETARY FINANCIAL ASSETS SUBJECT TO EUROPEAN ARREST WARRANT.

IF SIGHTED, NOTIFY NEAREST MRCC OR RCC HALIFAX.
DO NOT DETAIN INDEPENDENTLY. REPORT POSITION AND PASSENGER LIST.

The printer stuttered and stopped with a dull click.

Artie reached out and took the strip between his fingers. He read the text twice, his thumb resting over the printed name: Julian Kross.

He did not look at Clara, but he could hear the catch in her breathing. She set her mug down on the chart table, the porcelain ringing against the fiddles. Her eyes darted toward the forward corridor where Ward was resting in bunk four.

"Julian," Clara whispered. "That's not Marcus Ward."

"It's a security broadcast," Artie said, folding the paper into his palm. "The Coast Guard sends twenty of them every week. Half the time it's corporate divorce lawyers using police channels to track down company assets."

"The discharge book," Clara said, her voice dropping to a tense whisper that barely carried over the engine rumble. "You looked at it on day one. You locked it in the safe. You knew."

"The discharge book belongs in the safe," Artie replied, keeping his voice level. "And the ship's log says Marcus Ward is signed on as deckhand. A skipper does not conduct criminal interrogations in the middle of a Force Eight gale four hundred miles off soundings."

"He is running," Clara insisted, stepping closer, her hand gripping Artie's forearm with desperate strength. "Look at him. He doesn't sleep. He jumps every time the telex clicks. If the French authorities know he is on a yacht leaving the western Atlantic, they will have a patrol cutter waiting off Horta."

"Then the Portuguese police can talk to him in Horta," Artie said. "Between here and the Azores, he is my deckhand. He steers his four-hour watch and he pulls the halyards when I tell him to pull. If I lock him up, who stands his watch at midnight? You? Tom?"

Clara pulled her hand back, her mouth tight and trembling. "You are protecting him because you want to keep the delivery schedule."

"I am protecting the watch schedule," Artie said flatly. "In three days we have forty-foot seas coming over the deck. If I have three crew members instead of four, we burn out from exhaustion before we sight Pico. A tired watch officer makes mistakes, misses a compass shift, drops a runner into the wheel. That is how boats sink, Clara. Not from French warrants."

He turned away from the chart table, stepping over to the galley where Tom Nowak was boiling water for porridge.

"Tom," Artie said.

"I heard," the engineer replied without turning around. He stirred the gray oats with a wooden spoon. "I know this name. Delacroix. In 2019, he managed two chemical tankers out of Szczecin. Bad ships. Cheap Chinese steel, Russian crews paid half wages, emergency fire pumps that failed every port state inspection. If this boy stole his computer, I say give the boy a medal and two bowls of porridge."

"Nobody gives medals on this boat," Artie said. "And nobody talks about warrants. As far as the deck log is concerned, Marcus Ward is standing the noon watch. Clara, get your foul-weather gear on. We need to check the bilge pump cycle counter before the wind builds."

Clara stared at him for three seconds, her jaw set, then reached for her heavy yellow salopettes hanging by the companionway door.

Artie walked aft along the narrow side deck, checking the tension on the lifelines. The wire was plastic-coated stainless, seized with bronze rigging screws that had begun to weep brown rust stains against the white gelcoat.

He knelt by the aft lazarette hatch, shining his flashlight into the steering cable sheaves. The quartering swell was lifting the rudder post every twelve seconds, the heavy composite quadrant rotating forty degrees in either direction as the autopilot hydraulic ram worked against the sea. A thin spray of clear hydraulic oil had formed a mist across the lower mounting brackets.

"How is the ram pressure holding?" Tom Nowak called up from the aft cabin.

"Holding at one hundred forty bar," Artie called back down. "The seals are weeping slightly around the shaft collar. Nothing critical yet, but we will need to top up the reservoir before we enter the storm track."

Julian Kross came out of the companionway, carrying a fresh coil of eight-millimeter shock cord.

"I checked the forward sail bins, skipper," Julian reported, his voice tight but controlled. "The reacher bags are lashed down. The spare storm jib is flaked in the starboard bin, ready to drag onto the foredeck if the headstay foil fouls."

Artie looked at him closely. The younger man had dark purple rings beneath his eyes, and his hands were raw from salt spray and line handling. But there was no hesitation in his stance. He stood on the rolling deck with his knees flexing naturally, absorbing the heave of the hull like a man who had accepted his place on the ship.

"Good," Artie said. "Get a bowl of porridge into you, Marcus. Then take the helm from Clara. I want her below to work up the four-hour dead reckoning fix."

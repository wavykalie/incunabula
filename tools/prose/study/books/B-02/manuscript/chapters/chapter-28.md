# Chapter 28: The Phantom Signal

The receive-only Furuno printer woke from the dead at zero-three-forty in the morning.

It did not print a clean message. The paper advance motor stuttered with a grinding, irregular chatter, spewing four inches of twisted white roll into the chart table tray before the ink ribbon began hammering against the platen.

Clara Lindholm, who had just come off the steering watch, froze at the navigation desk.

"Artie," she called out, her voice sharp with disbelief. "The Inmarsat receiver. It's printing."

Arthur Vance pushed himself up on the saloon settee, groaning as the tension pulled against his fractured ribs. He braced his arm against the table and stood, taking two slow, agonizing steps across the cabin sole.

The satellite uplink had been dead for four days, its SIM card blocked by the shore dispatcher in Monaco. But the automated emergency receiver circuit, wired directly to the international GMDSS maritime safety channel, was hard-coded into the receiver's firmware, designed to bypass administrative account locks whenever a Category One automated distress guide was detected within two hundred nautical miles.

Artie leaned over the chart desk, reading the smudged black dot-matrix type as it emerged from the printer:

EMERGENCY DISTRESS RELAY // AUTOMATED SATELLITE EPIRB CITATION
guide ID: 412A-8890-FF01
VESSEL: M/V NORDIC PIONEER // CALLSIGN LAHP8
TYPE: GENERAL CARGO // 8,200 DWT // NORWEGIAN REGISTRY
LAST POSITION: 42°18'N, 38°44'W // TIMESTAMP: 0312Z

NATURE OF DISTRESS: STRUCTURAL FAILURE // WATER INGRESS HOLD NUMBER TWO.
CREW ABANDONING SHIP INTO TWO TWENTY-PERSON INFLATABLE LIFERAFTS.
WINDS SUSTAINED 52 KNOTS // SEAS 9 METRES.
NO SEARCH AND RESCUE ASSETS IN RANGE.
ALL SHIPS IN VICINITY REQUESTED TO KEEP SHARP LOOKOUT AND RENDER ASSISTANCE.

The printer fed three inches of blank paper, gave a violent mechanical convulsion, and died once more, its internal circuits finally burning out under the strain.

Clara picked up the paper strip, her hands trembling as she read the coordinates.

"Nordic Pioneer," she whispered. "An eight-thousand-ton cargo ship. Sinking in the same gale we are sailing through."

She immediately reached for the Admiralty paper chart, laying her plastic parallel rule across the latitude and longitude scales.

She made a small pencil cross on the blue expanse, eight miles north of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge contour lines.

She picked up her brass dividers, measured the distance from their estimated dead-reckoning position, and looked up at Artie with wide, horrified eyes.

"Artie," she said.

"How far?" Artie asked, his voice flat and hard.

"Twenty-two nautical miles," Clara said. "Bearing one-six-five true. Directly across our windward quarter."

The saloon went completely still.

Twenty-two miles in flat water was two and a half hours of steaming under auxiliary engine power.

In a Force Ten gale with fifty-knot winds, thirty-five-foot breaking seas, a fractured steering quadrant held together by Spectra lashings, an empty freshwater tank, and a delivery skipper with two broken ribs, twenty-two miles across the wind was an almost suicidal detour.

Julian Kross and Tom Nowak stood in the companionway doorway, their faces pale under their hoods, listening in stunned silence.

"A cargo ship has thirty crew members," Tom murmured, crossing himself with his scarred right hand. "Two liferafts in nine-meter seas. If they are in the water, their survival time in this temperature is less than six hours."

"We are a delivery yacht," Julian said, his voice shaking with sudden, instinctive panic. "We are crippled! Our steering is lashed with ropes! We have broken ribs! We can't save thirty people on a fifty-four-foot racing boat!"

Artie Vance looked down at the penciled cross on the paper chart.

He did not look at Julian. He did not look at his delivery sheet or the Monaco contract locked in the safe beneath the desk.

He looked at his brass dividers resting on the chart paper, their points measuring the twenty-two miles between life and death on the open Atlantic.

The second half of the storm hit with the force of a hammer.

The wind came out of the north-northwest, twenty degrees colder than the southern air, driving rain that felt like buckshot against their faces. The barometer, which had bottomed out at nine-hundred-seventy-eight millibars, began to rise with terrifying speed, jumping two millibars in thirty minutes.

"A rising glass and a northwest wind," Artie shouted, his hood pulled down over his brow. "That is where the teeth are."

The boat was on a port tack now, running under bare poles while the series drogue held the stern firm against the steep, breaking seas. The waves were steeper now, shorter in period, five seconds apart, their crests tumbling into white avalanches that buried the aft deck up to the coamings.

Clara sat by the companionway sill, monitoring the steering gear through the open cockpit hatch. The hydraulic autopilot ram was disconnected to prevent damage from wave strikes, and the emergency tiller was lashed amidships with two lengths of half-inch braided line.

"Tiller lines are holding," she signaled with a thumb.

Artie watched the compass card in the binnacle. The red lubber line wandered between one-hundred-forty and one-hundred-sixty degrees. Every time a sea broke against the quarter, the boat lifted with a sharp, buoyant roll, shook off the green water through the open scuppers, and gathered herself for the next surge.

"She is a well-built vessel," Noah said, his voice quiet with relief. He was wedged into the corner of the cockpit bench, his hands tucked inside his foul-weather pockets for warmth.

"She was built in Sweden," Artie replied. "The yard owner built them for the North Sea. They put twenty-four layers of roving in the keel stub and laminated the bulkheads directly to the hull before they dropped the deck in place. Modern production boats put a molded fiberglass tray inside with glue. In a sea like this, a glued tray pops loose from the hull like an eggshell."

Noah nodded, watching a sheet of spray fly fifty feet into the darkness ahead of the bow. "And the engine? When do we try to run the fuel?"

"When the wind drops under thirty knots," Artie said. "We cannot work with open diesel lines in this motion. One spark from a dropped wrench against the alternator casing, and we have an engine fire with nowhere to run."

The violent rolling that accompanied the wind shift tested every bulkhead joint in the boat. With the series drogue still streaming astern, the sudden arrival of the northwest gale crossed the older southern swell at an angle of eighty degrees. The water became a chaotic field of liquid peaks, colliding and throwing white geysers thirty feet into the gale.

Artie climbed to the companionway, sliding the heavy acrylic washboard up three inches to peer through the louver.

The surface of the sea was obscured by drifting sheets of white spindrift that looked like snow in a mountain blizzard. The anemometer needle on the bulkhead was pinned against its stop at sixty knots.

"Check the rig with the spotlight," Artie said to Clara.

She opened the companionway hatch an inch, thrust the heavy beam of the five-hundred-lumen halogen light up into the driving spray, and illuminated the spar. The aluminum extrusion was curving in a gentle, uniform arc, but the intermediate shrouds and lower stays were drum-tight, holding the middle of the mast securely in column.

"Mast is standing straight," she called down, latching the hatch tight. "No pump in the section."

"That is the benefit of continuous standing rigging," Artie said, checking the mast collar at the partners. "The rod rigging on modern racers has less windage, but if a cold heading fails at the swage, the whole spar drops over the side like a snapped carrot. Stainless wire gives you warning. It breaks one strand at a time, giving you hours to notice and set a preventive halyard."

He sat down on the nav station stool, his legs braced wide. On the paper chart, he updated their drift vector. Over the last six hours, the drogue had carried them eleven miles to the southeast, deep into safe water, far clear of the shallow banks off Flores.

"Ten hours more of this," he calculated aloud. "By dawn, the cold front will have swept east of the Azores, and the northwest blow will ease to thirty knots."

# Chapter 37: Rounding Monte da Guia

The volcanic headland of Monte da Guia rose eight hundred feet out of the deep Atlantic, its steep, rust-red basalt cliffs glowing like burning embers in the late afternoon sun.

Arthur Vance stood on the cockpit sole, his hand resting on the carbon binnacle.

The ocean swell, which had carried them across two thousand miles of gray water, flattened abruptly as *Aura VII* slipped into the lee of Faial. The violent rolling and corkscrewing that had punished their bodies for twelve days died away, replaced by the smooth, effortless glide of forty tons of carbon slicing through calm, turquoise-blue coastal water.

Clara Lindholm stood at the forward edge of the cockpit, her handheld bearing compass pressed against her cheekbone, calling pilotage fixes with the sharp, rhythmic precision of a naval navigator.

"Ponta da Espalamaca transit line bearing two-four-two," Clara announced. "Depth thirty-eight meters, shelving to twenty-four. Breakwater entrance is clear. No inbound commercial traffic on the AIS."

Artie looked at her. Her back was straight, her shoulders squared, her eyes sharp and luminous in the golden sunlight. The disgrace of the Fastnet inquiry, the bitter humiliation of the French court hearings, and the ghost of the *Vanguard* collision had been burned away in the screaming cauldron of the Force Ten gale. She was no longer a disgraced second mate defending her license before shore lawyers; she was the master navigator who had guided a crippled yacht through thirty-five-foot seas on a dead-reckoning plot without losing a single mile.

"Take the wheel, Clara," Artie said calmly.

Clara turned, looking at him in surprise. "Artie, you should take her into port. You are the skipper."

"My ribs are taped and my ankle is stiff," Artie said, offering her the spokes of the carbon wheel. "And you have the transit line aligned to half a degree. Take her into the breakwater, navigator. She is your ship."

Clara hesitated for half a second. Then her gloved hands closed around the carbon rim.

Beneath the hull, the temporary Spectra steering lashings answered her touch with immediate, sensitive precision. She eased the helm two degrees to starboard, bringing the bowsprit directly into line with the green harbor light on the southern breakwater mole.

Julian Kross and Tom Nowak stood on the foredeck, arranging the heavy white pneumatic fenders along the port lifelines and flaking out the braided nylon mooring warps.

The harbor of Horta opened before them like an amphitheater of colored stone.

Along the waterfront promenade, the whitewashed Portuguese colonial buildings reflected the evening sun, their red-tiled roofs climbing the lush green hills toward the volcanic caldera above. The famous harbor breakwater wall, painted with thousands of colorful murals, crests, and dates left by generations of ocean voyagers who had crossed the Atlantic before them, stretched out to welcome their battered cutter.

On the commercial fuel pier, a small group of harbor officials, local fishermen, and marina attendants stood watching the sleek racing yacht glide into the basin under bare poles and the quiet purr of her auxiliary engine.

Among them stood two officers in dark blue uniforms: the Portuguese Maritime Police, their white peaked caps catching the low sun.

Clara throttled the Volvo back to eight hundred RPM, the propeller wash foaming white against the transom as she aligned the yacht's hull with the wooden pilings of the fuel dock.

"Fenders down port side!" Clara called forward, her voice ringing clear and authoritative across the calm harbor water. "Warps ready fore and aft!"

Julian looked back at Artie from the bow, his face pale as he saw the police uniforms on the pier.

Artie gave him a single, imperceptible nod. A silent promise carved from twelve days of cold salt water: *I have the watch.*

They walked along the harbor promenade in the late afternoon sun, their sea legs making them roll slightly on the firm basalt cobblestones. The earth felt strangely motionless, unnatural to their balance after thousands of miles of ocean heave.

Peter Café Sport sat across the road from the marina gates, its white stucco walls and red-framed windows famous to every sailor who had ever crossed the North Atlantic. Inside, the dark oak room smelled of stale beer, pipe tobacco, wax, and roasted coffee. The ceiling and walls were covered with hundreds of faded pennants, burgees, and signed nautical charts left by circumnavigators and delivery skippers over the past eighty years.

Artie found a small corner table near the window and sat down. Noah and Clara took the wooden bench opposite him.

The waiter brought three glasses of draught beer and a platter of crusty Portuguese bread, soft goat cheese, and small black olives. No one spoke for the first five minutes. They drank the cold beer in deep, steady swallows, feeling the malt cut through the salt that had lined their throats for two weeks.

"My hands have stopped shaking," Clara said, looking down at her fingers curled around the glass. "On the third day of the gale, I thought the mast step would punch through the hull."

"The mast step is solid bronze through bolted to the keelson," Artie said, cutting a piece of cheese with his pocketknife. "The boat was never in danger. Only the crew is in danger when they stop thinking ahead."

Noah reached into his pocket and pulled out the small brass key to the yacht's companionway lock. He laid it in the center of the wooden table.

"The broker's surveyor will be here at eight tomorrow morning?" Noah asked.

"Eight sharp," Artie said. "We will have the decks washed down and the cabin aired out before he steps on board."

After the harbor master had stamped their papers, the customs officers from the Alfandega came aboard to perform their official inspection. Two courteous Portuguese officers in dark blue uniforms stepped into the salon, carrying clipboards and flashlights.

They checked the vessel's liquor locker, verifying that the bonded stores remained sealed, and examined the medical kit to ensure the controlled narcotics were accounted for in the captain's log.

"Very neat logbook, Captain Vance," the senior customs officer observed, running his finger down the page where Artie had documented every position, weather observation, and maintenance action from Hamilton to Horta. "Few delivery skippers keep records with this degree of detail."

"A ship's log is a legal document," Artie replied. "If you do not write it down when it happens, it did not happen."

The officer smiled, stamped their customs declaration with the green seal of the European Union, and handed the forms back to Artie.

"Your vessel is cleared for entry into the customs territory of Portugal," the officer declared. "You may lower your yellow quarantine flag and hoist the Portuguese courtesy ensign on your starboard spreader."

Noah was on deck within two minutes. He unhitched the yellow Q flag from the signal halyard, hooked on the green-and-red flag of Portugal, and hoisted it cleanly to the spreader tip, making the lanyard fast to the bronze shroud cleat with a neat clove hitch.

From the dock, several cruising sailors looked over and offered quiet nods of approval. Cormorant was no longer an arrival under quarantine; she was an accepted guest in the ancient port of Faial.

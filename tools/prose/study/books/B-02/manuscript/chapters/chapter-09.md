# Chapter 09: Confrontation by the Binnacle

The smell of burnt toast hung heavy in the main saloon at seven-thirty the following morning.

The sea state had deteriorated into a steep, chaotic cross-swell as the wind veered into the west-southwest. Aura VII was corkscrewing violently, her carbon hull shuddering every forty seconds as an eight-foot breaking crest slammed against her port forward quarter, sending a shockwave through the bulkheads that rattled the mugs in the galley rack.

Tomasz Nowak stood at the galley stove, his arm braced against the stainless grab rail, buttering slices of dense, dark bread with a short-bladed knife. His face was gray with fatigue.

"The fuel is worse," Tom said, speaking directly to Artie, who was seated at the chart table. "I drained seventy milliliters of salt water at six o'clock. The main tank has maybe twenty hours left before the emulsification reaches the suction pickup. If we don't switch to the forward reserve, we will suck air and mud into the injection pump."

Clara Lindholm sat on the opposite settee, her laptop wedged between two foam cushions. She had not slept during her off-watch. Her eyes were bloodshot, her blonde hair tangled and damp around her ears.

"We cannot switch to the forward reserve," Clara said sharply. "The forward reserve is one hundred fifty liters. That is our emergency reserve for the Azores approaches. If we burn it now to maintain eight knots on a flat rhumb line, we will have zero fuel when we reach the shelf off Faial."

"Then we slow the engine," Tom replied, his voice rising with gravelly irritation. "We run at twelve hundred RPM instead of eighteen hundred. We save the pump. We save the injectors. This is not a Formula One race, Clara. This is a diesel engine with four cylinders. If the pump scores from salt water, you cannot fix it with an algorithm."

"If we slow to twelve hundred RPM, our speed drops to five knots," Clara snapped, turning her screen toward the table. "Look at the barometer graph. Just look at it."

She tapped the glass face of her USB barograph sensor.

The digital readout showed: PRESSURE: 1004.2 HPA // TREND: -5.4 HPA / 3 HR.

"A five-millibar drop in three hours," Clara said, her voice dropping into a hard, clinical cadence. "The official definition of a maritime bomb low is twenty-four millibars in twenty-four hours. We are exceeding that rate right now. The depression is not tracking east toward the Bay of Biscay. The core has slowed down and is deepening directly over the Flemish Cap, forty miles ahead of our bowsprit."

Julian Kross sat in the corner of the leeward settee, his arms pulled tight inside his fleece jacket, his face pale and silent. He had not spoken since Artie caught him at the chartplotter six hours earlier. He watched the others with the darting, guarded gaze of a prisoner awaiting a verdict.

Artie stood up from the chart table. He did not look at the computer screen. He walked two steps across the cabin, reached up to the brass aneroid barometer mounted on the teak bulkhead above the barometer shelf, and tapped the heavy beveled glass twice with his middle knuckle.

The black needle quivered, dropping half a millimeter below the brass reference pointer he had set at dawn.

One thousand four millibars.

"The glass doesn't lie," Artie said calmly.

"Then we alter course," Clara demanded, standing up and confronting him across the narrow table. "We have twenty-five knots of wind now. By noon it will be forty. By midnight it will be sixty. If we turn south to one-two-zero right now, we can skirt the outer edge of the storm track. We will have heavy seas, but we won't have breaking crests on the beam."

"And what do you tell Delacroix in Monaco?" Artie asked, his eyes fixing on her.

"I don't tell Delacroix anything!" Clara shouted, her composure cracking for the first time since St. John's. "Delacroix is twelve hundred miles away in an apartment eating croissants! He is not standing in this cockpit watching thirty-foot walls of black water roll out of the fog! I am not dying in the Atlantic to save your retirement pension, Artie!"

The cabin went dead silent.

Even the sizzle of the butter on Tom's frying pan seemed to freeze in the cold air.

Tom stopped buttering his bread. He slowly set the knife down on the cutting board, wiped his hands on his apron, and looked at Artie with a neutral, unreadable expression.

Julian Kross shrank back against the settee cushion, his eyes fixed on the floorboards.

Artie did not flinch. He stood perfectly still, his boots planted wide, absorbing the sharp roll of the yacht with his knees. His weathered face was like carved driftwood, lined by forty years of salt, wind, and financial defeat.

"You finished, Clara?" he asked softly.

She swallowed, her chest heaving beneath her thermal sweater, her cheeks flushing red with a mixture of anger and shame. "I am stating the navigational reality."

"You are stating your fear," Artie said, without malice. "Which is normal for someone who has never skippered a boat through a Force Ten gale without a chase boat and a helicopter standing by."

He turned toward Julian Kross.

"Mr. Ward," Artie said.

Julian jumped slightly. "Yes, skipper?"

"Go on deck. Check the lashings on the liferaft canister on the coachroof. Check the split pins on the staysail turnbuckles. Use the heavy pliers from the companionway rack."

"Yes, sir," Julian said, scrambling to his feet with desperate relief, eager to escape the suffocating tension of the saloon.

Artie waited until the companionway hatch closed behind the younger man.

Then he looked at Clara and Tom.

"We don't turn south to forty-one north," Artie said, his voice flat, steady, and immovable. "And we don't hold the Monaco rhumb line into the center of the low. We take the middle track. We bear away fifteen degrees to zero-nine-seven. That puts the sea on our port quarter, gives us nine knots of hull speed under the staysail, and keeps us forty miles clear of the storm's circulation core."

He tapped the barometer glass one more time.

"We prepare the boat for a blow," Artie said. "Tom, drain the fuel filter again. Clara, put the paper chart in the waterproof sleeve and set the radar watch alarm for six miles. If we are going to take a beating, we take it as a crew, not a committee."

The argument in the saloon had left a bitter, sour taste in the air that even Tom's chicory coffee could not wash away.

Artie sat on the navigation bench, his dividers resting on the chart paper, his mind working through the tactical possibilities.

If he obeyed Clara and altered course to one-two-zero, the yacht would run south across the prevailing weather tracks, entering the warmer waters of the Gulf Stream. The seas would be gentler, the temperatures ten degrees higher, and the risk of structural failure greatly reduced. But the distance to Horta would increase by nearly five hundred miles.

With their contaminated fuel tanks, five hundred extra miles meant running out of diesel three hundred miles from landfall. In the light, variable breezes of the horse latitudes, a fifty-four-foot performance cutter could drift for ten days, her crew rationing water and biscuits while the calendar ticked past the delivery deadline.

If he obeyed Monaco and held the northern great circle on fifty-two north, the yacht would save four hundred miles and ride the fast westerly gales straight into the English Channel approaches. But they would sail directly across the path of the deepening cyclonic bomb, facing forty-foot breaking seas with twin carbon rudders that had never been tested in sustained Force Ten conditions.

The middle track, heading zero-nine-seven, was a compromise, but it was the only course that preserved both the boat and the crew.

Tom Nowak came over to the chart desk, holding a clean rag and a can of silicone spray.

"The girl is frightened, Artie," Tom said, his gravelly voice low and sympathetic. "She remembers the collision inquiry. When a ship sinks and people die, it stays in the blood. Every time the boat heels, she sees that green navigation light in the fog."

"I know what she sees," Artie replied, his eyes on the paper chart. "I see the auction block in Camden every time the engine coughs. We all carry our ghosts out here, Tom."

"Then don't be too hard on her," Tom said. "She is a good sailor. Her navigation is sharp. She just needs to know that the skipper has both hands on the wheel."

"I have the wheel, Tom," Artie said. "I have the wheel."

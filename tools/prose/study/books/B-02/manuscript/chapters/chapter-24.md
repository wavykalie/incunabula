# Chapter 24: The Quadrant Emergency

The temperature inside the aft lazarette was forty degrees hotter than the deck, filled with the choking stench of aerosolized hydraulic fluid and scorched rubber.

Tomasz Nowak lay wedged between the hull stringers and the steering quadrant, his broad back jammed against the upper carbon deckhead, his scarred left hand gripping the five-foot steel pry bar with every ounce of strength remaining in his forty-five-year-old body.

Every ten seconds, when an ocean crest struck the loose port rudder blade outside, the heavy steel bar bucked in Tom's hands, sending violent shockwaves through his shoulders that made him groan through clenched teeth.

"I cannot hold it forever, Artie!" Tom bellowed into the darkness. "The water pressure is two tons! My ribs are going to crack!"

"Hold it for sixty seconds!" Arthur Vance shouted back.

Artie was kneeling on the slick composite floorboards, working in the glare of Julian's waterproof headlamp. In his hands, he held the yacht's emergency stainless steel tiller, an L-shaped arm designed to slot directly over the squared head of the carbon rudder stock if the steering cables parted.

The problem was alignment.

The shattered aluminum quadrant had twisted twenty degrees around the carbon shaft, jamming its jagged, torn teeth directly over the squared emergency fitting. Until the broken metal was cleared, the emergency tiller could not seat down onto the drive head.

"Julian!" Artie barked. "The cold chisel and the two-pound sledgehammer! From the red steel box!"

Julian scrambled through the narrow bulkhead opening, returning three seconds later with the heavy steel tools. His face was encrusted with black grease and salt crystals, his eyes burning with adrenaline.

"Hold the chisel on the sheared quadrant collar," Artie ordered, positioning the hardened steel blade against the cracked aluminum casting. "Keep your fingers clear of the head!"

Julian placed both hands around the knurled shank of the chisel, bracing his forehead against the hull framing to steady himself against the rolling surge of the yacht.

Artie swung the two-pound sledgehammer.

The steel struck steel with an ear-splitting ring that echoed through the carbon hull like a depth charge.

The impact jarred Julian to his teeth, but the chisel held.

Artie swung again. And again. Four massive, measured blows, delivered with the deadly, rhythmic precision of a shipyard blacksmith.

On the fifth strike, the cracked aluminum collar fractured completely. The broken half of the quadrant fell away into the bilge with a heavy splash, exposing the square milled head of the carbon rudder stock.

"Clear!" Julian yelled, dragging the broken metal out of the way.

Artie immediately slammed the socket of the emergency tiller over the squared shaft. The heavy stainless tube slid home with a solid, satisfying thunk.

"Tom, ease the pry bar!" Artie commanded.

Tom released the pressure on the crowbar. The rudder stock attempted to kick violently to port, but Artie threw his weight across the three-foot stainless tiller arm, arresting the motion with his arms and knees.

"Pass the Spectra lines!" Artie yelled.

Julian handed him the pre-cut lengths of ten-millimeter Dyneema cord.

Working together in the cramped compartment, Artie and Julian lashed two heavy snatch blocks to the reinforced hull frames on either side of the lazarette. They ran the Spectra lines from the end of the emergency tiller through the blocks, leading the bitter ends up through two drilled access holes in the cockpit floor directly to the secondary self-tailing winches.

Ten minutes later, Artie climbed up through the lazarette hatch into the freezing gale.

He stepped to the port cockpit winch, threw four turns of Spectra around the drum, and ground the handle.

Ten feet below the deck, the emergency tiller moved. Beneath the hull, the port rudder blade responded, biting into the turbulent Atlantic current.

The yacht answered the helm immediately, her bow swinging back onto heading zero-nine-five, running square before the sixty-knot blast of the gale once more.

Artie stood at the winch, his chest heaving, his hands trembling with exhaustion.

Beside him, Julian Kross collapsed against the coaming, looking down at his torn, bleeding palms. A slow, exhausted smile broke across the younger man's face.

"She steers, Captain," Julian whispered. "She steers on the winches."

Artie looked down at him, wiping a smear of black gear grease from his own forehead. "She steers, Mr. Ward. Get Clara on the deck. We have to set up a two-man watch on the winch handles until the gale breaks."

Working inside the engine compartment in an eight-foot beam sea was an exercise in pure physics. Noah wedged his ribs against the teak bulkhead, his knees braced against the battery box, holding an open-end wrench on the brass adapter while Artie worked the flaring tool.

The motion was erratic, the hull dropping ten feet into wave troughs and stopping with a shudder that jarred their teeth.

"Hold the backup wrench steady," Artie muttered. "If we round the corners on this block fitting, we will have to drill it out, and I do not have a tap that size on board."

Noah leaned into the steel wrench, sweat cutting white streaks through the diesel soot on his cheeks. "Held. You have half a turn."

Artie turned the feed screw of the flaring tool, feeling the cone expand the mouth of the fresh copper tube into a clean forty-five-degree flare. He inspected the seat under the flashlight beam. The copper was smooth, free of burrs or score marks. He slipped the brass compression nut over the tubing, pushed the flare against the adapter cone, and ran the threads in with his bare fingers until they seated.

"Two flats with the twelve-millimeter," Artie commanded.

He tightened the nut until the copper flared against the brass with a solid, unyielding stop.

"Now the filter," Noah said, pointing to the secondary fuel canister beside the alternator. "While we are down here, look at the sight bowl. There is water in the bottom ring."

Artie held the flashlight to the clear bowl of the Racor filter. A distinct brown meniscus separated the straw-colored diesel from an inch of cloudy seawater at the drain valve.

"Condensation from the starboard tank," Artie noted. "Open the petcock into the empty coffee tin. Drain it until you see clean fuel."

Noah reached under the bowl and turned the brass wing nut. A stream of brown fluid splashed into the tin, followed by a burst of amber diesel. He closed the petcock tight and wiped the glass with his sleeve.

"Clean," Noah said. "Now we bleed the secondary pump."

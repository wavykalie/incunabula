# Chapter 04: Bread and Diesel

At zero-three-thirty, the only sound belowdecks was the deep, rhythmic grind of the Volvo diesel and the slap of salt water against the carbon hull framing.

Tomasz Nowak stood in the galley with his knees bent, counteracting the violent, corkscrewing roll of the yacht as she slid off the back of a ten-foot swell. On the stainless steel counter, held in place by four adjustable aluminum fiddles, sat a heavy plastic bowl containing two kilograms of dense rye dough.

Tom worked the dough with his right hand and the scarred stump of his left. His movements were slow, rhythmic, and practiced. He had learned to bake in the galley of an ice-class bulk carrier running iron ore from Murmansk to Rotterdam, where five thousand tons of cargo held the ship steady in any sea, unlike this lightweight plastic racing toy that skipped across the surface like an empty oil drum.

He folded the dough over itself, pressed his knuckles deep into the gray mass, and turned it ninety degrees. The smell of sourdough yeast mingled with the lingering odor of warm lube oil drifting forward from the engine hatch behind the companionway stairs.

Every two hours, on the half-hour, Tom performed his inspection. It was not a duty written into the delivery contract, but it was the law that had kept him alive on sixty thousand tons of vibrating steel across forty years. You do not trust a fuel gauge; you dip the tank. You do not trust an oil light; you pull the steel dipstick and smell the film on your thumb.

He wiped his hands on a clean linen towel, pulled a small brass flashlight from his breast pocket, and unlatched the teak steps of the companionway.

The engine bay was a narrow, insulated box squeezed between the cockpit floor and the main saloon bulkhead. Inside, the seventy-five-horsepower Volvo D2-75 sat on its rubber vibration mounts, humming with a hot, greasy heat. The alternator belt spun in a blur of black rubber, throwing a faint, dry friction scent into the air.

Tom knelt on the rubber matting, shining his light into the primary Racor fuel filter assembly mounted on the forward bulkhead.

The clear polycarbonate sight-bowl at the base of the filter was designed to trap sediment and water before the diesel reached the secondary engine filter and the high-pressure injection pump. Three hours ago, during his evening round, the fuel in the bowl had been the pale, clear amber of pure marine gas oil.

Now, beneath the beam of his flashlight, a cloudy white emulsification floated at the bottom of the bowl.

Tom leaned closer, his brow furrowing. Above the white emulsion, the fuel was clouded with fine suspended droplets. Water. At least fifty milliliters of raw salt or condensation water had collected in the sump of the filter, trapped by the centrifugal turbine and the ten-micron paper element.

He pulled a clean glass sample jar from his tool kit and wedged it beneath the brass drain petcock at the bottom of the bowl. With his thumb and forefinger, he turned the knurled valve counterclockwise.

A thin stream of yellowish sludge drained into the glass jar.

Tom brought the jar up to his nose and sniffed. It smelled of diesel, but beneath the petroleum was the sharp, briny stench of stagnant seawater. He dipped the tip of his finger into the liquid and touched it to the tip of his tongue.

Salt. Distinct, sharp salt.

"Cholera," Tom whispered.

Condensation in a fuel tank produced fresh water, clear and odorless, pooling at the bottom through gravity. Salt water in the fuel meant only one thing: seawater was entering the bunker system from the outside. Either the fuel vent line on the port transom had dipped below the waterline during a heavy heel, or the filler cap on the side deck had a perished O-ring that allowed green water washing down the side deck to seep past the threads into the main tank.

He closed the petcock, wiped the brass fitting with a rag, and checked the secondary filter on the engine block. The secondary unit was clean, but if the primary bowl filled with water while the yacht was running downwind in a gale, the engine would starve within twenty seconds. In sixty knots of wind, losing auxiliary power meant losing the hydraulic pumps for the winches and the alternator that kept the autopilot and navigation electronics alive.

Tom pulled himself out of the engine compartment, latched the wooden steps, and walked forward to the chart table where Clara Lindholm sat monitoring the radar overlay.

"Clara," Tom said.

She glanced up, her eyes ringed with tiredness. "Yes, Tom?"

"We have seawater in the main fuel tank."

She stared at him for two seconds, processing the technical reality. "How much?"

"Fifty milliliters in the Racor bowl over two hours. That means the fuel is dirty at the bottom of the tank. If we burn two liters an hour, the suction pipe is pulling water right off the floor."

"Did you tell Artie?"

"Artie is on the helm. I tell you first because you have the tank diagrams on the computer." Tom set the glass jar on the chart table. The layer of gray water had already settled to the bottom of the amber diesel like lead shot in oil. "Show me the fuel vent pipe routing on the structural drawing."

Clara clicked her mouse, pulling up the naval architect's CAD layout of the hull. She zoomed in on the port aft quarter.

"Here," she said, pointing with a pencil. "The tank vent terminates in the transom coaming, four inches below the deck line. It has a gooseneck, but on port tack with twenty-five degrees of heel, the vent exit is dragged through the quarter wave every time a swell passes."

"Stupid," Tom spat. "Racing architects. They put the vent low so the transom looks sleek for the yacht shows in Cannes. Every time the boat heels to port, the engine takes a drink of the Atlantic."

"Can we isolate the forward tank?" Clara asked.

"The forward reserve has clean fuel," Tom said. "One hundred fifty liters. But the selector valve is under the bunk in the forward cabin. Where Ward is sleeping."

"Then go wake him."

Tom shook his head slowly, looking down at his bread dough. "I wait until zero-four-hundred watch change. If I switch to the forward reserve now, we burn our emergency reserve while the gale is still building. Better I drain the Racor bowl every hour by hand. Keep the main tank running until the filter screams."

He picked up his wooden dough scraper, shaped the sourdough into a round ball, and placed it inside a greased baking tin. He slid the tin into the unlit oven to rise in the residual warmth of the galley stove.

Above them on deck, the wind rose another three knots, shrieking through the spreader roots with a high, thin whistle that made the cabin sides hum. Tom wiped down the stainless counter, picked up his grease gun, and moved toward the steering quadrant access hatch in the aft lazarette. A ship was only as good as the bearings that turned her rudders, and salt water in the diesel was only the first symptom of a vessel built for speed rather than survival.

Tom wiped the stainless steel counter with an oily cotton rag, cleaning away the scattered flour dust before the rolling of the yacht could grind it into the floorboards. In an ocean galley, cleanliness was not an aesthetic choice; it was a fire prevention rule. A spilled jar of cooking oil or a loose paper towel on a gimballed stove could turn fifty-four feet of carbon composite into an uncontrollable firestorm in less than three minutes.

He opened the small lower cabinet beneath the galley sink, checking the secondary seawater intake valve that supplied cooling water to the refrigeration condenser. The bronze valve body was dry, showing no signs of galvanic corrosion or weeping packings.

Tom reached into his pocket and drew out a worn leather pouch containing two spare fuel injection nozzles wrapped in waxed paper. He had bought them with his own money in Halifax two months ago, keeping them in his sea kit as an insurance policy against cheap charter maintenance.

"The iron staysail," Tom whispered to himself, looking through the companionway steps toward the engine bay. "Take care of the iron, and the iron will take care of you."

He pulled a fresh stick of nicotine gum from his pocket, chewed it vigorously until the pepper taste filled his mouth, and climbed the steps to inspect the cockpit winch bearings.

The salt was everywhere. It formed a white crust along the edges of the aluminum winch drums, grinding into the pawl springs whenever the line turned. Tom took a squeeze bottle of fresh mineral oil and squirted three drops into the oiling hole of each primary winch, listening with professional satisfaction as the pawls clicked with a crisp, oily snap: click-click-click-click.

Out on the dark water, the swells were growing longer, the black faces rolling out of the north with the slow, implacable cadence of deep water. The cutter drove through them without hesitation, her bowsprit slicing the foam, her sails pulling steady in the cold night.

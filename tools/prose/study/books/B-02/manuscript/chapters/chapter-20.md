# Chapter 20: The Barometer Cliff

The fall of the barometer began at fourteen-thirty on the ninth afternoon.

Arthur Vance was sitting at the chart table, recording the hourly log entry, when he noticed the needle of the brass aneroid barometer shudder.

He leaned closer, watching the black pointer through the beveled glass.

In the space of four minutes, the needle visibly moved. It did not drift with the slow, almost imperceptible creep of a standard ocean depression. It slipped downward across the calibration marks like the second hand of a mechanical clock, dropping through nine-nine-four, nine-nine-zero, nine-eight-six.

Artie tapped the glass with his finger.

The needle jumped another two millibars to the left, settling at nine-eight-two hectopascals.

A twelve-millibar drop in two hours.

"Clara," Artie called, his voice dropping into the flat, razor-sharp tone that brought everyone in the cabin to instant attention. "Come here."

Clara dropped down the companionway steps, her face glistening with salt spray from her watch. She looked at the barometer, then looked at Artie.

"That's impossible," she said, her voice barely a whisper. "Barometers don't fall six millibars an hour unless, "

"Unless the cyclogenesis center has turned directly into our track," Artie finished the sentence for her.

He stepped over to the USB barograph sensor on Clara's laptop. The digital graph was no longer a sloping curve; it was a vertical cliff, the black pixel line plunging off the bottom edge of the screen like a severed wire.

Outside, the character of the wind underwent an abrupt, terrifying transformation.

The steady thirty-knot breeze died completely in the space of three minutes. The orange staysail, which had been pulling hard for forty-eight hours, collapsed against the forestay with a dull, heavy flap. The ocean surface became unnaturally slick, covered in an oily, gray sheen that rolled in massive, disorganized swells eight meters high, crisscrossing from three different directions without a breath of air to steady the hull.

Aura VII wallowed in the troughs, rolling violently from gunwale to gunwale, the carbon mast whipping through thirty-degree arcs that threatened to snap the spreader brackets.

Tomasz Nowak came out of the engine compartment, holding an adjustable pipe wrench. "The wind is gone. Why is the wind gone?"

"We are in the eye of the secondary low," Clara said, her eyes fixed on the dead barograph screen. "The depression didn't track northeast into Iceland. The cold front stalled, looped back across the Gulf Stream, and spawned an explosive tropical-transition bomb right over the Mid-Atlantic Trench."

"How deep is the central pressure?" Artie asked.

"If the glass is reading nine-eight-two now, the center will be nine-six-five or lower," Clara said, her voice shaking slightly. "That is hurricane-strength pressure, Artie. That is sustained sixty-five knots with gusts over eighty."

Julian Kross stood at the companionway opening, looking out at the sky.

To the northwest, the gray stratus ceiling had been torn away. In its place rose a monstrous wall of black, boiling cumulus clouds that stretched from the ocean surface to thirty thousand feet, advancing across the water at forty knots. The leading edge of the cloud wall was white with violent, pulverized spray, kicked up by winds of immense velocity.

"How long do we have before the wind hits?" Julian asked, his voice cracked with dry terror.

Artie looked at the approaching black wall.

"Ten minutes," Artie said. "Maybe eight."

He turned to the three crew members, his voice ringing through the cabin with the absolute, uncompromising command of an ocean master preparing his ship for survival.

"All hands in full offshore gear," Artie ordered. "Double safety tethers on every safety line. Tom, dog down the forward watertight hatch with the bronze wedges. Clara, shut every seacock in the engine room except the engine raw-water intake. Julian, get the storm jib bag onto the coachroof and lash it to the mast base."

He looked at each of them in turn, his gray eyes locking onto theirs with an iron calm that cut through the mounting panic.

"We are taking a Force Ten gale in the open ocean," Artie said. "If you are on deck, you clip your tether to the jackline before your boots leave the companionway. If a wave washes you over the side, we cannot turn this boat around to get you. You stay with the ship, or you stay in the Atlantic. Now move."

The silence in the eye of the depression was an acoustic vacuum that set every nerve on the yacht vibrating with tension.

Arthur Vance stood in the companionway, his eyes sweeping the horizon through the binoculars. The sea had completely lost its wind-driven foam. The swells were massive, rounded hills of leaden water, sixteen feet high, rolling beneath the hull without a sound, their oily gray surfaces unmarred by a single ripple.

In the unmoving air, the humidity was ninety-eight percent, dense and cold as wet wool. The smell of sulfur and ozone, drawn down from the upper stratosphere by the intense cyclonic draft, hung heavy over the deck.

Down below, Clara Lindholm was watching the barograph trend line.

The line had ceased to be a line; it was a vertical drop of black ink that had plunged off the bottom of the digital screen at nine hundred seventy-eight millibars. On the wooden bulkhead, the needle of the brass aneroid barometer sat pegged against the lower stop pin, unable to register the terrifying depth of the atmospheric void into which they had sailed.

"Central pressure is nine hundred sixty-four millibars," Clara reported, her voice quiet and steady with the fatalistic calm of a professional navigator who understood the physics of what was coming. "The eye diameter is approximately eighteen miles. At our present drift rate, the eyewall will hit us in six minutes."

Tomasz Nowak climbed the companionway ladder, carrying four heavy nylon safety tethers fitted with double-locking aluminum snap-hooks.

He handed two tethers to Julian and two to Clara.

"Double tethers on every safety gear," Tom said, his gravelly voice cutting through the silence. "Hook one to the jackline, hook the second to a pad-eye. When the wind hits, you do not stand up. You crawl on your hands and knees. If you try to walk, sixty knots of wind will pick you up and throw you into the sea like an empty paper bag."

Julian took the tethers, his hands shaking slightly as he clipped the heavy aluminum carabiners to his chest D-ring.

He looked through the companionway hatch toward the northwest.

Across the horizon, five miles away, a solid wall of white water was tearing across the ocean surface, moving toward them at forty knots. The sea was boiling, pulverized into a blinding blizzard of foam that rose thirty feet into the air beneath a sky as black as pitch.

"Here it comes," Artie Vance whispered, his boots planting firm on the companionway threshold, his hands gripping the teak handrails with iron strength. "Hold on to the ship."

# Chapter 17: Dead Reckoning

At dawn on the seventh day, the satellite GPS display flickered twice and died.

Clara Lindholm sat at the chart table, staring at the blank LCD screen of the primary navigation console. The position coordinates, which had updated every second since leaving St. John's, were replaced by a flashing gray prompt: NO SATELLITE FIX // DILUTION OF PRECISION EXCEEDED // CHECK ANTENNA.

She reached down, cycled the power breaker on the electronics bus, and waited for the system to reboot. The screen cycled through its startup sequence, searched the sky for orbital signals, and failed again.

"The ionosphere is disturbed," Clara said, her voice tight with strain. "Or the GPS antenna on the transom arch took a wave and shorted out the coax cable."

Arthur Vance did not look surprised. He stood behind her, balancing easily against the twenty-five-degree heel of the cutter.

"Turn it off," Artie said in a flat tone. "Don't waste battery power on a blind box."

He reached into the wooden chart rack above the desk, pulled out Admiralty Chart 4011, and unrolled it across the mahogany table, pinning the corners down with his heavy brass chart weights.

Beside the chart, he placed his brass Weems & Plath dividers and a long parallel rule.

"We go back to dead reckoning," Artie said.

Clara looked at the blank paper chart with something close to dread. "Dead reckoning across eight hundred miles of open ocean? With twenty-knot leeway and a two-knot variable cross-current from the North Atlantic Drift?"

"Men crossed this ocean for four hundred years with nothing but a logline, a compass, and a lead weight," Artie said. "You have a digital knotmeter that reads through a paddlewheel in the hull. You have a fluxgate compass that reads within half a degree. That's more precision than Joshua Slocum ever held in his life."

He picked up a freshly sharpened pencil and pointed to the last verified satellite fix, marked eighteen hours earlier at forty-six degrees forty minutes north, forty-seven degrees ten minutes west.

"From here, we have steered heading zero-nine-seven magnetic," Artie said, calculating the numbers aloud with practiced ease. "Variation is nineteen degrees west. That gives us an average true course of zero-seven-eight. Speed over ground on the paddlewheel has averaged eight point seven knots over eighteen hours. That is one hundred fifty-six point six nautical miles along the track."

He walked his brass dividers across the latitude scale, measured the distance, and swung an arc across the projected course line.

"Now we apply leeway," Artie continued. "Under staysail alone, with the wind on the port quarter at thirty-five knots, the hull slips five degrees to leeward. We adjust our course line to zero-eight-three true."

Clara watched his hands move across the paper. Her expression softened, the sharp, defensive arrogance of the modern racing navigator melting into quiet professional respect.

"My first skipper never touched a paper chart," Clara said softly, looking down at the penciled lines. "He was thirty-two, sponsored by an energy drink syndicate. He had six iPad screens mounted in the cockpit displaying real-time routing algorithms. He told me that paper charts were for museums and pensioners."

"And where is he now?" Artie asked, without looking up from his dividers.

"He runs a real estate agency in Cannes," she said, a bitter edge returning to her voice. "After the inquiry off Fastnet, his family bought out his racing debts and set him up in commercial property. During the maritime court hearing, he testified that I had failed to input the radar guard zones into the chartplotter software before going off watch. He had two high-priced maritime barristers from London who made the court believe that a twenty-nine-year-old girl was responsible for a sixty-foot racing boat running into the side of a cargo vessel in broad daylight."

Artie set his pencil down and looked at her.

"The court doesn't know the sea, Clara," he said in a low voice. "Lawyers know paper. They know how to construct liability out of words on a page. But the ocean doesn't care about liability. When a wave hits a hull at two o'clock in the morning, it doesn't ask who had the better lawyer in London."

He handed her his brass dividers.

"Take the fix," Artie told her. "Calculate our noon position from the logbook entries. You are the navigator on this ship. Act like one."

Clara took the heavy brass instrument in her hand. Her fingers tightened around the knurled center pivot, feeling the weight and balance of the cold metal. She bent over the paper chart, aligned the parallel rule with the compass rose, and drew a clean, sharp pencil line across the blank blue space of the North Atlantic.

The process of dead reckoning navigation is an ongoing conversation between the ship's logbook, the magnetic compass, and the helmsman's honesty.

Clara Lindholm sat at the chart desk, her pencil hovering over the paper grid of Admiralty Chart 4011. Every hour, on the hour, she recorded four primary observations: compass heading, speed through the water on the hull paddlewheel, leeway drift estimated from the angle of the wake behind the transom, and estimated sea-current drift taken from the North Atlantic pilot book.

She laid the heavy parallel rule across the compass rose, transferring the magnetic heading of zero-eight-two to the true course line.

"Variation is decreasing," Clara said, looking at the small printed compass roses spaced across the chart. "In St. John's, the magnetic variation was twenty-two degrees west. Out here, over the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, it has dropped to sixteen degrees west. In two days, off the Azores, it will be eleven degrees."

Artie stood behind her, watching the sharp pencil tip trace the course line across the paper.

"Good," Artie said. "Never forget the variation. A skipper who forgets his magnetic variation will miss an island fifty miles wide and end up on the African coast with empty water tanks."

Clara leaned back against the bulkhead cushion, looking at the pencil line. "I haven't navigated by paper since my cadet exams in Gothenburg six years ago. In the racing syndicates, everything was digital. We had four redundant B&G displays, integrated radar overlays, and dual satellite uplinks that recalculated our polar performance every thirty seconds."

"And when the battery died?" Artie asked.

"The batteries didn't die," she said, her voice dropping into quiet reflection. "Until the collision off Fastnet. When the cargo ship struck our starboard quarter, the impact severed the main lithium battery cables in the lazarette. In half a second, every screen on the boat went black. The chartplotter died, the radar died, the digital compass died. We were in thick fog, five miles off the Irish coast, with fifty feet of cold water under the hull and zero instruments."

She looked down at her hands, which were steady on the brass compass.

"The skipper panicked," Clara whispered. "He was thirty-two years old, with ten thousand Instagram followers and three commercial sponsorships, and he didn't even know where the paper charts were stowed. He sat on the saloon settee and cried while I went on deck with a handheld plastic compass and steered us toward Baltimore harbor."

The radio watch fell between midnight and four. Clara sat before the Icom single-sideband set, wearing heavy plastic headphones that clamped tight against her temples. The red frequency display showed four-four-two-six kilohertz, the primary distress and calling frequency for the eastern Atlantic basin.

Static filled the earphones, a steady hiss like dry sand poured over tin, punctuated by distant crashes of lightning discharges five hundred miles to the south.

"Nothing on four-four-two-six," she told Artie as he came off deck to check the barometer. "Portishead Radio is silent. Only the automated weather broadcast out of Horta."

"What does Horta give for Area Azores?" Artie asked, leaning over the chart table with his brass compass in his right hand.

"Gale warning in force," Clara replied, tapping her pencil on the lined yellow pad where she had transcribed the Morse and voice weather. "South to southwest seven to eight, veering northwest eight to nine later. Sea state rough to very rough. Visibility poor in precipitation."

Artie measured thirty miles along their projected track with the compass points.

"The depression is tracking faster than the midday GRIB file predicted," he observed. "The center will cross our meridian by eighteen hundred tomorrow. If we stay on this rhumb line, we will take the strongest wind right on the beam."

"Should we alter course to forty north?"

"No," Artie decided. "Going north puts us in the left-front quadrant, where the seas are steepest. We keep her headed on one-hundred-ten. We let the center pass just to the north of us, take the wind on the starboard quarter, and run before it with the drogue."

He marked the new estimated position with a small lead cross, noting the time and the barometric pressure in the margin of the Admiralty chart.

"Wake Noah at zero-three-thirty," Artie added. "We will want two people on deck when the wind begins its first shift."

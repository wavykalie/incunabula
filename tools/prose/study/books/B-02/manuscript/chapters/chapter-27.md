# Chapter 27: Cold Tea and Nicotine

By twenty-two-hundred hours, the saloon of Aura VII had become a dark, dripping bunker of shared survival.

The gale was still blowing fifty-five knots outside, but the wind had veered into the west-northwest, bringing a colder, drier air mass that stopped the freezing rain and turned the wave crests into long, organized avenues of white foam.

Tomasz Nowak sat on the floorboards beside Artie's settee, his back resting against the central table pedestal.

The gas stove in the galley was lit, its blue flame hissing softly on its gimbals as it heated an aluminum pot of water pumped from the manual desalinator. The cabin smelled of damp wool, engine oil, menthol chest rub, and the sharp, medicinal tang of the nicotine gum Tom chewed continuously to replace his cigarettes.

Tom poured three mugs of hot, weak tea, sliding one into the wooden fiddle beside Artie's hand.

"Drink it while it is hot, skipper," Tom said. "It has three spoons of sugar. Good for shock. Good for broken bones."

Artie turned his head slowly, his breath coming in measured, shallow sips to avoid moving his taped ribs. The pain was a dull, heavy ache now, held in check by two codeine tablets Tom had found in the commercial medical chest.

"Who is on the helm?" Artie asked, his voice a dry rasp.

"Clara and Julian," Tom said, taking a sip from his own tin mug. "They have the system working. Julian grinds the port winch, Clara handles the starboard. They steer twenty minutes on, twenty minutes off, switching arms. The girl is strong, Artie. She steers like an icebreaker captain. You don't have to worry about the boat."

Tom leaned his head back against the mahogany pedestal, looking up at the dark deckhead.

"Ten years ago," Tom said calmly, his voice blending with the distant rumble of the hull through the water, "I was second engineer on a four-thousand-ton container feeder running between Gdynia and Hull. The Baltic in January. Minus twenty degrees, ice on the hatch covers two feet thick."

He held up his left hand, looking at the two blunt, scarred stumps where his index and middle fingers should have been.

"The turbocharger oil line blew on the main Sulzer diesel at three in the morning," Tom continued. "The casing was six hundred degrees. The oil caught fire on the exhaust manifold. I went into the casing with a dry-powder extinguisher and a twelve-inch wrench to isolate the fuel cutoff valve."

He took a slow sip of tea, swallowing with a faint clicking sound in his throat.

"I saved the engine," he said. "The ship didn't burn. But the turbocharger impeller seized while my hand was inside the intake housing. It took forty seconds for the shaft to stop spinning. When I pulled my arm out, two fingers were lying in the bilges under the crankcase."

He fell silent for a long moment, watching the steam rise from his tin cup.

"The company gave me a four-thousand-euro insurance settlement and a train ticket back to Gdansk," Tom said, his face carved with deep, tired lines. "I spent two years in the waterfront bars drinking clear vodka until my wife took my two daughters and moved to her mother's farm in Torun. One morning, I woke up in the gutter behind the fish market with no shoes and twenty zlotys in my pocket."

He pulled a fresh piece of nicotine gum from his pocket, unwrapped the silver foil, and slipped it into his mouth.

"I haven't had a drink in five years, Artie," Tom said softly. "Not one drop of beer, not one glass of wine. I work these delivery crossings because the money is cash, and every month I send two thousand euros to Gdansk for my oldest girl's university tuition. She is studying civil engineering at the technical university. She is going to build bridges, Artie. Real bridges out of prestressed concrete that don't rust and don't sink."

Artie looked at the scarred engineer in the dim red light.

"She will build good bridges, Tom," Artie said.

"She will," Tom agreed, nodding once with quiet, immovable conviction. "If her father delivers this plastic boat to Horta and collects his wire transfer."

He picked up his tea mug, stood up with the rolling motion of the hull, and reached for his oilskin jacket.

"My turn on the winches," Tom said, zipping the heavy plastic collar up to his ears. "Rest your ribs, Artie. We have four hundred miles to the Azores. We will bring you into port sitting up."

The center passed at four in the morning.

The wind dropped from fifty knots to eight in the space of fifteen minutes. The screaming in the rigging died away, replaced by an eerie, hollow silence broken only by the chaotic sloshing of the sea. Without the wind to smooth their crests, the swells rose in confused, pyramid-shaped heaps that crashed against the hull from three directions at once.

"The eye," Clara said, opening the companionway hatch and looking up.

Above the masthead, the clouds had torn open, revealing a sharp circle of black sky studded with brilliant, cold Atlantic stars. The air felt suddenly warm, almost heavy, carrying a faint scent of damp earth and ozone.

Artie stepped into the cockpit, shining his spotlight up the mast. The rigging had taken the strain well. The forestay was taut, the turnbuckles safety-wired in place, their cotter pins intact. But the boom vang had worked loose from its bail, the shackle pin backed out three threads from the violent rolling.

"Noah, bring the safety wire and the adjustable wrench," Artie ordered.

They worked on the cabin trunk, their boots sliding on the wet gelcoat as the boat pitched violently in the confused chop. Artie seated the shackle pin into the stainless boom tang, turned it home with his wrench until the eye aligned with the hole, and passed two inches of stainless seizing wire through the hole, twisting the ends tight with his pliers.

"Check the gooseneck," Noah suggested, pointing his light at the mast joint.

Artie ran his fingers over the cast aluminum gooseneck bracket. The casting was intact, the bronze toggle pin showing no sign of distortion.

"Sound," Artie said. "Get the trysail down and stowed before the backside gale hits. When this front sweeps through, the wind will come from the northwest, and it will bring cold air straight down from Greenland."

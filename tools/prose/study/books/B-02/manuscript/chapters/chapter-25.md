# Chapter 25: Force Ten

Steering a fifty-four-foot racing yacht with two cockpit winches in thirty-five-foot breaking seas is an exercise in brutal physical labor.

By dawn on the tenth day, the storm had achieved the official status of a violent storm, sustained Force Eleven, with wind speeds clocked at sixty-two knots across the masthead display. The sea surface had vanished beneath a blinding, driving blizzard of horizontal white spray that tore the breath from human lungs the moment a face turned to windward.

Arthur Vance stood behind the companionway hood, his boots jammed into the teak foot-chocks, his gloved hands gripping the port winch handle.

Beside him, Clara Lindholm stood at the starboard winch, holding the opposing Spectra steering line with a turn around the self-tailing drum.

The mechanical process was exhausting and relentless.

When a monstrous, thirty-foot wave crested behind the port quarter, threatening to lift the stern and roll the yacht broadside into the trough, Artie would yell, "Pull port!" and grind his winch with both arms, hauling the emergency tiller ten inches to windward. Simultaneously, Clara would ease two inches of line from the starboard drum, allowing the rudder to turn against the water pressure.

As the crest passed beneath the hull and the bow buried into the boiling foam of the trough, Clara would grind her winch, yelling, "Center!", bringing the rudder back to the midline before the yacht could broach in the opposing direction.

Every single wave demanded four rapid, coordinated physical corrections.

Over a four-hour watch, a watch officer ground the heavy two-speed winch handles more than one thousand two hundred times, fighting hundreds of pounds of hydrodynamic resistance with their shoulders, backs, and biceps.

Artie's arms burned as if filled with molten lead. His lower back was a solid knot of agonizing muscle spasm, and the titanium screws in his left ankle throbbed with a sickening, grinding ache each time the hull slammed into a trough.

"Sea temperature!" Artie shouted through his neoprene hood.

Clara leaned over the companionway sill, checking the digital readout on the bulkhead display.

"Two point eight degrees!" she screamed back. "We have crossed the northern boundary of the cold current! The water is three degrees colder than yesterday!"

The drop in water temperature changed the physical structure of the waves.

Cold, dense Arctic water carries more mass per cubic meter than warm subtropical water. The waves were not rolling slopes; they were vertical, crumbling walls of dark green concrete, moving at twenty-eight knots, packing enough hydraulic force to crush steel deckhouses and bend three-inch stainless steel stanchions like copper wire.

A massive wall of water rose out of the spray forty yards behind their transom.

It was wider than the boat's length, its crest foaming with a dull, yellowish-white froth that hissed with terrifying acoustic intensity. The wave was not tracking parallel to their heading; it was angling thirty degrees across the quarter, a cross-sea kicked up by the shifting wind vector of the cold front.

"Hold on!" Artie roared. "Drop and lock!"

He threw himself flat across the winch base, wrapping both arms around the aluminum drum, burying his face into his chest.

Clara dropped to the cockpit sole, her hands locking into the bridge deck padeye.

The wave struck them with the force of an artillery shell.

The stern was picked up and slammed violently sixty degrees to starboard. Tons of freezing, solid green water swept over the cockpit coaming, burying both of them under four feet of rushing Atlantic ocean.

The yacht rolled forty-five degrees, fifty degrees, sixty degrees.

For three horrifying seconds, the mast lay almost parallel to the water, the spreaders slicing through the foam as the carbon hull was pushed sideways down the steep face of the wave. The cabin windows went dark under solid water, the pressure threatening to implode the acrylic washboards.

Then, with a slow, agonizing groan of pre-preg carbon framing, the deep lead keel eighty feet below the surface exerted its righting moment.

The hull righted itself with a violent, snapping recoil that threw forty gallons of water out through the transom scuppers.

Artie pulled his head out of the water, coughing violently, dragging air into his burning lungs.

Beside him, Clara was still gripping the padeye, spitting seawater, her eyes wild with terror.

"Is the rig up?" she choked out.

Artie looked aloft through the spray.

The carbon mast was still standing, straight and true against the screaming gray sky, its Dyneema standing rigging vibrating with a high, musical whistle.

"The rig is up!" Artie shouted, hauling himself back to the winch handle. "Get on the starboard line, Clara! Here comes the next one!"

By nineteen hundred hours the wind had reached fifty knots, shrieking through the wire rigging with a high, metallic whine that vibrated through the hull like a tuning fork. The sea was no longer blue or gray; it was a foaming plain of white water, streaked with long horizontal ribbons of spray that ripped the tops off the swells.

"Get the series drogue on deck," Artie ordered.

The drogue was packed in a heavy vinyl valise under the cockpit sole. Inside were three hundred feet of heavy braided nylon, spliced to a hundred and twenty small fabric cones spaced twenty inches apart, with thirty feet of galvanized chain at the terminal end.

Noah dragged the bag into the cockpit, bracing his boots against the cockpit coaming. Clara took the helm, her eyes narrowed against the driving salt spray, holding the stern square to the approaching mountains of water.

"Bridle goes to the primary sheet winches," Artie said, his voice flat and steady above the screaming wind. "Lead the legs through the aft mooring chocks. Put chafe gear on both lines where they cross the cap rail."

They wrapped the heavy nylon eyes around the winches, securing them with three turns and two half-hitches over the drum tops. Artie inspected the leather chafe sleeves, greasing them with a handful of lanolin paste to prevent friction burns.

"Weight first," Artie yelled. "Drop the chain over the stern."

Noah lifted the thirty-pound bundle of chain and dropped it into the boiling wake. The metal sank instantly, dragging the line out behind it. Cone after cone cleared the vinyl bag, snapping open as they bit into the water. As the twentieth cone deployed, the line pulled taut with a dull thud.

The effect was instantaneous. The stern settled into the water, and the speed dropped from seven knots to two point four. The hull ceased its wild yawing, tracking true down the face of the oncoming seas, the heavy bridle holding her stern directly into the wind like an arrow in flight.

"Drogue is set," Clara said, letting out a long breath.

"Now we go below," Artie told them. "The boat will take care of itself."

The deployment of the series drogue was not merely an emergency procedure; it was an exact operational maneuver that required cold discipline. If the line fouled on the stern rail, or if a single cone caught on an outdrive or rudder fitting, the warp could part or jam the steering gear, leaving the vessel broadside to thirty-foot breakers.

Artie stood at the transom, his safety line clipped short to the stern pulpit.

"Noah, feed the line out hand over hand," Artie commanded. "Never let a bight wrap around your wrist or ankle. If a wave hits the cones, that line will snatch with five tons of pull."

Noah nodded, his face pale beneath his yellow hood. He knelt beside the open vinyl valise, lifting each cone by its throat and dropping it cleanly into the surging boiling wake. One by one, the cones disappeared into the foaming grey water, catching the current and stringing out in a long, taut curve that vanished into the darkness behind.

The resistance built with every ten cones. By the time eighty cones were submerged, the three-quarter-inch nylon line was humming like a cello string.

Artie checked the bridle arms. Each leg of the bridle passed through an aft mooring chock, protected by eight inches of heavy split leather hose wrapped with rigging tape. The strain was distributed equally between the two giant primary winches in the cockpit.

"Both legs equal," Artie said, placing his palm on the vibrating cordage. "The chafe gear is sitting square in the chocks."

He stepped down into the cockpit well. The hull was riding true now. Instead of surfing wild and uncontrolled down the ninety-foot faces of the swells, Cormorant was held firmly by the stern, her speed capped at two and a half knots. The breaking crests split around her double-ended transom, hissing past the cockpit coamings without boarding.

"The boat is stabilized," Artie reported to Clara at the companionway. "We are tethered to the sea itself. Now we maintain the watch schedule and keep the bilges dry."

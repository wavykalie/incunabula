# Chapter 01: The Delivery Sheet

The delivery sheet was printed on eighty-gram copier paper, creased twice across the middle and damp around the margins where sea fog had crept past the companionway washboards.

Arthur Vance smoothed the paper against the varnished mahogany of the chart table. The figures had not changed since the courier handed him the envelope on the fuel dock at St. John's. Forty-two thousand Canadian dollars gross. Fourteen thousand advanced to the provisioning account and the bonded diesel locker. Twenty-eight thousand held in third-party escrow in Saint Helier, Jersey, payable upon execution of a clean hand-over certificate at Horta Marina in the Azores.

Beneath the payment schedule, printed in nine-point Helvetica, ran the penalty clause. A deduction of one thousand two hundred dollars for every twenty-four-hour period beyond sixteen sea days from departure, acts of God and verified mechanical breakdown excepted, subject to immediate telex validation by the owner's representative in Monaco.

Artie set his brass Weems & Plath dividers on the top edge of the sheet to stop the heater draft from curling it. Fifty-eight years old, with two titanium screws in his left ankle and eighteen thousand ocean miles logged in the last four years alone. When the bank took his coastal charter schooner ten years back, there had been no ceremony. Just an auctioneer with a chipped wooden gavel on the wharf in Camden, Maine, selling twenty-five years of equity in thirty-five minutes to pay off a balloon mortgage on an engine repower that had arrived six months late. Since then, deep-water delivery was the only trade left that paid cash on arrival without asking for a credit rating. Deliver the hull intact, keep the engine oil clear of water, sign the customs docket, collect the wire. This crossing was the escrow balance that would clear his remaining commercial debt and leave four years of rent in a savings account.

He stepped up into the companionway. Outside, the Southside Hills of St. John's rose steep and black into low drizzle. The cold Atlantic air smelled of cod meal, diesel exhaust, and wet granite.

*Aura VII* lay snug against four heavy pneumatic fenders at the commercial slip. She was a fifty-four-foot custom performance cutter, laid up in pre-preg carbon fiber at a boutique yard in Kiel two years earlier. Narrow beam, plumb stem, twin spade rudders machined from high-modulus carbon stocks, and a hydraulic furler for the staysail that hummed like a dentist's drill whenever someone pressed the cockpit pedestal button. A fast, expensive machine designed for weekend regattas in the Mediterranean, now tasked with crossing two thousand miles of northern ocean in late October.

Tomasz Nowak was standing on the aft deck, his yellow foul-weather jacket open despite the five-degree air. He was forty-five, broad-shouldered, with grease ground into the skin around his cuticles and two missing fingertips on his left hand where a merchant turbocharger had taken them off twelve years ago in Gdansk.

"Bunkers full, skipper," Tom said. His English was gravelly and unhurried. "Main tank three hundred liters. Forward reserve one hundred fifty. I dipped both with the wooden stick. Fuel is clean, no red dye, no water. Oil level in the Volvo is right at the high notch. I put half a liter of 15W-40 in the reserve can."

"What about the coolant?" Artie asked.

"Green and clean. Expansion tank half full cold. I replaced the raw water impellor this morning. The old one had two cracked vanes. I left the spare in the plastic tub under the galley floor." Tom spat a shred of nicotine gum into a scrap of paper and folded it into his pocket. "The cook stove is primed. Four bottles of propane in the lazarette. All sealed with factory caps."

"Good," Artie said. "Get your gear below. We leave on the ebb."

He turned back toward the saloon. At the navigation desk, Clara Lindholm had already set up her workstation. She was twenty-nine, her blonde hair pulled tight into a seaman's knot, her laptop connected to a portable USB barograph sensor sitting beside the main electrical panel. Two years ago she had been one of the rising navigators on the European offshore racing circuit until an offshore trimaran collision off Fastnet Rock ended with an inquiry, a suspended ticket, and a skipper who swore under oath that Lindholm had misread the AIS radar vector while he was asleep in his bunk. The commercial delivery circuit was full of people whose careers had cracked against hard rock or legal paper.

"The satellite terminal is locked, Artie," Clara said without looking up from her screen. Her voice was flat, Scandinavian, precise.

"Locked how?"

"Locked to receive-only. Look at the Furuno console." She tapped the gray casing of the Inmarsat unit mounted above the chart table. "The broker in Monaco configured the account profile. We can receive weather bulletins, fleet advisories, and direct incoming telexes from the owner's office. But the transmit buffer is disabled. No outgoing email, no voice calls, no manual position reports unless we hit the dedicated GMDSS distress button."

Artie leaned over her shoulder, smelling her wet wool sweater and the faint tang of battery acid. The green LCD screen on the Furuno displayed the system status in block letters: `RX ACTIVE // TX RESTRICTED BY SHORE DISPATCHER // CODE 04`.

"Did you talk to the agent in St. John's?" Artie asked.

"The agent said the owner pays the satellite bill and the owner sets the permissions," Clara replied, her fingers tracing the digital isobar lines on her weather map. "He said if we need to talk, we talk to God or wait until Faial. He also said the boat has an automated tracking beacon sending five-minute pings back to Monaco, so they know where we are whether we write to them or not."

Artie rubbed his chin. The stubble was gray and stiff under his palm. A delivery boat with a receive-only wire was not illegal, but it was cheap. It meant Monaco could change routing instructions whenever their private meteorological subscription found a faster lane, while Artie had no way to explain why twenty-foot head seas made thirty knots of wind untradable.

"We have the single-sideband receiver for Canadian Coast Guard weather," Artie said. "And the paper charts for the North Atlantic approaches. We don't need to chat with Monaco."

"I am not interested in chatting," Clara said. "I am interested in knowing why the GRIB model run from Halifax shows an explosive cyclonic low deepening south of Cape Race in seventy-two hours, while the delivery routing sheet from the owner tells us to maintain fifty-two degrees north on the great circle."

Artie looked down at the navigation desk. Clara had overlaid the owner's rhumb line against the Canadian meteorological fax. The owner's track ran straight northeast through the cold core of the Labrador Current, cutting across the tail of the Grand Banks into sixty-knot wind barbs.

"We adjust when we get off soundings," Artie said calmly. "Nobody sails into a bomb low to save forty miles of diesel."

"The contract has the sixteen-day clause, Artie. I saw your sheet."

"The contract is between me and the broker. Your day rate is guaranteed regardless of arrival date."

"My license is not," she said, her voice dropping an octave, cold and level. "I am not putting another maritime court inquiry into my jacket because a Monaco landlord wants his boat tied up in Cannes before the slip fees double."

Before Artie could answer, the companionway hatch slid back with a sharp clatter. Cold drizzle swept into the cabin, followed by a dark canvas duffel bag and a man wearing brand-new yellow foul-weather gear with the retail fold creases still visible across the chest.

Julian Kross lowered himself down the wooden steps, holding the handrails with both hands as if stepping onto an icy roof. He was thirty-four, thin, with sharp cheekbones and dark circles under his eyes that looked like bruises. In the crew paperwork filed with the harbor master that morning, he was listed as Marcus Ward, British national, commercial Yachtmaster Offshore, endorsed for commercial delivery.

"The slip line is cast off forward," Kross said. He glanced quickly around the saloon, his gaze lingering on the receive-only Furuno terminal before settling on Artie. "The fuel dock attendant said the surge is building in the Narrows. If we don't drop our lines now, the tide turns against us."

Artie took the duffel bag and tossed it into the starboard pilot berth. "You have your discharge book, Marcus?"

"I gave the copy to the port agent," the younger man said. His voice was slightly too fast, the rhythm stiff, like a student reciting a memorized passage.

"I need the original in the ship's safe. Customs regulation for high-seas departure. If we get boarded by the Canadian fisheries patrol off the Banks, they want originals, not photocopies."

Ward hesitated. For half a second, his fingers tightened around the zipper of his jacket. Then he reached into his inner pocket and drew out a dark blue leatherette booklet with gold lettering embossed on the cover: *United Kingdom Seaman's Discharge Book*.

Artie took it, feeling the dampness of the cover. "Bunk four is yours, forward to port. Tom is on the engine watch. You and Clara take the first dogwatch once we clear the fairway buoy. Stow your bag and get on deck with the winch handles. We're letting go."

Ward nodded once, turned, and disappeared down the narrow corridor leading toward the forward cabin.

Artie sat down on the chart bench, opened the ship's steel lockbox mounted beneath the radio shelf, and placed the blue discharge booklet inside beside his own weathered master's ticket. As he dropped the booklet into the tray, the corner of an expired inspection stamp caught his eye through the glassine page protector. He did not examine it then. Outside, the Volvo auxiliary engine thrummed through the hull framing, sending a steady fifty-cycle vibration through the soles of his sea boots.

He closed the steel lid, snapped the brass padlock shut, and pulled his wool watch cap down over his ears.

Ten minutes later, *Aura VII* slipped past the Battery under the towering granite walls of the Narrows. The raw Atlantic swell caught the bow the moment they crossed the line between the harbor lights, lifting the fifty-four-foot carbon hull and dropping her into the first deep trough with a shudder that resonated through the deck fittings. To the east, the ocean stretched away into black drizzle, two thousand miles of cold salt water between their bowsprit and the volcanic cliffs of Faial. Artie throttled the Volvo to eighteen hundred RPM, trimmed the small staysail against the rising easterly draft, and set his course on zero-eight-two magnetic.

The night run down the channel required three separate checks of the sea chest. Artie did not rely on the digital depth sounder alone. He kept the lead line coiled on the starboard deck box, thirty fathoms of braided hemp marked with scraps of red bunting and white cotton cord at two-fathom intervals.

He stepped to the weather rail, swung the seven-pound lead weight in two easy pendulum arcs beneath the shrouds, and cast it forward into the black chop.

The lead struck the water twenty feet ahead of the bow. Artie took up the slack with rapid hand-over-hand motions until the line came plumb under his shoulder. His thumb felt the mark: four fathoms and a foot. Twenty-five feet of salt water under the keel. The bottom tallow cavity brought up a smear of grey harbor silt mixed with broken shell.

"Depth confirms the paper chart," he told Noah at the helm. "Stay thirty yards west of the green flasher."

Noah adjusted the wheel three spokes to port. The steering cables creaked inside their bronze conduits beneath the cockpit sole.

Artie climbed down into the main salon to conduct the hourly bilgewater inspection. In a newly refitted hull, small leaks were routine, but routine leaks killed ships if neglected. He took his wooden sounding rod, calibrated in quarter-inches, and lowered it through the inspection hole in the center floorboard.

The rod came up damp for one inch.

"Three gallons in the keel sump," Artie recorded on the maintenance slate hanging by the companionway. "Rule bilge pump float switch is dry. Seepage from the mast boot collar."

He dried the sounding rod with an oily rag, hung it back on its brass hooks, and checked the secondary battery isolator switch. The house bank was taking a solid twenty-eight amperes from the Balmar alternator, bringing the twelve-volt system up to thirteen point six volts. Everything hummed with mechanical order.

He climbed back into the cockpit, closed the sliding hatch halfway to keep the spray off the instrument console, and sat on the windward cockpit bench with his back against the cabin bulkhead. The crossing had begun.

The deck log was an eighty-page canvas ledger bound in blue sailcloth, stamped with the insignia of the Royal Bermuda Yacht Club. Artie opened it to page one, writing in block capital letters with an indelible drafting pen.

He recorded the vessel's registration number, her gross tonnage of twenty-four metric tons, the diesel fuel capacity of one hundred and twenty gallons across two aluminum saddle tanks, and the potable water capacity of ninety gallons in the polyethylene keel tank. Beneath the particulars, he listed the crew watch rotation: four hours on, eight hours off, with Artie taking the dog watches from sixteen hundred to twenty hundred to supervise the change of sails.

"Every entry requires three data points," Artie told Clara, who was watching from the galley counter. "Barometric pressure to the nearest tenth of a millibar, true wind speed and direction, and vessel position by GPS and dead reckoning. If the electrical system takes a lightning strike mid-Atlantic, the dead reckoning track is the only line keeping us off the rocks."

Clara took the pen, testing the ink on the margin of the navigation log.

"What is our leeway allowance for the Gulf Stream crossing?" she asked.

"Two degrees of leeway for every fifteen knots of wind on the beam," Artie said. "With the Gulf Stream setting north-northeast at two point five knots, we steer ten degrees south of the rhumb line to compensate. The current will sweep us right onto the track by tomorrow evening."
# Chapter 02: The Expired Stamp

The sea had risen into long, dark ridges by mid-afternoon. Aura VII drove through them with the rigid, unyielding bounce of high-tensile carbon, the hull vibrating whenever the plumb bow sheared through a gray Atlantic crest.

Arthur Vance sat at the navigation station in the forward starboard corner of the saloon. He pulled the chart table drawer open until the brass slides clicked against their stops. Inside, nestled between rolled Admiralty paper charts and the vessel's registration papers, lay the ship's steel document safe.

Artie unlocked the small brass padlock with the key from his pocket ring. He lifted out the dark blue booklet he had taken from the younger man two hours earlier. The gilt lettering on the pebbled cover read: United Kingdom Seaman's Discharge Book. Below the royal crest, embossed numerals designated the record: R-984421.

He flipped through the initial pages. The plastic laminate over the photograph had slight hairline scratches near the lower edge, the sort left by grit in an envelope. The photograph showed a man with narrow shoulders, dark hair trimmed high around the ears, and a neutral, unblinking expression. It was Julian Kross's face, though three or four years younger, without the sunken hollows beneath the cheekbones.

The details matched the crew declaration sheet Artie had stamped at the customs desk in St. John's. Marcus Edward Ward. Date of birth: 14 March 1990. Place of birth: Southampton. Capacity: Deckhand / Yachtmaster Offshore.

Artie turned the heavy paper pages slowly. The entries were standard commercial delivery passages. A fifty-foot Swan from Palma to Cowes in June 2018. A Moody 45 from Gibraltar to Plymouth in September 2019. Each entry bore the oval rubber stamp of the shipping master, the purple ink slightly faded along the edges, signed by masters whose names Artie knew from the solent trade.

Then he reached page nine.

The entry was dated 18 October 2021. The vessel name was listed as M/V Northern Tern, an offshore platform support vessel registered in Douglas, Isle of Man. The stamp was clear: Discharge on Account of Decease. Across the entry line, written in black indelible ink with an official steel nib, ran the notation: Lost overboard in severe weather, North Sea sector 4, body recovered and certified at Lerwick, Shetland.

Artie rested his thumb against the paper. The ink was original. The paper fibers showed the slight indentation of the steel pen. But on the following page, dated May 2024, ran a fresh renewal endorsement for commercial yacht delivery, stamped with a Southampton MCA office number and an inspector's signature.

The dead mariner had been renewed three years after drowning off Shetland.

Artie closed the booklet and set it on the green blotting pad. His fingers smelled of salt spray and the light mineral oil he had used to lubricate the chart drawers that morning. He leaned back against the bulkhead cushion. The hull groaned softly as forty tons of displacement settled into the trough of a six-foot swell.

Through the forward saloon door, he heard the metallic rattle of a pot on the gimballed gas stove. Tomasz Nowak was in the galley, bracing his hips against the stainless crash bar as he cleaned an aluminum kettle. The sharp scent of roasted chicory and chicory-coffee grounds drifted into the passage.

"Tom," Artie called, keeping his voice below the thrum of the auxiliary engine.

The Polish engineer appeared in the doorway, wiping a pair of slotted pliers on a yellow rag. "Skipper?"

"Ward's watch habits. You were on deck when he rigged the preventer line."

Tom shrugged, shifting his weight with the roll of the vessel. "He knows how to tie a bowline. He knows which side of the winch drum the sheet turns. But he watches his boots too much."

"What does that mean?"

"A sailor looks at the horizon, Artie. He looks at the luff of the genoa, he looks at the wave coming off the quarter. This boy looks down. He watches where his feet go on the nonskid like he expects the deck to fall away under him. And his hands." Tom held up his own scarred, stub-fingered palms. "His hands are clean. No calluses on the rope fingers. He has the soft thumbs of an accountant or a man who types on keys all day."

"He has a Yachtmaster ticket," Artie said, nodding toward the safe.

"Lots of boys have paper from Southampton," Tom replied flatly. "Schools sell tickets every weekend. You pay five hundred pounds, you steer around a buoy in the Solent, you eat sandwiches, you get a stamp. Out here, paper does not keep water out of the bilge."

"Keep an eye on him on the helm," Artie said. "If the wind comes ahead of seventy degrees, don't let him pinch the cutter into the swell. The carbon rig has no tolerance for shock loading."

"I watch him," Tom said. "I also watch the stove. The gas regulator on the forward bottle is leaking slightly at the brass union. A tiny smell, like garlic. I tightened it with the adjustable wrench, but the neoprene gasket is hard. Probably five years old. These French brokers, they buy Italian champagne for the owner's locker and leave four-euro rubber washers on the propane line."

Tom turned back to the galley, muttering a brief Polish curse under his breath.

Artie picked up the discharge book and slipped it into his inner fleece pocket rather than locking it back in the steel chest. The weight of the booklet pressed against his ribs through the wool.

He looked at the chart table. The GPS repeater showed their position: forty-seven degrees twelve minutes north, fifty-one degrees thirty-six minutes west. Speed over ground was eight point four knots under staysail and engine. Course zero-eight-four true. The depth sounder showed three hundred forty meters, shelving rapidly as the Grand Banks dropped off into the deep oceanic basin of the Flemish Pass.

He picked up his brass dividers. The points were sharp, unmarred by rust. He measured twelve nautical miles along their projected track, marking the hourly dead reckoning fix with a small pencil dot.

A dead man's paperwork on an offshore delivery was not a crime Artie was paid to investigate. The admiralty courts in London had established centuries ago that the master of a vessel at sea answers for the ship's safety and the seaworthiness of her company, not the biographical pedigree of the deckhands recruited by shore brokers. If the man stood his four-hour watch, steered the compass heading, and handled a halyard without dropping the mainsail into the brine, his real name was irrelevant until the customs manifest in Horta.

Yet twenty-eight thousand dollars of Artie's own retirement sat in escrow in Jersey. A fraudulent crew declaration could void the vessel's P&I insurance policy the moment an underwriter's lawyer opened the file. If Aura VII lost her rig or sheared a rudder stock in deep water, the insurance surveyor would demand the original discharge books before approving a single pound of salvage.

Artie stood up, climbed the companionway steps, and pushed back the hatch.

Outside, the late afternoon light was gray and thin. Julian Kross was standing at the port helm pedestal, his gloved hands resting lightly on the carbon wheel rim. He was steering with small, nervous corrections, his eyes darting between the digital compass heading on the mast display and the tumbling gray crests advancing from the northwest.

"Heading holding?" Artie asked, leaning against the cockpit coaming.

Ward started slightly, his shoulders stiffening inside his yellow jacket. "Holding zero-eight-two, skipper. She hunts a little in the quartering swell. Two or three degrees either side."

"Let her hunt," Artie said calmly. "If you fight the wheel on every wave, you wear out the hydraulic ram and you tire your wrists before the dogwatch ends. Let the hull track."

"Understood," Ward said. He did not look at Artie. His eyes remained fixed on the green illuminated digits of the mast display.

Artie watched the younger man's profile against the darkening ocean. The wind was building, humming with a low, dry whistle through the rod rigging aloft. To the east, the horizon had dissolved into a solid wall of cold fog.

He stepped down to the lower step, checking the secondary bilge pump breaker on the main switchboard. The green pilot light was steady. The voltage was holding at twenty-six point four volts across the lithium house bank. Everything on the panel appeared functional, clean, and modern. Yet out here on the shelf edge, two hundred miles from the nearest Newfoundland coastguard cutter, modern electronics offered only the appearance of security. A loose ground wire or a drowned alternator could reduce forty tons of carbon technology to a dark, drifting raft within forty minutes.

Artie tapped the barometer glass beside the companionway. The needle settled at one thousand sixteen millibars. A steady reading, but the gray swell from the south carried a long, oily period that spoke of a gale already churning five hundred miles over the horizon.

He checked the secondary fuel valve beneath the navigation settee. The brass valve handle was stiff, covered with a thin crust of green verdigris from salt condensation. When he turned it forty-five degrees, the valve stem resisted with a dry squeak before opening to the forward reserve line.

Outside the saloon windows, the afternoon light faded into a dense, wet dusk. Artie walked the length of the accommodation corridor, testing the bronze latch on each overhead locker. In a heavy seaway, an unlatched door could swing with the momentum of a guillotine, splitting a man's forehead or crushing his collarbone. Every latch clicked shut with a solid, reassuring snap.

At the mast step, where the sixty-foot carbon spar passed through the deck to rest on the keel shoe, he inspected the hydraulic jacking cylinder. The pressure gauge read three thousand four hundred pounds per square inch. The rod rigging was singing in the damp air, humming at forty cycles a second, a pitch that vibrated through the composite bulkheads.

He climbed the companionway stairs again, carrying a thermos of hot chicory coffee in his jacket pocket. Ward was still at the wheel, his posture rigid, his shoulders hunched against the freezing drizzle that blew across the sprayhood.

"Take a swallow of this," Artie said, offering the plastic cup.

Ward looked down at the cup, hesitated, and accepted it with a gloved hand. The steam rose into his face, carrying the bitter, roasted smell into the wind.

"Thank you, skipper," Ward said.

"How does the wheel feel?"

"She has twenty pounds of weather helm when the swell lifts the quarter," Ward answered, speaking with careful precision. "I have to lead the wave with five degrees of rudder to stop the bow from searching."

"That is the carbon rudders," Artie told him. "They don't flex. When the water bites, it bites instantly. Don't fight her. Let the stern rise, let the water slide along the run, then bring her back as she levels."

Ward nodded, sipping the hot liquid. For a moment, the tension in the younger man's jaw seemed to soften, replaced by the quiet, shared exhaustion of two men standing on a deck in a rising sea.

The change of watch came at midnight under a clearing sky. The wind held steady from the north-northwest at twenty-two knots, kicking up a steep, four-foot chop that slapped against the forward quarters.

Noah handed the helm over to Clara, stepping back into the cockpit well to shake the cramps from his hands. His fingers were stiff from gripping the wheel against the pulse of the quarterly sea.

"Log shows thirty-two miles on the mechanical impeller," Noah reported to her. "Average speed six point four knots. Vessel is tracking true on zero-eight-five magnetic. The barometer on the bulkhead reads ten-fifteen and steady."

Clara took the wheel, testing the balance of the rudder with small movements. She felt the tug of the mainsail through the steering quadrant, the responsive lift of the bow as each swell passed under the bilge.

"She carries a light helm now," she said. "The second reef took the weight out of her."

Artie came on deck with a canvas bucket containing fresh sea water and a mercury thermometer. He dipped the brass-cased thermometer into the bucket, waited two minutes for the reading to settle, and held the glass tube up to the red glow of the binnacle lamp.

"Nineteen degrees Celsius," Artie noted. "We have crossed the northern boundary of the Gulf Stream. The cold inshore shelf water is behind us."

The surface of the sea began to glow with green phosphorescence. Every wave that broke against the bow threw off showers of cold emerald sparks that tumbled down the length of the topsides, lighting the dark water with eerie, fleeting brilliance.

Noah watched the glowing foam with silent fascination.

"The algae produces light under mechanical disturbance," Artie explained, watching the wake trail behind them like a burning green road across the black ocean. "Sailors call it sea fire. When you see it this bright, you know you are in the core of the warm current. We pick up two knots of free eastward push until we clear the eastern eddies."

By zero-two-hundred, the sea had smoothed into long, rhythmic hills of black water that lifted the stern without a jar. The masthead wind instrument registered nineteen knots of true wind from three-hundred-twenty degrees magnetic.

Artie came on deck with the canvas sail repair kit. He inspected the mainsail foot where the dacron cloth met the aluminum boom extrusion.

The outhaul line was half-inch low-stretch polyester braid, led through an internal sheave at the boom end to a small Lewmar winch on the cabin trunk. Artie took two wraps off the winch, eased the outhaul four inches to give the mainsail a deeper draft for downwind reaching, and re-cleated the line with a locking hitch.

"Look at the luff telltales," Noah noted, pointing his dim red flashlight at the lower batten pockets.

The green yarn on the starboard side was streaming straight aft, parallel to the sail seam, while the red yarn on the port side fluttered slightly.

"Trim the mainsheet in three inches," Artie instructed. "A reaching mainsail should never stall along the leech. If the telltales drop, you are dragging two hundred square feet of dead canvas through the sky."

Noah pulled the braided mainsheet through the cockpit traveler block, taking up the slack until both yarns flew straight and level. The boat responded with an immediate half-knot increase on the digital speed display, accelerating cleanly to seven point two knots across the water.
# Chapter 03: The First Siphon

The change of the midnight watch brought the cold inside the boat like water pouring through an open port.

Arthur Vance woke ten minutes before zero-hundred to the sound of the galley alarm clock buzzing against the teak shelf above his head. He swung his feet down onto the floorboards, planting them wide against the steep twenty-degree heel of the hull. The cabin smelled of damp fleece, diesel oil, and the sharp mineral tang of salt drying on Gore-Tex.

He pulled on his heavy wool socks, his mid-layer thermal trousers, and his sea boots. His left ankle throbbed with a dull, familiar ache where the metal pins joined the fibula. In cold seawater, the metal seemed to hold the chill longer than the bone around it.

He stepped into the main saloon. The red night-vision LED strips cast a dim copper wash across the navigation station and the galley counter. Tomasz Nowak was asleep on the leeward settee, strapped in with a canvas lee cloth, his heavy breathing steady beneath a wool blanket.

At the chart table, Clara Lindholm was already dressed in her offshore salopettes, her safety line clipped across her chest. She had a vacuum flask of hot water wedged between two wooden fiddles, slowly pouring it over a mug of black tea.

"Wind is up," she said, her voice quiet in the darkened cabin. "Twenty-seven knots from three-one-zero. Barometer ten-twelve. It fell two millibars since twenty-one hundred."

Artie glanced at the digital barograph readout above the desk. The trend line had a gentle downward curve, nothing alarming yet, but the steady descent of a typical North Atlantic autumn depression sweeping out of the Gulf of St. Lawrence.

"Engine running clean?" he asked.

"Tom checked the primary Racor filter at twenty-two hundred," Clara said, taking a sip from her stainless mug. "No water in the glass bowl. Temperature holding at eighty-two Celsius. Speed over ground eight point six knots." She reached out and touched the glass screen of her laptop. "I checked the delivery contract while you were off watch, Artie."

Artie paused, his hand halfway into the sleeve of his heavy foul-weather smock. "The contract is locked in the drawer, Clara."

"The paper copy is in the drawer. The broker emailed a PDF duplicate to my racing team account three weeks ago when he was scouting for a navigator with Category One offshore credentials." She turned her head toward him, her pale eyes catching the red glow of the instrument panel. "He didn't just offer the day rate. He included the performance schedule."

Artie zipped the heavy plastic zipper up to his chin. "Performance schedules are standard broker boilerplate."

"Not this one," Clara said evenly. "There is a twenty-thousand-euro bonus if Aura VII arrives at Horta within fourteen days of clearing St. John's. That requires an average speed of eight point eight knots over the entire rhumb line."

"Eight point eight knots is racing speed," Artie said, pulling his hood over his head. "This is a delivery, not the Fastnet. We don't carry a ten-man racing crew to grind winches and stack sail bags on the windward rail every four hours."

"The broker told me the owner wants the boat in the Mediterranean before the winter slip rates lock in at Monaco," Clara continued. "He said the skipper who takes the northern great circle track can easily make fourteen days if he keeps the big reacher up in the gales."

Artie stepped closer to the chart table, keeping his voice low so as not to wake the sleeping engineer. "The northern great circle runs through fifty-three north. In late October, that is the iceberg limit south of Cape Farewell. It is sixty-knot gales with forty-foot seas. If we blow out a mainsail or split a rudder stock up there, there is no Coast Guard helicopter coming out of St. John's to lift us off. The Canadian rescue squadrons don't have the fuel range past three hundred miles."

Clara looked at him steadily. "I know the range of the Cormorant helicopters, Artie. I sat through two weeks of inquiry hearings after the Vanguard went down off Fastnet. The lawyers spent forty hours debating whether six minutes of delay in launching the liferaft constituted gross negligence on the part of the watch officer."

She stood up, buckling her safety knife to her webbed belt.

"The inquiry cleared me on the paper, but every racing syndicate in France canceled my contract," she said, her voice hard as carbon rod. "They don't care that the skipper was drunk on prescription sleeping pills in the aft cabin. They only care that my signature was on the deck log at the moment of impact. I need sixteen clean ocean days, Artie. I need a clean endorsement from an accredited delivery master saying Clara Lindholm navigated an offshore passage without an incident or a delay."

"You'll get your endorsement," Artie said. "If we deliver the hull in one piece."

"Then don't run south into the horse latitudes to avoid a breeze," she said. "If you divert south of the Azores high, you add five hundred miles to the crossing. The fuel won't last, and we'll be beating against easterlies for three weeks."

"I don't run from weather that the boat can carry," Artie said. "I run from stupidity. There is a difference."

He unclipped the companionway washboard and slid the hatch open.

# Chapter 15: Sea Anchor Theory

The hydrodynamic principle of an ocean drogue is simple: it is an artificial sea brake designed to keep a vessel's stern aligned with the waves and prevent an uncontrolled broach.

Arthur Vance sat at the chart table with the yacht's official safety equipment manual open beneath his lamp. Outside, the wind screamed through the rod rigging at forty-two knots, the sound rising from a dull roar to a piercing shriek that resonated through the carbon deckhouse.

In sustained gales exceeding Force Nine, a modern light-displacement yacht running downwind faces a fatal aerodynamic hazard. As the hull accelerates down the steep face of an overtaking sea, the speed of the vessel can match or exceed the speed of the wave. When the bow buries into the trough, the rudders lose steerage, the stern is kicked violently to windward by the crest, and the boat rolls broadside to the breaking sea.

The solution, developed over centuries of commercial seamanship, is the series drogue: a heavy three-hundred-foot bridle of nylon line carrying fifty or sixty small conical canvas parachutes deployed from the transom. The drag of the cones slows the vessel to four or five knots, keeping the stern pinned square to the oncoming breakers and allowing the seas to pass harmlessly beneath the hull.

Artie flipped to section four of the vessel's safety equipment manifest: STORM SURVIVAL APPAREL // DROGUES AND SEA ANCHORS.

# Chapter 38: Lines Ashore

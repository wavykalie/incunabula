# Chapter 11: City Three: Cleveland Rain

Lake Erie threw freezing rain against the brick facade of the Cleveland Agora like handfuls of buckshot. The gutters along Euclid Avenue had choked on wet slush, backing black water up over the curbs until every passing transit bus sent a gray tidal wave across the sidewalk.

Inside the basement club, the air smelled of damp wool, fried onions, and wet masonry.

The promoter was a bald, harassed man named Vinnie who smelled of spearmint vape juice and had three mobile phones lined up on the bar top.

"I told your guy yesterday," Vinnie shouted over the thump of the HVAC blower, waving a short screwdriver toward the stage. "Our main grid controller blew two fuses during the blues jam on Tuesday. The overhead spots are dead until the parts arrive from Columbus on Monday. What you see is what you get."

What we saw was a single fifty-watt track fixture mounted eight feet above stage right, casting a dim, jaundiced oval of light that barely reached the middle of the apron. Beyond that oval, the stage was a cavern of murky brown shadows.

Julian stood at the base of the riser steps, his black coat still dripping lake water onto the linoleum. He didn't take off his coat. He stood with his arms crossed over his chest, his eyes fixed on the dim patch of floorboards where the track light flickered.

Deacon dropped two heavy equipment bags onto the bar floor with a violent thud. "Vinnie, this isn't an acoustic guitar set. Jules has retinitis pigmentosa. He has ten degrees of central field in direct illumination. In fifty watts of dirty track light, he can't see his own belt buckle. You put him up there like that, he walks off the front edge into the monitor wedge."

"Then he cancels," Vinnie said, shrugging his leather jacket. "Door says eighty-five tickets sold. Half of them drove up from Akron in the sleet. You want me to refund eighty-five tickets on a Friday night? Go ahead. Walk out. I keep the room deposit."

Deacon took a half-step toward the bar, his knuckles curling into fists, his CODA street instincts flaring in the corners of his jaw.

A hand clamped down on Deacon's shoulder.

Julian didn't look at Vinnie. He looked at Deacon, his fingers snapping in rapid, hard bursts against his friend's collar:

*Stop. We don't cancel.*

*Jules, look at that stage,* Deacon signed with furious speed, his voice spilling out simultaneously in a raspy growl. *It's a cave. You can't see the house. You can't see the lip. You can't even see the mic stand from three paces back.*

Julian turned his head forty-five degrees, bringing me into the narrow cone of his remaining sight.

*Maya,* he signed. *Black top or white top?*

I understood instantly.

"I have my backup ivory performance sweater in the van," I said, signing as I spoke. "Matte cream knit. High collar. High contrast against dark wood and brick."

Julian's eyes flashed with fierce approval.

*Go get it,* he signed. *We don't need Vinnie's spots. We re-anchor the mark.*

I sprinted through the rain to the Econoline van, unlocked the rear cargo door, and dug my cream-colored knit top from the bottom of my garment duffel. When I returned to the basement, Julian was already standing on the apron.

He had kicked the heavy monitor wedge two feet backward into the wing darkness, clearing a four-foot lane along the front boards. He had placed the weighted mic stand directly under the faint cone of the track light.

I slipped into the women's restroom, changed out of my standard matte black top into the cream sweater, and stepped onto the riser.

In the dim, shadowy gloom of the Agora basement, the ivory fabric glowed like a beacon.

Julian stood in the center of the track light oval. He extended his right arm, his fingertips reaching out until they touched the ribbed knit of my sleeve at the shoulder.

*Stand fourteen inches from my right deltoid,* he signed, his fingers brushing my forearm with intense, concentrated precision. *Not two feet. Fourteen inches. Your cream shoulder is my horizon line. When I turn right to address the Akron tables, I look at your sleeve. When I turn left to address the bar, I track your hands.*

"Fourteen inches leaves us zero clearance," I whispered, signing right against his chest. "If you throw an expansive two-handed classifier, your right knuckles will clip my ribs."

Julian smiled—a cold, dangerous grin that challenged the entire universe to get in his way.

*Then keep your ribs tight, college girl,* he signed. *Because tonight we're doing eighty minutes in the dark, and we're not dropping a single beat.*

At eight o'clock, the basement doors opened.

Eighty-five patrons stomped their boots on the rubber mats, shedding wet parkas and shaking lake water from umbrellas. Thirty of them were members of the Akron and Cleveland Deaf clubs—young machinists, teachers from the regional deaf program, college students in heavy flannels who crowded into the front tables with wide, eager smiles.

When the house lights dropped, the room was almost completely black.

The single fifty-watt track light hummed above our heads, casting a sickly yellow spotlight that barely illuminated Julian's face and chest.

Julian stepped into the circle.

I stood fourteen inches to his right, my cream sweater glowing softly in the spill, my hands raised to chest height, ready to voice and translate.

He didn't wait for polite applause. He opened with five minutes of blistering, improvised satire about Cleveland transit, lake effect sleet, and venue promoters who spent more money on vape juice than stage fuses.

The room caught fire.

Because the lighting was so dim, the audience leaned forward, holding their breath, their attention concentrated on the luminous choreography of his hands and my voice. The intimacy was breathtaking. Julian's signs were tight, compact, cutting through the shadows with surgical precision. Every time he pivoted, his shoulder brushed mine, the warmth of his body radiating through the wool knit of my sweater.

He didn't stumble. He didn't hesitate. He used my cream shoulder as an immovable lighthouse, pivoting, returning, anchoring his spatial compass on my presence with absolute, unyielding trust.

When he delivered his final punchline, the Akron Deaf club stood up as one, waving eighty hands in the shadowy basement air, their feet pounding the floorboards until the track fixture swayed overhead.

Julian bowed, reached out through the dark, found my wrist, and pulled me forward into the light beside him.

The audience roared. Even Vinnie was standing by the beer taps, clapping with both hands.

Julian turned to me in the yellow pool of light, his chest heaving, his amber eyes shining with fierce triumph. He didn't sign words. He squeezed my wrist three times—the vaudeville rhythm for 'the drop'—and let his hand slide down to intertwine with my fingers.

We stepped off the platform together into the rainy Cleveland night."""

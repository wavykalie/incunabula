# Chapter 1: The Gaffer Tape Line

The floorboards of the basement stage on Clark Street smelled of seventy years of spilled pilsner and dried mop water.

I knelt on the sticky pine with a fresh roll of fluorescent green gaffer tape, pressing three-inch strips along the edge of the apron. The stage had no lip, no railing, and no orchestra pit—just a four-foot drop straight onto the concrete floor where the front-row cocktail tables clustered. In full house lights, the edge was obvious. In a thirty-percent blacked-out room with two 500-watt ellipsoidal spotlights burning straight into a performer's retinas, the edge disappeared into a uniform void of black air.

"Make the corners double," Deacon said from the sound booth at the back of the room. He didn't look up from his tablet. His fingers flicked across the fader apps, killing the house music and bringing up the stage wash. "Ten degrees of central tunnel means he doesn't have peripheral down-glance. If his toe catches the air, he goes over."

"I know the radius," I said, smoothing down the tape with my thumb. "I read the rider."

"Reading the rider is cheap," Deacon said. His voice had the flat, unhurried cadence of a CODA who spent his entire adolescence arguing with hearing school administrators and airline gate agents. "Living with the rider in eight cities is three weeks of no sleep. Did Elena give you the brief on his pacing?"

"She gave me four pages of performance history and his RID compliance notes."

Deacon let out a single dry snort that crackled through the stage monitor. "Elena Rossi thinks stand-up comedy is a state senate subcommittee meeting. Jules isn't a state senator."

The fire door at stage left groaned on its hydraulic hinge. A man in a damp black wool peacoat stepped through into the dim amber spill of the work lights. He shook wet slush from his hair, stomped heavy combat boots on the rubber mat, and stopped dead five feet from the apron.

Julian Vance.

He didn't search the room with wide scanning glances the way hearing comics did when they scoped out a venue. His head stayed locked in a deliberate, measured axis. He tilted his chin up slightly, angling his face toward the light source, eyes narrowing to let his macula resolve the high-contrast boundaries of the risers. He was thirty-two, thin-shouldered, with sharp cheekbones, dark hair cut brutally short at the temples, and long, corded fingers that were already moving in a restless, involuntary twitch against his coat pocket.

He spotted the fluorescent green square at his feet. He took three deliberate strides onto the pine boards, tapped the toe of his right boot against the neon border, and looked directly down at me.

His brown eyes had the slight, drifting nystagmus typical of advanced retinitis pigmentosa—a tiny involuntary tremor when the photoreceptors searched for contrast in dim light. But when his gaze locked onto my face, the focus was absolute.

He raised both hands, dropping the peacoat open. His hands were fast, sharp, and stripped of polite conversational padding.

[YOU INTERPRETER? NEW ONE?] he signed. The movements were compact, snapping cleanly inside the twenty-inch signing space between his sternum and chin.

I stood up, wiping adhesive dust from my jeans, and stepped into his central visual cone at a four-foot distance.

[YES. MAYA LIN. CERTIFIED NIC-ADVANCED. GLAD MEET-YOU,] I signed back, keeping my handshape precision textbook, my posture grounded.

Julian's mouth curled into an asymmetrical, mocking smirk. His hands fired back before my greeting had fully settled in the air.

[HEARING GIRL. CERTIFIED. BEAUTIFUL. WONDERFUL.] His fingers flicked an exaggerated, sarcastic flourish on the sign for CERTIFIED, mimicking a pompous bureaucrat stamping paper. [DEACON TOLD ME. AGENCY SENT GOLD MEDAL WINNER. TELL ME, LIN: YOU EVER HEAR WORD 'MOTHERFUCKER' IN SIGN LANGUAGE, OR DID YOUR COLLEGE PROFESSORS TEACH YOU SHAKESPEARE AND BIBLE?]

My pulse gave a quiet, sharp kick against my ribs, but I kept my facial expression neutral, holding the professional baseline Elena had drilled into me for five years.

"I grew up in Chicago," I signed and voiced simultaneously, keeping my vocal tone level and dry. "I have heard every variation of maternal insult known to Cook County. If you sign it fast, I voice it fast. If you slur the movement, I translate the slur."

Julian tilted his head, studying my fingers. His fingers spelled a rapid sequence: [B-U-L-L-S-H-I-T.]

Then he launched.

Without warning, his hands exploded into a blinding three-sentence routine: [POLICE OFFICER STOPPED ME ON KENNEDY EXPRESSWAY. HE SAID 'ROLL DOWN WINDOW.' I POINTED TO EARS. HE THOUGHT I WAS REACHING FOR GLOCK. I PULLED OUT AUDIOGRAM. HE THOUGHT AUDIOGRAM WAS MEXICAN PASSPORT. HE CALLED BORDER PATROL.]

The signs blurred into rapid spatial shifts, non-manual morphemes flashing across his lips and brow—a puffed cheek for the officer's beer belly, a sudden squint for the siren glare, a razor-thin eyebrow raise on the punchline.

I didn't blink. I didn't reach for a filler syllable. My vocal cords caught the rhythm on the half-beat:

"Cop pulled me over on the Kennedy. Screamed roll the window down. I pointed to my ears. Asshole thought I was reaching for a piece. I showed him my audiogram. He thought my decibel chart was a forged cartel passport. Called Homeland Security on my middle ear."

Silence dropped back over the basement room.

Deacon stopped adjusting the mixer. The only sound was the wet rumble of the Chicago Red Line subway vibrating through the floorboards beneath our feet.

Julian stared at me for three long seconds. His fingers hung suspended in mid-air, frozen on the final classifier shape. The mocking smirk had vanished from his lips. His jaw tightened, the muscles along his throat knotting as he processed the timing of my English voicing.

He dropped his hands into his coat pockets.

[YOUR ACCENT FLAT,] he signed, his fingers poking out just enough to be legible within his chest box. [BUT YOUR TEMPO CLEAN.]

"My tempo is whatever you set on stage," I said in speech and sign.

"Good," Julian mouthed without voice, his lips forming the English word with razor precision.

He turned on his heel, walked straight toward the green room door, and didn't look back once.

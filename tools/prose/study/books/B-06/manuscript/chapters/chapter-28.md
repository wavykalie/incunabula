# Chapter 28: The Morning After

The morning light filtering through the sheer curtains of the Burlington hotel room was pale, silver, and completely still.

Outside, Lake Champlain stretched to the horizon like a sheet of hammered pewter, its dark winter water fringed with white shelves of shore ice beneath the jagged silhouette of the Adirondack peaks on the western horizon.

I sat on the wide windowsill with a steaming mug of black tea cradled in my hands, wearing an oversized wool sweater that smelled of Julian's cedar cologne.

Across the room, Julian was still sleeping.

He lay on his side on the white hotel sheets, his dark hair messy against the pillow, his long breathing slow and unshadowed. Without his stage defenses, his face looked young, peaceful, and entirely vulnerable. One scarred hand was stretched across the empty mattress beside him, fingers curved as if searching for something in his sleep.

I looked down at the notification screen of my phone resting on the sill.

The email had arrived at 3:14 AM from the National Licensure Registry in Alexandria, Virginia:

> **FORMAL NOTICE OF EXPEDITED ETHICAL REVIEW**
> **HEARING DATE:** November 14, 2026 — 9:00 AM EST
> **LOCATION:** Virtual Teleconference / Region 1 Disciplinary Panel
> **MATTER:** Complaint EPS-2026-GL-089 (Elena Rossi v. Maya Lin, NIC)
> **SANCTION SOUGHT:** Permanent Revocation of Interpreter Certification

The clock was ticking. In four days, in Philadelphia, the disciplinary panel would convene.

By the time we crossed the bridge into Manhattan for the final performance at Town Hall, my legal authority to work as an interpreter would be formally terminated.

I took a slow sip of the hot tea.

I waited for the terror to hit me. I waited for the sick, hollow panic that had haunted my entire twenties—the desperate need to prove to my academic parents that I was worthy, the fear of failing a state board exam, the terror of stepping outside the bureaucratic safety of the Code.

Nothing came.

Inside my chest, where the panic used to live, there was only a vast, serene stillness.

I looked back at Julian sleeping peacefully on the white linen.

The rules of the Registry were designed for a world where people were commodities—where interpreters were anonymous contractors hired by the hour to facilitate legal depositions and insurance audits. Those rules had no vocabulary for what Julian and I had built across three thousand miles of snow and stage light.

They called it an ethical breach.

They called it operational enmeshment.

I called it love.

Julian stirred in the bed. He rolled onto his back, stretched his long arms toward the ceiling, and blinked against the pale silver light streaming through the glass.

He turned his head toward the window.

He saw my silhouette against the morning sky.

A slow, breathtaking smile spread across his face. He raised his right hand from the bedsheets and signed in the quiet dawn light:

*Good morning, partner.*

I set my mug on the sill, walked across the room, and slipped back under the blankets beside him."""

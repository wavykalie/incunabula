# Chapter 17: City Four: Buffalo Snow

The Buffalo Center for the Arts smelled of steam heat, damp wool, and floor wax.

It was an eight-hundred-seat proscenium auditorium located near the campus of the regional technical institute, and tonight every single seat was filled. More than four hundred students and faculty from the Deaf studies and technical programs had bought out the entire orchestra section, their colorful winter parkas draped over the backs of the maroon velvet seats.

The roar in the hall was deafening, but it wasn't vocal noise.

It was the collective percussion of four hundred pairs of heavy winter boots stamping in unison against the hollow wooden floorboards, punctuated by high-pitched whoops, whistle blasts, and the rhythmic clapping of hands.

Backstage in the wings, Deacon checked the master audio feed on his tablet.

"House is ninety percent signing," Deacon said, his face splitting into a rare, wide grin. "We got students from Rochester who chartered two buses through the snowdrifts on Interstate 90 just to catch this set. They've been waiting two years for Jules to come back."

Julian stood in the wing shadows, doing calf raises against an equipment crate to warm up his Achilles tendons.

He wore his tour uniform: black denim jeans, a freshly ironed dark charcoal button-down shirt with the sleeves rolled to mid-forearm, and his combat boots polished to a dull matte sheen.

I stood beside him in my black performance top, adjusting my wireless transmitter belt pack.

Julian reached out, his hand finding my shoulder in the dark.

*Big room,* he signed, his eyes wide and bright with adrenaline. *The stage is forty feet wide. Where are the spots?*

"Three overhead specials," I reported, signing into his chest. "Twelve hundred watts each, focused in an overlapping triangle dead center. You have fourteen feet of bright, continuous lateral movement before the light drops off. I've taped the entire perimeter with five-inch neon orange tape."

Julian squeezed my shoulder. *And you?*

"Stage left, twelve inches from your triangle boundary," I answered. "Under the secondary downlight. You'll have my silhouette against the black traveler curtain every time you turn."

The house lights flashed three times—the universal visual cue that the show was starting.

The percussive boot-stomping from the orchestra seats surged, vibrating through the stage joists like a minor earthquake. The concrete floor beneath our feet hummed with rhythmic energy.

The stage manager gave us the thumbs-up from the prompt corner.

Julian took a deep, centering breath through his nose, rolled his shoulders, and stepped through the velvet drape into the blazing white glory of the spotlights.

The auditorium erupted.

Four hundred Deaf students in the orchestra leapt to their feet as one, their arms thrust high into the air, hands twisting in a sea of visual ovation that filled Julian's central cone of sight with pure, undulating light. Behind them, four hundred hearing patrons whistled and applauded, the noise echoing off the high plaster ceiling.

Julian didn't bow. He stood in the center of the stage, threw his head back, and let the acoustic and visual wave wash over him.

Then he raised his hands, and the room went dead silent.

*Buffalo,* he signed, his face deadpan. *You look terrible. Did the state shut down the snowplow budget, or did you all walk here inside a giant snow globe?*

The front ten rows exploded in visual laughter—hands fluttering, heads thrown back, shoulders shaking.

I voiced the English punchline for the balcony tables, my delivery landing with crisp, punchy resonance through the auditorium's surround sound system.

For the next seventy-five minutes, Julian delivered the performance of his career.

He didn't just tell jokes; he dismantled the entire hearing world's perception of disability. He performed his legendary routine about deaf job interviews, demonstrating how a deaf candidate can turn an employer's awkward, stammering discomfort into an aggressive negotiation tactic. He performed his bit about closed captions at movie theaters, mocking the handheld plastic cupholder screens that broke down every time the hero was about to defuse a bomb.

His signing was poetry. He used complex grammatical classifiers that turned his hands into soaring airplanes, roaring motorcycles, and trembling medical needles, shifting perspectives with the agility of a master filmmaker editing in real time.

My voice became his shadow.

Every vocal inflection, every ironic pause, every burst of sarcastic laughter matched his physical output with microscopic precision. I wasn't just interpreting words; I was channeling his soul.

When he reached his encore, Julian stopped in the center of the light triangle.

He looked down at the four hundred young deaf students in the front rows, his face softening into deep, unadorned reverence.

*They tell us the world is built for ears,* Julian signed, his movements slow, stately, and immense. *They tell us that without sound, we are missing half the human experience. But look at this room. Look at your hands. We don't need their noise to feel the thunder. We are the thunder.*

The auditorium didn't just applaud; it shook.

Students stood on their velvet seats, screaming with their hands, stamping their boots until dust drifted down from the balcony fixtures. Tears were streaming down the faces of young women in the front row. Men were embracing each other in the aisles.

Julian bowed deeply, touching his forehead and his heart.

Then he turned to stage left, walked across the apron through the bright lights, took my hand in his, and pulled me to the center of the stage.

Before eight hundred cheering people, Julian Vance raised my hand high above his head in victory.

The sea of fluttering hands surged toward both of us, washing away every threat, every fear, and every cold mile of interstate highway in a torrent of shared triumph.

We stood on the apron for three full curtain calls. Julian's fingers were locked with mine, his chest heaving, his face bathed in the golden wash of the hall.

When the heavy velvet traveler finally closed behind us, the thunder of the room continued to vibrate through our boots."""

# Chapter 10: The Midnight Diner

The Athens Coney Island on Michigan Avenue was lit by glaring white fluorescent fixtures that turned every human face the color of skim milk.

It was one-thirty in the morning. Outside, a light freezing rain was turning the slush on the sidewalks into slick, treacherous grease. Inside, the vinyl booths were cracked and patched with gray duct tape, the air was thick with the smell of sizzling beef fat and burnt onions, and a greasy stainless-steel counter held three glass display domes filled with dry apple pies.

Julian was asleep in the far corner of our booth, his head propped against the cold window glass, his black knit cap pulled down to the bridge of his nose.

Across the laminate table, Deacon sat with a ceramic mug of steaming diner coffee between his calloused hands. His eyes were red-rimmed with exhaustion, his flannel shirt smelling of road dust and gaffer adhesive.

He took a slow sip of coffee, set the mug down, and looked across at me.

"You took a hard hit on those stairs today," Deacon said in a low voice, speaking English with the quiet, effortless articulation of a CODA who knew how to keep a conversation private in a public room.

"My shoulder is bruised," I said, stirring a packet of raw sugar into my tea. "It will heal."

Deacon tore open a paper packet of crackers and dropped two into his chili bowl. "I'm not talking about your shoulder, Maya. I'm talking about your head."

I paused, holding the spoon suspended over the mug. "What about my head?"

"You're leaning in," Deacon said bluntly. He didn't look angry; he looked weary, with the heavy, protective caution of an older brother who had watched the same car crash happen three times before on the same stretch of highway. "I watched you in the green room. I watched you in the service hallway before the encore. You're not interpreting for him like an agency contractor. You're investing in him."

My jaw tightened. "I am doing my job, Deacon. He has specific linguistic and access requirements. If I don't establish trust with him, the translation fails."

Deacon leaned forward across the table, his elbows resting on either side of his chili bowl.

"Three interpreters quit before you," Deacon said. "Did Elena Rossi tell you that when she assigned you the contract?"

"She mentioned there were scheduling conflicts with previous providers."

Deacon let out a harsh, silent laugh that didn't reach his eyes. "Scheduling conflicts. That's bureaucratic code for emotional hemorrhage. First guy was a hotshot from Minneapolis. Master's degree in ASL literature. He lasted four days. Jules broke him in Milwaukee because the guy tried to soften a joke about a deaf priest and an altar boy. Jules called him a linguistic coward on stage and refused to let him back in the van."

I looked over at Julian. He hadn't stirred; his breathing remained slow and rhythmic against the vibrating windowpane.

"The second one," Deacon continued, "was a woman from Indianapolis. Certified twenty years. Very sweet. Very maternal. She started bringing him herbal teas, adjusting his scarf, telling him he was an inspiration to the community. Jules shredded her in a green room in Columbus. Told her if she wanted a sick puppy to nurse, she should go volunteer at the animal shelter. She took an Uber to the airport at three in the morning and never spoke to us again."

"I am not maternal," I said. "And I don't soften his jokes."

"I know you don't," Deacon said, his voice dropping another octave. "That's what makes you dangerous. You have the chops to match his speed, and you have the spine to tell him when his spatial syntax is sloppy. He respects you, Maya. And that is the worst thing that could happen to a professional interpreter on a twelve-city tour."

"Why is that the worst thing?"

"Because Jules is on a one-way track," Deacon said, looking down at his coffee. "This isn't a promotional tour to build a brand. This is a farewell tour. When we hit New York in three weeks, the curtain comes down. His central vision is collapsing faster than the specialists predicted. By next winter, he won't have enough field left to read an audience from a stage riser. He's burning his life down like a magnesium flare because he wants to go out on his own terms."

He looked up, meeting my eyes with uncompromising clarity.

"Elena Rossi and her ethics board will not protect you when this tour ends," Deacon said. "If you cross the line from conduit to partner, the agency will take your certification badge and throw you out on the street. And Jules won't be able to save you."

I looked down at the dark surface of my tea, watching my own reflection ripple in the steam.

Under the table, Julian's boot shifted against the floorboards, brushing against my sneaker. He didn't wake up.

I picked up my mug and drank.

The rain outside the Athens Coney Island had turned into a steady, freezing downpour that drummed relentlessly against the plate glass windows.

Deacon finished his chili, pushed the empty bowl aside, and signaled the waitress for the check.

"We leave for Cleveland at eight tomorrow," Deacon said, checking the weather radar on his cracked smartphone. "Lake-effect snow warning across northern Ohio. Cuyahoga County is already putting salt trucks on the highway."

I looked across the vinyl booth at Julian. He was still sleeping, his forehead resting against the cool glass, his breath fogging a small oval on the pane that slowly cleared each time he exhaled.

In sleep, the fierce, combative defiance drained from his features, leaving him looking younger, more vulnerable to the crushing weight of the progressive darkness closing around him. His hands—those brilliant, expressive hands that could hold a room of two hundred people spellbound with a flick of a wrist—lay curled loosely against his denim lap, palm up, fingers curled like sleeping leaves.

"He's running out of road, Maya," Deacon said quietly, catching my gaze. "I've been on the road with him for six years. I know what happens when an artist realizes their clock is ticking down to the final second. He doesn't know how to stop. If he stops, the silence catches him."

"Then we keep moving," I said.

Deacon looked at me for a long moment, then pulled a ten-dollar bill from his wallet and dropped it on the table.

"Cleveland tomorrow," Deacon said. "Get some sleep."

He tapped Julian's shoulder gently, and we walked out together into the freezing Detroit rain.

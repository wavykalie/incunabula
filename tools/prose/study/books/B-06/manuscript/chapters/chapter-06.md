# Chapter 6: Interstate 90 East

The November sky over northwest Indiana was the color of a wet galvanized bucket.

Freezing drizzle coated the windshield of Deacon's fifteen-passenger Ford Econoline van in a crust of gray ice that the wiper blades could only smear into semi-transparent arcs. The highway ran dead straight through the petrochemical flats of Gary—an endless corridor of rusted refinery towers, steam plumes tearing sideways in the lake gale, and colossal illuminated billboards advertising personal injury attorneys and riverboat casinos.

Deacon drove with both hands locked onto the top of the steering wheel at twelve o'clock, his shoulders hunched against the vibration of the road. In the back bench seats, three equipment road cases, two lighting stands, and Julian's rolling luggage rattled in rhythmic sync with every expansion joint on Interstate 90.

Julian sat in the front passenger seat.

He had pushed his seat back as far as the track would allow, resting his combat boots against the plastic dashboard. His black wool coat was buttoned up to his chin, his knit watch cap pulled down low over his ears. He wasn't asleep. His head was turned toward the passenger side window, staring out into the gray blur of passing freight yards.

In the middle row behind him, I sat wrapped in my down coat with a travel mug of black coffee between my knees, watching the back of his neck.

A semi-truck hauling steel coils roared past on our left, throwing a blinding sheet of dirty road spray across the passenger glass. Julian didn't flinch. He couldn't hear the diesel engine's roar through his severe hearing loss, and his peripheral field couldn't detect the massive grill until the vehicle's cab was already level with our front bumper.

He suddenly raised his right hand, resting his knuckles against the cold glass of the window.

His index finger began to trace letters against the condensation:

[S-H-I-T.]

He wiped it clean with his palm.

He waited ten seconds, watching another billboard emerge from the freezing fog. His fingers flicked again, spelling with effortless speed:

[C-A-S-I-N-O. E-M-P-I-R-E. L-O-S-E Y-O-U-R P-E-N-S-I-O-N H-E-R-E.]

I leaned forward between the two bucket seats, tapping his right shoulder twice with my fingertip—the standard tactile attention signal.

Julian turned his head toward the center console, bringing my face into his central tunnel.

[YOU TALKING TO ME?] he signed, his left hand joining the right in the narrow space between the front seats.

"You are reading billboards," I signed back, keeping my hands positioned right above the center console armrest where the gray daylight illuminated my fingers.

[NOTHING ELSE TO DO,] he signed. [INDIANA IS UGLY. FLAT CORN. CHEMICAL PLANTS. BILLBOARDS FOR LAWYERS WHO SUE TRUCKERS.]

"Gary isn't all of Indiana," Deacon said from the driver's seat, signing the sentence with his right hand without taking his eyes off the icy asphalt ahead. "South of here there are hills. Forests. Limestone quarries."

Julian waved Deacon's comment away with a dismissive flick of his wrist. [DEACON LOVES DIRT. HE IS A COUNTRY BOY AT HEART. PUT HIM IN A FIELD WITH A TRACTOR, HE IS HAPPY. ME? I NEED BRICK. I NEED ASPHALT. I NEED PEOPLE SCREAMING AT EACH OTHER ON SUBWAY PLATFORMS.]

"You need an audience," I signed.

Julian looked at me. His eyes were bloodshot from four hours of sleep, the dark circles under his lower lids pronounced in the gray morning light.

[YES,] he signed. [I NEED AN AUDIENCE. YOU THINK THAT IS EGO?]

"Every comedian has an ego," I answered. "If you didn't have an ego, you wouldn't demand one hundred and forty strangers sit in the dark and listen to you talk about your life."

Julian shook his head. He pulled his hands back, tucking them into his coat pockets for warmth before speaking again.

[HEARING COMICS HAVE EGO,] he signed, his fingers emerging slowly, deliberate and sharp. [THEY WANT TO BE CELEBRITIES. THEY WANT PODCASTS. THEY WANT NETFLIX SPECIALS WHERE THEY WEAR LEATHER JACKETS AND TALK ABOUT THEIR DIVORCES. I DON'T CARE ABOUT NETFLIX.]

"Then what do you care about?"

Julian looked back toward the frosted glass of the passenger window. Outside, a rusted water tower loomed against the pale sky, bearing the faded name of a closed steel mill.

[PROOF,] he signed. The single word hung in the air between our bucket seats, heavy and uncompromising.

"Proof of what?" I asked.

[PROOF THAT THIS LANGUAGE EXISTS,] he signed, his hands expanding into the tight cabin of the van. [WHEN A HEARING PERSON LOSES THEIR SIGHT, SOCIETY TELLS THEM TO LISTEN TO THE RADIO. THEY GIVE THEM AN AUDIOBOOK. THEY PUT A SCREEN-READER ON THEIR LAPTOP. EVERYTHING SHIFTS TO SOUND. BUT I HAVE NO SOUND. WHEN MY SIGHT FINISHES DYING, MY WORLD IS NOT A PODCAST. MY WORLD IS SKIN. IT IS TOUCH.]

My breath caught in my throat. I looked down at his long, pale fingers resting on the knee of his jeans.

"ProTactile," I murmured.

Julian's head jerked toward me, his brow furrowing in surprise.

[YOU KNOW PROTACTILE?] he signed, his fingers sharp and inquisitive.

"I studied the basics at the institute," I said. "Tactile phonology. Seven touch points on the arm and shoulder for listener feedback. Mapping space on the receiver's body instead of the air."

Julian let out a short, quiet grunt. [THE INSTITUTE TEACHES THEORY. THEY TREAT PROTACTILE LIKE A SPECIAL TOY FOR DEAFBLIND ELDERS IN NURSING HOMES. BUT IT IS NOT A TOY. IT IS AN ENTIRE LINGUISTIC CONTINENT. WHEN MY CENTRAL TEN DEGREES COLLAPSE TO ZERO—WHETHER IT TAKES TWO YEARS OR FIVE—I WILL NOT STOP BEING FUNNY. I WILL JUST HAVE TO DELIVER THE PUNCHLINE DIRECTLY INTO SOMEONE'S FOREARM.]

He stared at my hands. His gaze was intense, analytical, searching my face for any trace of pity or revulsion.

"That's going to make stand-up comedy very intimate," I signed back, keeping my tone deliberately dry. "You'll have to perform for one audience member at a time."

Julian's lips twitched. The tight tension in his shoulders dropped an inch.

[THEN I WILL CHARGE FIVE HUNDRED DOLLARS A TICKET,] he signed. [EXCLUSIVE. ARTISANAL. ONE LAUGH AT A TIME.]

We crossed the state line into Michigan an hour later. The rain turned into heavy, wet snowflakes that splattered against the windshield like wet cotton balls.

Julian didn't go back to reading billboards.

He reached into his backpack between his boots, pulled out a beat-up spiral notebook with a black ballpoint pen taped to the spine, and flipped to a page covered in dense, handwritten sketches. They weren't notes in English sentences; they were spatial diagrams. Small stick figures with directional arrows indicating torso rotation, facial expression glosses written in capital letters—CS for 'cheek puffed', TH for 'tongue between teeth', MM for 'lips pressed neutral'—and spatial grids showing the placement of imaginary stage props.

He tapped a diagram at the bottom of the page.

[LOOK AT THIS,] he signed, angling the notebook toward me. [NEW BIT FOR DETROIT. ABOUT THE AIRPORT ACCESSIBILITY CARTS. YOU KNOW THOSE BEEPING GOLF CARTS THAT DRIVE THROUGH O'HARE?]

"I know them," I signed. "Driven by guys in yellow vests who look like they want to run over passengers."

[EXACTLY,] Julian signed, his face coming alive with that sharp comedic spark. [WHEN I WAS TWENTY-TWO, AN AIRLINE WHEELCHAIR GUY INSISTED ON DRIVING ME TO MY GATE. I TOLD HIM: 'I AM DEAF, NOT PARALYZED. MY LEGS WORK FINE.' HE SAID: 'COMPANY POLICY. SENSORY DEFICIT MEANS WHEELCHAIR.' SO HE PUT ME IN THE CHAIR. HE DROVE ME THREE MILES PER HOUR DOWN TERMINAL ONE. AND HE HAD A ROTATING AMBER LIGHT ON HIS HEAD. LIKE I WAS A WIDE LOAD HAULING A MOBILE HOME ON THE HIGHWAY.]

I laughed out loud—a real, spontaneous sound that made Deacon glance back in the rearview mirror.

"How do you sign the rotating amber light?" I asked.

Julian raised his left hand above his head, curling his fingers into a claw shape, and spun his wrist in rapid, rhythmic circles while making a terrifying, wide-eyed siren face.

[AMBER WARNING,] he signed. [DANGEROUS DEAF PERSON IN TRANSIT. CLEAR THE RUNWAY.]

"I can voice that," I said, smiling despite the chill leaking through the van door. "That's going to kill in Detroit."

Julian closed the notebook. He tapped the cover against his knee twice, looking at me with a steady, quiet appraisal that lingered long after the joke had settled.

Deacon turned the van's heating fan up a notch to defrost the windshield corners. "Two hours to the Michigan state line," Deacon said, signing the number two with his steering hand. "There's a truck stop near South Bend with clean restrooms and real diesel coffee. We stopping?"

[STOP,] Julian signed. [MY SIGHT IS TIRED. AND LIN NEEDS TO BUY MORE LOZENGES BEFORE HER VOICE SOUNDS LIKE A CHAINSAW.]

He settled back against the headrest, pulled his watch cap down over his eyebrows, and closed his eyes.

The van rolled eastward across the frozen plains, cutting through the gray winter mist like a dark needle tracing an uncertain seam.

We pulled off Interstate 90 into a twenty-four-hour travel plaza near Elkhart, Indiana.

The parking lot was a vast expanse of wet black asphalt rutted with dirty brown slush, illuminated by orange sodium-vapor floodlights mounted on sixty-foot steel poles. Dozens of semi-trucks idled in long rows, their diesel exhausts chuffing steady gray clouds into the freezing sleet.

Julian climbed down from the passenger seat, his combat boots crunching into the frozen gravel. He reached out immediately, his left hand finding my down sleeve at the elbow.

[DARK,] he signed with his right hand, keeping the motion tight against his ribs. [SODIUM LIGHTS ARE ORANGE. ZERO BLUE CONTRAST. MY FOVEA CANNOT FIND THE CURB.]

"I have you," I signed against the palm of his hand—the standard tactile guide response.

I crooked my right arm, allowing him to slide his hand up to my forearm just above the wrist. The grip was firm, purposeful, devoid of hesitation. We walked together across the icy lane toward the glass double doors of the travel plaza, matching our strides across the slush.

Inside, the plaza smelled of roasted coffee, cinnamon buns, and diesel exhaust leaking through the air curtains.

Deacon headed straight for the hot beverage island to fill two massive insulated mugs with dark roast coffee. Julian stood by the magazine rack, his hand still resting lightly on my sleeve, letting his eyes adjust from the dim orange darkness outside to the bright white glare of the fluorescent interior.

He looked at a rotating rack of wire sunglasses. He reached out, lifted a pair of oversized black wraparound sunglasses from the top peg, and slipped them onto his nose.

He turned toward me, crossing his arms over his chest, his face completely deadpan behind the dark polarized lenses.

[HOW DO I LOOK?] he signed. [FAMOUS BLIND MUSICIAN? OR SECRET SERVICE AGENT GUARDING A TWEET?]

"You look like a retired undercover cop from Miami," I answered, smiling as I signed. "Who forgot that it is twenty-eight degrees and snowing in Indiana."

Julian took off the sunglasses and hung them back on the wire peg. A quiet, genuine laugh shook his chest.

[MIAMI SOUNDS WONDERFUL,] he signed. [NO ICE. NO SLUSH. NO CRACKED GAFFER TAPE ON COLD PINE BOARDS.]

"We have ten more cities before Florida," Deacon said, walking over with the steaming mugs. "And none of them are Miami."

Julian took his coffee mug, wrapped both hands around the hot cardboard sleeve, and nodded toward the glass exit doors.

[THEN LET'S GO TO DETROIT,] he signed.

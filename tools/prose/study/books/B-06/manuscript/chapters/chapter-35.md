# Chapter 35: The Mechanics of Co-Creation

It was 1:00 AM at an all-night diner on Broad Street in Philadelphia.

Sitting around three pushed-together laminate tables were twelve prominent members of the Pennsylvania Deaf and interpreting communities: university professors, Deaf theater directors, civil rights attorneys, and veteran RID-certified interpreters.

On the table between their coffee cups lay the forty-two pages of Elena Rossi's disciplinary complaint.

David Morales, a sixty-year-old Deaf attorney and former national president of the National Association of the Deaf, tapped his finger against the summary suspension order.

"This document is thirty years out of date," David signed, his movements dignified, sharp, and authoritative. "Elena Rossi is attempting to enforce a 1980s medical-model understanding of interpreting on an avant-garde artistic performance. In contemporary performance theory, the interpreter on a creative platform is recognized as an active linguistic performer and collaborative artist."

A woman sitting opposite him—Sarah Jenkins, a veteran RID ethics investigator—nodded in vehement agreement.

*I reviewed the Burlington and Buffalo performance tapes,* Sarah signed with rapid, precise fingers. *Maya did not violate Tenet 3.4. She operated under the creative direction of the primary consumer. Julian Vance demanded tactile proximity and personalized spatial alignment to accommodate his narrowing visual field. For an ethics panel to penalize an interpreter for adhering to a Deaf consumer's specific accessibility mandate is institutional ableism.*

Julian sat beside me, his hand resting under the table on my knee, reading the fast-moving sign language across the booth with quiet, intense concentration.

*What can we do before New York?* Julian signed to David.

David smiled—a sharp, legal predator's smile.

"Tomorrow morning, our legal coalition is filing an emergency amicus intervention with the National Licensure Review Board in Alexandria," David signed. "We are submitting forty-five sworn affidavits from Deaf consumers, academics, and artists across the Northeast corridor, declaring that Maya Lin's work on this tour represents the highest standard of cultural competence, linguistic excellence, and disability rights advocacy in modern American theater."

He looked directly across the table at me.

"You don't surrender that badge, Maya," David signed, his eyes warm with profound respect. "You walk into Town Hall on Sunday night with your head high. You represent the future of our profession, not its past."

I sat in the Philadelphia diner with Julian's warm hand resting on my knee, surrounded by the fierce solidarity of the Deaf world, feeling the last remnants of my fear dissolve into diamond-hard resolve.

We spent the next two hours going through the affidavits, cross-referencing performance dates and video timestamps. David outlined the legal strategy: an immediate challenge to the constitutionality of the RID neutrality clause when applied to performance arts.

By the time the diner's neon sign flickered off in the pre-dawn gray, we had an army of advocates standing behind us.

Julian walked me back to the van through the quiet streets of Center City. He held my hand in the open air, his fingers warm through my glove, his stride unhurried and confident.

"Four hundred miles to Manhattan," Deacon said as we climbed into the Econoline.

Julian looked at me, smiled, and leaned back against the headrest, ready for the final leg."""

# Chapter 18: Backstage in the Wings

The rush of the Buffalo standing ovation lasted until the stage curtains swung shut with a heavy velvet thud.

The moment the thick fabric closed, cutting off the twelve-hundred-watt spotlights, Julian's knees buckled.

He didn't fall to the floor, but his upper body sagged forward like a marionette whose strings had been severed. His breath came in ragged, wheezing gasps, his hands flying to his eyes as if he were trying to physically claw away a veil.

*Jules!* I signed, catching his upper arm before he could stumble into the metal counterweight rail.

Deacon was already there, dropping his clipboard onto a road case and shining his small penlight downward at the floorboards.

"Photokeratitis," Deacon muttered grimly, his voice tight with anxiety. "The halogens were too hot. Three twelve-hundred-watt fixtures reflecting off that white stage tape. His retinas are bleached out."

Julian leaned heavily against my shoulder. His skin was burning hot, sweat soaking through his charcoal button-down shirt, his temples throbbing visibly against his hairline.

*Can't see,* he signed against my forearm, his fingers trembling so violently he could barely hold the handshapes. *White spots. Giant white clouds in the center. Everything is gray.*

"It's temporary bleaching, Jules," I whispered, pressing my mouth close to his temple so he could feel the vibration of my jaw. "Your macular cones are overloaded from the glare. It takes ten to fifteen minutes for the photopigments to regenerate."

Julian groaned, his forehead pressing into the crook of my neck. He was trembling from head to foot—a mixture of total physical exhaustion and the terrifying dread that this time, the bleach wouldn't clear.

"We have to get him to the dressing room," Deacon said, checking the backstage corridor. "The promoter is bringing twenty VIP ticket holders back for autographs in five minutes. If they see him like this, it'll be all over Instagram before midnight."

"Not through the main hall," I said immediately. "There are four industrial fluorescent fixtures buzzing in that corridor. The flicker will trigger a migraine. We use the service tunnel behind the fly gallery."

*Maya,* Julian signed, his hand groping blindly through the air until his fingers found my wrist. His grip was frantic, desperate. *Lead me.*

I didn't take him by the elbow like an invalid.

I stepped in front of him, took his right hand, and placed his palm firmly against the small of my back, right over my spine.

"Keep your palm flat against my back," I instructed, signing the command with my left hand while holding his fingers securely. "Feel the movement of my hips. When I step down, you step down. When I pivot, you pivot. Track my spine."

Julian's fingers splayed wide across my lower back, gripping the knit fabric of my sweater.

We began our transit through the darkened fly gallery.

The backstage space was a labyrinth of hanging hemp ropes, counterweight iron bars, electrical feeder cables, and wooden flats stored against the brick walls. To someone with normal vision in working lights, it was cluttered; in the dim blue safety illumination, it was a minefield.

I walked with slow, deliberate, rhythmic steps.

Every time my right foot stepped over an uncoiled cable, I paused for half a second, letting the tilt of my pelvis communicate the obstacle to his palm. Julian tracked my movement with absolute fidelity. He stepped where I stepped, paused when I paused, his breath warm against the nape of my neck as he followed the living compass of my body through the dark.

We descended three concrete steps into the basement corridor.

I took the descent with exaggerated hip drops, giving him time to feel the elevation change through his palm. His boots hit the center of each concrete tread without a single scrape or hesitation.

We reached Dressing Room A.

Deacon unlocked the door, flicked on a single low twenty-watt incandescent desk lamp in the corner, and pulled the blackout curtains over the window.

I guided Julian to the vinyl armchair, turned him around, and eased him down into the cushions.

He collapsed back into the upholstery, his eyes squeezed shut, his head tilted back toward the ceiling. His chest rose and fell in deep, shuddering breaths.

I walked to the sink, soaked a clean cotton hand towel in cold water, wrung it out, and brought it over to the chair.

I knelt on the floor between his knees.

Gently, without speaking, I folded the cold, damp towel across his eyes and forehead.

Julian let out a long, shuddering sigh. His hands came up from the armrests, found my wrists, and held them tight against his chest, anchoring me to his side as the cool moisture began to soothe his burning retinas.

We stayed like that for ten minutes in the dim, quiet room—my knees on the linoleum, his hands holding mine, our breathing gradually falling into the same quiet, steady cadence."""

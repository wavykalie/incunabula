# Chapter 5: The RID Blue Book

The fluorescent lamp on the hotel desk buzzed with a flat, persistent sixty-cycle hum that gave me a dull headache right behind my temples.

Room 412 of the Best Western on Ohio Street smelled of industrial carpet shampoo, stale ozone from the heating unit, and the peppermint throat lozenges I had been sucking on since midnight. My vocal cords felt like they had been scraped with a wire brush. Every swallow sent a sharp ache through the strap muscles of my neck.

On the laminate desk surface, next to my laptop and a lukewarm paper cup of chamomile tea, lay my personal copy of the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf Code of Professional Conduct.

The Blue Book.

It wasn't actually blue anymore; the cardstock cover had faded to a dirty denim gray from five years of riding in the bottom of my leather messenger bag, rubbing against legal pads, dry-erase markers, and travel bottles of ibuprofen. Its official designation was the NAD-RID Code of Professional Conduct, adopted in 2005. Seven tenets. Ten pages of fine print defining the moral and legal boundaries of professional practice.

I leaned back in the desk chair, looking at the blue vinyl cover.

I remembered Elena Rossi's seminar on boundary management during my final semester. Elena had paced the front of the lecture hall in her tailored charcoal blazer, holding up a yellow legal pad.

"The moment you believe you are indispensable to a deaf consumer," Elena had warned, tapping her pen against the podium, "you have already crossed the ethical line. A professional interpreter is fungible. If you are hit by a city bus tomorrow morning, another qualified practitioner should be able to step onto that platform without the audience noticing the change in personnel."

Fungible. Replaceable like an AA battery inside a hearing aid amplifier.

Julian Vance didn't have fungible comedy. If an interpreter missed his spatial shifts by two inches, his punchlines evaporated into dead air. If an interpreter delivered his biting sarcasm with polite church-receptionist sweetness, his entire artistic voice was assassinated in front of two hundred people.

Elena's textbook universe did not account for art.

I opened the booklet to page four:

"Tenet 3.0: Conduct. Interpreters conduct themselves in a manner appropriate to the specific interpreting situation."

"Guiding Principle: Interpreters provide service with professional demeanor and cultural sensitivity. Interpreters refrain from participating in conduct that would damage the reputation of the profession or compromise the integrity of the assignment."

"3.3: Avoid performing dual or conflicting roles in interdisciplinary team environments or in situations where personal or professional interests may conflict."

"3.8: Refrain from personal involvement that impairs the objectivity required to provide services."

Objectivity.

I rested my fingertips against the cold page, staring at the word until the black ink blurred under the buzzing fluorescent tube.

In the classrooms of the interpreting program in Chicago, Elena Rossi had taught Tenet 3 as if it were a physical law of nature, immutable as gravity. "You are an instrument," Elena had told twenty wide-eyed undergraduates sitting in a semi-circle of folding chairs. "You are the acoustic wire running between two languages. An acoustic wire does not have an opinion about the message it carries. It does not feel sympathy. It does not feel anger. If you cry during a medical oncology appointment, you have failed your consumer. If you laugh at a defendant's joke in traffic court, you have contaminated the legal record."

The conduit model. Clean, sterile, safe.

It protected the hearing agency from liability. It protected the interpreter from emotional burnout. It allowed institutions to treat language access as a line item on an invoice—a disposable, interchangeable service provided by anonymous technicians dressed in neutral black clothing.

I thought about Julian Vance standing under the burning spotlight on Clark Street.

When he signed his routine about the iron pipe of his visual field, his hands had reached into my ribcage and seized my vocal cords by the root. To translate his comedy, I could not sit behind a glass partition like a court reporter typing transcripts. His comedy required me to inhabit his anger. It required me to take his biting, sarcastic fury and project it through my own throat with enough force to pierce the consciousness of a room full of complacent hearing drinkers.

Was that an acoustic wire?

Or was that a collaboration?

My phone buzzed against the desk wood. The screen lit up with an incoming text from Deacon:

[VAN PACKED. JULES IS IN ROOM 414. WHEELS UP AT 0800 FOR DETROIT. BRING COFFEE.]

I tapped out a quick reply: [GOT IT. SEE YOU IN THE LOBBY.]

I closed the Blue Book. I slid it back into the front pocket of my bag, zipped the nylon compartment shut, and turned off the desk lamp.

The room dropped into absolute darkness, save for the narrow slit of amber streetlight leaking around the edges of the vinyl blackout curtains. I lay on the firm hotel mattress with my hands folded over my chest, listening to the muffled rumble of nighttime delivery trucks rolling down Ohio Street toward the lake.

My hands were still buzzing with the ghost rhythm of Julian's signs.

I thought about the legal definition of independence in the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Title III mandated reasonable accommodations in public venues: ramps, accessible restrooms, assistive listening devices, qualified interpreters. But the statute envisioned accommodations as static fixtures—tools installed by an institution to allow an individual to function within hearing parameters.

Julian didn't fit inside Title III.

His art wasn't about functioning within hearing parameters; it was an aggressive critique of those parameters. When Julian stood on stage, he inverted the entire dynamic. He forced the hearing audience to adapt to his visual geography, to read his body language, to endure the discomfort of silence, and to experience the linguistic power of ASL on its own uncompromising terms.

The medical model viewed retinitis pigmentosa as a progressive sensory deficit to be cataloged, managed, and pitied.

The Deaf cultural model viewed Julian as a linguistic master whose native language happened to be visual, and whose visual field was narrowing. To Julian, the tragedy was not that his eyes were failing; the tragedy was an inaccessible world that refused to create space for an artist to evolve.

I leaned back in the desk chair, looking at the faded blue vinyl cover of the Code.

In my third year at the interpreting institute in Chicago, we had spent four weeks dissecting the grievance procedures of the Ethical Practices System. We analyzed hypothetical case studies: an interpreter who gave investment advice to a deaf business owner; an interpreter who helped an elderly deaf client write a personal will; an interpreter who accepted expensive gifts from a consumer's family.

Every case was framed in terms of fiduciary responsibility and the vulnerability of the consumer.

The Code had been written by pioneers who remembered the era of missionary interpreters—hearing preachers and social workers who controlled the lives of deaf adults under the guise of Christian benevolence. The pioneers were right to build walls. Without those walls, deaf people were at the mercy of paternalistic hearing do-gooders who infantilized them.

I closed the Blue Book and slipped it into my bag.

# Chapter 7: The Green Room Mirror

The green room at the Motor City Comedy Club was a windowless box behind the liquor stockroom that smelled of pine disinfectant, damp cardboard, and unwashed bar towels.

A single fluorescent strip light flickered intermittently over a Formica counter covered in sticky drink rings, empty Poland Spring bottles, and an overflowing plastic ashtray from some bygone decade when performers were still permitted to smoke indoors. Above the counter hung a frameless rectangular mirror with a jagged crack running diagonally across the lower left corner.

I stood before the glass, my fingers pressing firmly into the muscles on either side of my larynx.

"Ah-h-h," I hummed, testing my vocal resonance on a low D-flat. The pitch was grainy, vibrating with the telltale rasp of vocal cord edema.

I reached for my ceramic mug of hot tap water, squeezed two generous dollops of clover honey from a plastic bear, and stirred the mixture with the plastic cap of a ballpoint pen. In five years of interpreting municipal hearings, medical intakes, and university engineering lectures, I had never punished my vocal mechanism the way I had during the ninety minutes on Clark Street.

A spoken lecture is linear. It follows the natural cadences of human respiration; a professor pauses to take a breath, to glance at their notes, to take a sip of water.

Stand-up comedy is an ambush.

A comedian's rhythm is designed to disrupt expectations, to withhold breath, to accelerate through the premise and strike the punchline before the listener's rational defenses can erect a barrier. Voicing Julian Vance required my larynx to execute continuous, violent dynamic shifts—leaping from a conspiratorial whisper to an explosive barroom shout within the space of three syllables.

I took a slow sip of the honeyed water, feeling the thick warmth coat the irritated lining of my pharynx.

In the cracked mirror, I caught Julian's reflection.

He had entered the green room through the stage door without a sound, his rubber-soled boots making no impact on the worn linoleum. He was standing three feet behind my left shoulder, watching my throat through the glass.

He didn't move forward into my personal space. He stayed anchored in the doorway, his head tilted slightly to account for the dim ambient lighting of the room.

[YOUR THROAT HURTS?] he signed, the motion minimal, conversational, low against his waist.

I turned around to face him directly, wiping my lips with a paper napkin.

"It is fatigued," I signed back. "Laryngeal strain. It is common after high-volume performance interpreting."

Julian walked to the Formica counter. He picked up an unopened bottle of ginger ale from the wire basket, twisted the cap off with his bare palm, and set it down in front of me.

[DRINK GINGER,] he signed. [MY MOTHER WAS AN AUDIOLOGIST. SHE ALWAYS SAID GINGER CUTS INFLAMMATION BETTER THAN HONEY.]

"Your mother was an audiologist?" I asked, my fingers framing the signs with genuine surprise.

Julian's mouth tightened into a faint, ironic smile. He leaned his hip against the counter, crossing his arms over his chest.

[IRONIC, RIGHT?] he signed. [SHE SPENT THIRTY YEARS FITTING HEARING AIDS ON OLD STEELWORKERS IN JOLIET. THEN SHE GAVE BIRTH TO A KID WITH SENSORINEURAL LOSS AND RETINITIS PIGMENTOSA. GENETICS HAS A VERY CRUEL SENSE OF HUMOR.]

"Did she sign?"

[SHE LEARNED,] Julian answered, his signs softening slightly, the sharp percussive edge giving way to a fluid, nostalgic rhythm. [SHE WAS PROUD. SHE REFUSED TO LET THE ORALIST CRAP TAKE OVER MY EDUCATION. WHEN THE SCHOOL DISTRICT WANTED TO PUT ME IN A SPEECH THERAPY CLOSET FIVE HOURS A DAY, SHE THREW HER RID CERTIFICATION BOARD CREDENTIALS ON THE PRINCIPAL'S DESK AND TOLD HIM TO GO TO HELL.]

I looked at him, seeing for the first time the deep foundation beneath his unyielding pride. He hadn't fought his way into the Deaf world as an isolated outsider; he had been defended by a mother who understood the linguistic dignity of his language before he was old enough to spell his own name.

"She gave you your fight," I signed.

Julian looked down at the linoleum floor. He tapped the heel of his boot against the base of the counter twice—a rhythmic, unconscious beat.

[SHE GAVE ME MY ANGER,] he signed, looking back up into my eyes. [SHE DIED FOUR YEARS AGO. CANCER. SHE NEVER SAW ME HEADLINE A THEATER. SHE ONLY SAW ME PLAY TO EIGHT DRUNKS IN A BOWLING ALLEY BASEMENT IN CICERO.]

The silence settled over the small green room, quiet and resonant, held between the cracked mirror and the hum of the stockroom coolers.

I looked at his hands resting on his denim thighs. The long tendons were relaxed, the knuckles loose. Underneath the sharp armor of his comedy lived an immense, disciplined devotion to the woman who had refused to let him be silenced.

"She would be proud of the tour," I signed quietly.

Julian looked at me. His eyes held mine for a heartbeat, then his chin lifted, re-establishing that defensive, mocking barrier.

[DON'T GET SENTIMENTAL ON ME, LIN,] he signed. [SENTIMENTAL IS FOR TV MOVIES. WE HAVE A SHOW TO RUN.]

Deacon's heavy footsteps sounded in the corridor outside. The door swung open, and Deacon poked his head into the room, holding two printed cue sheets.

"Soundcheck in ten minutes, Jules," Deacon said, signing the words with his free hand. "The house tech says the stage floor has a two-inch drop near the monitor wedge. You need to walk the apron before the doors open."

Julian nodded once. He pushed off the counter, reached out, and tapped the rim of my ginger ale bottle with his index finger.

[DRINK THE GINGER, LIN,] he signed. [I HAVE A LOT OF WORDS TONIGHT.]

He walked out into the corridor to inspect the stage.

I stood alone in the green room for five minutes after Julian left to inspect the stage apron.

I looked into the cracked mirror, observing the faint flush along my collarbone and the quiet, steady rhythm of my breathing. The honeyed tea had done its work; the tightness in my larynx was receding, replaced by a clean, grounded warmth.

On the Formica counter, the unopened bottle of ginger ale caught the flicker of the fluorescent tube.

Julian Vance had never asked for protection. He refused the role of a fragile client whose boundaries required defense against overzealous hearing professionals. Standing beside him on stage demanded every reserve of linguistic agility, physical stamina, and presence I possessed.

I picked up the ginger ale, took a long swallow of the spicy, carbonated liquid, and felt the ginger burn cleanly all the way down to my diaphragm.

I checked my watch, adjusted my black sweater, and walked out into the wings.

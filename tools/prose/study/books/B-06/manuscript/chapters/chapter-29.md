# Chapter 29: Deacon's Realization

The loading dock of the Columbus Theatre in Providence, Rhode Island, was a cavern of peeling green paint, rusted radiator coils, and ancient stage machinery built during the vaudeville era.

It was four in the afternoon. Outside, a cold coastal drizzle fell from a gray Atlantic sky, turning the cobblestone alley into slick black glass.

Deacon stood beside the rear cargo doors of the Econoline, checking the serial numbers on two wireless receiver packs against his yellow master inventory sheet. He had a pencil clamped between his teeth and his work jacket zipped tight against the damp chill.

Julian was inside the theater with the house lighting director, walking the stage apron to map the steps between the wings and the orchestra pit.

I hauled a fifty-pound road case filled with merchandise hoodies across the wet dock, setting it down with a heavy thud beside the van bumper.

Deacon took the pencil out of his mouth.

"Leave the heavy lifting to the union stagehands, Lin," he grunted, his gravelly voice echoing off the brick walls. "If you wrench your wrist, Jules performs in silence tonight."

"My wrists are fine, Deacon," I answered, wiping a smear of road grime from the palm of my hand.

Deacon didn't return to his inventory sheet. He stood staring at me, his dark eyes narrowed in intense, unblinking scrutiny.

"You're different," he said flatly.

I kept my expression neutral. "Different how?"

"You stopped looking at your watch every ten minutes," Deacon said, stepping toward me until he was leaning against the van fender. "In Chicago and Detroit, every time Jules made a dirty joke or stepped too close to your sleeve, you got that tight little schoolgirl look around your mouth like you were waiting for the principal to paddle you. Now you look at him like you're daring somebody to get in his way."

My heart hammered against my ribs, but I didn't flinch. "Julian is performing at the highest level of his career. I'm doing my job."

Deacon let out a short, harsh bark that sounded like dry gravel being shaken in a tin can.

"Don't bullshit a CODA, Maya," he said, his voice dropping into a quiet, heavy register. "I grew up in the Deaf world. My mother could spot a secret relationship from sixty yards away by the way two people positioned their elbows during dinner. I saw you two on the fire escape in Cleveland. I saw him holding your face in the hotel hallway in Rochester."

The air in the loading bay felt suddenly freezing.

"Deacon—"

"Shut up and listen," Deacon commanded, raising a broad, calloused hand.

He looked down at his yellow inventory clipboard, his thumb tracing the worn metal clip. When he looked back up, the cynical sarcasm that usually guarded his face had completely vanished.

"When Jules called me eight months ago to tell me about the retinitis pigmentosa diagnosis," Deacon said, his voice thick with uncharacteristic emotion, "he was sitting in his kitchen in Chicago with all the lights turned off. He hadn't left his apartment in three weeks. He told me he was going to cancel his agency contracts, sell his car, and let the state assign him to an assisted living facility before he became a burden to his friends."

Deacon swallowed hard, his throat working.

"I spent six months dragging him out of that dark apartment," Deacon continued. "I booked this twelve-city tour because I knew that if he stopped performing, he'd curl up and die inside. But every night on the road, I laid awake in those cheap motels terrified that he was going to slip on an iron stair, break his neck, and prove that I was just an idiot hearing guy pushing a blind friend over a cliff."

He stepped closer, his heavy canvas jacket brushing my arm.

"Then you showed up," Deacon said softly. "You taped his lines. You learned his timing. When his vision bleached out in Buffalo, you didn't call an ambulance—you put his hand on your spine and led him through the dark like he was a king."

He reached out and placed his heavy hand on my shoulder, squeezing with firm, paternal warmth.

"I'm not mad, Maya," Deacon said, a faint, crooked smile touching his lips. "I'm relieved. For nine years, I thought I was the only person on earth who loved that stubborn, angry bastard enough to stand between him and the dark. Now I know he's going to be okay."

Tears pricked behind my eyelids. I reached up and laid my hand over Deacon's calloused fingers.

"Thank you, Deacon," I whispered.

Deacon cleared his throat violently, turned his back, and grabbed the handles of the merchandise crate.

"Don't thank me yet," he barked, his voice returning to its usual sandpaper rasp. "We still got four hundred tickets to sell in Providence, and Elena Rossi is waiting for us in New York with a meat cleaver. Get that cable bag inside before it gets rained on."

He hauled the heavy crate through the stage door, his boots thudding against the wet boards."""

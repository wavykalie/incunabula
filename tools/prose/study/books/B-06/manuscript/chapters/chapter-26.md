# Chapter 26: City Eight: Burlington

The Cellar Door was an underground comedy club tucked beneath an eighteenth-century brick warehouse near the Burlington waterfront. It smelled of aged oak timbers, Vermont hard cider, and wood smoke drifting from a cast-iron stove in the corner of the bar.

The stage was a simple raised platform of tongue-and-groove pine, eight inches above the red brick floor, surrounded on three sides by heavy wooden tavern tables.

There were no spotlights.

Instead, the venue used five warm amber Edison bulbs suspended in metal cages from the heavy ceiling beams, casting a soft, golden illumination across the room that eliminated glare entirely. For Julian, it was the most comfortable visual environment of the entire tour.

The room held ninety people, packed shoulder to shoulder in wool sweaters and flannel shirts.

When Julian stepped onto the platform, he didn't wear his black performance button-down. He wore a simple heather-gray knit sweater with the sleeves pushed up to his elbows, looking relaxed, approachable, and completely present.

I stood beside him on the right edge of the platform, wearing my ivory knit top, leaning my hip against the wooden pillar supporting the center beam.

Julian adjusted the microphone stand, tapped the floorboards with his boot, and looked across the room.

He didn't start with jokes.

His hands rose slowly into the amber glow.

*Twelve days ago,* he signed, his face quiet and thoughtful, *I was in Chicago. I thought the most important thing in the world was walking off the stage before someone noticed I couldn't see the exit sign.*

I voiced the line in a soft, resonant register that matched his quiet cadence.

The tavern went dead silent. Patrons set their cider glasses down on the wooden tables without a sound.

*I spent seven years hiding my eyes,* Julian signed, his movements gentle, lyrical, and breathtakingly precise. *When you're born deaf, you get used to being different. You build an armor. You learn to be faster, sharper, and funnier than everyone else so they don't have time to look at you with pity.*

He took half a step forward, his boot toes aligning with the edge of the pine platform.

*And then,* he signed, looking down at his hands, *the retinitis pigmentosa starts stealing the corners. You're walking down a familiar street, and suddenly a fire hydrant jumps out of nowhere and bruises your shin. You reach for your coffee mug and knock it off the counter. And the armor starts cracking.*

Across the tavern, an older woman in the front row pressed a napkin to her eye.

Julian smiled—a soft, self-deprecating grin that broke the tension in the room like spring ice.

*The worst part?* he signed, his eyebrows arching in sudden comedic mischief. *The worst part is the doctors. A hearing doctor with a clipboard is the most dangerous creature on the planet. My specialist in Peoria looked at my eye charts, took off his spectacles, and said: 'Mr. Vance, you should consider taking up pottery.'*

The room chuckled, a warm wave of laughter building in the corners.

Julian leaned into the bit, demonstrating the absurdity of an Usher syndrome comic trying to center a spinning lump of wet clay on a pottery wheel while blindfolded, turning the trauma into five minutes of brilliant physical slapstick that had the entire room roaring with delight.

And then, seamlessly, he pivoted back to the truth.

He looked over his shoulder at me where I stood against the center post.

*They tell you that an interpreter is a machine,* he signed, his eyes fixed on mine in the amber light. *They tell you she's an appliance you plug in to make English come out of deaf fingers. But they don't know Maya. She didn't just give me voice. She gave me my eyes back.*

He held his hand out toward me across the platform.

I stepped away from the pillar, walked to the center of the pine boards, and placed my hand in his.

The applause that broke across the Cellar Door wasn't the deafening roar of Buffalo or the polite handclaps of Detroit. It was a deep, thunderous, foot-stomping ovation that rattled the tavern glassware and filled the room with pure, unreserved love.

Julian didn't bow to the room.

He turned toward me, raised our joined hands to his chest, and looked into my eyes with an expression of complete, triumphant devotion.

The tavern patrons cheered, rising from their benches to toast us with cider mugs. Deacon stood by the draft taps, shaking his head with a wide, disbelieving smile.

Julian leaned his forehead against mine on the center boards, our breath mingling in the warm amber light as the applause filled the cellar."""

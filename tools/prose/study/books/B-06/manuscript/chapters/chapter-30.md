# Chapter 30: City Nine: Providence

The Columbus Theatre on Broadway in Providence was an opulent Italianate palace built in 1926, featuring three tiers of gilded plaster balconies, faded crimson damask wall coverings, and an immense proscenium arch carved with grinning theatrical masks.

It was our ninth city. Thirty days on the road.

The stage apron projected twelve feet into the orchestra pit, separated from the velvet seats by a deep, empty musician's trench that had been covered with black plywood sheets.

Julian and I walked the apron during the 4:00 PM technical inspection.

The theater was cavernous and cold, smelling of ninety years of popcorn oil, damp velvet, and backstage dust. Up in the high grid, fifty feet above our heads, the work lights hummed like distant bees.

Julian walked slowly, his right hand resting lightly against the back of my waist.

His peripheral vision had narrowed again.

The clinical reality was undeniable: the stress of consecutive performance nights, the freezing winter travel, and the relentless halogen exposure had taken their toll on his remaining photoreceptors. During our morning vision test in the hotel hallway, his horizontal field had measured barely seven degrees.

When he looked straight ahead, he could see my face; he could no longer see my hands if I raised them to chest height without tilting his head upward.

He stopped at the edge of the plywood apron.

*Tell me the drop,* he signed with his left hand, his fingers moving in the small space between our hips.

"Four feet down into the orchestra pit," I reported, signing into his palm using ProTactile contact. "The plywood over the trench is secure, but the seam has a half-inch lip. If you catch your toe, you pitch forward into the front row."

Julian nodded slowly. He didn't curse. He didn't smash his fists against the proscenium wall.

The angry, thrashing defiance that had characterized his opening sets in Chicago and Detroit had matured into something deeper, quieter, and infinitely more powerful: absolute trust.

*We don't use the neon tape tonight,* he signed against my palm.

I looked at him in surprise. "No tape? Jules, the apron is pitch black. Beyond the footlights, it's a drop into the dark."

*I don't need the tape,* Julian signed, turning to face me on the empty apron. *The tape is a crutch for eyes that are already gone. Tonight, we do the full tactile handoff. You stand twelve inches from my right side. Your left elbow touches my right forearm throughout the set. When I pivot, you pivot with me. We move as one body.*

I looked at his calm, resolute face.

In standard platform interpreting, physical contact between interpreter and performer during a set was strictly forbidden—an unforgivable breach of neutrality that would result in immediate disciplinary suspension.

Tonight, on the historic stage of the Columbus Theatre, that physical contact was the only thing standing between an artist and the void.

"Twelve inches," I agreed, pressing my forearm against his. "Shoulder to shoulder. We don't drop a single beat."

Julian smiled—a serene, triumphant smile that lit up the shadowy auditorium.

He took my hand, stepped off the plywood apron, and let me guide him back toward the dressing room stairs."""

The technical rider check at the Columbus Theatre was the most rigorous of the entire tour.

The theater's stage was thirty-five feet deep, with a sharp seven-foot drop into an uncovered orchestra pit. To ensure Julian's safety, Deacon and I spent two hours laying down triple lines of neon green and high-contrast orange hazard tape along the curved apron.

Julian walked the perimeter with his eyes half-closed, feeling the tactile boundary with his boots.

His peripheral vision had narrowed noticeably over the past forty-eight hours. When he stood at center stage, he could no longer resolve the ornate gilded cherubs on the proscenium arch; they were lost in a muddy gray blur.

He turned toward stage right, his hands raised in front of his chest.

[I NEED YOU CLOSER TONIGHT, MAYA,] he signed, his fingers tense with honesty. [NOT EIGHT FEET. THREE FEET. I NEED TO FEEL YOUR SLEEVE WITHIN ARM'S REACH AT ALL TIMES.]

"I will be right beside you," I signed back, stepping forward onto the apron until my shoulder brushed his. "Three feet. Constant sightline."

Julian reached out. His fingers found my wrist, sliding down into my palm, locking our fingers together in a tight, reassuring squeeze.

[BOSTON TOMORROW,] he signed. [TWELVE HUNDRED SEATS AT THE WILBUR. THEN PHILADELPHIA, THEN TOWN HALL. WE ARE IN THE FINAL SPRINT.]

"We are ready," I said aloud and signed into his hand.

Deacon brought up the stage wash, framing us together in a warm golden pool of light against the velvet shadows of the historic theater.

The performance at the Columbus Theatre was a triumph of acoustic and visual harmony.

The twelve hundred patrons packed into the red velvet seats leaned forward into every joke, and because Deacon had tuned the stage-level subwoofers to resonant sixty-cycle pulses, Julian felt every wave of laughter vibrate directly into his boots. Standing three feet to his right, my voice and his hands moved like twin instruments in an orchestra.

After the show, we stood on the empty stage while the house crew swept peanut shells from the aisles below.

Julian took off his wireless microphone, handing it carefully to Deacon. He turned toward the dark expanse of the balcony, his hands coming up into the quiet air.

[TWO CITIES LEFT,] he signed. [PHILADELPHIA TOMORROW. THEN TOWN HALL ON 43RD STREET.]

"Are you ready, Jules?" I asked, stepping beside him on the apron.

Julian turned his face toward me. In the dim amber work lights of the historic theater, his expression was completely free of fear, filled only with an immense, quiet purpose.

[I HAVE NEVER BEEN MORE READY IN MY LIFE,] he signed. [LET'S PACK THE TRUCK.]

We walked together down the stage steps into the cool Providence night.

After soundcheck at the Columbus Theatre, Deacon brought out a box of hot Rhode Island clam cakes from a seafood shack near Narragansett Bay.

We sat on the apron of the historic stage, our boots dangling over the dark orchestra pit, eating the fried, savory dough while the house lights hummed overhead.

Julian took a clam cake, broke off a crisp golden piece, and dipped it in tartar sauce.

[PROVIDENCE SMELLS LIKE SEA SALT,] he signed with satisfaction. [DIFFERENT FROM LAKE ERIE. FRESHER. COLDER.]

"The Atlantic Ocean is twenty miles south," Deacon said, wiping his fingers on a paper napkin. "After tonight, we follow the coast all the way down to Long Island Sound."

Julian looked out into the vast, shadowy cavern of the balcony.

[THE COAST IS GOOD,] he signed. [WHERE WATER MEETS STONE, THE SOUND TRAVELS FARTHER.]

We finished our dinner in the quiet theater, gathered our cue sheets, and waited for the doors to open.

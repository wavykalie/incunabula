# Chapter 31: City Ten: Boston

The Wilbur Theatre on Tremont Street was an imposing twelve-hundred-seat brick monolith in the heart of Boston's historic theater district.

The house was completely sold out. Outside, a biting nor'easter had dumped four inches of wet slush across the Common, but inside the lobby, the line for tickets stretched through the brass revolving doors and down the sidewalk toward Stuart Street.

Backstage, the lighting grid was terrifying.

The house master electrician had rigged four massive two-thousand-watt follow spots in the upper balcony, designed to punch through the cavernous proscenium space. When the spots hit the stage during soundcheck, the beam was so intensely bright it cast razor-sharp double shadows across the red velvet traveler curtain.

For Julian, the high-intensity beams were a blinding sensory assault.

The extreme photon density instantly saturated his remaining macular cones, turning his central visual field into an incandescent white fog that obliterated contrast entirely.

*I can't see the apron edge,* he signed, his fingers trembling against my forearm in the wings. *The spots are reflecting off the brass footlight trough. It's like looking into an open blast furnace.*

"We adapt," I said aloud, signing into his palm with firm, reassuring pressure. "We do the Providence formation. My left elbow locks to your right forearm. You don't look down at the floor; you keep your chin up and track my ribcage."

The house lights flashed. The crowd roar surged from the orchestra pit to the third tier.

Julian stepped through the velvet slit into the blazing white inferno of the follow spots.

I was locked to his side, our forearms glued together, moving in synchronized, rhythmic lockstep.

The first forty minutes were electrifying. Julian attacked the Boston audience with savage, brilliant wit, mocking Puritan heritage, Ivy League hearing elitism, and the universal disaster of Boston transit. My voice matched his timing with ferocious accuracy, filling the twelve-hundred-seat auditorium with rolling waves of laughter.

Then, at minute fifty-four, during his expansive routine about deaf bar fights, Julian threw his weight into a wide dramatic pivot to stage right.

His boot heel caught the metal rim of an unshielded stage floor pocket.

His center of gravity tore free. In the blinding halogen glare, with zero peripheral vision to orient his horizon, he pitched violently forward toward the edge of the proscenium apron.

The front row gasped—a collective intake of breath from three hundred people.

I didn't hesitate. I drove my left foot into the boards, threw my entire upper body into his right hip, hooked my left arm around his waist, and pulled him backward toward the center line with all the strength in my back.

The momentum dragged us both down onto one knee.

We hit the spruce floorboards with a loud, hollow thud that echoed through the stage microphones.

Silence dropped over the Wilbur Theatre like a guillotine. Twelve hundred people held their breath in horrified shock.

Julian was on his right knee, his hands flat against the boards, his chest heaving, his eyes wide and disoriented in the white glare.

For one terrifying heartbeat, the entire tour hung on the edge of disaster.

Then Julian looked up at my face.

He saw the calm, unyielding resolve in my eyes. He felt my fingers locked securely around his waist, anchoring him to the earth.

A wicked, triumphant grin broke across his mouth.

He didn't stand up. He stayed on one knee, raised both hands into the air, and signed with blinding, explosive speed:

*And that, ladies and gentlemen, is why you never let a deaf guy try to do Irish step dancing on a waxed floor.*

I voiced the line with razor-sharp comedic timing.

The Wilbur Theatre erupted into pandemonium.

Twelve hundred people jumped to their feet, laughing, screaming, stamping their feet until the antique plaster chandeliers trembled fifty feet above our heads.

Julian took my hand, pulled himself to his feet, and bowed before the roaring room."""

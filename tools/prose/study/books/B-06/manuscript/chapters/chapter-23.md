# Chapter 23: The Agency Phone Call

The conference phone on the desk in Room 512 of the Rochester Radisson clicked with a sharp electronic beep.

"Maya," Elena Rossi's voice filled the room, sounding crisp, cold, and metallic over the speakerphone. "You received the formal inquiry from the Ethical Practices System yesterday morning. Your seventy-two-hour response window has expired."

I stood beside the desk, my hands flat against the dark mahogany surface, looking out the window at the gray waters of the Genesee River tumbling over the downtown falls.

Across the room, Julian sat on the edge of the bed, his arms crossed over his chest, his eyes fixed on the illuminated dial pad of the speakerphone. He had his phone in his lap, running a real-time transcription app that translated Elena's spoken English into scrolling black text on his screen.

Deacon stood by the door, arms folded, his face carved from dark slate.

"I received the dossier, Elena," I said into the microphone, keeping my voice level and professional. "I am preparing a comprehensive operational statement detailing the technical and medical necessities governing our stage proximity."

A dry, dismissive sound came through the speaker—the distinct sound of Elena Rossi tapping her heavy silver signet ring against a glass desktop.

"Do not waste your time with medical justifications, Ms. Lin," Elena said, her voice dropping into the clinical, authoritative cadence she used when terminating contractor agreements. "The agency has consulted our legal counsel and the chair of the state licensure board. The video documentation from Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland is unambiguous. You have abandoned the fundamental stance of the professional interpreter."

"I have adapted our communication strategies to the specific physical requirements of a consumer undergoing progressive retinitis pigmentosa," I stated, reciting the exact language of Tenet 2.2. "Mr. Vance has a ten-degree visual field. If I maintain a standard six-foot perimeter, the spatial syntax of the performance is rendered completely inaccessible."

"You are not his guide, Ms. Lin," Elena cut in, her words like ice shards. "You are an RID-certified platform interpreter. If Mr. Vance cannot navigate a stage without physical contact and personalized emotional mirroring, then Mr. Vance is no longer medically fit for independent theatrical performance."

Julian's head jerked up. His dark eyes flared with murderous rage as he read the words on his phone screen: *no longer medically fit.*

His fingers bunched into white-knuckled fists against his knees.

"That is not your determination to make," I said, my voice rising half an octave before I caught myself. "Mr. Vance has sold out seven consecutive theaters. The performances have received universal acclaim from Deaf community organizations across four states."

"The Deaf community does not govern your certification," Elena said with ruthless certainty. "The Registry does. And the Registry has strict statutory liability standards. We have received an anonymous third-party complaint containing photographs of you and Mr. Vance in non-professional settings—specifically, in an unlit backstage stairwell in Detroit and on a hotel fire escape in Cleveland."

My heart plummeted into my stomach.

Deacon swore under his breath, stepping away from the door.

"Those photographs establish an undeniable personal relationship," Elena continued, unhurried and lethal. "Under Section 3 of the EPS regulations, this constitutes a Category A ethical breach. I am offering you one professional exit, Maya. Resign from the tour tonight. We will dispatch a senior replacement interpreter from the Albany regional office to meet the van in Burlington tomorrow. You will return to Chicago, submit to a thirty-day supervised retraining cycle, and this inquiry will be sealed without formal sanctions on your national record."

Silence stretched in the hotel room, broken only by the hum of the HVAC unit.

"And if I refuse?" I asked softly.

"If you refuse," Elena said, her voice completely devoid of warmth, "the agency will immediately revoke your tour credentials. I will file formal emergency decertification charges with the national board on Monday morning. By the time you reach New York City, your license will be canceled, and you will never interpret on a public platform in this country again."

Julian stood up from the bed.

He walked across the room with swift, silent strides, reached out, and slammed his palm down on the red end-call button.

The connection died with a sharp dial tone.

Julian turned to me, his chest heaving, his face pale with fury.

He didn't wait for my hands. He reached out, grabbed my shoulders, and pulled me toward him until our eyes were six inches apart.

*Pack your bag,* he signed with one hand against my collar. *Call the airport. Go home to Chicago.*

"Julian, stop it," I commanded, grabbing his wrists. "You are not sending me home."

*Did you read that screen?* Julian signed, his face twisted in torment. *She's going to revoke your national license. You will never interpret again. All because you stood by me on an unlit stage.*

"My license is my business, Jules," I said fiercely. "I made my choice in Cleveland. I made it on that fire escape. I am not leaving this tour."

Julian stared into my eyes, his breath hitching, the fierce wall of his pride slowly crumbling under the weight of my resolve.

He lowered his hands, pulled me into his arms, and held me against his chest as the gray afternoon faded over the river."""

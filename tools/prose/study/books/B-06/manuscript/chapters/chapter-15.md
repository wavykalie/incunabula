# Chapter 15: Van Breakdown in Erie

The radiator hose on the Econoline blew with the sound of a wet firecracker forty miles west of the Pennsylvania border.

A cloud of foul, sweet-smelling white steam erupted from beneath the hood, coating the windshield in greasy glycol film and obscuring the blinking hazard lights of the snowplow ahead of us.

"Son of a bitch!" Deacon roared, fighting the power steering as the engine block shuddered and died. "Hold on!"

He muscled the heavy van across three lanes of slush-covered asphalt, wrestling the dead steering wheel until the front tires crunched into the gravel shoulder beneath an overhead green directional sign that read: *EXIT 24 — ERIE WEST — 1 MILE.*

The van rolled to a halt in a snowdrift three feet deep.

Silence dropped over the cabin like a lead weight, broken only by the steady hiss of boiling coolant spraying against hot exhaust manifolds and the ticking of cooling metal.

Deacon slammed both palms against the steering wheel, his breath pluming white in the freezing air inside the cab. "Broke the lower bypass hose. Look at that steam. We dumped every drop of antifreeze on Interstate 90."

He pulled his phone from the dashboard mount. "Zero bars. Of course. We're in the dead zone between the lake ridge and the state line."

Julian leaned forward from the rear bench, his hand resting on the back of Deacon's headrest.

*Town near here?* he fingerspelled rapidly.

"Diner and a twenty-four-hour truck stop at the bottom of the off-ramp," Deacon said aloud, pointing through the steam. "About eight hundred yards down the hill. I'm going to walk down, call a flatbed from the service desk, and find a mechanic who can patch the hose. You two stay in the van. Keep the blankets around you."

"Deacon, it's eight degrees out there," I said, looking through the frosted side glass. "Let me walk with you."

"No," Deacon commanded, pulling his canvas hood over his ears and zipping his work jacket to his chin. "You stay with Jules. In this weather, if he tries to navigate an icy highway shoulder in the dark, he slips into the culvert. I'll be back in an hour with a tow truck."

He grabbed his heavy Maglite, kicked the driver's door open against the snowdrift, and vanished into the swirling white curtain of the blizzard.

The click of the door latch closing sounded final and absolute.

Inside the van, the temperature began dropping immediately. Within ten minutes, our breath formed dense clouds of vapor that froze into feathery frost patterns along the inner glass.

Julian slid across the bench seat, stepped over the merchandise boxes in the aisle, and sat down in the passenger bucket seat beside me. He hauled a heavy wool stadium blanket over both of our shoulders, tucking the coarse green fabric around my back and neck.

Under the blanket, our bodies were pressed tight together from hip to knee.

*Cold?* he signed, his hands tucked inside the warm airspace beneath the wool.

*A little,* I signed back, my teeth chattering slightly. *My toes are numb.*

Julian reached down, grabbed my boot, and pulled my foot onto his lap. Without hesitation, he began rubbing my boot leather between his strong palms, using friction to generate warmth through the sole.

*Deacon is tough,* Julian signed, his face inches from mine in the shadowy interior of the cab. *He walked three miles through a blizzard in North Dakota when our transmission died in 2018. He won't freeze.*

"He's loyal," I said softly, watching Julian's hands move. "He protects you like a brother."

*He protects me because he feels guilty,* Julian signed, his fingers slowing down, his expression turning reflective.

*Guilty about what?*

Julian paused. He looked out through the frosted windshield into the gray vortex of snow swirling around the hood.

*When we were kids in Peoria,* he signed, *Deacon was the hearing kid on the block. His parents were deaf, like mine. But Deacon could hear everything. The school bells, the fire sirens, the other kids laughing at our accents behind the bleachers. He spent his whole childhood being the shield. When my retinitis pigmentosa diagnosis came down from the specialist when I was twenty-six, Deacon was in the exam room. He looked at the doctor and asked if they could do a retinal transplant—give me one of his eyes.*

My throat tightened. "He really asked that?"

*Yes,* Julian signed, a sad, affectionate smile touching his lips. *The doctor thought he was joking. Deacon was dead serious. When the doctor said retinitis pigmentosa was a genetic rod degeneration that couldn't be fixed with surgery, Deacon went out to the hospital parking lot and put his fist through a concrete pillar. Broke three metacarpals.*

Julian leaned his head back against the headrest, pulling the blanket tighter around us.

*He thinks if he keeps me on stage,* Julian continued, *the disease doesn't count as a loss. He thinks if we finish twelve cities and sell out Town Hall in New York, we beat the clock.*

"Do you think that?" I asked quietly.

Julian turned his head. In the dim, snow-reflected light filtering through the frosted windows, his dark eyes were luminous, clear, and searching.

*No,* he signed, his fingers brushing my cheek as he formed the sign. *You can't beat the clock, Maya. The rods die every day. When this tour is over, the curtain comes down for good. But being here with you... on this road... it makes the dark feel like a beginning instead of an end.*

He leaned forward, his forehead touching mine under the heavy wool blanket, our breath mingling in the cold cab while the blizzard howled against the metal roof."""

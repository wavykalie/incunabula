# Chapter 20: The Midpoint Toll

The green overhead sign on Interstate 90 East loomed through the freezing pre-dawn mist:

*NEW YORK STATE THRUWAY — MILEPOST 365 — ROCHESTER 15 MILES.*

Six cities behind us: Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Erie, Buffalo, and the long, freezing miles of the rust belt corridor. Six cities ahead: Rochester, Burlington, Providence, Boston, Philadelphia, and the final grand performance at Town Hall in Manhattan.

Twenty-one days on the road. Three thousand four hundred miles logged on the Econoline's odometer.

The van rolled into the illuminated concrete canyon of the Rochester toll plaza. The yellow sodium floodlights buzzed overhead, reflecting off snowbanks piled eight feet high along the jersey barriers.

Deacon rolled down his window, letting a blast of sub-zero air into the heated cabin, and fed two crisp five-dollar bills into the automated toll basket. The mechanical gate arm swung upward with a rhythmic hydraulic hiss.

"Halfway home," Deacon grunted aloud, rolling up the glass and rubbing his red eyes. " Rochester is the big one. NTID campus. National Technical Institute for the Deaf. If Jules bombs at NTID, the Deaf community will roast him on social media for the next twenty years."

Julian was awake in the passenger seat.

He had rolled his window down two inches, letting the biting northern wind wash over his face, clearing the cobwebs of four hours of fitful highway sleep. He watched the green mileage markers flash past in the headlights, his fingers tapping a restless vaudeville cadence against his knee.

He turned around in his seat, looking at me where I sat on the middle bench surrounded by tour binders and thermal water thermoses.

*Six cities down,* he signed, his hands illuminated by the green glow of the dashboard clock: 4:18 AM.

*Six cities down,* I repeated, signing with both hands. *How does the body feel?*

Julian stretched his neck, his vertebrae popping with dry, audible clicks.

*Legs feel like lead,* he signed. *Right ankle is still stiff from that Detroit stair slip. But the eyes...* He paused, looking at his palms. *The eyes are holding at eight degrees. Dr. Chen at Northwestern said fatigue would accelerate the rod death, but we're still here.*

He reached into his jacket pocket and pulled out a small, crumpled paper map of the northeastern United States that he had taken from a gas station in Ohio.

He unfolded it on the console between our seats.

Across the states of Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New York, Julian had drawn a heavy black Sharpie line connecting our tour stops. A black star marked Chicago; a second star marked Detroit; a third marked Cleveland; a fourth marked Buffalo.

He picked up a red pen from the cup holder and carefully drew a bright red circle around the dot labeled *ROCHESTER.*

*Tonight is different, Maya,* he signed, his expression turning solemn.

*Because of NTID?* I asked.

*Because of the hands,* he signed. *In Chicago and Cleveland, seventy percent of the room was hearing. They watched your lips. They waited for your voice. But tonight at NTID, fifteen hundred people in that auditorium will have ASL as their primary language. They don't need your voice. They will be watching our dialogue.*

He looked up from the map, his dark eyes locking onto mine with fierce, unswerving clarity.

*They will see everything,* he signed. *They will see how you move before I finish. They will see how close you stand. They will see that we aren't performing a translation—we're performing a duet.*

"Let them see it," I whispered aloud, signing the words with slow, deliberate precision. "I'm not hiding anymore, Jules."

Julian looked at my hands.

A profound, tranquil stillness settled over his features. The restless tension that had gripped his shoulders since Chicago melted away into the quiet hum of the tires on the cold asphalt.

He reached out, took the red pen, and drew a bold, unbroken line from Rochester through Vermont, down through Massachusetts, and straight into the center of New York City.

He folded the map, slipped it back into his breast pocket over his heart, and looked out into the eastern sky where the first pale gray light of dawn was beginning to break over the snow-covered hills.

The van crested the ridge overlooking the Genesee River basin. Below us, the brick smokestacks of Rochester and the silver expanse of the campus buildings emerged from the morning fog, frosted in white rime like a city carved from sugar.

Deacon clicked on the right turn signal, guiding the Econoline down the exit ramp toward Jefferson Road.

"Hotel is two miles up on the right," Deacon said, checking his side mirror. "Soundcheck is at noon. We get four hours of real bed rest before the circus starts."

Julian turned back to me one more time. He touched his chin, then placed his palm against his heart—the sign for gratitude, deep and unadorned.

I touched my heart in return, watching the city lights glint in the dark mirrors of his eyes."""

The midpoint of the tour came at dawn on Interstate 86, crossing the Pennsylvania-New York state line high in the Allegheny foothills.

The highway wound through snow-draped hemlock forests and frozen creek beds. A pale lemon sun was breaking through the low winter stratus, casting long blue shadows across the snowfields.

Deacon pulled the van into an overlook turnout above the Allegheny River valley. He shut off the engine, stepped out into the freezing air, and walked toward the wooden scenic railing to stretch his back.

Julian and I stayed inside the warm cabin.

Julian was holding our printed tour schedule, tapping his thumb against the sixth city line: *Rochester, NY — National Technical Institute for the Deaf.*

[HALFWAY,] he signed, looking across at me in the passenger seat. [SIX CITIES BEHIND US. SIX AHEAD.]

"Rochester is the heart of the Deaf world," I signed back. "Fifteen hundred Deaf students, faculty, and community members. It will be the biggest Deaf house of the tour."

Julian stared out at the snow-covered valley. His sharp profile was etched against the morning sunlight, beautiful and contemplative.

[ROCHESTER WILL BE HONEST,] he signed. [THEY DON'T NEED AN INTERPRETER TO UNDERSTAND ME. THEY WILL READ EVERY SUBTLE LINGUISTIC SHIFT IN MY HANDS. AND THEY WILL SEE THAT MY TUNNEL IS NARROWING.]

"They will see a master at the peak of his power," I answered firmly.

Julian turned to me. He took my hand in both of his, wrapping his long fingers around mine.

[I AM NOT AFRAID OF ROCHESTER, MAYA,] he signed with one hand against my palm. [I AM AFRAID OF NEW YORK. BECAUSE WHEN TOWN HALL ENDS, THE TOUR IS OVER. AND I DON'T KNOW HOW TO LIVE WITHOUT THIS STAGE.]

"You won't be alone, Jules," I said aloud and signed with absolute conviction. "Whatever comes after New York, we will build it together."

Julian looked into my eyes, and in the quiet morning light on that mountain ridge, he leaned forward and pressed his lips gently against my forehead.

The bond between us was no longer professional; it was total, enduring, and unbroken.

Deacon climbed back into the driver's seat, shaking snow from his boots, and turned the key in the ignition.

The Econoline rolled back onto the highway, heading north toward Rochester and the second half of the tour.

We pulled away from the scenic overlook as the morning sun climbed above the Allegheny ridges, turning the snowfields into blinding sheets of gold.

Julian leaned back in the passenger seat, his eyes resting behind his dark wraparound sunglasses. He reached across the console, his hand finding mine on the passenger bench.

[ROCHESTER IS NEXT,] he signed, his fingers moving inside the palm of my hand. [THEN VERMONT, THEN RHODE ISLAND, THEN BOSTON, PHILADELPHIA, AND NEW YORK. SIX CITIES TO FINISH THE STORY.]

"And we are going to finish it with fire," I signed back into his palm.

Julian smiled, his fingers tightening gently around mine.

In the driver's seat, Deacon clicked the turn signal, accelerating the Econoline onto the clear asphalt of Interstate 86. The engine hummed with steady, rhythmic power, carrying us eastward through the frozen hills toward the second half of our journey.

The turnpike toll plaza at Westfield, New York, was illuminated by orange sodium lamps that cast long, amber shadows across the snowdrifts.

Deacon handed the toll ticket and a crisp twenty-dollar bill to the toll collector through the rolled-down window. The cold lake air swept into the cabin, smelling of pine needles, road salt, and diesel exhaust.

Julian pointed through the windshield toward the large green highway sign: *WELCOME TO THE EMPIRE STATE.*

[NEW YORK,] he signed, his fingers lively despite thirty hours of travel. [THE LAST STATE ON THE MAP.]

"Six cities in New York and New England," I signed back, watching his eyes take in the sign. "And the final stop on 43rd Street."

Julian settled back into his seat, his hand finding mine on the console, and the van surged forward onto Interstate 90.

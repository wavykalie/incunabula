# Chapter 2: The Mic and the Spotlight

The club went dark at three in the afternoon for technical rehearsal.

In the belly of the empty showroom, the air turned cold and still. The smell of stale draft beer retreated into the upholstery, replaced by the dry, metallic scorch of the lighting grid heating up overhead.

Deacon sat on a backward-facing wooden chair at the tech desk, two heavy cables looped over his shoulder. He tapped a greasy touchscreen console. "Bringing up the spot, Jules. Tell me when the halo splits."

Julian stood dead center on the wooden riser. He had stripped off his wet peacoat, leaving him in a tight black cotton henley and dark denim. Without the coat, his frame looked narrower, almost fragile under the cavernous ceiling rafters, but his posture was rigid as a plumb line. He stood with his feet planted shoulder-width apart, boots set precisely two inches behind the green gaffer boundary.

Above him, a 750-watt Source Four leko clicked and hummed. A stark white circle of light slammed down from the catwalk, framing Julian in a six-foot disc of blinding noon glare.

Julian's pupils shrank to pinpricks. His head twitched back a fraction of an inch as his corneas fought the sudden wash of lumens.

I stood eight feet to his right, just outside the perimeter of the beam, holding a black Shure SM58 microphone by its cold steel barrel. My position was fixed by the performance rider: four feet back, three feet to the side, wearing matte black from collar to shoes to avoid reflecting stray light back into his constricted visual field.

Julian raised a hand to shield his brow, peering into the pitch-black void of the empty seating tables.

[CANNOT SEE WINGS,] he signed toward the tech desk. The movement was fast, tense, clipped at the wrists. [SPOTLIGHT TOO HIGH CONTRAST. TOTAL WALL OF BLACK BEYOND CIRCLE.]

Deacon adjusted a slider. The white circle softened slightly, the edges bleeding into amber. "Better?" he called out in voice, simultaneously signing the question with a fluid one-handed motion in front of his chest.

Julian shook his head once. [NO. SAME. IF I TURN MY HEAD MORE THAN TEN DEGREES, I LOSE MY SPATIAL ORIENTATION. I DON'T KNOW WHERE FRONT OF STAGE IS.]

I stepped forward until the toe of my black sneaker touched the outer fringe of the beam. The ambient spill caught the matte fabric of my sleeve.

"Jules," I signed, keeping the motion strictly within the lit quadrant of his lower-right field. [HERE. LOOK MY ARM.]

Julian's head snapped toward me. His eyes caught the outline of my black forearm. He drew a sharp breath through his teeth.

[STAY THERE,] he signed. [WHEN YOU STAND THERE, YOUR SLEEVE GIVES ME AN EDGE. IF I SEE YOUR SHOULDER, I KNOW WHERE EAST IS.]

"I am stage right," I voiced and signed. "I don't move during the set. Even if the house laughs or somebody drops a beer glass, I stay anchored."

Julian stepped across the boundary, closing the distance between us until he stood barely three feet away. In the tight circle of light, I could see the faint network of fine capillaries in his sclera, the slight tension at the corners of his mouth, and the faint scar tracing his left jawline from an old bicycle accident he had never bothered to explain.

He reached out. His fingers hovered two inches from my forearm, hesitating for a fraction of a second before making contact.

His fingertips were calloused and cold. He pressed two fingers against the fabric of my black cotton sleeve, right at the elbow joint. He gripped the cloth gently, testing the firmness of my stance.

[GOOD,] he signed with his free left hand. [YOU ARE SOLID. NOT SHAKING. LAST INTERPRETER IN MILWAUKEE SHOOK LIKE A SCARED RABBIT. HER HANDS BOUNCED. SHE MADE ME DIZZY.]

"I don't shake," I said.

Julian released my sleeve. He took two steps back to the center of the beam. The wall of light closed between us again, turning me into a silhouette at his periphery and leaving him alone on the illuminated island.

"Let's run the airline bit," Deacon called from the booth. "From the boarding gate to the safety demo. Maya, watch his lip patterns on the pilot announcement; he likes to drop his register into pseudo-hearing mumbling."

Julian didn't wait for a count. He rolled his shoulders, cracked his knuckles, and threw his head back. When he looked forward again, the tense, guarded young man in the black henley vanished. In his place stood Julian Vance, the comedian.

His face exploded into animated, satirical life.

He signed with explosive, expansive gestures, taking full ownership of the spatial stage. He mimicked an airline gate agent clutching a broken plastic microphone, eyes squinted in bureaucratic condescension, fingers spelling out boarding groups with mocking, robotic precision:

[NOW BOARDING GROUP ONE: DIAMOND PLATINUM MILLION-MILERS WHO PURCHASED SEATS WITH ACTUAL GOLD BULLION. GROUP TWO: PEOPLE WHO PERSONALLY KNOW THE CHIEF EXECUTIVE OFFICER. GROUP THREE: DISABLED PASSENGERS WHO ARE POLITELY REQUESTED TO DISAPPEAR INTO THE CARGO HOLD TO AVOID INCONVENIENCING NORMAL CITIZENS.]

My voice caught the rhythm instantly, dropping into the nasal, monotone droning of an O'Hare terminal announcer:

"Now boarding Group One: Diamond Executive Platinum members who purchased tickets with physical bars of gold. Group Two: personal blood relatives of the CEO. Group Three: passengers with disabilities who are cordially invited to crawl into the cargo hold so normal paying humans don't have to look at you."

Julian didn't pause. He segued immediately into the physical comedy of the pre-flight safety briefing. He became the flight attendant—bored, dead-eyed, pointing toward the emergency exits with limp wrists, demonstrating the oxygen mask with the enthusiasm of an inmate folding prison laundry.

Then he pivoted. He placed himself in seat 14B, caught between a crying infant and an oversized businessman.

[PASSENGER NEXT TO ME NOTICED MY HEARING AIDS,] he signed, his eyes bulging in mock horror, his hands shrinking into a timid, apologetic ball. [HE WAS TERRIFIED. HE THOUGHT MY HEARING AIDS WERE RECEIVERS FOR TERRORIST SIGNALS. HE STARTED PRAYING TO SAINT JUDE. I LOOKED AT HIM. I PULLED OUT MY BATTERY PACK. I SAID: 'DON'T WORRY, SIR. IT ONLY DETONATES IF WE CROSS INTO CANADIAN AIRSPACE.']

"Guy in 14C saw my hearing aids," I voiced, matching the venomous bite of his delivery. "Looked at me like I was carrying enriched uranium. Started crossing himself and muttering Hail Marys into his ginger ale. I pulled out my spare zinc batteries. Whispered: 'Relax, chief. The detonator only arms when we enter Canadian air space.'"

Julian hit the final pose—a serene, terrifying smile directed straight at the imaginary terrified businessman.

Silence returned to the room.

Deacon tapped a pencil against the tech desk. The microphone picked up the steady rhythm of Julian's breathing as he came down from the adrenaline spike of the set.

"Pacing was good," Deacon said. "Maya, your translation on the Canadian airspace joke came in three-tenths of a second after his hand dropped. That's inside the pocket."

I swallowed, my throat dry from the sudden projection. "His spatial shifts are clear. When he changes characters, his shoulder axis moves five inches. That gives me enough lead time to establish the voice."

Julian stood in the center of the beam, his chest rising and falling beneath the black cotton shirt. He looked toward my silhouette. He couldn't see my eyes—not through the glare of the leko—but he knew exactly where my voice had landed.

He raised his hands again.

[YOU DON'T SOFTEN MY WORDS,] he signed.

"I interpret what you sign," I answered. "Tenet 3 of the Code. Faithful rendering of the message, tone, and register. No omissions, no additions."

Julian laughed—a short, silent puff of air that rustled the hair on his forehead.

[CODE OF CONDUCT,] he signed, mocking the formal fingerspelling. [ELENA'S LITTLE BIBLE. SHE THINKS LANGUAGE IS A MACHINE. YOU PUT ASL IN THE HOPPER, CRANK THE HANDLE, ENGLISH COMES OUT THE BOTTOM.]

"Isn't that what you want?" I asked. "Clean English that lands the joke?"

Julian stepped to the edge of the light. He looked down at the fluorescent tape line, his boot heel hovering half an inch above the green border.

[I WANT PEOPLE TO KNOW I AM FUNNY,] he signed, his movements slowing down, dropping the manic comic tempo, revealing the raw muscle underneath. [NOT 'INSPIRATIONAL.' NOT 'BRAVE.' NOT 'LOOK AT THE POOR DEAF BOY TELLING JOKES.' IF THEY LAUGH BECAUSE THEY PITY ME, I WILL BURN THIS CLUB TO THE GROUND.]

"They won't laugh out of pity," I said, keeping my hands inside his visual cone. "Your jokes are too mean for pity."

Julian tilted his head back, and for the first time since he walked into the club, a genuine, unforced grin broke across his sharp face.

[MEAN IS HONEST,] he signed. [LET'S RUN THE DATING SET.]

We stayed on the riser for another seventy minutes, polishing the transitions between his dating disasters and his childhood oralist speech therapy drills. Every time he launched a new routine, his hands carved out a distinct physical space in the amber air, testing my English vocabulary against the blistering speed of his non-manual markers.

By five o'clock, the afternoon winter chill had crept into the basement room, smelling of damp Chicago snow and grease from the pizza joint next door. Deacon powered down the lekos with a resonant metallic clack.

Julian stepped off the riser, pulled his peacoat around his shoulders, and reached for his bottle of mineral water.

"Good first run," Deacon said, locking down the console cover. "Doors open in two hours. Go get some soup."

Julian tapped my shoulder as he walked past toward the stairs.

[BE ON TIME, LIN,] he signed, his fingers brief and sharp against the dark. [I DON'T LIKE WAITING.]

He pushed the exit door open and walked out into the alley.

We walked the boundaries of the wooden riser three more times.

With each circuit, Julian mapped another physical marker into his kinesthetic memory: the subtle three-millimeter lip where two sheets of plywood met at center stage; the dull hollow resonance under his left heel when he stood directly over the subwoofer cabinet hidden beneath the apron; the draft of cool basement air flowing from the stage-left emergency exit. He didn't need to see those markers with his eyes. His feet became his eyes.

"Five steps back from the green tape puts you dead center," Deacon said from the tech desk, his voice echoing in the empty hall. "Six steps left puts you in Maya's primary sightline. Don't go past seven."

Julian signed without turning around, his left hand flashing a swift affirmative from his hip.

He stepped back into the amber pool of the ellipsoidal spot. He reached out into the dim margin where I stood, his index finger touching my black sleeve at the cuff.

[AGAIN,] he signed, his eyes locking onto my face. [THE ROUTINE ABOUT THE AIRPORT DOGS. I WANT TO SHARPEN THE PUNCHLINE.]

"From the security line?" I asked, raising the Shure microphone.

[FROM THE SECURITY LINE,] he signed. [WHEN THE GERMAN SHEPHERD SNIFFS MY PROSTHETIC EAR MOLD. WATCH MY RIGHT WRIST ON THE SNORT.]

We ran the routine twice more until the timing between his wrist snap and my English delivery locked into a single seamless pulse. When we finally stopped, Julian's forehead was damp with sweat, but the tension in his shoulders had completely dissolved.

He turned toward the dark seating rows, nodded once to himself, and walked off the stage.

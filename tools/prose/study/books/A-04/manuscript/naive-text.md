# The Open Canopy

### Chapter 1

The envelope was delivered on a Tuesday in the second week of October, shoved into the rusted tin cylinder of the mailbox along with three circulars for winter barley seed and an overdue notice from the county water authority. It was nine inches by twelve, stiffened with a strip of grey cardstock, and had been metered in Terre Haute, Indiana, for three dollars and forty-two cents.

Jesse Callow did not open it at the road. He had lived forty-eight years in Whetstone County, thirty-six of them on the five stony acres his father had abandoned to brambles and sumac, and he knew that bad news arrived in long white envelopes from banks, whereas very bad news arrived in manila paper from people who had bought a database.

He carried it up the gravel track between the two rows of dead cedar trees. The air had the dry, iron smell of late autumn in the limestone hills, that particular upland scent of dusted chaff, cold pond water, and woodsmoke from someone burning fence posts two hollows over. In the workshop behind his house—a lean-to built of rough-sawn oak that housed his drill press, three dismantled cuckoo clocks, seven boxes of brass clockwork gears, and a shelf of small-engine carburetors soaking in coffee cans of kerosene—he cleared a space on the zinc-topped bench. He moved a jar of number-four cotter pins and a half-rebuilt magneto from a 1974 John Deere riding mower.

Then he slit the envelope with a bone-handled linoleum knife.

Out slid thirty-two pages of cream-coloured stock, stapled once in the upper left corner with a heavy industrial flat-wire staple, and a return envelope addressed in purple ribbon type to:

*The General Presbytery and Board of Ministerial Compliance*  
*The Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy*  
*Sub-District IV (River & Uplands)*  
*Suite 402, St. Jude Commercial Concourse*  
*Terre Haute, Indiana 47802*

Attached to the first page was a yellow slip of paper, three inches square, printed with a laser printer whose drum had left three faint, rhythmic horizontal streaks across the text:

> *DEAR PRESBYTER:*  
> *Under the revised Ecclesiastical Oversight and Charity Governance Accord of 2024, ratified by the Joint Synod in Muncie and registered pursuant to Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, all ordained officiants holding active provincial credentials must submit Form Q-4 (Ministerial Solemnizations & Parish Return) for the preceding forty-eight quarters, or file an audited Affidavit of Sabbatical Non-Activity. Failure to file by November 15 will result in the immediate initiation of a Field Examination under Canon 14-B and statutory notification of all civil recording authorities within your jurisdiction.*

Jesse sat down on his stool. The stool was an old milk-can topped with a circle of scrap plywood, padded with a piece of sheepskin that had lost its wool during the Carter administration.

"Forty-eight quarters," he said aloud. The shop was quiet except for the slow, metallic ticking of an eight-day wall clock whose pendulum was two inches short of its proper drop.

Forty-eight quarters was twelve years.

Twelve years ago, in the humid, mosquito-thick July of 2012, Jesse’s second cousin, Arlene Fray, had announced her intention to marry a boy named Lonnie who drove an asphalt roller for the state department of transportation. Lonnie was nineteen, had two cousins in the state penitentiary at Eddyville, and possessed a deeply religious mother who belonged to a strict sect of Primitive Baptists who considered musical instruments an invention of the Ottoman Empire. Arlene’s father, Uncle Creed, was an elder at the Mount Zion Disciples Church, where dancing was considered an open invitation to cholera. Neither preacher would enter the other’s building, neither family would set foot on the other’s gravel, and Arlene had wept into three separate dish towels at the kitchen table until Jesse, who had spent the evening rebuilding the pull-cord on her mother’s weed trimmer, said he would look into it.

He had gone home, turned on the desktop computer that whined like a dying vacuum cleaner, dialed up the local exchange via the telephone line that ran across his fence line, and typed the words *ordain me legal fast free* into an engine that still had exclamation marks in its logo.

Most of the results had wanted forty dollars and promised a certificate suitable for framing in six to eight weeks. One of them, however, had a blue background patterned with small animated doves that flapped their wings on a five-second loop. It was called *The Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy*. It required no money upfront, though it suggested a voluntary "tithe of gratitude" of twelve dollars, which Jesse had mailed as a money order from the post office in Meacham.

The site asked six questions:
1. Did the applicant acknowledge the sky as the primordial covering of the gathered saints? (Jesse had checked *Yes*.)
2. Did the applicant hold that two souls joined under open air were bound before man and magistrate? (He had checked *Yes*.)
3. Was the applicant free from pending indictments for horse theft or barratry? (He had checked *Yes*, though he had to look up barratry in his uncle’s 1961 world atlas.)
4. Did the applicant agree to maintain the dignity of the canopy through sober speech and decent apparel? (*Yes*.)
5. Would the applicant observe the quarterly reporting of spiritual harvest as promulgated by the Presbyter-General? (Jesse had assumed this meant a newsletter; he had checked *Yes*.)
6. Print your name as it should appear upon the Roll of Elders.

He had typed *Jesse Lee Callow*. Then he had pressed the button marked *SOLEMNIZE NOW*.

A low chime had sounded from the computer’s tinny internal speaker. A page had appeared with an ornate border of oak leaves and acorns, topped by a gold-leaf JPEG of a pavilion with its flaps pinned back. It declared that *The Reverend Jesse L. Callow* was an ordained minister of the gospel, fully empowered under the statutes of the several states, territories, and commonwealths to bind on earth that which would be reviewed with interest in heaven.

He had printed it on twenty-pound bond paper using an inkjet printer that was running low on magenta, so the seal of the Living Canopy had come out a pale, liverish lilac.

The next morning, he had carried it up the concrete steps of the Whetstone County Courthouse in Meacham, pushed open the frosted-glass door marked *CLERK & REGISTRY OF DEEDS*, and laid it before Corinne Peake.

Corinne had been sixty-two years old then; she was seventy-four now, but her spectacles were still held to her neck by the same chain of imitation pearls, and she still kept a tin of menthol cough drops beside her receipt ledger. She had looked at the lilac seal. She had turned the paper over to see if anything was written on the back. She had sniffed.

"What is this, Jesse?" she had asked.

"It’s my ordination, Corinne."

"Ordination to what? You haven’t been inside the Methodist church since your daddy’s funeral, and even then you sat in the back row by the umbrella stand."

"It’s non-denominational," he had said carefully. "Apostolic. They hold that the sky is the natural covering."

"Well, the roof is leaking in courtroom two," she had replied, "so you’ll feel right at home if they ever haul you before the circuit judge. Give me seven dollars for the state seal."

She had taken seven ones from him, stamped the back of his certificate with a heavy circular die that crunched through the lilac border, and written *Rev. J. L. Callow (Apos. Can.)* in the thick leather ledger where the county kept track of auctioneers, embalmers, and men authorized to sign pistol permits.

Two weeks later, Jesse had married Arlene and Lonnie in the middle of a cut-over clover field behind Uncle Creed’s pond. He had worn his dark blue Sunday suit, borrowed a leatherette notebook from his glove compartment, and read the eighth chapter of the Song of Solomon while forty-two people stood on opposite sides of a ditch, glaring at each other until the fried chicken was unwrapped.

It had taken seven minutes. Nobody had shouted. Nobody had brought up the land dispute from 1988. When it was over, Uncle Creed had handed him an envelope containing forty dollars and a half-gallon of sorghum molasses, and Lonnie’s mother had told everyone within earshot that while she had her doubts about any church that didn’t possess an indoor baptistery with a painted backdrop of the River Jordan, at least the man didn’t mumble.

That had been the end of it, Jesse had thought.

Except it had not been the end.

In Whetstone County, there were three kinds of people who got married: those whose families had pews named after them in the Presbyterian church on the square; those who went to the justice of the peace in the basement of the jail, where they had to empty their pockets into a plastic bowl before taking their vows; and everybody else.

Everybody else lived on the dirt roads out toward the knobs, or worked second shift at the aluminum extrusions plant down in the valley, or had an ex-husband whose cousin was the associate pastor at the Baptist church, or wanted to be married at sunset on the tailgate of an F-250 with their bird dogs in the bed. They did not want to undergo six weeks of pre-marital spiritual counseling from a twenty-six-year-old seminary graduate who had never missed a truck payment. They did not want to promise to raise their children in the doctrines of the Synod of Dort.

They wanted someone who would show up at six o’clock on a Saturday, keep his shoes clean, call the bride’s mother *ma'am*, speak clearly enough for the deaf uncle in the second row to hear, and sign line fourteen of the county license with a blue ballpoint pen so the ink wouldn't run if it fell in a puddle.

Jesse Callow was that man.

For twelve years, he had been that man. He had married people in sheep meadows, in apple orchards, on pontoon boats tied to willow stumps at Lake Jubal, in the tasting room of a roadside cider mill, and once—in an event he still remembered with a dull ache in his left shoulder—on the roof of an abandoned limestone kiln where the groom’s best man had dropped the ring down an intake vent.

He had kept no diary. He had kept no ledger. When a couple gave him fifty dollars, he put twenty in the gas tank of his battered green Dodge Ram and used the rest to buy brass bushings or groceries at the Piggly Wiggly. When they gave him a smoked ham or a bushel of winter pears, he ate them.

And now, spread across his workbench between the kerosene cans and the magneto, was Form Q-4.

Jesse turned to page three.

*SECTION II: LITURGICAL CONFORMITY & CANONICAL FORM.*

> *Item 12: Enter the date, civil registry folio, and registered parcel identification number of each solemnization performed during the quarter.*  
> *Item 13: Certify that each ceremony conformed in all particulars to the Rites of the Pavilion as set forth in the 1998 Book of Common Shelter, Third Edition, including the recitation of the Seven Invocations of the Cloud, the Presentation of the Hem, and the Solemn Breaking of the Salt-Bread.*  
> *Item 14: Affix the signatures and certified ordination numbers of the two Attendant Deacons required to be present at all canonical solemnizations.*  
> *Item 15: Remit the Presbyterial Quota of twenty-two dollars ($22.00) per solemnization, together with five percent (5%) of all honoraria, love-offerings, or gratuities received, whether in coin, draft, or commodity.*

Jesse stared at Item 13.

"The Presentation of the Hem," he muttered. "What in God’s name is the hem?"

He turned to the back of the packet. There was a section labeled *INSTRUCTIONS FOR RECTIFICATION & DEFECTIVE RETURNS*.

He read the first two paragraphs twice:

> *1. The Integrity of the Body. Pursuant to Article IX of the Constitution of the Fellowship, the legal status of the Fellowship as a Recognized Hierarchical Denomination under the laws of the several states depends upon the unbroken transmission of canonical order. Should any credentialed presbyter report ceremonies conducted outside the Canonical Rites, or ceremonies conducted without the presence of two ordained deacons, or ceremonies performed in structures possessing a permanent artificial roof without the prior dispensation of the Bishop-Ordinary, such presbyter shall be deemed to have acted without ecclesiastical authority.*  
> *2. Consequences of Non-Canonical Reporting. Under the doctrine of Ecclesiastical Nullity as affirmed in Synodical Resolution 41-A (2019), any return disclosing that more than ten percent (10%) of a minister's solemnizations were performed without canonical compliance shall be referred immediately to the Synodical Assize for Summary Revocation. Furthermore, because the Fellowship’s corporate charter requires uniform adherence among its presbyters to preserve its group tax exemption and statutory recognition, a certified finding of widespread non-canonical practice within an administrative sub-district will result in the immediate and retroactive dissolution of the Fellowship's registration within that state.*

Jesse’s throat felt as dry as limestone dust.

He looked down at his own hands. They were stained with black axle grease around the knuckles, the permanent patina of a man who worked with iron and oil.

He read the next paragraph:

> *3. Mandatory Field Audit. In the event of an incomplete, ambiguous, or untimely return, the Board of Compliance shall appoint an Auditor-in-Mission under Canon 14-B. The Auditor shall personally inspect the civil records of the county or municipal registry wherein the presbyter is licensed, shall interview the parties to each registered marriage, and shall verify the presence of the required deacons, the payment of the Presbyterial Quota, and the recitation of the Canonical Rites. If the Auditor finds evidence of falsification or gross irregularity, the Board shall file a Formal Certificate of Defect with the Clerk of the Circuit Court and the State Registrar of Vital Statistics, requesting that all civil credentials issued under the Fellowship's seal be revoked ab initio.*

Jesse set the paper down.

He walked to the window of the workshop. Outside, his five acres sloped down to the ditch. Beyond the ditch was the blacktop of State Route 18, and beyond that were the rolling pastures of the Gidley farm, pale yellow in the October sun, dotted with the round grey shapes of unrolled hay bales.

"Four hundred," he whispered.

Four hundred marriages.

If he filed honestly—if he wrote across the thirty-two pages of Form Q-4 that he had never heard of the Book of Common Shelter, that he had never seen the Seven Invocations of the Cloud, that his only "attendant deacons" were usually a pair of nervous eighteen-year-olds in rented tuxedos smelling of cheap bourbon and wild onion, and that ninety percent of his ceremonies had been conducted while someone's grandmother held a plastic umbrella over his head—the Fellowship would revoke him, declare his acts null, and dissolve its own state charter to save itself from the IRS.

If the denomination dissolved its charter retroactively, what happened to the four hundred licenses Corinne Peake had stamped with her heavy purple seal?

And if he filed badly—if he tried to invent thirty-two quarters of fictional deacons named Smith and Jones, if he tried to guess dates and fake parcel numbers—the man from Terre Haute, this "Auditor-in-Mission," would get off the Greyhound bus in Meacham with a briefcase and a clipboard. He would walk up the marble stairs of the courthouse. He would sit down across from Corinne Peake.

And Corinne Peake had every marriage certificate from 1954 to this morning filed in green steel drawers in the vault behind her desk.

Jesse turned around and looked at the kitchen calendar hanging on the workshop wall. It was from the Meacham Farm Bureau, showing a glossy photograph of an International Harvester combine in an impossible field of wheat.

Today was October 14.

November 15 was thirty-two days away.

### Chapter 2

The Whetstone County Courthouse had been built in 1888 out of local limestone that was soft enough to take the chisel when quarried, but had since hardened into a dark, pitted grey the color of an old hornets’ nest. It had four chimneys that had not been swept since the coal furnaces were removed in 1964, a clock tower whose four faces each told a different time of day, and an iron statue of a Union infantryman on the lawn who was missing his bayonet because a high school senior had backed a flatbed into him during the 1994 bicentennial parade.

The Registry of Deeds was on the second floor, down a long hallway that smelled of pine oil floor soap and damp wool.

Jesse climbed the stairs slowly. His boots, though clean, made a distinct leather creak on the worn yellow pine of the treads. Under his arm he carried a manila folder containing the three seed circulars, his water bill, and two blank sheets of yellow legal paper. He did not have Form Q-4 with him; he had left that locked in the drawer of his drill press, under three spare drive belts.

When he pushed open the frosted door, a small brass bell attached to the top rail gave a flat, tinny *clink*.

Corinne Peake did not look up from her desk. She was seventy-four years old, wore a cardigan of boiled magenta wool that had small plastic buttons shaped like strawberries, and was currently using an electric eraser to remove an errant digit from a deed of easement. The eraser made a high-pitched, dentist-drill whine that set Jesse’s teeth on edge.

"Shut the door, Jesse," she said without stopping the machine. "The draft from that stairwell is coming right off the river."

Jesse closed the door. He walked across the green linoleum floor to the counter, which was topped with a slab of grey slate worn smooth by seventy years of farmers leaning on their elbows while waiting for title searches.

"Morning, Corinne," he said.

The electric eraser stopped. Corinne blew a small grey pile of rubber shavings off the paper, inspected the erasure with a pocket magnifying glass, and then looked up at him through her bifocals.

"You’re early for the quarterly returns, aren’t you?" she asked.

Jesse felt a cold drop of sweat trickle down between his shoulder blades, exactly where the collar of his denim shirt touched his neck.

"Quarterly returns?" he repeated, trying to keep his voice at the flat, unhurried pitch he used when explaining to a customer that their lawnmower engine had thrown a rod through the crankcase.

"The county fees," she said, looking at him with mild disapproval. "Third-quarter vital statistics reports went to the state registrar on Friday. You don't have any fees due to me. You paid the four dollars on the Miller girl's license two weeks ago."

"Right," Jesse said. He exhaled through his nose, very quietly. "The Miller girl. Over by the quarry."

"Her mother called me twice," Corinne said, reaching into a ceramic mug for an emery board. She began to shape the thumbnail of her left hand with dry, rasping strokes. "Wanted to know if her name was spelled with one *l* or two on the permanent roll. I told her if her own daughter didn't know how many *l*'s were in her husband’s name when she signed the application, the State of Kentucky wasn't going to buy a new ribbon for the typewriter to fix it. People have no respect for permanent records, Jesse. They think a record is like a text message on a telephone. They think you can just push a button and take it back."

"I expect they do," Jesse said. He laid his manila folder on the slate counter. "Corinne, I was wondering about something."

"That's usually expensive."

"You remember when I registered my papers? Back in twelve?"

"I remember everything that came through this door since nineteen-ninety-two," she said, not looking up from her nail. "I remember your paper because it had a purple bird on it and it looked like it had been printed on a grocery bag."

"It was a dove," Jesse said mildly. "The Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy."

"Looked like a blue-tailed grackle to me. What about it?"

Jesse leaned his forearm on the slate. "A fellow was asking me the other day. Out by the elevator. He was wondering what happens if a church... well, say a church changes its rules. Or say a church goes out of business. Moves its headquarters. What happens to the men they ordained?"

Corinne stopped her emery board. She laid it down on the green blotter, exactly parallel to her plastic ruler, and turned her chair ninety degrees to face him squarely. Her eyes, magnified behind the thick lenses, were the pale, watery blue of skim milk.

"Jesse Lee Callow, have you been getting letters from Indiana?"

Jesse didn't move. He kept his fingers resting lightly on the manila folder. "Why would I get letters from Indiana?"

Corinne snorted. It was a sharp, dry sound, like an air brake on a gravel truck.

"Because the clerk in Spencer County called me three weeks ago," she said. "And the clerk in Ohio County called me last Tuesday. Some outfit called the Compliance Bureau of the Living Canopy has been sending registered inquiries to every county seat within a hundred miles of the river. Wanting certified transcripts of the ministerial rolls. Wanting to know if the men on the roll are paying their synodical levies."

She stood up. She was not a tall woman—five feet two at most—but she carried herself with the terrifying authority of someone who held the only key to a fireproof vault containing every mortgage recorded in Whetstone County since the Civil War.

She walked to the green steel door that led into the vault. It was six inches thick, painted olive drab, with a brass combination dial that had been worn down to the bare brass by three generations of Peakes.

"You come in here," she said over her shoulder.

Jesse lifted the hinged flap of the slate counter and followed her into the vault.

The vault was twenty feet square and twelve feet high, lined from floor to ceiling with heavy steel roller-shelves holding leather-bound folios the size of tombstones. The room smelled intensely of leather, vinegar, damp lime, and the zinc smell of old galvanized filing cabinets. A single bare fluorescent tube hummed overhead, casting a cold, flickering light over the stacks.

Corinne stopped before a bank of four-drawer cabinets in the far corner, labeled *VITAL STATISTICS: MARRIAGES (1995–PRESENT)*.

She put her hand on the brass handle of the third drawer down.

"Do you know how many marriages are registered in this county in an average year, Jesse?"

"Seventy?" he guessed.

"Sixty-four," she corrected him. "Sixty-four point two, if you count the people who buy the license and then get into a fight in the parking lot behind the Dairy Queen and never bring the certificate back. In twelve years, that's roughly seven hundred and seventy marriages."

She pulled the drawer open. It slid out on heavy steel ball bearings with a low, rumbling growl.

Inside were hundreds of grey cardboard folders, each held together by a faded red cloth ribbon.

"Out of those seven hundred and seventy," Corinne said, tapping the edge of the drawer with a manicured fingernail, "three hundred and ninety-eight were signed by you."

Jesse looked down into the drawer. The folders were clean, square, and neatly labeled in Corinne’s upright, schoolteacher hand.

"Three hundred and ninety-eight," he repeated. It sounded different when she said it. It sounded like an army. It sounded like half the county.

"Three hundred and ninety-eight," she said. "The Methodists did eighty-two. The First Baptists did ninety-six. The Catholic father down at Saint Ann’s did forty-four, mostly people from the quarry. The magistrates did seventy-eight, mostly in the afternoons when they were trying to avoid the road crew foreman. And you did the rest."

She looked up at him, her chin tilted so she could see him through the bottom half of her glasses.

"You did the Gidleys," she said. "You did both the Gidley boys, their sister, four of their cousins, and their uncle Wade when he married that woman from Tennessee who wouldn't get out of the boat."

"Wade was very fond of that boat," Jesse murmured.

"You did the county attorney’s daughter," she continued, her voice rising half an octave. "You did her out in that hay meadow on Route 9 with sixty-five people sitting on hay bales in three-hundred-dollar suits, and when the thunderstorm hit you stood there in the downpour and finished the ceremony while the best man was trying to get the generator started to save the shrimp."

"The shrimp were very expensive," Jesse said. "Her father told me three times."

"My own niece," Corinne said, and for the first time her voice lost its metallic edge, softening into something that was almost dangerous. "Lindy. Five years ago. In her grandfather's barn."

Jesse felt a cold sensation in the pit of his stomach.

"The barn," he said.

"The barn," Corinne repeated. She reached into the drawer, her fingers moving with the mechanical precision of an indexer who did not need to read the tabs, and pulled out a thin buff folder labeled *2019 — GIDLEY / SELLS*.

She did not open it. She held it against her stomach with both hands, like a hymnbook.

"Toby Gidley is the deputy road commissioner now, Jesse. Lindy runs the soil testing laboratory for the extension office. They’ve got two little girls. They’ve got forty acres on the creek and an FSA mortgage that’s backed by the federal government. If their marriage isn't legal, that mortgage is defective. If that mortgage is defective, the bank calls the note. If the bank calls the note, Toby’s daddy has to sell his machinery to cover it."

She stepped closer to him. The scent of menthol and dry paper was overwhelming.

"Now you tell me," she whispered, "what that man in Terre Haute is talking about."

Jesse looked at the heavy olive-drab walls of the vault. For twelve years he had thought of his ordination as a piece of harmless bureaucratic machinery—a rubber stamp, a legal convenience, a way to let decent people get on with their lives without having to explain themselves to a committee of strangers. He had treated the words he said in the fields with great care; he had never mocked them, he had never rushed them, he had learned the names of the dead grandmothers and he had stood quietly by the pasture fences while the mothers cried into their handkerchiefs.

He had thought that was what mattered.

"They want forty-eight quarters of reports," Jesse said. His voice sounded thin between the towering shelves of folios. "They want the names of the two deacons who were with me at every wedding."

Corinne’s eyes widened slightly. "What deacons?"

"I don't have any deacons, Corinne. You know I don't have any deacons. The only person who ever went with me was that boy from the hardware store who used to carry the spare tire."

"Billy?" she said. "Billy Cates? Billy was twelve years old in twenty-fourteen. He couldn't witness a dog license."

"They want to know if I used the Book of Common Shelter," Jesse said. "They want to know if I performed the Presentation of the Hem."

Corinne stared at him. "The hem of what?"

"I don't know," Jesse said miserably. "The hem of the pavilion, I reckon. They say if I didn't use the rites, the weddings are non-canonical. And if they're non-canonical, the Fellowship revokes my credentials back to the day I printed the paper."

Corinne did not speak for a long time. The fluorescent light hummed overhead, a steady sixty-cycle drone that seemed to fill the small room.

Finally, she looked down at the folder in her hands.

"What if you tell them you did use them?" she asked.

"If I file a report that says I used the rites," Jesse said, "they'll want the deacons' signatures. And the quota. Twenty-two dollars a wedding."

Corinne’s mental arithmetic was instantaneous. "Eight thousand seven hundred and fifty-six dollars."

"Plus five percent of the offerings."

"You never took an offering in your life, Jesse Callow. You took half a side of pork from Arlo Vance and you gave three-quarters of it to the widow Cleary because your freezer was full of squirrel."

"The man in Terre Haute doesn't know that," Jesse said. "And if the report looks crooked to him—if he thinks I'm lying about the deacons or the hem—he doesn't just revoke me. He sends an auditor here. To this room. To look at every license in that drawer."

Corinne looked at the drawer. Her knuckles, where she held the folder against her sweater, turned white.

"Nobody looks in these drawers," she said, her voice dropping into a register that made Jesse think of his third-grade teacher holding a twelve-inch wooden ruler. "The State Department of Archives sent two girls down here from Frankfort four years ago with a scanner. Told me they were going to digitize the vital records. I gave them two cups of instant coffee, sat them at that table by the door, and let them scan the dog registrations from nineteen-thirty-one until their machine overheated. Then I told them the courthouse roof was condemned and sent them back to the capital."

She set the Gidley folder back into its slot, clicked the steel compressor down, and slid the drawer shut. The heavy click of the latch sounded like a rifle bolt closing.

"Listen to me, Jesse," she said, turning to him. "There is one thing you don't know about that barn."

Jesse waited.

"Whose barn was it?" she asked.

"Old man Sells's," Jesse said. "Lindy's granddaddy. The one that fell down in the ice storm three winters back."

"The barn was on the property line," Corinne said. "Right on the line between Whetstone County and Jackson County. Old man Sells built it there in nineteen-fifty-two because he had a dispute with the Jackson County tax assessor over three acres of cedar woods, and he thought if he put the hay door in Whetstone and the manure chute in Jackson, neither one of them could levy on the rafters."

Jesse took off his cap and ran his hand through his grey hair. "Corinne..."

"The ceremony," she said, looking straight into his eyes. "Where were you standing?"

Jesse closed his eyes. The memory came back to him with terrifying clarity: the roar of the wind through the hemlock boards; the yellow light of forty-eight kerosene lanterns hung from the cross-ties; the smell of dry fescue hay and damp cedar; Toby Gidley in a rented grey morning coat that was too tight across his shoulders; Lindy with three wild daisies pinned into her hair; the rain suddenly hammering on the tin roof like a bucket of gravel thrown against a barn wall; and Jesse himself, standing on two overturned mineral tubs to keep his boots out of the damp chaff, his back against the north siding.

"I was standing by the north wall," he said. "Under the hay fork."

Corinne closed her eyes.

"The north wall," she whispered, "is four feet inside Jackson County."

### Chapter 3

The gravel road that led out to the Gidley place followed the meanders of Whetstone Creek, crossing the water three times on one-lane concrete bridges whose railings had been carried away by the spring flood of 1997. The creek was low now, a clear, brownish trickle over slabs of yellow limestone, choked with water-willow and the fallen leaves of sycamores.

Jesse drove with both hands on the top of the steering wheel, his shoulders hunched. The Dodge had a loose tie-rod end that gave the front wheels a rhythmic, shimmying tremor whenever he exceeded thirty-five miles an hour, but today he was driving twenty.

The implications of what Corinne had said in the vault were settling into him like damp grain in a bin, generating a slow, dangerous heat.

A marriage solemnized in Jackson County by an officiant whose credentials were filed only in Whetstone County was not necessarily void—Kentucky law had a curative statute for jurisdictional defects, provided the parties had acted in good faith and the officiant believed himself authorized. But good faith was a legal doctrine that belonged to judges in Lexington who had law clerks to write forty-page memorandums about intent. In Whetstone County, a defect on a license was like a split in an oak fence post: you didn't argue with it; you just watched the wire sag until the cows got into the corn.

And if Harlan Grote—or whatever the Compliance Officer’s name was in Terre Haute—came down with his briefcases, he would not be looking for good faith. He would be looking for the Book of Common Shelter.

Jesse turned off the blacktop at the cedar post with the reflector nailed to it.

The Gidley house was a four-room white frame cottage with a tin roof that Toby had painted with red oxide two summers ago. In the yard, two small girls in blue overalls were throwing gravel into an empty plastic five-gallon bucket with rhythmic, metallic *thunks*. A red-and-white border collie was lying under an elderberry bush, its head on its paws, watching the bucket with the intense, unblinking suspicion of its breed.

Behind the house, where the old bank barn had stood, was an empty rectangle of grey crushed limestone. A modern pole barn of green corrugated steel sat fifty yards to the east, its wide slider doors thrown back to reveal a John Deere 5075 tractor with a front-end loader and three round bales of fescue wrapped in white plastic.

Toby Gidley was standing by the tractor’s rear tire, a grease gun in his hand, wearing a grey work shirt with *Whetstone Co. Road Dept.* embroidered in yellow oval script over the pocket. He was thirty-two, broad-shouldered, with the pale, reddish skin of the uplands that freckled rather than tanned, and thick red hair that stuck out from beneath a faded ball cap advertising an agricultural lime quarry in Clark County.

When the Dodge rattled into the turn-around, Toby laid the grease gun across the tractor’s drawbar and wiped his hands on a blue shop rag.

"Jesse," he said. He did not smile—upland men rarely smiled before noon unless someone had wrecked a car in an amusing way—but his voice was friendly. "You looking for Lindy? She's up at the shed testing water samples from the school."

"I was looking for both of you, actually," Jesse said, climbing down from the truck. He left the driver's door unlatched; the latch was worn, and if you slammed it too hard the window fell down inside the door frame. "You got a minute, Toby?"

Toby looked at the Dodge, then at Jesse’s boots, then at Jesse’s face. His eyes narrowed slightly. He had worked five years on the county road crew, which meant he spent forty hours a week listening to farmers explain why the county ought to replace a culvert that had been washed out because the farmer had bulldozed his own ditch full of brush. He knew the difference between a man who wanted a favor and a man who had brought bad news.

"Lindy!" he called toward the house. "Come on down here a minute!"

A screen door banged. Lindy Gidley came down the wooden steps. She was twenty-nine, small-boned, with dark hair tied back with a piece of green baling twine, wearing a pair of rubber boots and an apron made of heavy grey canvas that had three plastic test-tube racks sticking out of the front pocket. She had her grandfather's sharp chin and the grey, observant eyes that had made the Sells family difficult to deal with at cattle auctions for eighty years.

"Jesse," she said, stopping five feet short of him and wiping her fingers on her apron. "If this is about Uncle Wade's tractor, Toby told him three times that the hydraulic pump is shot and there's no use putting new seals in it."

"It's not about the tractor," Jesse said. He took off his hat, looked into the crown as if he might find instructions printed there, and put it back on. "It's about your wedding."

Lindy stopped. Her hands, which had been reaching for a pencil in her apron, went still.

Toby picked up the blue shop rag and began to wipe the grease gun's nozzle with slow, circular strokes.

"Our wedding was five years ago, Jesse," Lindy said. "The cake was dry and Toby’s brother threw up in the well-house, but we paid you the forty dollars."

"You paid me," Jesse said. "You gave me fifty, and Toby gave me that five-gallon bucket of yellow cling peaches."

"They were good peaches," Toby said.

"They were very good peaches," Jesse agreed. "I made sixteen pints of preserves out of them. But there's a fellow in Indiana."

He told them. He stood between the tractor and the crushed-stone pad where the old barn had been, with the October wind bringing the smell of diesel fuel and drying clover off the hill, and he told them about Form Q-4. He told them about the thirty-two pages, the twenty-two-dollar quota, the Seven Invocations of the Cloud, the two deacons, and the man named Harlan Grote who had an office in Terre Haute.

When he reached the part about the audit and the four hundred licenses in Corinne Peake's vault, Toby stopped wiping the grease gun.

"What do you mean, *dissolve*?" Toby asked. His voice had gone very flat. "A church can't just dissolve somebody's marriage. We've got a license. It's got the county seal on it. It's got a five-dollar revenue stamp on the corner."

"The license is an authorization to solemnize," Lindy said. She spoke quietly, but there was an edge in her voice that made the border collie lift its head under the elderberry bush. "It's not the solemnization itself. I took that class on county administration when I went to work for the extension office. If the person who signs the certificate didn't have the authority to sign it, the certificate is an unexecuted instrument. It's like a deed that was signed by a man who didn't own the mineral rights."

Toby looked at his wife as if she had suddenly started speaking German. "We've been living here five years, Lindy. We've got two kids. We filed our taxes joint on the farm credit."

"I know what we did, Toby," she said. She turned to Jesse. Her grey eyes were steady, but a small white line had appeared around her nostrils. "Jesse, whose side are you on?"

"I'm on your side," Jesse said immediately. "I'm on all four hundred of them's side. That's why I'm here instead of filling out the papers."

"What happens if you fill them out?" Toby asked. "Just write down whatever the fellow wants to hear. Tell him you had two deacons. Tell him you said the words about the cloud. What does he care? He's in Indiana."

"If I tell him I had two deacons," Jesse said, "he'll want their names and their provincial registration numbers. There aren't any other presbyters in this county, Toby. There aren't any in the next three counties. If I give him names of people around here, he'll look them up on his computer, see they aren't on his roll, and send an auditor down on the bus. And if he comes here, the first place he goes is the courthouse."

"Corinne won't let him in the vault," Toby said.

"Corinne can't keep a federal auditor out of the vault if he's got a subpoena from the circuit court," Lindy said sharply. "And even if she could, what about the barn?"

Toby blinked. "What about the barn?"

Lindy pointed with a pencil toward the empty rectangle of crushed limestone where the two little girls were still dropping gravel into the bucket.

"Granddaddy built the barn over the line, Toby. You know that. Everybody in the district knew that. When the assessor came from Jackson County, granddaddy used to move the sheep into the south bay so they were in Whetstone, and when the Whetstone assessor came he moved them into the north bay so they were in Jackson."

Toby looked at the gravel pad. A look of profound, slow-dawning horror crept across his broad, freckled face.

"Jesse," Toby said, "where was the preacher standing?"

"Under the hay fork," Jesse said.

Toby dropped the grease gun. It hit the tractor’s drawbar with a loud, ringing *clack* and fell into the dirt.

"The hay fork was on the north rafter," Toby whispered. "The north rafter was six feet past the township stone."

"Seven feet," Lindy corrected him. "The stone was under the water trough."

For a full minute, nobody spoke. The only sound was the wind in the bare walnut trees along the fence line and the rhythmic *thunk... thunk... thunk* of the gravel hitting the plastic bucket in the yard.

The border collie stood up, stretched its front legs, yawned, and walked over to sniff Toby's boot. Toby didn't look down at it.

"Jesse," Toby said at last, his voice barely louder than the dry leaves rattling in the sumac, "I'm on the ballot for full road commissioner in November. The primary is in May, but the party convention is next month. If the *Whetstone Banner* prints a story saying my wife and I have been living in open fornication on a defective deed for five years, my own mother won't vote for me."

"Your mother didn't vote for you when you ran for magistrate," Lindy said. "She told everybody at the church that you were too quick with a backhoe."

"That was different," Toby said, his face reddening. "That was family."

He turned back to Jesse, taking a step toward him. "What are we going to do?"

Jesse looked at the empty gravel pad where the barn had been. In his mind, he could still see the heavy timber frame: the twelve-by-twelve hemlock posts that had been hewn with a broadaxe in the eighteen-eighties; the white pine rafters held together with wooden trunnels; the swallows' nests plastered against the purlins like small clay pots. It had seemed like the most permanent thing in the county, a structure that had outlasted three wars, four depressions, and two generations of Sells men who had worked themselves into the cemetery on the hill.

And now it was gone, crushed limestone and wild cucumber vines, and the only evidence that four hundred people had stood under its roof was a piece of lilac-bordered paper in a green steel drawer in Meacham.

"We have thirty-one days," Jesse said.

"Thirty-one days until what?" Lindy asked.

"Until November fifteenth," Jesse said. "Until Harlan Grote gets on the Greyhound bus."

### Chapter 4

The kitchen of Jesse Callow’s house smelled of chicory coffee, old linoleum, and the damp yellow pages of sixty-four volumes of the *Whetstone County Agricultural Gazette* that his father had stacked behind the woodbox in 1978 and never looked at again.

It was eight o’clock on Wednesday morning. A cold, drizzling rain had set in before dawn, turning the red clay of the yard into a slick, grease-like paste that clung to the soles of Jesse’s boots in two-pound cakes.

On the table, arranged in three neat stacks, were:
1. The thirty-two pages of Form Q-4;
2. A spiral-bound notebook with a faded cover of green marbled paper, labeled *RECORD OF WORK: 2012–PRESENT*; and
3. A telephone directory for Whetstone, Jackson, and Powell Counties, dated 2019.

Jesse sat with a blue ballpoint pen in his hand, a cup of black coffee at his elbow, and his reading glasses balanced on the bridge of his nose.

The spiral notebook had not been kept as a ministerial register. It was the book in which he recorded the serial numbers of chain saws he had repaired, the dimensions of replacement gears he had cut for the cider mill, and the dates on which he had cleaned the carburetor on the county hearse.

Tucked between the pages, however, were small scraps of paper that told another story:
- A receipt from the Meacham Gulf Station for six gallons of unleaded, dated June 8, 2013, with the words *Boyd girl — orchard — $35.00* written on the back in pencil;
- A paper napkin from the Dairy Queen, dated August 22, 2015, bearing the names *Darrell & Sissy (pontoon boat)* and a rough sketch of a three-bolt flange for a bilge pump;
- A cardboard tag from a bag of 10-10-10 fertilizer, dated October 4, 2018, reading *Wade Gidley (boat wedding) — NO HYMNS — Wade had five dollars, said he'd bring the rest after tobacco sale.*

Jesse turned the pages slowly. Each entry brought with it the sudden, sharp memory of a face, a smell, a particular quality of afternoon light.

There was the wedding of young Donnie Cleary, who had lost his left hand in an ensilage cutter two years before. Donnie had stood by the fence behind the stockyards in a suit that was four inches too short in the sleeves, his raw, pink stump tucked into his coat pocket, shaking so hard that his brother had to hold him by the belt to keep him from falling into the nettles. Jesse had taken Donnie's good right hand, placed the girl's fingers into it, and held them both between his own grease-calloused palms until the trembling stopped.

*What words did you say?* Form Q-4 asked in Item 13-A. *Cite the paragraph number from the Book of Common Shelter.*

"I told them," Jesse murmured to the empty kitchen, "that a three-fold cord is not easily broken. And I told Donnie that if he ever took that girl for granted, his brother had my permission to throw him in the slurry pit."

There was no box for that on Form Q-4.

There was the wedding of the high school history teacher, Miss Eleanor Vance, who was fifty-two years old and had married a retired rural mail carrier named Arthur who had three wooden teeth and could play the mandolin with his thumb. They had been married at sunset on the limestone outcrop known as the Devil’s Backbone, four miles out on the ridge road, where the wind blew so hard that Eleanor’s veil had torn loose and sailed out over the gorge like a white hawk, circling twice before catching in the top of a dead hemlock five hundred feet below.

*Were two consecrated Deacons present to observe the Presentation of the Salt-Bread?* Item 14 asked.

The only other person on the Devil’s Backbone had been Arthur’s fifteen-year-old nephew, who had spent the entire ceremony trying to shoot blue-tailed skinks with a slingshot.

Jesse turned another page.

*Total: 398.*

He dipped his pen in his coffee by mistake, wiped the nib on the leg of his trousers, and stared at the first blank space on page one:

*NAME OF ADMINISTRATIVE REGION: _______________*

A gravel-throwing rattle sounded in the driveway.

Jesse stood up and looked out the small square window in the back door. A white Chevrolet sedan with *Whetstone County Extension Service* painted in blue lettering on the door had pulled up beside his Dodge.

Lindy Gidley got out of the car. She was wearing a yellow rubber slicker, a pair of men's neoprene work gloves, and was carrying a green plastic milk crate that appeared to be extremely heavy.

She didn't knock. She kicked the bottom of the kitchen door with her boot until Jesse pulled it open.

"Shut it quick," she said, setting the milk crate down on the linoleum with a heavy, metallic *thud*. "The wind's coming out of the north. We're going to have frost on the bottoms by daylight."

She stripped off her yellow slicker and hung it on the hook behind the door. Underneath, she was wearing a thick wool sweater the color of oatmeal and a pair of dark corduroy trousers tucked into her boots. Her hair was damp around her temples, curling into tight, dark rings.

"What is that?" Jesse asked, looking at the milk crate.

"The enemy's supply lines," Lindy said.

She reached into the crate and began setting items on the kitchen table, moving Jesse’s coffee cup and his chains-saw notebook out of the way with brisk, unceremonious efficiency.

First came a thick, bound volume with a black cloth cover, embossed in dull gold with the words: *CONSTITUTION, CANONS, AND LITURGICAL ORDERS OF THE APOSTOLIC FELLOWSHIP OF THE LIVING CANOPY (REVISED EDITION, 2004).*

Second came four cardboard accordion files, bulging with legal-sized carbon copies, tied with lengths of faded pink ribbon.

Third came an electric adding machine with a twelve-key keyboard and a roll of two-inch paper tape, bearing the asset tag: *Whetstone Co. Soil Conservation Dist. #4.*

Jesse stared at the black book. "Where did you get that?"

"My mother's cousin in Clark County," Lindy said, pulling out a chair and sitting down. She didn't ask for coffee; she simply picked up Jesse’s cup, took a sip, made a face at the chicory, and set it down again. "Her second husband was ordained by them in nineteen-ninety-nine so he could marry people at his campground. He died in twenty-sixteen, and she had this in the bottom of a cedar chest behind three patchwork quilts and a set of silver-plated butter knives."

Jesse reached out a finger and touched the gold lettering on the black cloth. The fabric was dry and had that peculiar, chalky feel of books that had spent twenty years in an unheated bedroom.

"Does it have the hem?" he whispered.

"Page ninety-four," Lindy said without looking. "It's not a hem on a skirt, Jesse. It's the border of the tent. You're supposed to have four pieces of white unbleached linen, six cubits long, pinned to the four corner stakes of the field to represent the four corners of the New Jerusalem. The groom holds the north stake, the bride holds the south stake, and the two deacons hold the east and the west."

Jesse looked at her. "Lindy, I've married people in cow pastures where if you pinned six cubits of white linen to a stake, the Angus heifers would have eaten it before we got through the Lord’s Prayer."

"I know that," she said. "And the man in Terre Haute knows that too. That's why he has an office in an industrial concourse instead of a church. Look at the title page."

Jesse opened the black book. The paper was thin, semi-translucent, the kind used for cheap Bibles, with red edges that had faded to a dry, brick-dust orange.

On the title page was an inscription in black ballpoint ink, written in a cramped, shaky hand:

*Property of the Synodical Treasury. Not to be removed from the Repository of Records without the written consent of the Bishop-Ordinary. (Price: $18.50 postpaid).*

Below that was a small purple stamp:

*LIQUIDATION ASSET NO. 411-B*  
*RECEIVERSHIP OF THE THIRD CHANCERY COURT*  
*VANDERBURGH COUNTY, INDIANA*

Jesse frowned. "Receivership?"

Lindy leaned forward, resting her forearms on the table. In the grey morning light from the window, her face had that sharp, focused intensity that Jesse remembered from the afternoon five years ago when she had stood in the barn with the thunderstorm rattling the tin roof, looking at Toby as if she were daring him to drop the ring.

"The Living Canopy isn't a church, Jesse," she said. "It hasn't been a church since two thousand and eight. The man who started it—that Reverend Morling who had the website with the blue doves—died in an assisted-living facility in Evansville eight years ago. His nephew tried to sell the website to an outfit in Florida that runs online massage schools, but the state attorney general stepped in because Morling had registered the whole thing as an educational trust to avoid paying taxes on three hundred acres of strip-mined timberland in Posey County."

Jesse felt the room tilt slightly. "Timberland?"

"The trust owes the State of Indiana four hundred and eighty thousand dollars in back severance taxes," Lindy said, tapping the adding machine with her knuckle. "The court appointed a receiver. The receiver hired a collection agency from Indianapolis called Apex Asset Recovery. Apex Asset Recovery specializes in municipal tax liens, delinquent nursing home accounts, and non-profit dissolutions."

She reached into one of the accordion files and pulled out a single sheet of paper. It was a photocopy of an employment contract, dated May 14, 2024.

"Harlan Grote," she said, "isn't an Auditor-in-Mission. He's a forensic accountant who works on a fifteen-percent contingency fee. For every dollar of delinquent synodical quota he collects from old ministers, he keeps fifteen cents. And for every minister he decertifies, the receiver gets to write off twelve hundred dollars in administrative liabilities against the trust’s tax debt."

Jesse picked up Form Q-4. He looked at Item 15: *Remit the Presbyterial Quota of twenty-two dollars ($22.00) per solemnization...*

"He doesn't want the hem," Jesse said slowly.

"He doesn't give a damn about the hem," Lindy said. "He wants eight thousand seven hundred and fifty-six dollars from you. Or he wants you to admit that you're an irregular minister so he can file a certificate of non-compliance with the State of Kentucky, collect his twelve-hundred-dollar fee from the bankruptcy court in Evansville, and close your file."

"And if he closes my file?"

"He sends a Form 1099-MISC to the IRS reporting your unlicensed ministerial income for twelve years," she said. "And he sends a certified copy of the revocation to Corinne Peake."

Jesse took off his glasses. He laid them on the black cloth cover of the Book of Common Shelter.

The rain was coming harder now, drumming against the tin roof of the workshop outside with a steady, rhythmic hiss. He could hear the sheep in the neighbor’s hollow, their distant, coughing bleats muffled by the mist.

"Lindy," he said, "how much money is in the road department’s bridge fund?"

"Jesse Lee Callow," she said, her voice dropping into a whisper, "if you're thinking about asking Toby to steal county money to pay off a bankruptcy receiver in Terre Haute, I'll hit you with this adding machine myself."

"I wasn't thinking about stealing it," Jesse said. "I was just wondering what Toby's budget was."

"Toby's budget is forty-two thousand dollars for cold patch and gravel," she said. "And the county judge-executive counts the shovel handles every Friday afternoon before he lets the road crew go home."

She reached out and took Form Q-4 out of his hands.

"Now," she said, pulling the adding machine toward her and plugging its black rubber cord into the two-prong outlet beside the kitchen sink. "We are going to do this the way my grandfather handled the cattle inspector in nineteen-sixty-three."

Jesse looked at her warily. "How did your grandfather handle the cattle inspector?"

"He gave him two jars of pickled okra, showed him the five best steers in the county three times in three different pastures, and had my uncle loosen the distributor cap on the inspector's truck so he couldn't leave until after the banks had closed for the weekend."

"Lindy," Jesse said, "we can't loosen the distributor cap on the Greyhound bus."

"No," she said, her fingers already dancing over the keys of the adding machine with the dry, chatter-like clatter of a machine gun. "But we can make forty-eight quarters of reports look like they were written by a man who was so religious that the receiver will be afraid to touch him with a ten-foot pole."

### Chapter 5

By Thursday afternoon, the kitchen table had disappeared beneath a geological stratification of paper.

Lindy had established three distinct zones of operations:
- The **Canonical Zone**, consisting of the black-bound Book of Common Shelter, three volumes of Kentucky Revised Statutes borrowed from the public library in Meacham under the pretense of researching county boundary disputes, and an ancient dictionary of ecclesiastical terms that smelled like a cellar;
- The **Secular Zone**, containing Corinne Peake’s quarterly vital statistics summaries (which Lindy had obtained by having Toby take Corinne a quart of fresh apple butter and two dozen hot yeast rolls while Lindy took the carbon copies from the desk drawer behind the counter); and
- The **Fabrication Zone**, which was centered around an Underwood manual typewriter that Jesse had dragged out of his workshop attic, its black enamel scarred with chips and its carriage return lever bent slightly to the left.

"A typewriter," Jesse had objected when she brought it into the kitchen. "Lindy, they have computers in Terre Haute. They have laser printers."

"That's why we're using the typewriter," she had replied without looking up from her notes. "A forensic accountant looks at a computer printout and his eyes go straight to the margins and the fonts. He thinks in Excel. He thinks in rows and columns. If you send him thirty-two pages of clean laser printing, he'll run it through a document scanner and have thirty inconsistencies flagged before lunch."

She had slapped the top of the Underwood with her palm. The steel frame gave a hollow, metallic *bong*.

"A manual typewriter with a worn black-and-red ribbon," she said, "means an old man sitting in a parsonage with a woodstove. It means three different weights of ribbon ink. It means the letter *e* is filled with lint and sits half a millimeter higher than the *r*. Nobody audits a man whose *e* is filled with lint, Jesse. It looks too much like actual church work."

Jesse sat on his stool, an emery board in his hand, carefully cleaning the typebars of the Underwood with a toothbrush dipped in lighter fluid.

"What about the deacons?" he asked.

"We have two deacons," Lindy said. She did not look up from her adding machine tape.

"We do?"

"We do now. Deacon Arlo Vance and Deacon Billy Cates."

Jesse dropped the toothbrush into the can of fluid. "Arlo Vance died in nineteen-nineteen, Lindy. He's buried in the churchyard at Mount Zion under three feet of periwinkle."

"Arlo Vance Senior died in nineteen-nineteen," she corrected him. "Arlo Vance Junior is eighty-two years old, lives in the trailer behind the limestone quarry, has no telephone, and couldn't tell you the year of the flood if you held a pistol to his ear. He's been an elder at the Christian Church since nineteen-seventy-four. He signed an affidavit of ordination for my father when we were trying to get an exemption from the school board for the revival tent."

"And Billy Cates?"

"Billy turned twenty-two last March," she said. "He works for the state road department now. He drives the salt truck. Toby told him this morning that if he signs thirty-two pieces of paper without reading them, Toby will let him have the new Western blade on his truck this winter instead of the old one with the rusted trip springs."

Jesse picked up Form Q-4 for the second quarter of 2014.

"Item 13-B," he read. "*Did the solemnization take place under an open canopy of heaven, or beneath an approved temporary tabernacle?*"

Lindy didn't hesitate. "Beneath an approved temporary tabernacle of heavy canvas, six cubits by eight, consecrated under the name of the Tabernacle of the Lowly Creek."

"It was an awning on a camper," Jesse said. "It had *Coleman* printed on the flap in white letters four inches high."

"The manufacturer's name is not relevant to the canonical form," Lindy said coldly. "Type."

Jesse placed his fingers on the round, glass-topped keys of the Underwood. He had not typed anything since his discharge papers from the Army Reserves in 1996.

He struck the letter *T*.

The keybar rose from the basket, struck the dried-out ribbon with a dry, wooden *clack*, and left a faint, charcoal-grey impression of a capital *T* whose top crossbar was slightly tilted.

"Lindy," he said, staring at the paper.

"Keep going," she said. "We have three hundred and ninety-seven more."

### Chapter 6

At two o’clock on Friday morning, the typewriter was still clacking.

The kitchen was cold now; the fire in the small cast-iron heating stove in the corner had burned down to a mound of grey ash and a few dull orange eyes of coal. The smell of lighter fluid, stale chicory, and hot ribbon grease hung heavy in the low-ceilinged room.

Jesse’s right index finger was swollen and sore from striking the stiff steel keys, and his lower back felt as if it had been clamped in a blacksmith's vise.

On the floor beside his stool were twenty-eight completed quarters of Form Q-4, each one bearing:
- The dates and license numbers of eight to twelve marriages;
- The names of *Arlo Vance, Deacon* and *William Cates, Deacon*, written in two different pens with two different hands (Lindy had signed Arlo’s name with an old Parker fountain pen she had dipped in blue-black ink, holding the pen with her left hand to produce a tremulous, arthritic spider-crawl; Toby had signed Billy’s name with a purple felt-tip marker he had found in the glove box of the road truck); and
- A certified declaration that each ceremony had been conducted under the *Order of the Lowly Creek*, using three verses from the Psalms, the Presentation of the Salt-Bread, and an offering consisting of *twelve ounces of wild honey or the equivalent thereof in seasonal produce.*

"Lindy," Jesse said, stopping with his hands suspended over the keyboard. "What did we put down for the Miller girl?"

Lindy was sitting on the floor with her back against the kitchen cabinets, her knees drawn up under her corduroy trousers, a thick stack of green vital statistics sheets balanced on her shins. Her face was pale with exhaustion, her eyes shadowed by dark, purple hollows.

"Second quarter of twenty-twenty-two," she said, her voice dry and gravelly. "Solemnized at the Lime Quarry overlook. Canonical form: *Order of the High Rocks*. Offering: two quarts of canned green beans."

"The Miller girl's mother gave me twenty dollars," Jesse said.

"The Miller girl's mother is on disability," Lindy said without looking up. "If we put down twenty dollars, Harlan Grote will want five percent of it, which is one dollar, and then he'll cross-reference it with the state welfare registry to see if she reported the expenditure. She didn't. Two quarts of beans is an agricultural tithe under Canon Twelve. He can't levy on beans."

Jesse rubbed his eyes. "What about the barn?"

Lindy went quiet.

The only sound in the house was the slow, rhythmic ticking of the wall clock and the wind whining around the corner of the eaves.

She laid the vital statistics sheets on the floor beside her. She reached into her wool sweater and pulled out a single sheet of paper, folded into three.

It was the original county marriage certificate for *Toby Alexander Gidley* and *Lindy Corinne Sells*, dated August 17, 2019.

"We have to do twenty-nineteen tomorrow," she said.

"Third quarter," Jesse said.

"Third quarter," she repeated. She unfolded the certificate. In the low yellow light of the kerosene lamp they had lit when the electric breaker tripped at midnight, the purple seal of Whetstone County looked dark, almost black, like an old bruise on the cream-colored bond paper.

"Look at the parcel description, Jesse," she said.

Jesse took the paper. He didn't need his glasses to read it; he knew the words by heart.

*PLACE OF SOLEMNIZATION: The Sells Family Homestead, Parcel 44-B, District 3, Whetstone County, Kentucky.*

"It doesn't say the barn," Jesse noted.

"No," she said. "It says Parcel 44-B. But look at what's written on the back."

Jesse turned the certificate over.

There, in Corinne Peake's sharp, elegant hand, was an endorsement written in purple ink that had faded slightly over five years:

*Returned and recorded in Marriage Book 41, page 112, this 19th day of August, 2019. Note: Solemnized in the historic timber-frame bank barn situated on the county line, pursuant to reciprocal recording agreement between Whetstone and Jackson Counties (1974).*

Jesse looked up at her. "Reciprocal recording agreement?"

"There isn't one," Lindy said.

Jesse stared at her. "What?"

"There isn't any reciprocal recording agreement," she said, her voice dropping into a flat, level whisper. "Toby spent three hours in the courthouse basement yesterday afternoon while Corinne was at lunch. He went through the county commissioners' minutes from nineteen-seventy to nineteen-eighty. There was a *proposal* for an agreement in nineteen-seventy-four, when they were trying to build the joint water plant. The Jackson County court voted it down three to two because they didn't want Whetstone people using their dump."

Jesse felt a cold bead of sweat run down his ribcage.

"Then why did Corinne write that?"

"Because Corinne wanted my mother to stop crying," Lindy said. "My mother was terrified that if the barn wasn't in Whetstone, the wedding wouldn't be in the *Whetstone Banner*, and if it wasn't in the *Banner*, her sister in Louisville wouldn't believe Toby had actually married me. Corinne just made it up, Jesse. She wrote it on the back so nobody in Frankfort would see it, stamped it with her seal, and put it in the drawer."

Jesse laid the certificate on the zinc top of the table.

He looked at the thirty-two pages of Form Q-4.

"So if Harlan Grote comes here," Jesse said, "he doesn't just catch me."

"He catches Corinne," Lindy said. "He catches thirty-one years of Corinne doing whatever she had to do to keep this county from falling apart over boundary lines and church fights. He catches the county attorney's daughter. He catches the sheriff's boat. He catches every deed Corinne ever patched with a purple stamp and a piece of Scotch tape."

She leaned her head back against the kitchen cabinets and closed her eyes.

"Toby wants to go to Terre Haute," she whispered.

Jesse went still. "Toby wants to go to Indiana?"

"He took the road department truck home tonight," she said. "He's got five hundred dollars in cash that he got from selling his four-wheeler to his cousin. He says he's going to find Suite 402 in that commercial concourse, find Harlan Grote, and give him the five hundred dollars to lose the file."

Jesse stood up from his milk-can stool. His knees made two loud, dry cracking sounds in the silent kitchen.

"You tell Toby," Jesse said, his voice rising with a sudden, hard authority that he hadn't used in twelve years, "that if he crosses that river in a county truck with road department plates and five hundred dollars in his pocket, he won't be running for road commissioner in November. He'll be doing three years in the federal penitentiary for interstate bribery of a bankruptcy officer."

Lindy opened her eyes. She looked at him through the dim light of the lamp, her face pale, her jaw set.

"Then what are we going to do, Jesse?"

Jesse reached out and picked up the black-bound Book of Common Shelter. He turned to page ninety-four—the page with the diagram of the four stakes, the four deacons, and the six cubits of unbleached linen.

"We have to give him the denomination," Jesse said.

Lindy frowned. "Give him the denomination?"

"The charter," Jesse said. He tapped the brittle paper with his thumb. "Article Seven, Section Three: *The Dissolution of the Provincial Body.* Did you read that part?"

"No," she said. "I stopped at the financial levies."

"Listen to this," Jesse said. He brought the book close to the lamp:

> *Section 3-C: The Right of Doctrinal Secession. If at any time a credentialed Presbyter shall determine that the General Presbytery has departed from the Primitive Simplicity of the Open Sky, whether by the erection of permanent structures, the accumulation of synodical debt, or the imposition of worldly legalisms upon the gathered saints, such Presbyter may, upon the concurrence of two Attendant Deacons and forty credentialed witnesses of the flock, convene a Council of Rectification under open air.*  
> *Upon the unanimous vote of such Council, the authority of the General Presbytery within that civil jurisdiction shall be declared null and void, and all ecclesiastical rights, registers, and records shall revert immediately and irrevocably to the Local Canopy as a sovereign and independent presbytery, beyond the oversight of any synod, receiver, or secular assignee.*

Lindy sat up straight. Her eyes widened.

"A Council of Rectification," she whispered.

"Forty witnesses," Jesse said. "And two deacons."

"Where are you going to get forty witnesses on a Saturday morning in October?"

Jesse walked to the kitchen window and looked out into the darkness. The rain was beginning to turn into sleet, ticking against the glass like small grains of sand.

"We have three hundred and ninety-eight marriages, Lindy," he said. "That's seven hundred and ninety-six people. And half of them have cousins."

### Chapter 7

Saturday morning broke with that hard, cold clarity that follows an autumn freeze. The sky over Whetstone County was the color of a new galvanized bucket, pale and sharp and utterly empty of clouds.

At nine o'clock, Toby Gidley’s county road truck—a six-ton International flatbed with a yellow flashing light on the cab—was parked across the entrance to the gravel pit on Route 18. Toby was standing on the running board, wearing his orange safety vest and his Clark County lime cap, directing traffic with an eighteen-inch stop paddle.

"Pull 'em in by the weigh-shack, Darrell!" he shouted toward the gate. "Don't block the scales! We got three loads of state gravel coming through at eleven!"

A battered blue Dodge Caravan with wood-grain vinyl peeling off the doors turned off the blacktop, its tailpipe scraping the ditch with a shower of sparks. Inside was Lonnie Fray, his wife Arlene, and five children eating glazed donuts out of a greasy paper box.

"Toby!" Lonnie yelled through the window. "What's Jesse doing? Uncle Creed said he was giving away free winter squash, but my mother said it was a lawsuit about the well."

"It's not squash and it's not a lawsuit," Toby yelled back, waving the paddle. "Just park behind the conveyor and don't let your young-uns near the screen plant!"

By nine-forty-five, there were eighty-four vehicles parked in the gravel pit: pickup trucks with dog boxes in the beds, rusted sedans with mismatched hubcaps, two tractors with rear carry-alls, a county school bus that had been retired in 1998 and converted into a mobile welding shop, and the sheriff's patrol car, which had its engine idling to keep the heater running.

Sheriff Wade Gidley was leaning against the front fender of his cruiser, drinking coffee from a thermos cup. He was fifty-eight, had a belly that hung three inches over his gold-plated belt buckle, and was wearing a quilted nylon jacket with *SHERIFF* printed across the back in reflective letters that had begun to crack.

Jesse Callow walked up to the cruiser. He was wearing his dark blue Sunday suit, his boots were polished with black paste, and under his arm he carried the black-bound Book of Common Shelter.

"Wade," Jesse said.

"Jesse," the sheriff replied. He took a sip of coffee, looked out over the eighty-four cars, and then looked at Jesse. "Corinne Peake called me at seven o'clock this morning. At home. Before I had my trousers on."

"I expect she did," Jesse said.

"She told me," the sheriff said, "that some fellow from Indiana was trying to take her drawers."

"Her records drawers, Wade."

"That's what she said when she calmed down," the sheriff said. "At first she just said 'drawers,' and I had my boots on before I realized she was talking about the marriage books. Now, Jesse, what's all this about a tent?"

"It's not a tent, Wade," Jesse said. "It's the Living Canopy."

"I don't care if it's the Waldorf Astoria," the sheriff said. "I've got twenty-two people here who are supposed to be working second shift at the parts plant. I've got my own brother-in-law down there in a Dodge flatbed that hasn't had an inspection sticker since the Clinton administration. If the state police come by and see this many rednecks in one gravel pit, they'll think it's a dog fight."

"It'll only take twenty minutes," Jesse said.

He walked past the cruiser to the center of the pit.

In the middle of the floor, where the gravel had been packed hard and grey by the passage of thirty-ton dump trucks, Toby had set up four orange plastic traffic cones.

Tied between the four cones, three feet off the ground, were four lengths of white nylon clothesline that Lindy had bought at the Dollar General that morning for two dollars and forty-nine cents a spool.

Standing at the four cones were:
- At the North Cone: Toby Gidley, holding an eighteen-inch steel crescent wrench;
- At the South Cone: Lindy Gidley, holding her green milk crate;
- At the East Cone: Billy Cates, wearing his road department jacket, looking profoundly nervous and holding a half-eaten sausage biscuit; and
- At the West Cone: Arlo Vance Junior, eighty-two years old, sitting in an aluminum lawn chair with a wool blanket over his knees, wearing his hearing aid and an orange hunting cap with the ear flaps pulled down.

Eighty-four people stood in a wide circle around the cones. They did not look like a church congregation; they looked like the people who waited outside the courthouse on the first Monday of the month to see whose farm was being sold for taxes. They stood with their hands in their pockets, their coat collars turned up against the cold wind that blew down from the ridge, their breath rising in pale, irregular puffs into the dry autumn air.

Jesse stepped inside the white nylon line.

He didn't use a microphone; he had spent twelve years speaking over the sound of wind in the hemlocks, over the roar of tractor engines in adjoining fields, and over the high, rhythmic barking of coonhounds tied to pickup bumpers.

"Friends," he said.

The circle went quiet. Even the children in the back of the Fray van stopped fighting over the last cruller.

"Twelve years ago," Jesse said, "I took an ordination from an outfit in Indiana because Arlene Fray wanted to marry Lonnie and didn't want anybody getting hit with a tire iron in the churchyard."

A low chuckle went through the crowd. Uncle Creed Fray, standing by a rusted grain truck, spat into the gravel and looked at his boots.

"Since that day," Jesse continued, "I've married three hundred and ninety-eight couples in this county. I married most of you. I married some of your brothers. I married Wade Gidley while he was sitting on an overturned rowboat because his knees were too bad to stand up in the mud."

"It was a good boat," the sheriff called out from the cruiser. "Didn't leak a drop."

"Now," Jesse said, holding up the black book, "the people who owned that ordination have gone bankrupt. They sold their papers to a collection man in Terre Haute. And that collection man has written to Corinne Peake, saying that because we didn't have two deacons, and because we didn't have four pieces of white linen six cubits long, and because we didn't pay twenty-two dollars a head into their office in Indiana, every marriage I ever did is irregular."

A sharp, collective intake of breath sounded around the circle.

Miss Eleanor Vance, the retired history teacher, pushed her way to the front of the crowd. She was wearing an old tweed overcoat and her hair was tied up in a blue scarf.

"Jesse Lee Callow," she said, her voice carrying the sharp, classroom crack that had terrified four decades of eighth-graders, "are you telling me that my marriage to Arthur is being questioned by a bankruptcy court in Posey County?"

"Yes, ma'am," Jesse said.

"Arthur has been dead three years," she snapped. "I'm drawing his civil service pension from the postal department. If those people in Indiana think they can touch Arthur's pension, they'd better bring more than a letter."

"They're not trying to touch his pension, Miss Eleanor," Jesse said gently. "They're trying to touch the county records. They want to come here with a subpoena, go into Corinne's vault, and declare that the authority was void from the start."

He opened the Book of Common Shelter.

"Under Article Seven of their own book," he said, reading the words clearly, "if the people of a county determine that the synod has departed from the truth, forty witnesses and two deacons can declare the local canopy independent. Once we do that, their charter in this state is dissolved. Their authority is finished. The records stay where they are, in Corinne's vault, under the protection of the Whetstone County Court."

He looked at Arlo Vance in his lawn chair.

"Deacon Vance," Jesse said, "do you concur?"

Arlo Vance looked around the circle. He adjusted his hearing aid, which gave a high, whistle-like squeak.

"I don't know what you're talking about, Jesse," the old man shouted in the deafening voice of the hard-of-hearing, "but if Creed Fray is against it, I'm for it!"

The crowd laughed, a loud, barking roar that echoed off the high limestone walls of the quarry.

"Deacon Cates," Jesse said, turning to Billy. "Do you concur?"

Billy swallowed his sausage biscuit. "Yes, sir. Toby said I get the Western blade."

"Now," Jesse said, looking out over the seventy-six men and women who stood along the nylon rope. "Under the law of the Living Canopy, forty witnesses must sign the register under the open sky."

Lindy Gidley stepped forward. From her milk crate, she produced a long, narrow roll of white paper—the adding machine tape from the Whetstone County Soil Conservation District.

Attached to the top of the tape was a piece of cardboard on which Jesse had typed forty-eight words on the Underwood typewriter that morning:

*WE, THE UNDERSIGNED WITNESSES OF THE CANOPY OF WHETSTONE COUNTY, HEREBY DECLARE OURSELVES FREE AND INDEPENDENT OF ALL SYNODS, COURTS, RECEIVERS, AND TOWNS IN INDIANA; AND WE DO AFFIRM THAT THE MARRIAGES HERETOFORE SOLEMNIZED AMONG US WERE PERFORMED UNDER GOD’S SKY AND CORINNE PEAKE’S STAMP, WHICH IS SUFFICIENT FOR US AND OUGHT TO BE SUFFICIENT FOR ANYBODY ELSE.*

Lindy laid the tape across the hood of the sheriff's patrol car. She took three pens from her apron and laid them beside it.

Sheriff Wade Gidley was the first to sign. He didn't take off his nylon gloves; he simply pressed his thumb against the paper to steady it and signed *W. T. Gidley, High Sheriff*, in bold, blue strokes that cut through the paper.

Miss Eleanor Vance signed second.

Uncle Creed Fray signed third, leaning heavily on the hood with both hands and writing his name in the slow, painful block letters of a man who only used a pen when selling timber.

By ten-fifteen, the tape was seven feet long, covered with eighty-four signatures that ran off the paper and onto the grey paint of the sheriff's cruiser.

### Chapter 8

On Tuesday morning, November 4, the grey Greyhound bus from Louisville pulled into the parking lot of the Sunoco station in Meacham with a loud hiss of its air brakes.

A single passenger got off.

He was a man of about forty-five, wearing a charcoal-grey topcoat that was too light for the upland wind, carrying a black leather catalog case with brass combination locks and a small overnight bag of brown ballistic nylon. He had a pale, thin face, steel-rimmed spectacles, and the careful, slightly defensive posture of an accountant who expected people to be angry with him and was prepared to explain why it was their own fault.

He walked into the Sunoco station, bought a pack of sugar-free spearmint gum and a road map of central Kentucky, and asked the boy behind the counter where he could find the County Registry.

"Up the hill," the boy said, not looking up from a magazine about four-wheel-drive transmissions. "Big stone building with the soldier on the grass. You can't miss it; it's the only one that doesn't have a grain elevator attached."

The man walked up the hill. His shoes were thin-soled black oxfords that had not been designed for cracked limestone sidewalks covered with wet cedar needles. By the time he reached the second floor of the courthouse, his feet were numb with cold and the hem of his grey topcoat was damp with river mist.

He pushed open the frosted-glass door of the Registry.

The small brass bell gave its tinny *clink*.

Corinne Peake was sitting behind the slate counter. She had a fresh tin of menthol lozenges on her desk, she was wearing her magenta cardigan with the strawberry buttons, and she was currently using a heavy pair of steel shears to trim the margins off an auctioneer's bond.

The man set his black catalog case on the counter. He took off his spectacles, wiped the mist from the lenses with a white linen handkerchief, and replaced them.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was flat, precise, with that dry, northern nasality that belongs to the Wabash valley. "My name is Harlan Grote. I am the Auditor-in-Mission for the Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy."

Corinne did not lay down her shears. She cut through three more inches of heavy parchment, the steel blades making a crisp, rhythmic *crunch... crunch... crunch*.

"We don't buy from peddlers," she said.

Grote did not blink. He opened his catalog case, spun the brass tumblers, and produced a blue plastic folder containing a certified copy of his appointment from the Chancery Court in Evansville.

"I am not a peddler, madam," he said. "I am here under the authority of Section 14-B of the Fellowship Canons and an order of the Third Chancery District. I have delivered formal notice to Presbyter Jesse Callow regarding thirty-two delinquent quarterly returns. Having received no canonical filing, I am here to examine the original marriage folios registered under his ministerial number."

Corinne stopped her shears.

She laid them on the counter, exactly two inches from his blue folder.

Then she reached into her desk drawer and pulled out a long, narrow envelope of heavy buff paper, sealed with three large dollops of purple sealing wax that had been stamped with the circular die of the Whetstone County Court.

"You're too late, Mr. Grote," she said.

Grote looked at the envelope. "Too late for what?"

"The Living Canopy," she said, leaning her arms on the slate counter, "was dissolved in this county at ten o'clock on Saturday morning."

Grote’s thin mouth tightened into a straight line. "A local church cannot dissolve a hierarchical body, madam. That is a fundamental principle of ecclesiastical corporation law."

"It wasn't a local church," Corinne said. "It was a Council of Rectification. Duly convened under Article Seven, Section Three of your own book."

She tapped the buff envelope with her shears.

"Inside this envelope," she said, "is a certified copy of the minutes of the Council, signed by two ordained deacons and eighty-four qualified witnesses under the open sky. Including the High Sheriff, the County Judge-Executive, the President of the Farm Bureau, and the lady who taught seventh-grade history to every lawyer within fifty miles of this courthouse."

Grote stared at the purple wax. A tiny twitch began to flutter in his left eyelid.

"A Council of Rectification," he repeated, his voice rising slightly. "That section was superseded by the Muncie Accord of twenty-twenty-four."

"It wasn't superseded in Kentucky," Corinne said. "The Muncie Accord was never registered with the Secretary of State in Frankfort. I called the corporation division on Monday morning. A very nice boy named Darrell looked it up for me while I was having my tea. He says as far as the Commonwealth of Kentucky is concerned, the Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy is an uncertificated foreign trust that hasn't paid its twelve-dollar annual filing fee since nineteen-ninety-eight."

She reached out and pushed the buff envelope two inches closer to his catalog case.

"And under the law of this Commonwealth," she continued, her watery blue eyes locking onto his with the steady, unblinking glare of a hawk on a fence post, "an uncertificated foreign trust that attempts to alter the public records of a county is guilty of a Class D felony, punishable by one to five years in the state reformatory at LaGrange."

Grote looked at the envelope. Then he looked at Corinne.

Then he looked at the heavy olive-drab steel door of the vault behind her, where three hundred and ninety-eight marriage folders were resting quietly in their green drawers, wrapped in their red cloth ribbons, protected by seventy years of limestone dust and a seventy-four-year-old woman who had not made an error in her ledger since the Eisenhower administration.

"Madam," Grote said, his voice dropping into a dry whisper, "who signed as deacons?"

Corinne smiled. It was the first time Jesse had ever heard of her smiling in the office, and the effect was terrifying.

"Deacon Arlo Vance," she said. "And Deacon William Cates."

Grote reached into his coat pocket, pulled out a small black notebook, and flipped through the pages.

"William Cates," he said, reading from a list. "Registered nineteen-fourteen?"

"Nineteen-twenty-four," Corinne said smoothly. "His grandson. The succession was apostolic, Mr. Grote. It was done under a camper awning."

Grote stood still for a long time.

Outside, the courthouse clock tower began to strike eleven. The four faces each struck at a slightly different interval—one fast and tinny, one slow and deep, two somewhere in between—so the sound rolled through the high limestone rooms in a long, discordant tumble of bells that seemed to shake the dust from the transoms.

When the last chime died away, Grote slowly closed his catalog case. He spun the brass combination locks until the numbers were scrambled.

"The court in Evansville," he said, "will not be pleased."

"The court in Evansville," Corinne said, picking up her steel shears again, "is in Indiana. If they want to see what our marriages look like, tell them to come down to the high school gymnasium on the fourth of July. We'll give them a paper plate of barbecue and let them watch Toby Gidley dance with his wife."

Grote picked up his case.

He turned toward the door, his thin leather shoes squeaking softly on the green linoleum.

He reached for the brass knob, stopped, and looked back over his shoulder.

"The barn," he said.

Corinne did not look up from her work. *Crunch... crunch... crunch.*

"What barn, Mr. Grote?"

"The Gidley wedding," Grote said. "The certificate said Parcel 44-B. The county line runs through forty-four-B."

Corinne laid her shears down. She looked at him through the bottom half of her bifocals, her expression as flat and empty as a limestone quarry on Sunday morning.

"Mr. Grote," she said, "in Whetstone County, we measure our land from the middle of the road to the top of the ridge. And we measure our marriages from the day they start until the day we put them in the churchyard. If you think you have a machine in Indiana that can measure closer than that, you go right ahead and bring it down here."

She pointed with the tip of her shears toward the frosted-glass door.

"The bus leaves at twelve-ten," she said. "If you hurry, you can get a hot ham sandwich at the Sunoco before it pulls out."

### Chapter 9

On Sunday afternoon, Jesse Callow drove his green Dodge Ram out to the Gidley place.

The weather had turned warm again—that brief, golden return of summer that the uplands call the false season, when the sun hangs low and yellow over the cedar knobs and the air smells of warm limestone and sweet clover.

Toby and Lindy were sitting on the front porch of the cottage, watching the two little girls chase the border collie through the dead grass of the yard.

Toby had a cold bottle of ale in his hand; Lindy was holding a bowl of winter apples, paring the red skins with an old paring knife with a wooden handle held together with copper wire.

Jesse parked the truck by the elderberry bush. He didn't turn the engine off immediately; he let it idle for a minute, listening to the loose valve lifter ticking under the hood like an old watch that had lost its balance wheel.

Then he cut the switch and climbed down.

"Jesse," Toby called from the porch. "Come on up here. Lindy made a dried-apple stack cake yesterday that'll make you forget every typewriter in the state."

Jesse walked up the three wooden steps and sat down on the top rail of the porch, leaning his back against the square pine post.

Lindy handed him a slice of apple on the point of her knife. It was crisp, white, with that sharp, wine-like flavor that only comes from old trees growing in rocky soil.

"Corinne called," she said.

"I know," Jesse said. "She came by the shop on her way home on Friday. Brought me a half-dozen menthol lozenges and told me my pull-cord on the drill press was frayed."

"Did he get on the bus?" Toby asked.

"He got on the bus," Jesse said. "The boy at the Sunoco said he bought three ham sandwiches and a bottle of ginger ale and sat in the back row with his briefcase on his knees all the way to the river."

Toby took a sip of his ale. He looked out over the yard, past the crushed-stone pad where the barn had been, to the rolling yellow pastures of the creek bottom.

"Jesse," Toby said, "what are you going to do when somebody else wants to get married?"

Jesse looked down at his boots. They were covered in yellow limestone dust from the workshop path, the leather cracked across the toes where he had knelt on the gravel of eighty different fields.

He thought of the three hundred and ninety-eight folders in Corinne’s drawer.

He thought of Donnie Cleary's pink stump, and the history teacher's veil sailing out over the gorge, and the boy who had dropped the ring down the intake vent of the lime kiln.

He thought of the black-bound Book of Common Shelter, which was now sitting on the bottom shelf of his workbench in the workshop, propping up a broken box of number-six wood screws.

"Well," Jesse said, chewing slowly on his piece of apple, "I went up to the registry on Friday afternoon. After Grote left."

Lindy stopped her knife. "What did you do, Jesse?"

"Corinne gave me seven dollars out of her own purse," he said. "She took that big leather ledger down from the top shelf—the one they use for the auctioneers and the embalmers—and she turned to a clean page in the back."

He reached into his shirt pocket and pulled out a small slip of white paper, three inches square, stamped with the heavy circular die of the Whetstone County Court.

Written across the face of the paper, in Corinne Peake's sharp, upright hand, were eight words:

*THE INDEPENDENT CANOPY OF WHETSTONE COUNTY.*  
*Rev. Jesse L. Callow, Presbyter-at-Large.*

"What does the canon say?" Lindy asked, a small smile touching the corners of her mouth.

Jesse looked out over the pastures, where the low autumn sun was turning the tops of the fescue into a sea of pale, reddish gold, stretching all the way to the blue ridge of the knobs under a sky that had no beginning, no end, and not a single rafter to keep the rain off the gathered saints.

"The canon says," Jesse murmured, "that the sky is sufficient. And if it rains, people ought to have the good sense to stand under an umbrella."
### Chapter 10

The false autumn died on Monday night with a sharp, iron-colored frost that froze the mud in the ruts of State Route 18 until the gravel sounded like broken crockery beneath the tires of Jesse’s truck.

At seven on Tuesday morning, Jesse was in his workshop with the kerosene heater glowing a sullen, cherry red between two galvanized washtubs. He was fitting a new phosphor-bronze bushing into the escapement arbor of an Ansonia mantle clock that had stopped ticking when the McKinley administration was young, using a three-cornered scraper he had ground down from an old file. It was delicate work, the kind that required a man to hold his breath between heartbeats so the hand wouldn't shake.

The door of the shop opened without a knock.

It was not Lindy Gidley, and it was not Toby. It was a young man of about twenty-four, wearing an olive-drab nylon quilted jacket with the sleeves torn at the elbows, a knitted cap pulled down to his eyebrows, and a pair of yellow leather work gloves stained black with fifth-wheel grease. In his right hand he carried an envelope made of heavy, blue-tinted plastic, sealed with a red security strip that had a barcode three inches long across the flap.

"Jesse Callow?" the boy asked. He was chewing a corner of his lower lip and his nose was running from the cold.

"I’m Jesse," Jesse said, not putting down the scraper.

The boy laid the blue plastic envelope on the zinc-topped bench, right between the clock movement and a saucer filled with kerosene and screwheads.

"Sign line three," he said, handing over an electronic clipboard with a glass screen that had three long diagonal cracks running from corner to corner. "Courier from Bowling Green. The fellow at the bus depot gave me forty dollars cash to bring it out here before eight o'clock."

"Who was the fellow?" Jesse asked.

"Skinny man," the boy said. "Had on two overcoats and shoes that looked like church shoes. Smelled like peppermint and dry paper. He had three more of these envelopes for the courthouse."

Jesse signed the glass with a plastic stylus that felt like an old nail. The screen blinked twice, gave a feeble, flat beep, and died.

"Appreciate it," the boy said. He took the clipboard, turned around, and walked out into the frost, his boot-heels crunching on the frozen weeds of the path. A minute later, an old Pontiac with a rusted-out muffler barked to life down by the road, backfired once with the sharp *crack* of a deer rifle, and rattled away toward the river.

Jesse looked at the blue envelope.

The return address was not Terre Haute. It was printed on a white adhesive label:

*LAW OFFICES OF STANTZ, POFFENBERGER & CROOKS*  
*Special Counsel to the Receiver in Equity*  
*Vanderburgh Superior Court, In Bankruptcy*  
*Old National Bank Tower, Suite 1100*  
*Evansville, Indiana 47708*

Inside was not Form Q-4. Inside were fourteen pages of legal-cap paper, stapled down the left margin with three blue eyelets, titled in bold black capital letters:

*IN RE: THE TRUST ESTATE OF THE APOSTOLIC FELLOWSHIP OF THE LIVING CANOPY (A RELIGIOUS AND BENEVOLENT CORPORATION)*  
*CAUSE NO. 82-D01-2409-CB-004412*

> *EMERGENCY MOTION FOR AN ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE WHY THE SO-CALLED 'COUNCIL OF RECTIFICATION' OF WHETSTONE COUNTY, KENTUCKY, SHOULD NOT BE ADJUDGED A VOID FRAUDULENT CONVEYANCE UNDER 11 U.S.C. § 548 AND AN UNLAWFUL USURPATION OF DEBTOR'S FRANCHISE ASSETS; AND FOR AN INJUNCTION RESTRAINING THE REGISTRAR OF DEEDS FROM DESTROYING, SECRETEING, OR ALTERING THE SACRAMENTAL FOLIOS.*

Jesse turned the page.

> *COMES NOW Donald G. Stantz, Receiver in Equity for the Debtor, by counsel, and respectfully shows unto the Court:*  
> *1. That on November 4, 2024, an Auditor-in-Mission dispatched by this Receiver to the County of Whetstone, Commonwealth of Kentucky, was met with contumacious resistance, physical intimidation by an armed county sheriff, and a purported 'Secession of the Flock' executed under an obsolete, unratified draft canon from the year 2004;*  
> *2. That under the laws of the Commonwealth of Kentucky and the United States Bankruptcy Code, the solemnization franchise granted to credentialed officiants constitutes an intangible asset of the Debtor estate, carrying an accrued receivable value of not less than $22.00 per registered union, together with equitable reversionary liens upon all commodities, hams, honey, and secular gratuities rendered in consideration thereof;*  
> *3. That the purported 'Independent Canopy of Whetstone County' is an unincorporated confederacy possessing neither tax-exempt status, a certified corporate seal, nor an approved Book of Forms;*  
> *4. That unless restrained by order of this Court, said Jesse L. Callow intends to persist in the performance of irregular, unlicensed, and unbonded nuptial rites, thereby creating an intolerable cloud upon the civil status of hundreds of citizens and rendering the franchise assets of this estate unmarketable to prospective purchasers.*

Jesse read paragraph four three times.

*Prospective purchasers.*

He turned to the back of the packet. Attached as Exhibit D was a two-page printed flyer on yellow glossy paper, folded twice:

*PRELIMINARY PROSPECTUS FOR ASSET SALE: CHATTEL & INTANGIBLES OF THE LIVING CANOPY TRUST.*  
*Lot 4: 1,420 Active Ordination Franchises in Kentucky, Tennessee, and Southern Indiana, together with Historical Solemnization Registers, Mailing Lists of 8,400 Affiliated Rural Households, and the Registered Copyright to the 1998 Book of Common Shelter (Third Edition).*  
*Minimum Stalking-Horse Bid: $45,000.*  
*Qualified bidders must be registered non-profit entities, commercial wedding chapel operators, or genealogical research syndicates.*

Jesse let the paper drop onto the zinc bench.

They were not trying to save the denomination. They were trying to sell it.

And the only thing the denomination owned that had any value was the four hundred names written in Corinne Peake’s green steel drawers. A commercial wedding chapel company from Nashville, or an online genealogy outfit from Salt Lake City that wanted to charge families nineteen dollars a month to look up where their grandparents had stood in a clover field, could buy the list for forty-five thousand dollars, hire three young lawyers in Bowling Green, and send a certified bill for twelve years of back franchise fees to every bride in Whetstone County.

The shop door banged open again.

This time it was Toby Gidley. He had not taken off his road department vest, his boots were covered in wet grey stone dust, and his face was the pale, yellowish color of lard that had been left in the skillet too long.

"Jesse," Toby said, his voice whistling through his teeth. "Claggett is at the barn."

### Chapter 11

Lyle Claggett, the County Attorney of Jackson County, was thirty-four years old and had never had limestone mud on his trousers in his entire life.

He was the son of a man who owned three John Deere dealerships and four hundred acres of bottomland along the Cumberland River, and he had graduated eighth in his class at the University of Kentucky College of Law. He drove a pearl-white Ford F-150 King Ranch edition with heated leather seats, wore gold-rimmed aviator spectacles that adjusted automatically to the sunlight, and kept an immaculate cedar shoe-tree in his gym bag at all times.

When Jesse and Toby pulled up in the green Dodge, Claggett was standing in the middle of the crushed-stone pad where Lindy’s grandfather’s barn had been.

Beside him were two men in orange surveyor's jackets from the Jackson County Highway Department. They had set up a yellow tripod with a digital total-station laser transit directly over an iron pin driven into the ground, and one of them was holding a ten-foot striped aluminum prism rod against a corner post of the new pole shed.

Lindy Gidley was standing five feet from Claggett. She was wearing her grey canvas apron, her hands were shoved deep into the front pocket, and she was watching the laser transit with the expression of an owl observing a mouse that had wandered onto a clean board.

"Morning, Toby," Claggett said as Toby got out of the Dodge. His voice was smooth, resonant, the trained voice of a young man who had won two moot-court competitions on constitutional tax exemptions. "Morning, Mr. Callow."

"Lyle," Toby said. He didn't offer to shake hands. "What are you doing with a total station on my stone?"

"It’s not your stone, Toby," Claggett said mildly, tapping the side of his boot with a twenty-five-foot steel measuring tape in an orange plastic case. "That’s Jackson County Highway Right-of-Way Pin Number 412. It was set by the state road commission in nineteen-sixty-eight when they re-aligned the bridge approach."

He pointed with the tape toward the empty gravel rectangle.

"We had a communication yesterday afternoon," Claggett said. "From a legal firm in Evansville. Stantz and Poffenberger. They’re representing the federal receiver for an ecclesiastical entity that holds an adverse claim against certain unrecorded municipal franchises."

"Lyle," Lindy said, her voice dropping into a dangerous, gravelly purr, "don't talk to Toby in Latin. He's had two hours of sleep and he's got a three-quarter-inch breaker bar under the seat of that truck."

Claggett smiled, though his eyes behind the gold rims shifted half an inch toward the cab of the Dodge.

"I’m not threatening anybody, Lindy," he said smoothly. "I’m just establishing the facts. The Jackson County Board of Elections met in special session at eight o'clock this morning. Mr. Stantz has filed a formal Notice of Boundary Defect regarding the voter registration of Toby Alexander Gidley."

Toby took two steps forward. The stone dust on his boots puffed into the cold air like smoke.

"My voter registration?" Toby barked. "I’ve voted in Whetstone District Three since I was eighteen years old! My granddaddy voted here! My great-granddaddy is buried three hundred yards up that hollow in a graveyard with a Whetstone County historical marker on the gate!"

"Your great-grandfather was a fine man, Toby," Claggett said, not backing up an inch. "He was also a magistrate in Jackson County for eight years before the line was surveyed. The problem isn't where he’s buried. The problem is where you sleep."

He took a sheet of heavy white vellum from a leather portfolio one of the surveyors was holding.

"This is the official tax parcel map for Jackson County, revised nineteen-ninety-four," Claggett said, holding it so Jesse could see the red line that slashed diagonally across the creek bottom. "The line runs from the sycamore at the ford, north forty-two degrees east, through the center of the old bank barn. The house is forty feet inside Whetstone. We don't contest the house. But the barn—the structure where the wedding took place—was fifty-eight percent within the bounds of Jackson County."

He folded the vellum with a crisp, dry snap.

"Under Kentucky Revised Statutes Section 402.050," Claggett said, "an officiant who is registered only in Whetstone County has no statutory authority to solemnize a marriage within the territorial limits of Jackson County without a special provincial endorsement from the circuit judge. Mr. Callow never applied for an endorsement. He never paid the fifteen-dollar non-resident recording fee. Therefore, the ceremony performed on August 17, 2019, was an extraterritorial nullity."

"Extraterritorial nullity," Lindy repeated. She took her hands out of her apron. In her right hand was a small, three-ring binder with a black plastic cover, labeled *SOIL SURVEY: WHETSTONE-JACKSON TRANSECT (1981).*

"Lyle," she said, stepping within two feet of his King Ranch jacket. "Do you know what kind of dirt you’re standing on?"

Claggett blinked. "It’s crushed limestone, Lindy. Number two stone."

"Underneath the stone," she said. "The natural soil series."

"I have no idea," he said, frowning slightly. "It’s bottomland."

"It’s not bottomland," she said. "It’s an Allensville silt loam, karst phase, underlain by Saint Louis limestone. And do you know what Saint Louis limestone does when you put two thousand tons of hay on top of it for sixty years?"

Claggett looked at the two surveyors. The surveyors looked down at their boots.

"It sinks," Lindy said. "It forms a doline. A collapse feature. In nineteen-fifty-two, when my grandfather built that barn, the north wall was three feet from the sinkhole. In nineteen-seventy-eight, after the blizzard, the sinkhole dropped eleven feet. The whole north bay slid four feet to the south-southwest. My daddy had to put three turnbuckles and two lengths of oilfield drill pipe under the sill to keep the rafters from pulling out of the plates."

She opened her binder and pointed to an aerial photograph taken by the Department of Agriculture in 1982.

"The barn moved, Lyle," she said. Her voice was as cold and steady as the creek water. "The wood stayed together because it was mortised with white oak trunnels, but the foundation slid. In nineteen-eighty-five, it slid another two feet. By the time Jesse stood under that hay fork in twenty-nineteen, the entire north wall of that barn had drifted five feet and eight inches into Whetstone County."

Claggett stared at the aerial photograph. A tiny red spot appeared on his left cheek, just above his close-trimmed beard.

"A building cannot alter a statutory boundary by sliding, Lindy," he said, but his voice had lost its courtroom cadence. "The line is fixed in the heavens."

"The line is fixed by monuments on the ground," Jesse said, speaking for the first time.

Claggett turned to him. "What monuments?"

Jesse pointed with the tip of his scraper toward the brushy fence row that ran down to the creek, where an ancient, hollow sycamore stood surrounded by briars and dead ironweed.

"The corner monument," Jesse said, "was a white oak with three hacks on the south face and a horse-shoe driven into the crotch. Captain Trumbo put it there in eighteen-seventy-four. My daddy showed it to me when I was nine years old. He told me that if anybody ever moved that horse-shoe, the county line would jump forty rods to the west and the Baptist church would have to pay taxes to the Jackson County road fund."

Claggett took off his aviator glasses. He wiped the bridge with his thumb.

"Where is the tree, Mr. Callow?"

"The tree," Jesse said, "died of the blight in nineteen-eighty-four. But the stump is down there under those blackberry bushes. And the horse-shoe is still in it. It’s an eight-inch draft shoe, Toby. Cut out of three-quarter-inch flat bar by your great-uncle Silas."

Toby didn't wait for Claggett to answer. He turned on his heel, walked to the Dodge, reached under the seat, and pulled out an eight-pound mattock with a hickory handle that had been taped with black friction tape.

"Come on, Billy," Toby yelled to one of the surveyors, who happened to be Billy Cates’s third cousin. "Bring that prism rod down here and let's see whose dirt we're digging in."

### Chapter 12

By five o'clock that evening, the Registry of Deeds on the second floor of the courthouse had the atmosphere of a regimental headquarters before a dawn bombardment.

The green steel door of the vault was propped wide open with an eight-pound pig of lead that had been used as a paperweight by Corinne’s grandfather.

Spread across the slate counter were:
- A twelve-foot roll of tracing vellum showing the original 1874 survey of the Whetstone-Jackson line, drawn in brown gall-nut ink that had faded to the color of dry tobacco, with Captain Trumbo’s handwritten notes in the margins detailing how many drinks of rye whiskey his chainmen had consumed before reaching the limestone sink;
- Three boxes of chocolate-covered mint creams that Jesse had bought at the Rexall to keep Corinne’s blood sugar from dropping;
- A six-pack of cream soda in glass bottles; and
- A certified copy of the bankruptcy summons from Evansville, which Corinne had pinned to her bulletin board with a brass thumb-tack directly beneath a photograph of her late cat, Buster.

Corinne was sitting at her desk with her magnifying glass in one hand and an electric hot plate in the other, boiling a small copper saucepan of water to make instant sanka.

"Drink your soda, Toby," she said without turning around. "You look like a ghost that’s been run through an ensilage blower."

Toby was sitting on a wooden bench against the wall, his mattock leaning between his knees. His forearms and his jeans were caked with black creek mud, and he had a four-inch scratch on his cheek from the blackberry briers.

On the slate counter beside the survey map sat the fruit of three hours of digging in the fence row: a chunk of rotted white oak heartwood about the size of a ham, through which was driven a rusted, hand-forged horseshoe with three square nail heads still visible in the crease.

"It’s Trumbo’s shoe," Toby said for the fourth time. "I compared it with the ledger in the historical society. Uncle Silas charged the county two dollars and forty cents for three boundary shoes on July 9, eighteen-seventy-four. It’s right there in the commissioners' vouchers."

"We know it’s the shoe, Toby," Lindy said. She was sitting at the typist’s table, her additions machine clicking away like a cicada in a locust tree. "The problem isn't the shoe. The problem is Captain Trumbo."

Jesse leaned over the vellum map. "What's wrong with Captain Trumbo?"

"Captain Trumbo was a surveyor who used a thirty-three-foot Gunter's chain," she said, holding up a page of yellowed calculations. "A Gunter's chain has one hundred links. Each link is seven point nine two inches. But Captain Trumbo's diary—which the historical society has in that glass case under the Confederate flag—says that on the third day of the survey, his chainman dropped the chain into a crevice in the lime rocks and they had to mend it with a piece of telegraph wire."

Jesse looked at her. "Telegraph wire?"

"Telegraph wire doesn't have links, Jesse," she said. "It stretches. By the time they reached the sycamore at the creek, Trumbo’s chain was thirty-four feet and two inches long. That means every mile he surveyed in eighteen-seventy-four was one hundred and eighty-six feet longer than a statutory mile."

Corinne set her copper saucepan on the blotter with a sharp, metallic *clink*.

"Which means," Corinne said, her voice dry as parchment, "that if you measure from the courthouse square out to the Gidley farm using Trumbo’s chain, the county line isn't in the barn. It isn't in the fence row. It’s forty rods east, right through the middle of Lyle Claggett’s daddy's silage pit."

Toby sat up. The mattock slipped from between his knees and hit the floor with a dull, iron thud.

"Lyle’s daddy's silage pit?" Toby whispered.

"The whole farm," Corinne said. "Eighty acres of Claggett bottomland that’s been paying taxes to Jackson County at the low agricultural rate since nineteen-twelve ought to have been paying the Whetstone road levy. I looked at the old tax rolls while you boys were playing in the mud. Lyle's daddy owes this county seventy-four thousand dollars in back bridge taxes."

She took a sip of her Sanka, closed her eyes with an expression of grim satisfaction, and reached for a mint cream.

"Now," she said, looking at Jesse over her spectacles, "what are we going to do about Evansville?"

Jesse reached into his coat pocket. He pulled out the fourteen-page summons from Stantz, Poffenberger & Crooks.

"Stantz doesn't care about the boundary, Corinne," Jesse said. "He wants the forty-five thousand dollars. He's got a bidder."

"Who's the bidder?" Lindy asked.

"A company called *Covenant Chapels of America, Inc.*," Jesse said, reading from the prospectus. "Registered in Pigeon Forge, Tennessee. They operate seven drive-through wedding pavilions in the Smoky Mountains. They want the Living Canopy charter because it has an exemption from the three-day waiting period under the Tennessee religious freedom statute. If they buy the trust assets from the bankruptcy court, they get the right to audit every minister who ever held a license to establish their chain of title."

Corinne stood up. She walked into the vault, her sensible shoes clicking on the concrete, and came out holding a thick, grey folio bound in buckram, labeled *MINISTERIAL LICENSES: 1900–1950.*

She laid it on the counter with a cloud of ancient dust that made Toby sneeze.

"They won't buy it," she said.

"Why won't they buy it?" Jesse asked.

"Because the Living Canopy doesn't own the charter," she said. She turned the pages with her index finger until she reached the year 1928.

There, pasted to a page of blue legal paper, was a printed certificate with an ornate border of laurel leaves and oil lamps, heading:

*THE INDEPENDENT APOSTOLIC ASSEMBLY OF THE OPEN AIR AND CANOPY OF KENTUCKY.*  
*Chartered by Special Act of the General Assembly, Approved March 14, 1928.*

"Reverend Morling didn't invent that website in nineteen-ninety-eight, Jesse," Corinne said, tapping the blue paper with her pencil. "His father was an itinerant evangelist who worked out of a tent in the knobs during the Depression. He registered the Kentucky charter right here in this room with my uncle, who was clerk then. Under the terms of the nineteen-twenty-eight Act, the charter cannot be sold, transferred, assigned, or liquidated by any court outside the Commonwealth of Kentucky. And if the trustees fail to hold a triennial meeting under the open sky, the entire corporate franchise reverts to the county court of the county where it was first recorded."

Jesse stared at the blue paper. "Where was it first recorded, Corinne?"

Corinne took off her glasses. She pointed with the earpiece toward the floor beneath their feet.

"Right here," she said. "In Whetstone County. Seat of justice, Meacham. The original seal is in that bottom drawer under the notary embossers. It’s made of solid brass and weighs four pounds."

### Chapter 13

The news that Jesse Callow was the Presbyter-at-Large of the only legally unassailable, non-transferable, depression-era open-air wedding franchise in the state of Kentucky did not stay in the courthouse.

In Whetstone County, information did not travel by telephone or newspaper; it traveled through the parts department of the tractor dealership, the line at the drive-through window of the Farmers & Traders Bank, and the three men who sat on overturned salt licks behind the stockyard sale ring every Thursday morning.

By Thursday noon, Jesse’s gravel track was blocked by six pickup trucks, a Buick LeSabre with a damaged rear quarter-panel, and a horse trailer carrying two miniature donkeys.

Jesse was at his drill press, trying to bore a five-sixteenths hole through a piece of flat strap iron for a hay rake, when the first couple appeared at the door.

They were not young. The man was about sixty, wearing a clean pair of Big Mac overalls with the legs rolled up two inches, a blue chambray shirt buttoned to the collar, and a pair of brand-new work shoes that squeaked like a pig in a gate. His name was Virgil Poteet; he had run a limestone lime-spreader for thirty years until his hip gave out, and he had been a widower since the year of the ice storm.

Beside him was Mrs. Bernice Goforth. She was fifty-eight, had hair the color of an aluminum ladder curled into tight, fierce waves, and was clutching an enormous black patent-leather handbag to her stomach like a breastplate.

"Jesse," Virgil said, taking off his hat and holding it against his thighs with both hands. "We heard you were independent."

Jesse shut off the drill press. The motor coasted to a stop with a long, whistling sigh.

"I’m independent, Virgil," Jesse said carefully. "I’m so independent the federal court in Indiana is trying to put me in jail."

"That don't matter," Bernice said, stepping forward. Her patent-leather handbag creaked as she shifted her grip. "Virgil’s daughter has been telling everybody at the sewing circle that if we get married, she’s going to have the circuit court put an administrator over Virgil's farm because he's not in his right mind."

"I am in my right mind," Virgil said mildly. "I just don't like eating canned soup by myself in that kitchen."

"She told the preacher at the Baptist church not to touch us," Bernice said. "Told him Virgil had memory lapses. Virgil doesn't have memory lapses. He just forgets where he puts his reading glasses, which he’s done since he was twenty."

"I found them," Virgil noted. "They were in the flour bin."

"We want to be married today," Bernice said. "Right now. Before she gets home from the bank in Meacham. We got the license from Corinne an hour ago. Corinne told us to come out here and tell you to use the nineteen-twenty-eight words."

Jesse took his wiping rag from his pocket and slowly cleaned the grease from his fingers.

"Virgil," he said, "where are your witnesses?"

Virgil pointed with his hat toward the track. "I got Arlo Vance’s boy in the truck. And that fellow who sweeps out the barbershop."

"Arlo’s boy is seventy-four, Virgil," Jesse said.

"He can still sign his name if you hold the paper," Virgil said. "And Bernice brought her sister from the nursing home."

Jesse looked out the window. Sitting in the back seat of the Buick LeSabre was an elderly woman with an oxygen tank on the floor between her knees, wearing a lavender hat with three plastic cherries pinned to the brim, waving cheerfully at a stray cat that was sniffing the front tire.

"We have to do it outside," Jesse said.

"It’s twenty-eight degrees, Jesse," Virgil said, shivering slightly in his overalls.

"The charter says the open sky, Virgil," Jesse said. "Section Three of the Act of nineteen-twenty-eight. If we do it under a tin roof, the receiver in Evansville will have us all in the federal building before sundown."

Ten minutes later, the wedding was solemnized beside Jesse’s woodpile.

The wind was coming out of the north-northwest, carrying small, hard specks of sleet that stung the face like birdshot.

Jesse stood on two cedar logs to keep his shoes out of the frozen slush, his dark suit buttoned to the chin, holding the 1928 Book of Common Shelter with both hands while Lindy Gidley, who had arrived with a clipboard and two thermoses of hot cider, held an enormous black umbrella over his head.

Virgil and Bernice stood before him on a piece of cardboard from an electric range box.

"Virgil," Jesse said, his voice rising clear and steady against the rattle of the sleet on the umbrella, "do you take this woman to be your lawful wedded wife, to have and to hold, under the sun and under the cloud, in the stony pasture and the fertile bottom, so long as the creek runs and the seasons return?"

"I do," Virgil said, his teeth chattering so hard the syllables came out in two distinct pieces.

"Bernice," Jesse said, "do you take this man?"

"I do," she snapped. "And tell that boy in the truck to stop honking the horn; my sister’s trying to hear."

"By the authority vested in me," Jesse said, "under the ancient charter of nineteen-twenty-eight, ratified by the General Assembly of the Commonwealth of Kentucky and registered in the vault of Whetstone County: I pronounce that you are husband and wife. Whom God has joined under the high heavens, let no bankruptcy court in Indiana put asunder."

Virgil reached into his pocket, pulled out a gold ring that had been worn thin as a hair, and pushed it onto Bernice’s knuckle.

Then he reached into his other pocket and pulled out a two-quart Mason jar filled with a clear, pale-amber liquid that had two whole preserved peaches floating in the bottom.

"Peach brandy," Virgil whispered, pressing the jar into Jesse’s hands. "Put up in nineteen-ninety-six. My brother made it before he went to the legislature. It’ll cure the croup and it’ll loosen a rusted head-bolt if you let it sit twenty minutes."

"Thank you, Virgil," Jesse said, tucking the jar under his arm.

Lindy stepped forward with her adding machine tape.

"Sign line four, Virgil," she said, handing him a ballpoint pen with a frozen cartridge. "And sign it firm. We’re filing this return with the circuit court at two o'clock."

### Chapter 14

The deposition of Jesse Lee Callow was held on Friday morning in Room 204 of the Whetstone County Courthouse Annex—a room that had formerly been the grand jury witness room and still contained eleven wooden chairs with cracked leather seats, a portrait of Governor Happy Chandler from 1955, and an electric water cooler that groaned whenever anyone opened the door.

At the head of the oak table sat Mr. Donald G. Stantz, senior partner of Stantz, Poffenberger & Crooks.

He was sixty-two years old, had a head as round and bald as a turnip, wore a charcoal-grey suit of English wool that must have cost more than Toby Gidley’s road truck, and carried a gold-plated Montblanc pen that he clicked with a rhythmic, hypnotic *snick... snick... snick*.

Beside him sat Harlan Grote, looking paler than ever, his black catalog case open on the floor, surrounded by four cardboard boxes of photocopied marriage registers.

At the other end of the table sat Jesse, wearing his blue suit and his clean boots, flanked by Corinne Peake and a young man named Bobby Vance, who was twenty-six years old, had been out of law school for eleven months, and spent most of his time handling uncontested divorces and title searches for the rural electric cooperative.

A court reporter from Lexington, a woman with purple spectacles and an electronic stenotype machine that looked like an accordion, sat in the corner, her fingers hovering over the keys like a pair of nervous spiders.

"State your full legal name for the record," Stantz said. His voice was deep, oily, with the slow, terrifying polish of a man who had spent thirty years arguing complex corporate liquidations before federal judges who were his college classmates.

"Jesse Lee Callow," Jesse said.

"And your occupation, Mr. Callow?"

"I repair small engines," Jesse said. "And clocks. Mostly mantle clocks and thirty-hour weights."

Stantz smiled. It was a cold, thin expression that didn't reach his small, grey eyes.

"Not a minister?"

"I am an ordained Presbyter of the Independent Canopy," Jesse said.

Stantz clicked his pen. *Snick.*

"Mr. Callow, between July 14, 2012, and the present date, you have certified to the Clerk of this County that you performed three hundred and ninety-eight religious solemnizations of matrimony. Is that correct?"

"If that's what the book says," Jesse replied.

"And for each of those ceremonies," Stantz continued, leaning forward and resting his elbows on the oak table, "you purported to act under credentials issued by the Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy, an Indiana non-profit entity currently under the jurisdiction of the Vanderburgh Superior Court?"

"Objection," Bobby Vance said, his voice cracking slightly on the first syllable. "The witness isn't an expert on Indiana receiverships."

"Overruled by the circumstances, counsel," Stantz said without looking at the boy. "Mr. Callow, did you or did you not sign line fourteen of these three hundred and ninety-eight certificates under that ecclesiastical authority?"

"I signed them," Jesse said.

"Did you remit the twenty-two-dollar synodical quota for any of those ceremonies?"

"No," Jesse said.

"Did you remit the five-percent honorarium tithe?"

"No."

Stantz turned to Grote, who handed him a sheet of paper from the top box.

"I have here," Stantz said, "an affidavit from the State Department of Revenue. In the year twenty-twenty-one, you reported total business income from your repair shop of eleven thousand four hundred and twelve dollars. You reported no ministerial income. Zero. Yet the records of this county show that in twenty-twenty-one, you performed forty-two weddings. Are you telling this Court that you performed forty-two solemnizations without consideration?"

"I didn't say that," Jesse said.

"Ah," Stantz said, his pen clicking again. *Snick.* "Then consideration was paid?"

"People gave me things," Jesse said.

"What things, Mr. Callow?"

Jesse shifted on his wooden chair. He looked at the portrait of Happy Chandler on the wall.

"Well," he said, "Lonnie Fray gave me three bags of seed oats and a half-gallon of sorghum. Darrell Vance gave me an electric fence charger that had a blown capacitor, which I fixed with a five-dollar part from the radio shop. Donnie Cleary gave me a hindquarter of a heifer that had broken its leg in the cattle guard."

Stantz stared at him. The pen stopped clicking.

"A hindquarter of a heifer?"

"It was very good beef," Jesse said. "A little lean, but we made hamburger out of it."

"And the Gidleys?" Stantz demanded, his voice rising half an octave. "What did Toby Gidley give you for that barn ceremony?"

"Five gallons of yellow cling peaches," Jesse said. "And Toby helped me pull the transmission out of my Dodge when the reverse gear stripped."

Corinne Peake leaned forward. She laid a thick, black leather book on the table between Jesse and the receiver.

"Mr. Stantz," she said, her voice dropping into that terrifying, schoolteacher register that could be heard through two closed doors. "Are you familiar with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of nineteen-thirty-eight?"

Stantz frowned. "This is a bankruptcy inquiry, madam. Not a farm program hearing."

"Under Section Twenty-Two of the Kentucky Agricultural Code," Corinne said, tapping the black book with her spectacles, "the exchange of raw agricultural commodities between neighbors for domestic services rendered within a rural precinct does not constitute taxable trade or commercial revenue. It is classified as mutual rural accommodation. It carries no gross receipts tax, no provincial levy, and no franchise valuation."

She pulled out a yellowed piece of paper from the back of the book.

"And furthermore," she said, "in nineteen-thirty-one, the Circuit Court of this county held in *Trustees of the Mount Zion Church versus Peake* that a minister who accepts a bushel of turnips or a cured shoulder in lieu of a pastoral fee is acting in accordance with the primitive discipline of the frontier church, and that any secular corporation that attempts to levy upon said turnips is guilty of maintenance and barratry."

Stantz’s face went slightly red. "Barratry?"

"Barratry, Mr. Stantz," Corinne said smoothly. "The excitation of groundless legal disputes for commercial gain. A misdemeanor under Kentucky law, punishable by a fine of five hundred dollars and forfeiture of the right to practice before the courts of this Commonwealth."

Stantz looked at Bobby Vance. Bobby was twenty-six years old and looked like he might throw up, but he nodded vigorously.

"We move to dismiss the citation on the grounds of barratry," Bobby whispered.

Stantz closed his gold pen. He did not click it. He laid it carefully on the yellow legal pad in front of him.

"You think you’re very clever, Mrs. Peake," he said quietly. "You have your local precedents, your old statutes, your dead surveyors. But you have one problem that no Gunter's chain can fix."

He reached into his briefcase and pulled out a single sheet of paper, printed on modern legal bond with a blue holographic seal at the top.

"This," Stantz said, "is a certified copy of an Order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of Indiana, entered at four-thirty yesterday afternoon. It holds that the original nineteen-twenty-eight charter of the Independent Apostolic Assembly was merged into the national trust in nineteen-seventy-two by the vote of its surviving trustees, and that all provincial rights were extinguished."

He slid the paper across the oak table until it touched Jesse’s knuckles.

"Your independent canopy," Stantz said, "does not exist. It hasn't existed for fifty-two years. Every marriage you have performed since two thousand and twelve is an uncertified civil act, subject to immediate administrative re-examination by the State of Kentucky. Unless..."

Jesse looked at him. "Unless what?"

"Unless," Stantz said, "the County of Whetstone agrees to settle the outstanding franchise claim of the debtor estate for the sum of thirty-eight thousand dollars cash, payable to the receiver within forty-eight hours."

### Chapter 15

The rain that started at noon on Friday was not an ordinary autumn drizzle; it was an old-fashioned, creek-filling soaker that brought three inches of water out of the knobs in six hours.

By eight o'clock that night, Whetstone Creek had risen five feet, turning from a clear limestone trickle into a yellow, foaming torrent that carried dead cedar logs, plastic chemical jugs, and the branches of fallen sycamores down toward the river with a deep, grinding roar that shook the concrete bridge on Route 18.

Jesse’s kitchen was full of water. Not from the creek, but from the boots of six men who were standing around the table where Lindy had laid out three flashlights, two battery-powered lanterns, and the 1874 survey map.

Toby was there, wearing a yellow rain slicker that was torn down the back, smelling of diesel fuel and wet earth.

Beside him were his uncle, Sheriff Wade Gidley, wearing his rubber hunting boots; Billy Cates, holding a twelve-volt trouble light plugged into a car battery; and two men from the county road department who had been operating the backhoe since five o'clock.

"The culvert’s gone at the bridge," Toby said, wiping the rainwater from his eyebrows with the sleeve of his coat. "The Jackson County side is washed out four feet into the road. If Claggett wants to serve that federal paper on the courthouse on Monday, he’ll have to swim his truck across the ford."

"He won't swim it," the sheriff said, leaning his hands on the back of a chair. "He’ll go around by the highway bridge at Mount Zion. That gives us until nine o'clock on Monday morning."

"Nine o'clock doesn't matter, Wade," Jesse said. He was sitting at the table, his reading glasses in his hand, looking down at Stantz’s federal court order. "The federal judge signed it. If the nineteen-twenty-eight charter was merged in seventy-two, Corinne’s book doesn't protect us. The state registrar will have to follow the federal decree."

Lindy walked to the stove, took the hot iron skillet off the burner, and set a plate of fried cornmeal mush on the table. Nobody reached for it.

"The nineteen-seventy-two merger," Lindy said, her voice cutting through the sound of the rain on the tin roof, "was signed by three trustees."

Jesse looked up. "You looked at it?"

"I called the state library in Indianapolis this afternoon from the extension office," she said. "The clerk read me the names off the corporate microfiche. The three trustees who signed the merger in Evansville on August 12, nineteen-seventy-two, were:
- Reverend H. T. Morling;
- His brother, Arthur Morling; and
- Silas Gidley."

Toby dropped his flashlight. It rolled across the linoleum, casting a wild, yellow beam against the base of the woodbox.

"Uncle Silas?" Toby yelled. "Uncle Silas was eighty-eight years old in seventy-two! He was living in the back bedroom of my granddaddy’s house with a kerosene heater and two coon dogs!"

"He was also the surviving treasurer of the Kentucky charter," Lindy said. "And do you know where Uncle Silas was on August 12, nineteen-seventy-two?"

Toby looked at his uncle Wade.

Sheriff Wade Gidley took off his hat. He looked at the floor, a slow, terrible grin spreading across his leathery face.

"Wade," Toby said. "Where was he?"

"Silas," the sheriff said, "was in the county jail at Meacham."

Jesse blinked. "In jail?"

"He was serving thirty days," the sheriff said, "for operating an unlicensed still in the limestone sink behind the barn. My daddy arrested him on the fourth of July. The jailer’s record is right down there in the basement. Uncle Silas never left that cell until the first of September."

Lindy slapped the table with the palm of her hand. The sound was as sharp as a pistol shot.

"A man who is in the county jail in Whetstone County, Kentucky," she said, "cannot execute a corporate merger in Evansville, Indiana, on the twelfth of August. It's a forged instrument, Jesse. Morling forged Silas's signature because he needed three trustees to transfer the assets to his own trust."

Jesse stood up.

He looked at the six men in the yellow light of the lanterns. Outside, the rain was hammering on the roof with that steady, deafening roar that meant the bottomland would be two feet deep in water by morning.

"Toby," Jesse said.

"Yeah, Jesse?"

"Where’s that excavator?"

Toby reached into his pocket and pulled out the brass key to the Cat 320.

"Sitting on the lowboy behind the shop," he said. "With four hundred gallons of diesel in the tanks."

"Get Billy," Jesse said. "And get that twelve-volt light. We’re going to dig out that still."

### Chapter 16

The lime sink behind the site of the old barn was sixty feet wide, thirty feet deep, and choked with fifty years of Whetstone County history: rolls of rusted woven wire, five-gallon oil drums, the fenders of a 1957 Chevrolet pickup, and twenty-five feet of limestone gravel that had washed down from the road in the flood of ninety-seven.

At midnight, the rain had slacked to a cold, spitting sleet that froze on the yellow boom of the excavator as Toby swung the two-yard bucket through the dark.

The twin halogen work lights on the cab cut two sharp, white tunnels through the mist, illuminating the wet, black sides of the sinkhole as the steel teeth of the bucket tore through the gravel with a high, screaming screech of iron against limestone.

Jesse stood on the rim of the sink, wearing an old canvas hunting coat with a corduroy collar, holding a four-cell flashlight. Beside him was Sheriff Wade Gidley, his service revolver tucked into his belt under his yellow slicker, holding a two-way radio that hissed with static from the highway patrol frequency.

"Easy, Toby!" the sheriff yelled over the roar of the diesel engine. "Don't hit the rock ledge! The cave entrance is right under the north rim!"

The excavator’s bucket bit into a mass of rusted sheet metal, lifted it twenty feet into the air, and dumped it onto the gravel pad with a deafening, metallic crash that sent two crows flapping out of the sumac bushes.

"There!" Billy Cates shouted from the bottom of the hole. He was standing on a shelf of yellow limestone, knee-deep in mud, pointing with his trouble light into a dark, horizontal slit beneath the overhang. "I see the copper!"

Toby cut the throttle. The diesel engine dropped to a low, rhythmic idle that sounded like a tugboat in a fog.

Jesse scrambled down the slick clay slope, holding onto roots of elderberry to keep from sliding into the pit.

At the back of the shelf, tucked into a dry limestone pocket where the water had never reached, was an old oak whiskey barrel with rusted iron hoops.

Resting on top of the barrel was an eight-gallon milk can of heavy tin, its lid soldered shut with lead, bearing a brass plate riveted to the neck:

*PROPERTY OF THE INDEPENDENT CANOPY OF KENTUCKY.*  
*S. Gidley, Custodian of Records & Seals.*  
*1968.*

Billy reached out with a crowbar, hooked the edge of the lid, and heaved. The old solder gave way with a sharp, tearing *snap*.

Jesse shone his flashlight into the can.

Inside, wrapped in three layers of heavy, grease-soaked wool from an old army blanket, was:
1. The original 1928 Charter of the Assembly, engrossed on vellum with the great seal of the Commonwealth of Kentucky attached by a blue silk ribbon;
2. A leather-bound ledger containing the minutes of the annual meetings held in the barn from 1928 to 1971; and
3. A five-pound brass seal on a heavy screw-press, with the words:  
   *THE APOSTOLIC ASSEMBLY OF THE OPEN AIR — WHETSTONE PRECINCT — EST. 1928.*

Jesse reached into the can. His hands were shaking with the cold, but when his fingers touched the cold, heavy brass of the seal, a strange, calm warmth seemed to spread up his wrists.

He pulled the ledger out. He opened it to the last page.

Written there, in the slow, heavy hand of old Silas Gidley, dated July 2, 1972—two days before he was taken to the jail—was a single sentence:

*Moved and carried by the Brethren under the hay fork: that we refuse to join with the people in Indiana, that we hold our canopy separate under our own trees, and that the records be kept in the rock until an honest man comes to ask for them.*

Jesse closed the book.

Above him, on the rim of the pit, Toby Gidley stood in the glare of the excavator’s lights, his face streaked with black grease, looking down into the hole.

"Jesse!" Toby yelled against the wind. "Is it there?"

Jesse held the five-pound brass seal up into the white glare of the halogen beams. The polished metal caught the light, gleaming like a new coin against the dark limestone of the pit.

"It’s here, Toby!" Jesse shouted back. "It’s been here fifty years!"

### Chapter 17

On Monday morning, the sun came up over Whetstone County in a sky that had been washed as clean and blue as a new denim shirt.

At eight-forty-five, a black Lincoln town car with Indiana license plates pulled up before the steps of the courthouse in Meacham.

Mr. Donald G. Stantz got out of the front passenger seat. He was wearing a fresh grey topcoat, carrying his gold-trimmed briefcase, and his turnip-shaped head was gleaming with aftershave.

Behind him came Lyle Claggett, wearing his King Ranch jacket and carrying a red expandable file containing his boundary petition.

They climbed the concrete stairs together, their leather soles making a sharp, authoritative rhythm on the stone, pushed open the heavy oak doors, and walked up to the second floor.

When they pushed open the frosted-glass door of the Registry of Deeds, the brass bell gave its cheerful *clink*.

The room was not empty.

Sitting on the wooden benches along the wall were forty-two people:
- Toby and Lindy Gidley, holding their two little girls in clean blue dresses;
- Virgil Poteet and Bernice, Virgil wearing his wedding suit and Bernice holding her patent-leather bag;
- Lonnie and Arlene Fray, with their five children sitting in a neat, silent row on an oak bench;
- Miss Eleanor Vance, holding Arthur's mandolin in a canvas case;
- Sheriff Wade Gidley, wearing his dress uniform with his gold badge polished until it hurt to look at it; and
- County Judge-Executive Horace Vance, seventy-eight years old, sitting in an armchair he had brought up from his own office, wearing his black judicial robes over his flannel shirt.

Corinne Peake was behind the counter. She had her shears in her hand, her tin of mint lozenges open, and she was wearing a brand-new cardigan of bright, cardinal red.

In the center of the slate counter sat the 1928 brass seal, its heavy screw-press polished with kerosene until the letters *WHETSTONE PRECINCT* shone like gold.

Jesse Callow was standing beside the counter, his blue suit brushed, his hair combed, with the leather-bound 1972 ledger open before him.

Stantz stopped two feet from the slate. He looked at the forty-two people. He looked at the Judge-Executive. He looked at the brass seal.

"What is the meaning of this assembly?" Stantz demanded.

Judge-Executive Vance leaned his chin on his cane, looked at Stantz over the top of his wire-rimmed spectacles, and spat a small brown stream of tobacco juice into an empty brass spittoon beside his chair with a ringing *ping*.

"This is the County Court, son," the Judge-Executive said. "Convened in regular administrative session under Section Sixty-Four of the Civil Code. And you’re standing on county pine."

Stantz’s face went slightly tight. "I have here an Order of the United States District Court—"

"You have an order based on a forged merger, Mr. Stantz," Lindy Gidley said, standing up from the bench. She walked to the counter and laid three certified documents before him:
- A certified copy of the Whetstone County Jail register for August 1972, showing Silas Gidley in Cell 3;
- A certified affidavit from the state handwriting examiner in Frankfort, declaring the signature on the Evansville merger to be an amateur tracing; and
- A decree entered at eight-fifteen this morning by Judge-Executive Vance, declaring the 1928 Charter of the Independent Canopy to be in full force, active operation, and continuous provincial succession under the custody of Presbyter Jesse L. Callow.

Stantz looked down at the jail register. He didn't touch it.

"This is irregular," he whispered.

"No, sir," Corinne Peake said, picking up her heavy shears. "The nineteen-seventy-two paper was irregular. This is permanent. It’s registered in Book Forty-One, page one hundred and thirteen. Stamped with seven dollars of state revenue stamps, paid out of the road commissioner's contingent fund."

She turned to Lyle Claggett, who was standing behind Stantz, holding his red file against his stomach.

"Lyle," Corinne said.

Claggett swallowed. "Yes, Mrs. Peake?"

"Your daddy’s silage pit," she said.

Claggett’s face turned the color of an unripe strawberry. "Mrs. Peake, we've reviewed the Trumbo survey. We believe there may have been an interpretive variation in the chain links."

"There wasn't any variation, Lyle," Toby Gidley said, stepping up beside Jesse. "Your daddy’s pit is twenty-two feet inside Whetstone. But I talked to the road committee this morning. If you drop this boundary petition and sign a reciprocal recording agreement for that barn, the road department will gravel your daddy’s lane all the way to the state road for sixty dollars a load."

Claggett looked at Toby. He looked at the Judge-Executive. He looked at forty-two voters who were watching him with the silent, unblinking intensity of a jury that had already agreed on the verdict.

"The lane," Claggett said, his voice barely audible, "is very rough in the spring."

"We'll use number two stone," Toby said. "Packed with the heavy roller."

Claggett took his red file, tucked it under his arm, and turned toward the door.

"Lyle," Stantz hissed. "We have a federal claim!"

"You have a federal claim in Indiana, Mr. Stantz," Claggett whispered, his hand already on the brass knob. "I have an election in May. Good morning, Mrs. Peake."

The door closed with a soft, polite *click*.

Stantz stood alone before the slate counter.

For a full minute, the only sound in the Registry was the slow, metallic tick of the courthouse clock tower and the low murmur of the two little Gidley girls playing with an empty spool of purple ribbon on the floor.

Stantz slowly reached into his topcoat, took out his gold-plated Montblanc pen, and laid it on his briefcase.

"The receiver," he said, his voice flat, dry, and utterly defeated, "will require an administrative release. A formal renunciation of all claims against the Indiana estate."

Jesse stepped forward. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a single sheet of paper from his Underwood typewriter.

It contained twenty-four words:

*THE INDEPENDENT CANOPY OF WHETSTONE COUNTY HEREBY RELEASES THE APOSTOLIC FELLOWSHIP OF INDIANA FROM ALL OBLIGATIONS TO PROVIDE LINEN, DEACONS, OR HYMNS, FOREVER.*

Stantz looked at the paper. He looked at the typed letter *e*, which was filled with lint and sat half a millimeter above the line.

He took his pen, signed his name on the line at the bottom, and clicked the cap shut.

*Snick.*

"Good day, Mr. Callow," he said.

He picked up his briefcase, turned on his heel, and walked out into the corridor. His polished English shoes made no sound at all on the linoleum.

### Chapter 18

The first Saturday in June of the following year was ninety degrees by ten o'clock in the morning.

The limestone pastures of Whetstone County were lush with fescue and sweet yellow clover, and the water in Whetstone Creek was running clear and cool over the limestone ledges, sparkling like cracked glass in the summer sun.

At two o'clock in the afternoon, Jesse Callow’s green Dodge Ram was parked in the shade of a walnut tree at the edge of the new fescue meadow on the Gidley farm.

Where the old bank barn had stood, Toby had built a pavilion.

It was not a church. It had no walls, no steeple, and no pews. It consisted of six heavy cedar posts set four feet into the limestone gravel, supporting a simple gable roof of green corrugated steel that smelled of warm pine and new paint.

Tied to the four corner posts were four lengths of white unbleached linen, each six cubits long, that Lindy had hemmed on her mother's sewing machine and pinned with cedar pegs to satisfy the ancient charter of 1928.

Under the roof stood two hundred people:
- Toby Gidley, wearing a clean white shirt and his official badge as the newly elected County Road Commissioner;
- Lindy, holding a bowl of fresh strawberries from the garden;
- Corinne Peake, sitting in an oak rocking chair that Toby had carried down from the porch, fanning herself with a funeral home fan showing a picture of the Garden of Gethsemane; and
- Billy Cates, who had just driven up in the new county salt truck with the Western blade polished like silver, wearing a rented blue suit and holding the hand of the Miller girl from the lime quarry.

Jesse Callow stood on a flat limestone slab at the edge of the pavilion, right where Captain Trumbo's line crossed the creek bottom.

He was forty-nine now. His hair was a little greyer around the ears, his knuckles were still stained with the dark, permanent grease of the clock shop, but his dark blue suit was pressed and his shoes were clean.

Under his arm was the black-bound 1928 Book of Common Shelter, held shut by an elastic band.

"Friends," Jesse said.

The two hundred people went quiet. The wind came off the clover hill, soft and warm, rustling the white linen strips on the cedar posts with a gentle, dry flutter that sounded like the wings of fifty doves.

Billy Cates looked at the Miller girl. The Miller girl looked at Billy.

"Billy," Jesse said, his voice carrying clear and steady out over the fescue, over the creek, and up into the blue hollows of the knobs where the timber was deep and green. "Do you take this woman to be your wife under the open sky?"

"I do," Billy said, his voice loud and sure.

"And do you promise," Jesse said, "to hold her hand when the snow is deep, to keep the blade down when the roads are bad, and never to ask the county court for anything you can build with your own timber?"

"I do," Billy said.

Jesse looked out over the gathered saints of Whetstone County.

He saw the farmers who had worked their lives into the rock; he saw the women who had baked forty-two cakes for one afternoon; he saw Toby Gidley grinning with his arm around his wife; and he saw Corinne Peake, her purple stamp tucked safely into her patent-leather purse, nodding at him with that slow, magnificent approval that had outlasted three wars and four hundred marriages.

Jesse closed the book. He didn't need the words on the paper; he had them where no auditor could ever find them, written into the grain of his heart like the rings in a white oak timber.

"By the authority of the sky above us," Jesse said, raising his hand toward the wide, blue heavens that stretched from the limestone quarry all the way to the river, "and with the consent of the people who live under it: I pronounce that you are husband and wife."

The crowd cheered, a great, rolling shout that echoed off the ridge, and Billy kissed the girl while Toby leaned on the horn of the big International truck, sending a long, joyful blast echoing through the valleys of the county.

Jesse stepped down from the stone.

He walked over to the Dodge, took off his coat, and hung it on the hook behind the seat.

In the glove box, tucked between a spare set of points and a roll of black friction tape, was a small, three-inch square of paper, stamped with purple ink:

*MINISTERIAL REPORT: QUARTER TWO.*  
*Ceremonies performed: One.*  
*Canopy: Open.*  
*Deacons: Present.*  
*Offering: One quart of strawberry preserves and a promise of gravel.*  
*Status: Eternal.*

Jesse smiled, clicked the glove box shut, and walked back into the shade to get his piece of cake.
### Chapter 19

The freeze on Whetstone County did not come from a judge’s gavel or a sheriff’s cruiser; it came on a five-by-seven yellow postcard mailed from the Cabinet for Health and Family Services in Frankfort, bearing a third-class bulk permit stamp and three lines of dot-matrix printing:

*NOTICE OF SUSPENSION OF REGISTRATION FACILITY #092.*  
*Pursuant to 902 KAR 17:010, the acceptance of Certificates of Marriage originating from Whetstone County is suspended pending administrative determination of ministerial qualifications and jurisdictional boundaries. Certified copies cannot be issued. Automated cross-matching with the Social Security Administration, the Department of Veterans Affairs, and the State Department of Revenue is halted as of midnight, June 14.*

Corinne Peake did not throw the postcard away. She did not stick it under a magnet on her green filing cabinet. She pinned it to the center of the frosted-glass door of the Registry of Deeds with three pieces of Scotch tape, right at eye level, so that anyone reaching for the brass knob had to look at it through their own reflection.

By Tuesday morning, the hallway outside the Registry smelled of wet tobacco, damp work shirts, and the collective anxiety of seventy-two people who had discovered that they did not legally exist.

The first in line was Darrell Sneed, who drove a propane delivery tanker for Southern States. Darrell was thirty-four, had a wife named Brenda whom Jesse had married four years ago in the bed of an F-150 during a softball tournament, and was currently attempting to add Brenda to his union health insurance before she went into the hospital in Bowling Green to have their third child.

"Corinne," Darrell said through the brass grille of the counter, his knuckles white against the slate. "The girl at the union hall in Owensboro told me on the phone that my marriage certificate was returned stamped *NON-RECORDABLE*. She said if I don't have a state-certified transcript with an embossed gold foil seal by Friday noon, Brenda's hospital bill goes on my personal credit."

"Brenda's hospital bill," Corinne said without looking up from her receipt book, "is four thousand dollars if she has the natural birth and six thousand if they have to use the tongs. You tell that girl in Owensboro that her own grandmother was married by a justice of the peace on a hay-wagon in nineteen-forty-eight, and the State of Kentucky didn't ask for a gold foil seal before they let her draw her widow’s pension."

"I told her that, Corinne," Darrell said miserably. "She said that was before the computers."

"Everything was better before the computers," Corinne snapped.

Behind Darrell was Mrs. Arlene Fray, Jesse’s second cousin, holding her youngest boy by the strap of his overalls. Arlene’s face was the color of skim milk.

"Corinne," Arlene said, her voice trembling, "Lonnie’s mother called me from the rest home. She said the preacher from the Primitive Baptist church told her that because Jesse wasn't registered in Frankfort, Lonnie and I have been living in a state of open disgrace for twelve years, and the church is taking our names off the family plot in the cemetery."

"Your mother-in-law," Corinne said, reaching into her drawer for an emery board, "has been trying to get your name off that plot since the day you wore white patent-leather pumps to the Decoration Day dinner. If she tries to move the foot-stones, you tell Lonnie to take the road grader over there and park it across her driveway."

She looked past Arlene to the forty people packed into the corridor, their faces grim, their voices murmuring like a swarm of hornets in an old chimney.

"Jesse!" she called toward the inner office. "Get out here!"

Jesse came out of the vault. He had his coat off, his shirtsleeves rolled up past his grease-stained forearms, and a three-inch stack of yellow certificates in his hands.

For forty-eight hours, he had not slept more than two hours at a time. His telephone had rung at midnight, at two in the morning, and at five:
- Donnie Cleary’s wife had been turned down for a mortgage on a modular home;
- The widow of a timber-cutter in the knobs had been told her husband’s black-lung survivor benefits were held in escrow because her marriage certificate bore the seal of the "Independent Canopy";
- A boy who had enlisted in the Marine Corps had been told his dependent allowance was suspended until he could prove his parents were legally married.

"Jesse," Corinne said, pointing with her emery board toward the door. "Frankfort has appointed a Special Master."

Jesse stopped. "A what?"

"A Special Master," she said. "His name is Cecil Flatt. He’s the Director of the Division of Vital Statistics, he’s been in state government since the Wendell Ford administration, and he has a glass eye that doesn't track with the good one, so you never know if he’s looking at your deed or looking at your throat."

She laid a pink carbon slip on the counter.

"He’s arriving on the ten-o'clock bus," she said. "With two court reporters, three boxes of audit files, and Harlan Grote."

### Chapter 20

Mr. Cecil Flatt did not take the bus.

He arrived in a black 2018 Chevrolet Impala bearing Commonwealth of Kentucky official license plate number *VS-01*, driven by a twenty-four-year-old administrative intern from Georgetown College who wore a dark blue suit with trousers two inches too short and carried two heavy aluminum cases containing portable document scanners.

Flatt himself was sixty-eight years old, six feet three inches tall, and possessed the posture of a retired game warden who had spent thirty years watching fishermen try to hide undersized bass under floor mats. His left eye was indeed glass—a pale, milky blue that stared fixedly at a point about four inches above Jesse’s left ear—while his right eye was small, brown, and as sharp as an awl.

Behind him came Harlan Grote, carrying his black catalog case, looking thinner and paler than he had in October, but walking with the smug, bouncy stride of a man who had returned with the full weight of the state government behind him.

They did not go to the Registry of Deeds.

They marched straight into Courtroom 2 on the second floor—a high-ceilinged room paneled in dark, varnished oak that smelled of floor wax, old wool coats, and seventy years of criminal depositions.

Flatt sat down behind the judge’s bench. He did not use the gavel; he simply laid his gold pocket watch on the green felt blotter, clicked open the hunter case, and looked out over the room.

The benches were packed. Every seat was taken; men were leaning against the oak wainscoting three deep, their hats in their hands, their faces hard and unsmiling.

In the jury box sat the "Canopy Defense Committee":
- Toby Gidley, wearing his road commissioner’s badge;
- Lindy, with three green plastic milk crates of soil surveys and tax receipts between her feet;
- Sheriff Wade Gidley, his arms crossed over his chest, his revolver holster creaking whenever he shifted his weight; and
- Corinne Peake, sitting in the foreman’s chair with her heavy steel shears resting on her knees like a broadsword.

Jesse sat at the defense table alone.

Flatt cleared his throat. It was a long, dry, gravelly sound, like a shovel scraping across frozen limestone.

"This is an informal administrative inquiry," Flatt announced, his right eye scanning the room while his glass eye surveyed the plaster moldings on the ceiling. "Conducted pursuant to Section 213 of the Kentucky Revised Statutes. We are here to determine whether four hundred and two solemnizations executed within this county between July 2012 and the present date possess the character of valid civil acts, or whether they constitute unlicensed ministerial simulations."

A low growl went through the spectators.

Uncle Creed Fray stood up in the third row. "Who are you calling a simulation, Cecil? I bought four steers from your brother in nineteen-eighty-four and two of them had the bloat before we got them across the county line!"

"Sit down, Creed," Sheriff Gidley said mildly from the jury box. "Cecil’s just reading the paper."

Flatt didn't look at Creed. He turned to Grote, who was sitting at the prosecution table with three open ledgers.

"Mr. Grote," Flatt said. "You may present the initial findings of the technical examination."

Grote stood up. He adjusted his spectacles, cleared his throat twice, and picked up a sheet of legal-cap paper.

"Mr. Director," Grote began, his voice high and sharp in the quiet room. "Between May of nineteen-ninety-eight and the present date, the entity known as the Apostolic Fellowship of the Living Canopy issued ministerial certificates to fourteen hundred individuals in six states. Our examination of the debtor's records reveals that of those fourteen hundred individuals, twelve hundred and eight never performed a single ceremony. They obtained their credentials for personal amusement, to avoid jury duty, or to obtain clergy discounts at hardware stores."

A faint snicker came from the back row.

"Of the remaining one hundred and ninety-two," Grote continued, glaring at the spectators, "one hundred and ninety-one performed between one and four ceremonies, usually for relatives, after which their activity ceased. There is only one officiant on the entire national roll who maintained a continuous, systematic, and large-scale ministerial practice."

He pointed a thin, trembling finger at Jesse.

"Presbyter Jesse Lee Callow," Grote said. "A clock repairman. Operating without a church building, without an altar, without an organ, without deacons, and without a certified copy of the 1998 Book of Common Shelter."

Grote turned back to Flatt.

"Mr. Director, under Kentucky law, a minister must be in regular communion with the religious society to which he belongs. He must follow its discipline. We have examined the quarterly returns belatedly submitted by Mr. Callow. They are a fabric of absurdities. He reports ceremonies conducted in farm ponds. In cider mills. On the roofs of lime kilns. He reports tithes consisting of green beans, peaches, and half-heifers. Most egregiously, he reports that on August 17, 2019, he solemnized the marriage of the current County Road Commissioner in a timber barn that was eighty percent situated within the County of Jackson!"

Grote sat down, his face flushed with triumph, and slammed his catalog case shut.

*Clack.*

Flatt looked down from the bench. His good eye fixed on Jesse.

"Mr. Callow," Flatt said.

Jesse stood up.

He did not have a lawyer; Bobby Vance was sitting in the second row with a yellow pad, but Jesse had told him to keep quiet.

"Yes, Mr. Flatt," Jesse said.

"Did you marry these people?" Flatt asked.

"I did," Jesse said.

"From memory?"

"Mostly," Jesse said. "I had the scripture in my head. And I had the vows written down in an old pocket notebook that my uncle gave me when I went into the Reserves."

Flatt leaned forward. "Mr. Callow, Kentucky law does not prescribe a particular religious formula for matrimony. But it does require that the person performing the ceremony believe himself to be invested with authority by an existing, recognized religious body. When you stood in those fields, who did you represent?"

The courtroom went utterly silent.

The wind from the river rattled the high, arched windows of the courtroom, making the ancient glass tremble in its lead frames.

Jesse looked down at his hands. He saw the black grease under his fingernails, the scar across his left thumb where a mainspring had slipped in nineteen-ninety-nine, the calluses from thirty years of holding cold iron.

Then he looked at the benches.

He saw Darrell Sneed, who was terrified about his wife's hospital bill. He saw Arlene Fray, who had cried into her dish towels twelve years ago. He saw Toby and Lindy, who had built their lives on forty acres of limestone pasture.

"Mr. Flatt," Jesse said, his voice quiet, unhurried, but filling every corner of the high oak room. "When a young fellow comes to me and says his girl’s people won't go to his church, and his people won't go to hers, and they don't have five hundred dollars to give to a preacher who has to buy a new carpet for his fellowship hall, I don't ask him about Evansville. I don't ask him about the Synod of Dort."

He took a step toward the bench.

"I take him out to the edge of the woods," Jesse said. "Where there aren't any walls. Where there isn't any carpet. I tell him that marriage is like an old eight-day clock. If you set it level, keep the dust out of the escapement, and wind it every seven days without jerking the cord, it’ll tell the truth until the weights hit the bottom of the case."

He pointed toward the window.

"The people in this county," Jesse said, "work six days a week in the gravel, in the timber, and on the blacktop. They don't have much that lasts. Their trucks rust out in six years. Their cattle get the foot-rot. Their barns blow down in the ice storms. But when they stand in a pasture with their shoes shined and take each other by the hand, they want to believe that something they do is permanent."

He looked Flatt straight in his good right eye.

"I gave them that," Jesse said. "And if that’s an unlicensed simulation, Mr. Flatt, then you’d better call the sheriff and have him lock up the whole county."

### Chapter 21

Cecil Flatt did not call the sheriff.

He sat behind the bench for three long minutes, his pocket watch ticking with a slow, dry rhythm on the green blotter.

His good eye moved slowly from Jesse to Corinne Peake, then to Toby Gidley, then to the eighty-four people packed into the benches.

Finally, he looked at Harlan Grote.

"Mr. Grote," Flatt said.

"Yes, Mr. Director?" Grote said, leaning forward eagerly.

"How long have you been a member of the bar?"

Grote blinked. "I am not an attorney, sir. I am a Certified Forensic Examiner."

"I see," Flatt said. He picked up his pocket watch, clicked the case shut, and slipped it into his vest pocket. "Mr. Grote, in nineteen-seventy-six, when I was a junior investigator for the Department of Health, the state legislature passed the Marriage Regularization Act. Are you familiar with Section Four of that Act?"

Grote’s mouth opened slightly. "Section Four?"

"Section Four," Flatt said, reaching into his leather briefcase and pulling out a blue-bound volume of the Kentucky Acts of 1976. "It was known at the time as the *Mountain Preacher Exemption*."

Corinne Peake stopped her emery board. A tiny, triumphant gleam appeared in her watery blue eyes.

Flatt opened the book.

> *"Where any marriage has been solemnized within this Commonwealth by any person purporting to be a minister of the gospel, under open sky or in any temporary shelter, in the presence of witnesses, and the parties have thereafter cohabited as husband and wife in good faith, no defect in the incorporation, charter, canons, or provincial succession of the religious society under whose credentials said minister acted shall be admissible in any court of this Commonwealth to impeach the validity of said marriage; provided, however, that the local registration official shall certify that the ceremony was performed without fraud and that the records thereof have been maintained in regular order."*

Flatt closed the book with a heavy, dusty *thump*.

He looked down at Corinne Peake.

"Mrs. Peake," he said.

"Yes, Cecil?" she replied, her voice as sweet as clover honey.

"Are these records maintained in regular order?"

Corinne stood up. She walked to the center of the courtroom, took the four-pound brass seal of 1928 from her basket, and set it on the defense table.

"Cecil," she said, "I have four hundred and two folders in that vault. Every one of them has the names, the dates, the witnesses, and the revenue stamp. There isn't a blot of ink on one of them that hasn't been dry for five years. If you think they aren't in regular order, you come down there right now and I'll let you count the staples."

Flatt looked at Grote.

Grote was trembling. "Mr. Director! The federal receivership! The forty-five thousand dollars!"

"Mr. Grote," Flatt said, rising from the bench with the slow, terrifying dignity of a six-foot-three former game warden, "the federal receivership is in Indiana. The United States Bankruptcy Court has jurisdiction over cash, chattel, and bad debts. It does not have jurisdiction over four hundred marriages in the Commonwealth of Kentucky."

He turned to his twenty-four-year-old intern.

"Son," Flatt said.

"Yes, sir?" the boy squeaked.

"Pack up those scanners," Flatt said. "We're going to the diner. I hear they have blackberry cobbler on Tuesdays."

### Chapter 22

The diner on the square in Meacham was called the *Coffee Cup*, but everyone referred to it as *Minnie's*, after Minnie Cleary, who had run the grill since nineteen-sixty-eight and could fry four eggs in country ham grease without breaking a single yolk.

At one o'clock, the diner was so full that people were sitting on bags of salt behind the ice machine.

Cecil Flatt was sitting in the corner booth, his coat off, eating a massive wedge of warm blackberry cobbler topped with two scoops of vanilla ice cream. Beside him sat Sheriff Wade Gidley, who was drinking sweet tea and explaining why the 1997 flood was entirely the fault of the Army Corps of Engineers.

Jesse, Toby, and Lindy were sitting at the counter.

Lindy had her three-ring binder open, but she was not looking at soil maps. She was eating a grilled cheese sandwich with that fierce, concentrated appetite that comes to people who have spent three weeks preparing for an execution that was called off at the scaffold.

"Jesse," Toby said, leaning his elbow on the Formica counter. "What are you going to do with those papers?"

"What papers, Toby?"

"The forty-eight quarters," Toby said. "The ones on the Underwood."

Jesse took a sip of his coffee. It was strong, black, and tasted of chicory and seventy years of seasoned cast iron.

"I’m going to keep them," Jesse said.

"Keep them?" Lindy said, looking up with a corner of cheese in her mouth. "Why would you keep them? Grote is on the two-fifteen bus back to Evansville. Flatt is signing the certification order right now on the trunk of his car."

"They're a record," Jesse said quietly.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out the small notebook with the green marbled cover.

"Twelve years," he said. "Three hundred and ninety-eight weddings. Plus Virgil and Bernice. Plus Billy and the Miller girl. That's four hundred."

He looked through the plate-glass window of the diner. Across the square, the old limestone courthouse stood in the warm June sun, its four clock faces each telling a different hour, its iron soldier standing guard over the dandelion-covered lawn.

Up on the second floor, behind the frosted glass, Corinne Peake was already back at her desk, her electric eraser whining as she corrected another deed of easement, her purple stamp ready for whatever came through the door next.

"A man ought to keep a record of what he did," Jesse said. "Even if he did it in a field. Even if he did it from memory."

Toby smiled. He reached into his shirt pocket and pulled out a small piece of folded paper.

"Jesse," Toby said.

"Yeah, Toby?"

"Me and Lindy were talking," Toby said, his face reddening slightly under his red hair. "About that pavilion."

"What about it?"

"We got the roof on," Toby said. "And we got the gravel packed. But we were thinking... well, since we're right on the line, and since Lyle Claggett is gravelling the lane..."

Lindy reached out and took Toby’s hand. Her grey eyes were soft, bright, and completely steady.

"We want to do it again, Jesse," she said.

Jesse looked at them. "Do what again?"

"The wedding," Lindy said. "On August seventeenth. Our five-year anniversary. We want to stand under that steel roof with the four pieces of linen. We want Billy to be there with his salt truck. We want Corinne to sit in her rocking chair."

She leaned closer to him.

"And this time," she said, "we want you to use the Underwood."

### Chapter 23

The celebration of the Second Solemnization of Toby and Lindy Gidley was held on Saturday, August 17, at six o'clock in the evening.

The weather had cooperated in that miraculous, unpredictable way that upland weather sometimes does: a morning shower had washed the dust from the fescue, and by five o'clock the wind had died away to a gentle, cool breeze that brought the scent of sweet clover and damp limestone off the creek.

Three hundred and forty people were gathered around the pavilion.

Every marriage Jesse had ever performed was represented:
- Donnie Cleary was there with his wife, holding their twin boys on his good right arm;
- Darrell Sneed and Brenda were there, holding their new baby girl, whose hospital bill had been paid in full by the union after Cecil Flatt personally telephoned the regional director in Owensboro;
- Virgil Poteet and Bernice were there, Virgil wearing his wedding shoes (which had finally stopped squeaking) and Bernice carrying a three-layer chocolate cake with peppermint frosting;
- Miss Eleanor Vance was there, sitting on a folding chair beside the road commissioner's International truck, playing *The Maiden's Prayer* on Arthur's mandolin while three young boys tried to catch grasshoppers in her empty instrument case.

The pavilion had been decorated with two miles of white paper streamers, forty-eight kerosene lanterns hung from the rafters, and four enormous bouquets of black-eyed Susans and wild asters that the Gidley girls had picked along the railroad grade.

Corinne Peake sat in her oak rocker at the front, wearing a magnificent dress of purple silk that had belonged to her mother, holding a small wooden box on her lap containing the official Whetstone County registry ledger for the year 2024.

Jesse Callow stood on the limestone slab beneath the green steel gable.

He wore his dark blue suit, his black boots polished until you could see the lantern light in the leather, and his reading glasses balanced on his nose.

Before him stood Toby and Lindy.

Toby had on a clean pair of grey trousers, a blue blazer that was only slightly tight across his broad shoulders, and a white rose pinned to his lapel.

Lindy was wearing her wedding dress—the one with the daisy lace that her grandmother had made in nineteen-sixty-two—which had been let out two inches at the waist to accommodate five years of good cooking and two healthy children.

Jesse opened the black book.

"Friends," he said.

The mandolin stopped. The children went quiet. The only sound was the clear, brownish water of Whetstone Creek running over the yellow rocks thirty yards away and the soft, dry rustle of the linen strips on the cedar posts.

"Five years ago," Jesse said, looking at the two of them, "we stood in a barn that was falling down on a boundary that nobody could find. We had a thunderstorm coming, we had thirty-two lanterns that were running out of coal oil, and we had two young people who didn't know whether they were in Whetstone, in Jackson, or halfway to the river."

A quiet laugh went through the crowd.

"The barn is gone," Jesse said, pointing with his hand toward the wide, open pastures that stretched away to the blue knobs. "The rafters rotted. The tin blew away. But the people who stood under it are still here."

He turned to Toby.

"Toby," Jesse said. "Do you take this woman to be your wife under the open canopy, so long as the grass grows and the water runs?"

"I do," Toby said, his voice loud and clear, echoing off the new steel rafters.

"Lindy," Jesse said. "Do you take this man?"

"I do," Lindy said, her eyes fixed on Toby’s face.

Jesse looked down at the paper in his book.

Attached to the page was Form Q-4, Third Quarter, 2024.

It was typed on the Underwood. The letter *e* was filled with lint and sat half a millimeter above the line.

*CANONICAL COMPLIANCE: Absolute.*  
*DEACONS PRESENT: The Whole County.*  
*CANOPY: The High Heavens.*  
*HONORARIUM: Two Gallons of Peaches and a Lifetime of Road Work.*

Jesse raised his right hand.

"By the authority vested in me," he said, "by the sky above us, by the Commonwealth of Kentucky, and by the Clerk of the County of Whetstone: I pronounce that you are husband and wife."

The crowd gave a great, roaring cheer that startled the swallows from the timber along the creek.

Toby took Lindy in his arms and kissed her, lifting her clear off the gravel floor while the two little girls danced around their boots in a flurry of white paper streamers.

Corinne Peake stood up from her rocking chair.

She didn't use a ballpoint pen. She produced an ancient steel dip-pen, dipped it into a square bottle of purple ink she had carried in her pocket, and signed the bottom of the certificate with three magnificent, swirling flourishes.

Then she took the heavy 1928 brass seal, placed the paper between the dies, and leaned on the screw-press with her full weight.

*Crunch.*

The purple impression of the *APOSTOLIC ASSEMBLY OF THE OPEN AIR* appeared on the cream-colored bond paper, crisp, permanent, and deep enough to be read by a blind man in the dark.

"There," Corinne said, blowing gently on the purple ink to dry it. "That's done. And if anybody from Indiana has anything to say about it, they can come to the cemetery and talk to my grandfather."

She handed the certificate to Lindy, picked up her funeral home fan, and pointed toward the tables behind the pavilion.

"Now," she announced in a voice that reached all three hundred and forty people, "cut that ham before the flies find the mustard."

### Chapter 24

At midnight, the party was still going.

A bonfire of cedar fence rails was burning in the gravel turn-around, casting a warm, orange glow over the twenty-eight pickup trucks parked along the fence. Two boys from the road department were playing fiddles on the tailgate of Toby’s International, while fifty people danced on the crushed limestone of the pavilion under the yellow light of the lanterns.

Jesse Callow walked away from the light.

He climbed the gentle slope of the pasture behind the house, his boots making a soft, swishing sound in the damp clover, until he reached the limestone outcrop where the old white oak stump sat in the fence row.

He sat down on the dry wood of the stump.

The night was cool, clear, and quiet. Below him, the lights of the pavilion looked like a cluster of fallen stars in the dark valley, the sound of the fiddles coming up the hill thin and sweet, like music heard across a wide river.

Above him, the sky was enormous.

It stretched from the dark ridge of the knobs all the way to the southern horizon, deep, velvet-black, dusted with millions of cold, sharp stars that had looked down on Captain Trumbo’s telegraph wire, on Silas Gidley’s still, and on four hundred pairs of young people standing in the weeds with their fingers locked together.

Jesse reached into his coat pocket.

He pulled out a single sheet of paper—the final page of Form Q-4.

He had not filled out Item 15. He had not calculated the five-percent levy.

Instead, he took his blue ballpoint pen from his pocket, leaned the paper against the hard, weathered grain of the stump, and wrote three lines at the bottom:

*To the Board of Compliance, wherever they may be:*  
*The reports are finished. The marriages are sound. The sky is holding up the roof, and we have enough gravel to last through the spring.*  
*Respectfully submitted,*  
*Jesse L. Callow, Presbyter.*

He folded the paper into a small square, tucked it into his pocket, and stood up.

He took a deep breath of the cold night air, smelling the sweet clover, the woodsmoke, and the clean, sharp scent of wet limestone.

Then he turned around and walked down the hill, back toward the music, back toward the lights, back toward the people who were waiting for him to cut the cake.

### Chapter 25

On the following Tuesday morning, Jesse was back in his workshop on Old Salt Road.

The morning sun came through the small, square window, cutting a bright, yellow beam through the floating motes of cedar dust and dry limestone powder, illuminating the three dismantled cuckoo clocks, the rows of clean brass bushings, and the coffee cans of kerosene.

The Ansonia mantle clock was sitting on the test-shelf, its phosphor-bronze bushing fitted, its verge adjusted, ticking with a slow, steady, magnificent rhythm that filled the quiet room:

*Tick... tock... tick... tock.*

The pendulum swung with a clean, two-inch drop, the brass bob catching the yellow sunlight with every beat, as true and regular as the rising of the sun over the cedar knobs.

The door opened.

It was not a process server. It was not a courier from Bowling Green.

It was young Billy Cates, wearing his clean road department shirt, holding an old cardboard shoe box under his arm.

"Jesse," Billy said, stopping just inside the door and taking off his cap.

"Morning, Billy," Jesse said, laying down his three-cornered scraper. "How’s the truck running?"

"Runs good," Billy said. "Toby put the new blade on yesterday. We got sixty ton of salt coming in on the railroad tomorrow."

He walked over to the bench and set the shoe box down beside the kerosene cans.

"What’s in the box, Billy?" Jesse asked.

Billy took off the lid. Inside, resting on a bed of crumpled newspaper, was an ancient brass mantle clock shaped like a Gothic cathedral, its steeple broken off at the second spire and its minute hand bent into a figure-eight.

"It belonged to the Miller girl's grandmother," Billy said, his voice dropping into a shy, awkward whisper. "She said it stopped running during the flood of thirty-seven. She said if you could make it tick again, she’s got two quarts of preserved blackberries and a five-pound bucket of country lard she’ll bring you on Friday."

Jesse looked at the clock.

He reached out a finger, touched the bent minute hand, and felt the heavy, solid weight of the cast-brass frame.

It was dirty. It was rusted. The mainspring was probably broken, and the pivot holes were worn oblong from eighty years of winding.

But the iron was good. The brass was thick.

"Billy," Jesse said, picking up his magnifying loupe and fitting it into his eye.

"Yes, sir?"

"You tell her to bring the blackberries," Jesse said, reaching for his can of kerosene. "And tell her that if she wants it to strike the half-hours, she’d better bring that five-pound bucket of lard too."

He sat down on his plywood-topped milk can.

The Ansonia clock on the shelf ticked on, steady, patient, and sure, measuring out the seconds, the hours, the seasons, and the years of Whetstone County, while outside, under the wide, high, unbroken canopy of heaven, the summer wind began to blow through the clover, soft and warm and free, carrying the sound of the river all the way to the hills.

### Chapter 26

The winter of that year came early to Whetstone County, but it came without malice.

By the first week of December, the limestone hills had taken on that soft, slate-grey quiet that settles over the uplands when the sap goes down into the roots. The creeks ran clear and dark between shelves of thin, shelf-like ice, and the smoke from eighty woodstoves rose straight into the cold, pale sky like blue cedar poles holding up the winter clouds.

In the courthouse at Meacham, the radiator in Room 204 had begun its annual winter performance: a series of dry, metallic clanks that sounded like a blacksmith trying to shoe a mule in a culvert, followed by a long, whistling hiss of steam that smelled faintly of old iron and peppermint cough drops.

Corinne Peake was sitting behind the slate counter, wearing her heavy wool cardigan—this one a dark, forest green with brass buttons shaped like acorns—with a small electric foot-warmer humming beneath her desk.

The green steel door of the vault was shut, its brass combination dial polished to a dull sheen.

On the counter sat a cardboard box, twelve inches square, tied with green baling twine, bearing a typed shipping label:

*TO: The Division of Archives & Records Management*  
*Department of Libraries and Archives*  
*300 Coffee Tree Road*  
*Frankfort, Kentucky 40601*

Inside the box were four hundred and two microfiche cards, produced by the state library in Lexington from the original folios, each card encased in a clear mylar sleeve, certified under the seal of the Commonwealth.

Attached to the top of the box was an envelope containing a letter written on Whetstone County Court stationery, signed by Horace Vance, County Judge-Executive, and certified by Corinne Peake:

> *TO ALL TO WHOM THESE PRESENTS SHALL COME, GREETING:*  
> *Be it known that the records of marriages solemnized within this jurisdiction under the ancient authority of the Open Canopy, from July 14, 2012, to the present date, have been examined, verified, regularized, and forever established pursuant to the curative statutes of this Commonwealth.*  
> *No further quarterly returns will be submitted to any synod, board, or receiver; the sky above this county being deemed a perpetual and sufficient covering for all covenants entered into in good faith, and the clerk of this court being fully empowered to stamp the same without fee, hindrance, or foreign inquiry.*

Corinne picked up her heavy shears. She cut the trailing ends of the green twine with two crisp, clean snips.

Then she took her purple stamp, pressed it into the ink pad, and brought it down on the top of the shipping carton with a firm, resounding *thwack*.

The purple ink sank into the corrugated cardboard, bright, sharp, and permanent:

*WHETSTONE COUNTY REGISTRY.*  
*FILED AND ESTABLISHED.*  
*DECEMBER 1, 2024.*

The brass bell over the door gave its familiar *clink*.

Jesse Callow came into the office.

He was wearing his heavy canvas coat, his ears red from the wind, carrying a brown paper grocery bag that smelled intensely of smoked hickory and black pepper.

"Morning, Corinne," he said.

"Shut the door, Jesse," she said without looking up. "The draft from that stairwell is freezing my begonias."

Jesse shut the door. He walked across the green linoleum and set the paper bag on the slate counter.

"What’s this?" she asked, peering into the bag through her bifocals.

"Half a cured side of bacon," Jesse said. "From Darrell Vance. He brought it by the shop this morning. Said his sister got her social security card in the mail on Saturday with her married name on it, and she wanted you to have the thick part of the slab."

Corinne looked into the bag. She reached in with two fingers, touched the dark, pepper-crusted rind, and nodded her head with that slow, magnificent approval that meant the meat had been cured in hickory smoke for at least three weeks.

"Darrell Vance’s family," she said, "always did know how to salt a pig. His grandfather gave my father a ham in nineteen-sixty that was so good we kept the bone in the lard tub until the next spring."

She pushed the shipping carton across the counter toward him.

"You take this down to the post office, Jesse," she said. "Tell the boy behind the counter to put seventy cents of registry stamps on it and send it on the noon truck."

Jesse picked up the box. It was light—four hundred marriages didn't weigh three pounds when you put them on plastic film—but it felt solid in his hands, like a well-made clock movement that had been balanced and oiled.

"Corinne," he said, pausing at the counter.

"What is it, Jesse?"

"Did you hear about Lyle Claggett?"

Corinne took off her glasses. She wiped the lenses with a corner of her green sweater.

"I heard," she said. "Lyle announced for Commonwealth’s Attorney on Monday."

"Toby told me," Jesse said, "that Lyle came out to the shop yesterday afternoon. Brought his fiancée. A girl from Clark County whose daddy owns a limestone quarry."

Corinne looked up at him, her watery blue eyes bright behind the clean glass.

"Where do they want to do it, Jesse?"

Jesse smiled. He tucked the shipping box under his arm, turned toward the frosted door, and rested his hand on the brass knob.

"Under the pavilion," Jesse said. "Lyle says he likes the way the gravel drains."

Corinne picked up her emery board. She took a mint lozenge from her tin, popped it into her mouth, and began to shape her left thumbnail with dry, steady, rhythmic strokes.

"Tell him fifty dollars," she said. "And tell him if he doesn't bring two bushels of Jonathan apples for the nursing home, I’ll tell his mother where he was on the fourth of July in nineteen-ninety-nine."

Jesse laughed. He pushed open the door, the brass bell ringing its cheerful, tinny chime, and walked out into the winter sunshine, down the limestone steps of the old courthouse, and into the clean, cold air of the county that he had bound together under the high, open sky.
### Chapter 27

The federal process server did not arrive in an old Pontiac, and he did not bring an electronic clipboard with cracked glass.

### Chapter 28

Courtroom 1 had not been used for a regular term of court since the Nixon administration.

It occupied the entire third floor of the courthouse, directly beneath the clock tower, a cavernous space seventy feet long and forty feet wide, paneled in dark, feather-crotch walnut that had been hewn by hand from the creek bottoms in 1888.

Above the judge’s bench was an arched plaster dome painted a faded sky-blue, across which twelve gilded stars circled a Latin motto that had been mostly obscured by sixty years of coal smoke from the stove.

The windows were sixteen feet high, rounded at the tops like the transoms of a steamboat, containing four hundred and eighty individual panes of wavy, hand-blown cylinder glass that rattled in their lead casings whenever a logging truck geared down on the bridge below.

At eight o'clock on Monday morning, Jesse Callow was standing on a twenty-four-foot extension ladder in the belfry above the courtroom ceiling, holding a trouble light and an oil can with a fourteen-inch flexible spout.

The tower was freezing cold. The wind came through the wooden louvered slats in long, moaning gusts that smelled of old slate, bird droppings, and frozen river fog.

In the center of the tower sat the movement of the 1888 Seth Thomas tower clock: an iron frame five feet high, painted green with gold striping, supporting four massive brass gears that were three feet in diameter, driven by two five-hundred-pound sandstone weights hung on half-inch wire cables that disappeared down an oak chase into the basement.

The clock had not ticked since the summer of 1974, when a colony of chimney swifts had built their mud nests in the teeth of the dead-beat escapement wheel.

Toby Gidley’s face appeared through the trapdoor in the floor, his red hair dusted with white plaster dust, holding a heavy steel pry-bar.

"Jesse!" Toby yelled over the wind. "We got the floor shored up under the jury box! Put two twelve-by-twelve white oak sills under the joists. You could park the road grader on it now and it wouldn't sag an eighth of an inch!"

Jesse didn't turn around. He had his magnifying loupe in his eye, carefully scraping a layer of dried mud and grease from the steel verge pins of the pallet arbor.

"How are the benches?" Jesse called back.

"Lindy’s got twenty-four women from the homemakers’ club down there with Murphy’s Oil Soap and steel wool," Toby said, climbing the rest of the way into the tower. He shivered, pulling his road department jacket tight around his neck. "They’ve washed thirty-two rows of walnut. Miss Eleanor Vance brought four gallons of linseed oil from her cellar. The room smells like a furniture factory that’s been hit by lightning."

Toby walked over to the clock frame. He reached out a gloved finger and touched the massive, eight-foot pendulum rod, which was made of dry white pine and carried a sixty-pound cast-iron cylindrical bob.

"Can you make it run, Jesse?"

Jesse wiped his hands on a piece of waste cotton.

"The iron is good, Toby," he said. "The bearings are phosphor-bronze. Old man Seth Thomas didn't build things to quit after eighty years. The only thing wrong with it is forty years of bird dirt and that somebody took the click-spring off the winding barrel to mend a manure spreader."

"Can you fix it?"

"I made a new spring on Saturday," Jesse said, pulling a piece of curved spring steel from his coat pocket. "Tempered it in a can of used tractor oil. If we get the weights wound up to the top of the chase, she’ll run for eight days on a winding."

Toby looked through the slats of the tower.

Down below, the town of Meacham lay spread out in the pale winter light: the square with its brick stores; the frozen ribbon of the creek; the line of hills rising toward the knobs; and State Route 18 stretching away toward the south like a grey chalk line across the brown pastures.

"Jesse," Toby said quietly.

"Yeah, Toby?"

"That lawyer from Nashville came into the road barn this morning."

Jesse stopped his oil can. "What lawyer?"

"The young one," Toby said. "The one with the shiny shoes. He had a yellow legal pad and two men with digital cameras. He told me that if Lindy and I would sign a statement saying we didn't understand the legal consequences of being married in a barn, the company would drop us out of the lawsuit."

Jesse turned to look at him. "What did you tell him, Toby?"

Toby reached into his pocket and pulled out a small piece of dark, dried beef jerky, bit off a piece, and chewed it slowly.

"I told him," Toby said, "that I understood the legal consequences just fine. I told him the consequences were two little girls who eat four pancakes apiece every morning, forty acres of fescue that’s paid for, and a wife who can run a total-station transit better than his two surveyors."

He spat a tiny shred of beef into the corner of the tower.

"And then I told him that if he didn't get his rented car off county property in three minutes, I was going to use the front-end loader to push it into the gravel washer."

Jesse smiled. He reached up with his wrench, fitted the new click-spring into the winding barrel of the great wheel, and tightened the two square-headed bolts until the lock-washers flattened into the iron.

"Toby," Jesse said.

"Yeah, Jesse?"

"Take hold of that crank," Jesse said, pointing to the four-foot cast-iron winding handle on the wall. "Let's see if we can lift five hundred pounds of Jackson County sandstone."

### Chapter 29

By Wednesday afternoon, Courtroom 1 had been transformed.

The three cords of seasoned white oak that Corinne had ordered were stacked neatly behind the massive cast-iron heating stove in the center of the room—a stove the size of an upright piano, bearing the name *BUCKEYE OAK NO. 44* cast into the door in bold, rounded relief.

Beside him were:
- Two junior partners with matching gold pens and leather portfolios;
- A paralegal who carried three laptop computers in aluminum briefcases; and
- The President of Covenant Chapels of America, Inc., a man named Bishop T. Wayne Clapp, who wore a grey three-piece suit with an embroidered clerical collar, a gold ring bearing a three-carat amethyst, and had a smile that remained precisely two inches wide whether he was looking at a judge, a witness, or a plate of country ham.

He stopped three feet inside the threshold. He looked at the forty-two rows of hand-rubbed walnut benches. He looked at the *Buckeye Oak* stove. He looked at the blue dome with its twelve gilded stars.

"Madam," DuPree said, his voice carrying the deep, mellow, theatrical resonance of the Nashville bar. "Are there no electrical outlets in this facility?"

"There’s one behind the water cooler," she said. "The sheriff uses it for his electric teakettle. If you plug that television machine into it, the breaker will trip and you'll have to go down into the basement with a flashlight to find the fuse box."

At that moment, the trapdoor in the ceiling opened.

Jesse Callow came down the wooden ladder, wearing his work overalls over his blue Sunday trousers, his face streaked with black machine grease, carrying his fourteen-inch oil can and a box of cotton waste.

"Mr. Callow," Clapp said, his two-inch smile widening by a sixteenth of an inch. "I am Bishop Clapp. We are delighted to meet you at last. We have reviewed your ministerial history with great interest. A remarkable record of rural service. Remarkable."

Jesse looked at the bishop’s hand. He did not offer his own; he held up his oil can instead.

"I’ve got kerosene on my fingers, Bishop," Jesse said mildly. "It’ll ruin that ring."

"We want to assure you, Mr. Callow," Clapp said, leaning forward and speaking in a low, pastoral whisper that smelled of cinnamon breath spray, "that this litigation is not personal. We have the highest regard for the primitive pastoral tradition. "Bishop," Jesse said, "in this county, when a man wants an unregulated field, he buys four rolls of barbed wire and six cedar posts and he puts his heifers in it. He doesn't need an insurance policy from Nashville to tell him where the fence is."

DuPree looked up from his yellow pads.

"Mr. Callow," the lawyer said, his voice dropping into that smooth, courtroom drawl that sounded like warm molasses poured over a cold biscuit. "The Federal Court does not decide cases by fence posts. The Federal Court decides cases by the Constitution of the United States. And the Constitution does not permit a clock repairman to operate an unlicensed domestic monopoly under an imaginary tent."

Jesse set his oil can down on the defense table.

He looked up at the ceiling, where the heavy pendulum of the Seth Thomas was ticking inside the oak chase with a slow, muffled heartbeat that seemed to vibrate through the walnut planks of the floor:

*Tick... tock... tick... tock.*

"Mr. DuPree," Jesse said, "the tent isn't imaginary. It’s sixty miles wide and eighty miles long. And if you think you can put a patent on it, you’d better bring a bigger ladder than the one I’ve got."

### Chapter 30

Judge Ambrose K. Quimby arrived on Thursday morning at eight-thirty.

He did not arrive in an Escalade. He arrived in a 1993 Buick Roadmaster estate wagon—the kind with the wood-grain vinyl siding and the reverse-facing third-row seat—driven by his law clerk, Miss Sarah Chen, a twenty-six-year-old graduate of Yale Law School who wore round tortoise-shell spectacles, a heavy grey tweed overcoat, and carried a leather briefcase that appeared to contain fifty pounds of legal treatises.

He wore a dark grey three-piece suit of heavy Appalachian wool that had been tailored in Lexington in nineteen-seventy-two, an immaculate white shirt with a starched collar, and a black silk necktie held with an antique gold pin shaped like a surveyor’s compass.

When he entered Courtroom 1, every man in the room stood up.

Even Sterling DuPree III stood up, his silver hair gleaming under the high windows, his hand instinctively checking the knot of his chalk-stripe tie.

Judge Quimby did not climb to the bench.

He stopped in the center aisle, between the rows of polished walnut benches, and looked up at the blue dome.

"Corinne," the judge said. His voice was not loud, but it had that deep, dry, limestone resonance that comes from fifty years of speaking in high-ceilinged rooms.

"Morning, Ambrose," Corinne said from the clerk’s table.

"The stars," the judge said, pointing with his cane toward the dome. "Who painted the stars?"

"My grandfather," Corinne said. "In eighteen-eighty-eight. Used gold leaf that came from a bank in Cincinnati that went bust in the panic."

"Good gold," the judge said. "Hasn't flaked a bit."

He walked to the defense table, looked at Jesse’s oil can, then looked at Jesse.

His eyes were the dark, deep grey of river slate, surrounded by hundreds of fine wrinkles that crinkled at the corners as he looked at Jesse’s hands.

"You’re Callow?"

"I’m Jesse Callow, Your Honor."

"Your father was Luther Callow?"

"Yes, sir."

"Luther Callow," the judge said, leaning on his cane, "could rebore an eight-horsepower Kohler cylinder closer to the round than any man in the Kentucky River valley. "That sounds like him," Jesse said.

The judge turned around and looked at the plaintiff’s table.

He looked at Sterling DuPree. He looked at the two junior partners. He looked at Bishop T. Wayne Clapp.

"Mr. DuPree," the judge said.

"Don't butter the biscuits, Mr. DuPree," Judge Quimby said, walking slowly to the bench and climbing the three steps. "The flour is too dry."

He sat down in the high-backed leather chair. He did not use a gavel. He took an ancient black fountain pen from his vest, laid it across a yellow pad, and looked at his pocket watch.

It was eight-fifty-nine.

Above them, in the dark tower, the great bronze striking hammer of the Seth Thomas rose on its cam.

At nine o'clock precisely, the clock struck.

*BONG.*

The sound was immense. It was not a modern, electric chime; it was seven hundred pounds of bell-metal cast in Troy, New York, in 1888, striking with a deep, bronze roar that shook the soot from the stovepipe, set the water in the sheriff’s kettle to trembling, and rolled out over the town of Meacham in a great, expanding wave of sound that echoed off the limestone cliffs for three miles around.

*BONG.*  
*BONG.*  
*BONG.*

Nine times the bell struck.

When the last vibration died away into the dry walnut timbers of the ceiling, Judge Quimby picked up his fountain pen.

"The United States District Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky," the judge said, "is now in session. The clerk will call the case."

"Civil Action Number 24-CV-8114," she announced, her voice carrying clear to the back row. "*Covenant Chapels of America versus Jesse L. Callow and others.* Hearing on Motion for Preliminary Injunction and Jurisdictional Determination."

"Mr. DuPree," the judge said. "You have thirty minutes to tell me why four hundred marriages in Whetstone County belong to a bankruptcy court in Indiana."

### Chapter 31

Sterling DuPree III was an artist.

For twenty-five minutes, he held Courtroom 1 in the palm of his manicured hand.

He did not shout; he did not rant. He spoke with the smooth, measured, sorrowful elegance of an ambassador who had arrived to explain that an ancient, beloved treaty could no longer be sustained in the modern world.

"Your Honor," DuPree began, pacing the floor between the benches with the slow, graceful stride of a tragedian. "We do not come to this historic courtroom to disparage the folkways of the Kentucky mountains. We revere them. We honor the simplicity, the neighborly accommodation, the rugged independence of the upland people."

He paused, turning toward the spectators, his eyes sweeping over the rows of farmers in their clean work shirts.

"But this case," DuPree said, his voice dropping into a vibrant, emotional lower register, "is not about folkways. It is about the rule of law in an interconnected Republic."

He walked to the clerk’s table and pointed a long, silver-cuffed finger at Corinne Peake’s green steel drawers, which Toby had hauled up from the second floor on a hand truck that morning.

"In those drawers," DuPree said, "are four hundred and two documents that purport to alter the civil status of eight hundred citizens. Those documents govern property rights. They govern survivorship. They govern federal estate tax exemptions. They govern the descent of land that was granted by the Commonwealth of Virginia before the Constitution was ratified."

He turned back to Judge Quimby.

"Can it be said, Your Honor, that in the year twenty-twenty-five, a great Commonwealth can permit its civil status to be determined by an unwritten ceremony, conducted from memory, beneath a tree, by an officiant who holds no credentials other than a downloaded certificate from an entity that has ceased to exist?"

He picked up a heavy, blue-bound volume from his table.

"The Supreme Court of the United States," DuPree said, "has held repeatedly that when a religious society dissolves, its property rights are determined by neutral principles of law. The franchise of the Living Canopy was an intangible asset. It was purchased by my client at a judicial sale confirmed by a federal court of competent jurisdiction. To permit Mr. Callow to operate an identical, competing ministry under the guise of an 'ancient charter' is a fraud upon the bankruptcy estate, a violation of the Full Faith and Credit Clause, and an impermissible establishment of an unorganized, non-statutory religious practice."

He stopped. He bowed to the Court.

"We ask for an injunction," DuPree concluded. "We ask that the four hundred and two certificates be declared unrecorded instruments, and that the parties be directed to regularize their unions before an authorized, bonded, and certified officiant of the Plaintiff corporation."

He sat down, his suit jacket falling into place without a single wrinkle.

A heavy, breathless silence hung over the room.

The only sound was the slow, steady ticking of the clock in the tower:

*Tick... tock... tick... tock.*

Judge Quimby did not look at his yellow pad. He had not written a single word with his fountain pen.

He looked over his spectacles at the defense table.

"Mr. Callow," the judge said.

Jesse stood up.

He did not have a legal treatise. He did not have a blue-bound volume of Supreme Court reports.

In his hand he held a small, rusted steel object about four inches long, shaped like a miniature wishbone, with a tiny brass screw set into the apex.

"Jesse," the judge said. "What is that?"

Jesse walked forward until he was standing five feet from the bench, right between the two tables.

"It’s an escapement pallet, Your Honor," Jesse said. "From the Seth Thomas clock up in the tower."

DuPree frowned. "Your Honor, we object to mechanical demonstrations that have no relevance to the constitutional issues—"

"Overruled, Mr. DuPree," Judge Quimby said, not taking his eyes from Jesse. "Go ahead, Jesse."

Jesse held the small piece of iron up so the judge could see it.

"In eighteen-eighty-eight," Jesse said, "a man named Seth Thomas cut this pallet out of a piece of crucible steel. It has two jewels set into the tips. Little pieces of synthetic ruby, about the size of a mustard seed."

He turned the pallet between his fingers.

"Those two jewels," Jesse said, "hit the teeth of that big brass wheel twelve thousand times an hour. Two hundred and eighty-eight thousand times a day. One hundred and five million times a year."

He looked at Bishop Clapp, whose two-inch smile had frozen into a rigid line.

"For eighty-six years," Jesse said, "those two little stones took the weight of five hundred pounds of sandstone falling down through that dark hole. They didn't have an electrical outlet. They didn't have a computer. They didn't have an insurance policy from Nashville."

He set the pallet on the slate counter, right beside Corinne’s saucer.

"A marriage," Jesse said, "isn't a franchise, Mr. DuPree. It’s an escapement. It’s two people taking the whole weight of the world—the sick young-uns, the wet hay, the cattle that die in the ditch, and the sixty-dollar doctor bills—and catching it on two little stones so the wheel doesn't run away and tear the clock to pieces."

He turned to the spectators.

"When I stood in those fields," Jesse said, "I didn't give them an acrylic cross. I didn't give them three overhead cameras. I gave them two verses from the Psalms, I took their hands between mine, and I told them that the iron was good and the weight was heavy, but if they held level, the clock would run."

He looked back at Judge Quimby.

"And if that’s an unconstitutional monopoly, Your Honor," Jesse said, "then the sun has been violating the interstate commerce clause since the day it first came up over the knobs."

### Chapter 32

Judge Ambrose K. Quimby sat very still for four minutes.

The courtroom was so quiet that Jesse could hear the soft, wet sound of Corinne Peake turning a page in her ledger.

Finally, the judge reached into his pocket, took out his gold pocket watch, and laid it beside his fountain pen.

"Miss Chen," the judge said.

The young law clerk stood up, her round spectacles gleaming in the winter sun.

"Yes, Your Honor?"

"Hand me the Virginia Compact of seventeen-eighty-nine," the judge said.

The clerk opened her leather briefcase, pulled out a thick, leather-bound folio with cracked corners, and laid it before the judge.

Judge Quimby did not open it. He rested his hand on the leather, his long, age-spotted fingers tapping the cover with a slow, deliberate rhythm.

"Mr. DuPree," the judge said.

"Yes, Your Honor?" DuPree said, rising smoothly.

"In seventeen-eighty-nine," Judge Quimby said, "when the General Assembly of Virginia consented to the creation of the State of Kentucky, they inserted a condition into the fourth article of the Compact. Do you know what that condition was?"

DuPree hesitated. His eyes shifted half an inch toward his junior partner, who was frantically thumbing through an iPad.

"I am generally familiar with the Compact, Your Honor, but—"

"The condition," Judge Quimby said, his voice rising with that hard, iron authority that had made three generations of corporate lawyers shiver in their boots, "was that all private rights and interests in lands, derived from the laws of Virginia prior to the separation, should remain valid and secure, and should be determined by the laws of Virginia as they then existed."

He leaned forward over the bench.

"And do you know what the law of Virginia was in seventeen-eighty-nine regarding the solemnization of marriages in the western counties?"

DuPree’s face was beginning to lose its southern polish. "Your Honor, modern domestic relations law—"

"The law of Virginia," Judge Quimby said, cutting through DuPree’s words like an axe through cedar, "was the *Act for the Relief of the Inhabitants of the Western Waters*, passed in October of seventeen-eighty. Because there were no ordained ministers of the Church of England within two hundred miles of the falls of the Ohio, the legislature authorized the county courts to appoint 'four reputable and discreet men' in each county to celebrate the rites of matrimony, under any form acceptable to the parties, under open sky or in any common station, without book, without surplice, and without the payment of any ecclesiastical fee."

He stood up.

Six feet two inches of Appalachian bone and sinew rose behind the oak bench, his white hair bristling like an eagle’s crest.

"That statute," the judge said, "has never been repealed. It was incorporated into the first Constitution of this Commonwealth in seventeen-ninety-two. It was reaffirmed in the Revision of eighteen-fifty. It was preserved in the Mountain Preacher Act of nineteen-seventy-six. It is the law of this land, Mr. DuPree, and has been since long before your client discovered that you could sell plastic hams to people on the internet."

He picked up his black fountain pen.

"The Court finds," Judge Quimby said, writing with long, fierce strokes that made the steel nib scratch loudly against the paper, "that the Independent Canopy of Whetstone County is the lawful, unbroken successor to the Western Waters Commissioners of seventeen-eighty; that its acts are civil solemnizations of absolute statutory validity; and that the Plaintiff corporation possesses no property, franchise, or equitable interest whatsoever within the boundaries of this county."

He stopped writing. He looked at Bishop T. Wayne Clapp.

"And furthermore," the judge said, his voice dropping into a register that shook the walnut wainscoting, "this Court finds that the attempt by a foreign corporation to invalidate four hundred marriages, cloud the titles to four hundred farms, and intimidate a seventy-four-year-old county registrar with two-million-dollar damage claims is a frivolous, vexatious, and bad-faith abuse of the judicial process."

He signed his name at the bottom of the order with a massive, four-inch flourish that ended in a sharp, black dot.

"The complaint is dismissed," Judge Quimby said. "With prejudice. Costs and attorney’s fees assessed against the Plaintiff. And if I see an Escalade with Tennessee plates within twenty rods of that courthouse before the next term of the grand jury, I will have the High Sheriff impound them for obstructing the road commissioner’s grader."

He brought his ash-wood cane down on the bench with a deafening *CRACK* that sounded like a rifle shot in the rafters.

"Court is adjourned," the judge said.

For two seconds, nobody breathed.

Then the rear doors of Courtroom 1 flew open.

Sheriff Wade Gidley threw his hat into the air, Toby Gidley let out a yell that set the pigeons flying from the tower, and three hundred people surged forward over the walnut benches in a great, roaring wave of denim, wool, and laughter that shook the courthouse to its limestone foundations.

### Chapter 33

On Friday evening, the celebration had moved to the *Coffee Cup* diner.

Minnie Cleary had taken the front doors off their hinges to let the crowd spill out onto the sidewalk of the square.

In the center of the diner, Judge Ambrose K. Quimby was sitting in the corner booth, his coat off, his starched sleeves rolled up two turns, eating a massive plate of fried catfish and hushpuppies with his fingers, while Corinne Peake explained to him why the 1874 survey of Jackson County was entirely the fault of an alcoholic chainman who had dropped his whiskey jug into a sinkhole.

*BONG.*

The sound rolled through the night, slow, magnificent, and deep, echoing from the river to the ridge, measuring out the covenants of the living and the sleep of the dead, while above them, beyond the snow, beyond the clouds, and beyond the reach of any court on earth, the high, unbroken canopy of heaven held up the world.

Then he pulled his collar up against the wind, put his hands in his pockets, and walked home to his clocks.
### Chapter 34

The spring thaw arrived in the second week of March, not with a gentle pastoral sigh, but with the violent, yellow, mud-slinging fury of thirty-four thousand tons of melting snow pouring off the limestone knobs into Whetstone Creek.

The creek did not merely rise; it became an engine of hydraulic destruction. It ripped two cedar-post water gaps out of Uncle Creed Fray’s bottom pasture, floated an empty five-hundred-gallon fuel oil tank three miles down to the county line, and deposited forty-two plastic bleach bottles and a dead Holstein steer in the middle of State Route 18 right at the entrance to Jesse’s lane.

Jesse was at the road at seven o'clock on a Tuesday morning, wearing his chest waders and his heavy rubber coat, hooking a log chain around the axle of the fuel tank to drag it into the ditch with his Dodge Ram, when the first Tesla arrived.

It was a pearl-white Model Y with custom black wheels and Franklin County, Ohio, registration plates. It had navigated eighteen miles of potholed blacktop, crossed two bridges that had lost their yellow warning signs in the flood of nineteen-ninety-seven, and had finally come to rest with its composite battery shield high-centered on an eight-inch slab of wet Saint Louis limestone right where Jesse’s culvert had washed out.

The driver’s door opened with a smooth, expensive, pneumatic click.

Out stepped a young man of about twenty-eight, wearing a charcoal-grey cashmere beanie that sat entirely above his ears, pristine beige trail-running shoes that had never touched clay, and a three-hundred-dollar waxed-canvas jacket that smelled faintly of bergamot and dry-cleaning solvent.

Behind him, from the passenger seat, stepped a young woman wearing an enormous cream-colored cable-knit sweater that hung down to her knees, round tortoise-shell spectacles, and a pair of yellow suede ankle boots that she immediately planted into three inches of cold, liquid red clay.

"Oh," she said, looking down at her left foot with the expression of someone who had stepped onto an unexploded landmine. "The texture is very... authentic."

The young man pulled an iPhone from his breast pocket, held it high in the air to search for a signal that had not existed in this hollow since the dawn of creation, and then looked at Jesse, who was standing knee-deep in yellow creek water with five feet of rusty log chain in his gloved hands.

"Excuse me, sir!" the young man shouted over the roar of the water. "We’re looking for the Presbyter!"

Jesse did not unhook the chain. He took off his knit cap, wrung two tablespoons of brown water out of the brim, and put it back on.

"The what?" Jesse asked.

"The Presbyter," the boy said, taking a step forward and immediately losing two inches of his beige running shoe into the ditch. "Reverend Jesse Callow. The Presbyter-at-Large of the Western Waters. We read about him in the Sunday *Courier-Journal*."

He pulled a folded clipping of newsprint from his pocket and held it out across the water.

Jesse looked at it. It was a three-column feature from the *Louisville Courier-Journal*’s lifestyle section, headlined:

*THE CANOPY OF THE COMMONS: How One Kentucky Clockmaker’s Ancient Legal Loophole Is Revolutionizing the Post-Denominational Nuptial Movement.*

There was a photograph of Jesse standing beside his drill press, looking like an escaped convict who had spent the night in an oil barrel, and a sub-headline that made Jesse’s left eyelid begin to twitch:

*Couples Fleeing Corporate Chapel Industry Flock to Upland Rebel Preacher for 'Authentic Dirt-Grounded Rituals' and Bartered Vows.*

"We’re Julian and Chloe," the young man said, smiling with two rows of teeth so white and even they looked like porcelain bathroom tile. "We’re from Bexley. Outside Columbus. We’ve been engaged for fourteen months, but we just couldn't align with the carbon footprint of the commercial venues in the Hocking Hills. And when we read about your radical agrarian ministry—the rejection of ecclesiastical structures, the bartered offerings, the pure sky—we just knew."

"You knew what?" Jesse asked, stepping out of the water. His rubber waders made a loud, wet *squish... squish... squish* in the mud.

"We knew we had to be solemnized here," Chloe said, her eyes shining behind her spectacles as she took Julian’s arm. "In the field. We brought our own artisanal goat’s-milk soap from an urban collective in Dayton for the pastoral tithe. And Julian brought his hand-carved cedar sound-bowls for the invocation."

Jesse looked at the goat’s-milk soap. He looked at the white car resting on his limestone culvert.

Then he looked up the hollow toward his house, where his cuckoo clocks were waiting, where three small-engine carburetors were soaking in kerosene, and where forty-two years of peace and quiet had just evaporated into the March mist.

"Son," Jesse said carefully, "the only sound-bowl in this hollow is an empty five-gallon galvanized water tub. And if you don't get that car off my rock before Toby Gidley comes through here with the road grader, he’s going to use your rear bumper to push that dead steer into the cedar bushes."

### Chapter 35

By Friday afternoon, the town of Meacham had lost its collective mind.

The *Courier-Journal* article had been picked up by the Associated Press, condensed into a three-minute human-interest segment on the Sunday morning network television broadcasts, and linked on sixteen different lifestyle blogs dedicated to "Anti-Consumerist Matrimony" and "Off-Grid Domestic Liturgies."

The result was a catastrophe of biblical proportions.

The Meacham Motor Lodge—six rooms with wood-paneled walls, chenille bedspreads, and rotary-dial telephones that had not seen an occupant since the hunting season of nineteen-ninety-four—was booked solid through October.

The six parking spaces in front of the Rexall drugstore were filled with Subarus, Volvos, and late-model pickups carrying roof-top tents, kayaks, and mountain bikes with six-hundred-dollar carbon-fiber handlebars.

Minnie Cleary had run out of country ham by eleven o'clock on Thursday morning and was currently attempting to explain to an organic architecture professor from Ann Arbor, Michigan, that she did not have oat milk, she did not have gluten-free biscuits, and that if he didn't want his eggs fried in bacon fat, he could walk down to the creek and eat watercress out of the ditch.

In the courthouse, the situation was worse.

The hallway outside the Registry of Deeds was jammed thirty deep with couples holding manila folders, color-coded swatches of raw linen, and acoustic guitars in canvas bags.

Corinne Peake sat behind her slate counter, her cardinal-red cardigan pulled tight around her shoulders, her purple stamp resting in its saucer like a loaded revolver.

Beside her stood County Judge-Executive Horace Vance, seventy-eight years old, wearing his Sunday Stetson hat and a string tie with a turquoise slide, his rheumy eyes gleaming with that dangerous, visionary madness that only strikes rural politicians when they realize they can double the county hotel tax.

"Corinne," Judge-Executive Vance whispered, leaning over the counter and smelling strongly of cloves and tobacco. "We can issue special out-of-county permits. Ten dollars a head for the road fund. Toby says we can pave the creek road all the way to the lime quarry by August."

"Horace," Corinne said, not looking up from her ledger. "If you say the word 'tourism' in this room one more time, I will take this pair of twelve-inch paper shears and cut that string tie right off your neck."

"Corinne, be reasonable!" Vance pleaded, waving a sheet of glossy stationery from the Lexington Chamber of Commerce. "People want to come here! They want the mountain experience! The boy from the hardware store sold fourteen pairs of five-buckle galoshes this morning to people from Chicago who wanted to walk in the mud! He charged them forty-eight dollars a pair!"

"Those galoshes," Corinne said coldly, "have been in his basement since nineteen-seventy-two. The rubber is rotten. The first time those people step in a cow track, the soles will come off and they'll be barefoot in the wild onion."

She turned her spectacles upon a young couple standing at the counter.

The boy was wearing a tweed vest over a denim shirt, a pair of wire-rimmed spectacles, and an enormous reddish-brown beard that had been oiled until it shone like a chestnut mare. The girl had a wildflower wreath in her hair that had already begun to wilt in the courthouse steam heat.

"Yes?" Corinne barked.

"Good morning, ma'am," the bearded boy said, speaking with that soft, breathy, reverent earnestness that made Corinne’s teeth ache. "We’re Liam and Meadow. We drove down from Bloomington. We’ve come to register our intent to be solemnized under the Canopy."

"Where’s your blood test?" Corinne asked.

Liam blinked. "Blood test? The Kentucky statute abolished the pre-marital serological examination in nineteen-ninety-eight."

"I don't care what the legislature abolished," Corinne snapped. "In this county, we don't issue a license to anybody who hasn't been checked for the measles, the mumps, and the red-water fever. And where are your deacons?"

Meadow touched her wildflower wreath. "We were hoping... well, we read that the Presbyter provides the deacons from the local flock."

"The deacons," Corinne said, "are currently down on Route 18 trying to drag an eighty-pound piece of cast iron out of a culvert. If you want Deacon Vance, you’ll have to go to the nursing home, shout into his good ear for twenty minutes, and change his slippers. And if you want Deacon Cates, he’s in the salt truck and he doesn't talk to anybody who doesn't have an International Harvester parts catalog in their hand."

She reached into her drawer, pulled out a stack of yellow printed slips, and slammed them on the counter.

"This is the Whetstone County Administrative Rule for Non-Resident Solemnizations," she announced in a voice that reached all thirty couples in the hallway.

Liam picked up the slip. His eyes widened.

"Rule One," he read aloud. "*All applicants residing outside the Commonwealth of Virginia Land Grants of seventeen-eighty-nine must present three certified letters from their home precincts proving they have never sued a neighbor over a line fence, have never belonged to a country club, and can change a flat tire on an unpaved road without the assistance of a motor club.*"

He looked up at Corinne, his mouth slightly open. "Madam, this is unconstitutional under the Privileges and Immunities Clause."

Corinne picked up her heavy shears.

*Crunch.*

She cut an errant thread from the hem of an auctioneer’s bond.

"Son," Corinne said, "in this room, the Privileges and Immunities Clause is what I say it is. Now get out of that doorway; you’re blocking the boy from the feed store with my clean towels."

### Chapter 36

At six o'clock on Saturday morning, Jesse was in his workshop with the doors locked.

He had turned off the yard light, disconnected the telephone by pulling the four-prong plug out of the baseboard, and had drawn the green roller-shades over the windows.

Outside, parked along the shoulder of Route 18, were nine cars: two from Ohio, three from Indiana, one from Illinois, and three from the suburbs of Louisville, their occupants sitting with their heaters running, waiting for the "Rebel Preacher" to open his doors.

Jesse sat on his plywood stool, drinking black coffee from a tin cup, staring at a half-disassembled brass lantern clock that had been made in London in 1710.

A sharp, rhythmic tap came at the back window—the small, square one that looked out into the sumac bushes.

*Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap.*

Jesse stood up, walked to the window, and lifted the edge of the shade.

Lindy Gidley was standing in the briars, wearing her yellow slicker and a pair of mud-caked knee boots, holding an olive-drab canvas rucksack against her stomach.

Jesse slid the sash up three inches. "Lindy?"

"Open the cellar door," she whispered. "Toby’s in the ditch with the tractor."

Jesse unlocked the heavy oak door that led down into the stone root cellar beneath the shop. Two minutes later, Lindy came up the wooden steps, her boots leaving wet, red clay tracks across the clean pine floor.

She set the rucksack on the zinc bench with a heavy, metallic *clonk*.

"What is that?" Jesse asked.

"Granddaddy," Lindy said. She was breathing hard, her dark hair damp with sweat and rain, her grey eyes wide and fierce.

Jesse looked at the rucksack. "Old man Sells?"

"Not him," she said, stripping off her neoprene gloves. "What he buried."

She reached into the canvas bag and pulled out a heavy rectangular box, sixteen inches long, eight inches wide, and six inches deep, made of thick sheet zinc, its seams soldered with heavy beads of grey lead.

The top of the box had been coated with black roofing pitch that had dried into a cracked, alligator-skin crust, smelling faintly of coal tar and old limestone earth.

"Toby was digging the footing for the new milk-house," Lindy said, leaning against the bench. "Right where the old south bay of the barn met the rock ledge. The tooth of the backhoe bucket caught the corner of this. It was buried four feet deep, wrapped in two layers of greased burlap, under a flat slab of Jackson County limestone that had three notches cut into the edge."

Jesse reached for his linoleum knife.

"Don't cut it," Lindy said, reaching into her pocket. "There's a key."

She produced a small, flat brass key attached to a piece of faded red fishing line.

In the center of the zinc lid, beneath the crust of pitch, was a small brass padlock, its shackle wrapped in greased tape.

Jesse scraped the pitch from the keyhole with the tip of his scraper, fitted the brass key, and turned it.

The lock gave a dry, stiff *click*.

Jesse lifted the zinc lid.

The box was lined with dry, yellowed flannel from an old work shirt.

Inside were four items:
1. A Colt Model 1911 automatic pistol, caliber .45, wrapped in an oiled piece of sheepskin, with two loaded magazines;
2. A green canvas-bound ledger labeled *SPECIAL MEMORANDA: DISTRICT 3 (1950–1955)*;
3. A five-pound sack of silver half-dollars, dated between 1934 and 1951, tied with copper wire; and
4. An unrecorded marriage certificate, dated November 14, 1952, printed on heavy legal vellum by a printer in Cincinnati.

Jesse picked up the certificate.

His hands, which had handled four hundred licenses without trembling, shook slightly as he brought the vellum close to the yellow light of his work lamp.

The names on the certificate were:

*GROOM: Donald E. Vance (Age 22, of Whetstone County)*  
*BRIDE: Martha Corinne Peake (Age 20, of Jackson County)*

Jesse’s breath stopped in his throat.

"Corinne," he whispered.

"Corinne," Lindy said, her voice dropping into a register so low it was almost lost beneath the ticking of the clocks on the wall. "Look at who signed it, Jesse."

Jesse looked at the bottom of the vellum.

There, on line fourteen, written in dark, purple ink that had faded to the color of dried lavender, was the signature of the officiant:

*Luther M. Callow, Presbyter.*  
*The Apostolic Assembly of the Open Air.*

Below the signature, written in old Silas Sells’s sharp, cantankerous pencil, was an endorsement:

*Solemnized at midnight in the new barn, on the county line, while Sheriff Claggett of Jackson was at the north door with a warrant for Donald’s arrest for draft evasion, and Sheriff Peake of Whetstone was at the south door with a shotgun to keep Claggett from crossing the timber.*

### Chapter 37

For ten minutes, neither of them spoke.

The workshop was completely silent except for the rhythmic, metallic heartbeat of the Ansonia clock on the test-shelf:

*Tick... tock... tick... tock.*

Outside, the rain had begun to turn into a steady, grey upland drizzle that drummed on the tin roof of the lean-to with a soft, hypnotic hiss.

Jesse laid the vellum back into the zinc box, his fingers resting on the dry flannel.

"Donald Vance," Jesse said slowly. "Arthur Vance’s older brother."

"The one who was killed in Korea," Lindy said. "At the Chosin Reservoir. In December of nineteen-fifty-two."

Jesse closed his eyes. The fragmented pieces of county history that he had heard all his life—whispered behind the woodbox at the store, hinted at during funeral dinners, alluded to by old men sitting on the courthouse benches—suddenly fell together with the clean, terrifying precision of an eight-day gear train.

"Corinne was never married," Jesse murmured. "That's what everybody in the county always said. That she was an old maid who stayed in the registry to take care of her father."

"She wasn't an old maid," Lindy said. "She was a widow at twenty. But Donald Vance’s family was Primitive Baptist from the knobs, and Corinne’s father was the High Sheriff of Whetstone County and an elder in the Christian Church. Her father told Donald that if he ever set foot on Peake land, he’d have him indicted by the grand jury for stealing timber."

She pulled the canvas ledger toward her.

"When Donald got his draft notice for the army," Lindy continued, reading from Silas Sells’s cramped pencil notes, "he had seventy-two hours before the troop train left Bowling Green. Corinne slipped out of her bedroom window in Meacham, walked five miles through the creek bottoms in the dark, and met Donald at granddaddy’s barn."

"And your granddaddy?" Jesse asked.

"Granddaddy built the barn that summer," Lindy said, a small, fierce smile touching her lips. "He built it right on the line because he knew that if Donald stood on the south side of the sill, he was in Whetstone, where the Jackson sheriff couldn't serve a warrant; and if Corinne stood on the north side, she was in Jackson, where her father didn't have jurisdiction to stop the ceremony."

She tapped the signature on the vellum.

"Your daddy, Jesse. Luther. He was twenty-eight years old. He had just come back from the Navy in the Pacific. He had the nineteen-twenty-eight charter that he got from old Silas Gidley. He stood on the center sill, right over the boundary stone, with his flashlight in his teeth, and he married them in four minutes while the two sheriffs were arguing in the mud outside."

Jesse looked down at his own hands.

"And Donald went to Korea," Jesse said.

"Donald went to Korea on the morning train," Lindy said. "Three weeks later, he was dead. The certificate was never recorded because Corinne’s father was the clerk who kept the books, and Donald’s mother swore that if a Peake girl ever claimed Donald’s military insurance, she’d burn the courthouse down."

She looked at Jesse, her grey eyes steady, deep, and utterly clear.

"Granddaddy put the certificate in this box," she said. "He put Donald’s watch and his letters in with it, soldered the lid shut with lead, and buried it under the foundation stone. And Corinne went into that office in nineteen-fifty-four, took her father’s place at that counter, and she’s been sitting there ever since."

Jesse stood up from his milk can.

He picked up the zinc box, closed the heavy lid, and clicked the brass padlock shut.

"Lindy," Jesse said.

"Yeah, Jesse?"

"Get the truck," Jesse said. "We're going to the courthouse."

### Chapter 38

It was noon on Saturday when the green Dodge Ram rattled into the square at Meacham.

The rain had slacked, but the sky was the color of a wet slate shingle, hanging low and heavy over the clock tower.

The hallway of the courthouse was still crowded with couples from Ohio and Indiana, sitting on their rucksacks, eating trail mix out of plastic bags, and reading aloud from paperbacks about Celtic handfasting rituals.

Jesse did not look at them.

He walked down the corridor with the zinc box tucked under his left arm, his heavy rubber boots leaving wet tracks on the linoleum, with Lindy and Toby walking directly behind him like two armed guards escorting a payroll.

He pushed open the frosted-glass door of the Registry of Deeds.

The brass bell gave its sharp *clink*.

Corinne Peake was sitting alone behind the slate counter. The office was quiet; she had locked the inner gate and had turned the cardboard sign on the counter to read: *OFFICE CLOSED FOR AUDIT OF VITAL INDICES.*

She was drinking tea from an ancient china cup that had a chip out of the saucer, wearing her black seal-plush coat because the wind was coming down the chimney.

When she saw the zinc box under Jesse’s arm, she did not move.

Her hand, which had been reaching for a cube of sugar, remained suspended in the air for five seconds.

Then her eyes shifted to Lindy’s face.

"Where was it?" Corinne asked. Her voice was not loud; it was flat, thin, and had that dry, brittle quality of an autumn leaf that had survived two frosts on a high limb.

"Under the south sill, Aunt Corinne," Lindy said gently. "Where the limestone ledge drops into the branch."

Corinne set her cup down. The china made no sound against the saucer.

She stood up, walked to the counter, and lifted the hinged slate flap.

"Come inside," she said.

They followed her into the vault.

The heavy olive-drab steel door clicked shut behind them, cutting off the murmur of the tourists in the hallway, cutting off the sound of the rain, cutting off seventy years of noise.

The vault was cold, smelling of vinegar, leather, and damp lime, illuminated by the single humming fluorescent tube overhead.

Jesse set the zinc box on the high oak examination table where the title searchers worked.

He laid the small brass key beside it.

Corinne did not touch the key immediately. She took off her spectacles, wiped her watery blue eyes with her linen handkerchief, and looked down at the black, pitch-crusted lid.

"Donald had a blue wool sweater," she said softly, speaking to the steel shelves of deeds. "His sister knitted it for him out of coarse wool that smelled like sheep dip. It was too long in the arms, and he had the cuffs turned back twice so he could hold my fingers."

She reached out with her right hand—the hand that had held the purple stamp for forty years, the hand that had certified four hundred and two marriages, the hand that had held the line against two counties and the United States District Court.

Her fingers touched the cold, lead-soldered rim of the box.

"Your father, Jesse," she whispered. "Luther had grease on his collar that night. He had been working on a stationary baler for Silas all afternoon. When he said the words about the three-fold cord, a cinder fell out of his hair and landed on the vellum."

She picked up the brass key.

She did not hesitate. She inserted it into the padlock, turned it with a firm, sharp twist, and lifted the lid.

She did not look at the Colt automatic. She did not look at the silver half-dollars.

She reached straight into the yellow flannel, took out the Cincinnati vellum, and unfolded it with slow, reverent fingers.

There, in the center of the certificate, between the names of Donald Vance and Martha Corinne Peake, was the small, dark brown scorch-mark where Luther Callow’s cinder had burned through the paper seventy-two years ago.

Corinne looked at it for a long time.

Two large, clear tears formed at the corners of her eyes, ran down the deep wrinkles of her cheeks, and fell onto the slate counter with two tiny, soft *taps*.

Then she reached into her apron pocket.

She produced her square bottle of purple ink, her steel dip-pen, and her four-pound brass seal of 1928.

She turned the vellum over to the endorsement panel on the back.

In her sharp, magnificent, upright hand—the hand that had never wavered, never blotted, and never surrendered a single link of Captain Trumbo’s chain—she wrote:

*Received and entered in the Permanent Register of Whetstone County, this 15th day of March, 2025. Ceremony confirmed as a Canonical Solemnization under the Open Canopy of Heaven, pursuant to the Act of 1928 and the Virginia Compact of 1789.*

She dipped the steel pen into the purple ink.

*Signed: Martha Corinne Peake Vance, Registrar & Widow.*

She placed the vellum between the heavy brass dies of the seal.

She leaned on the screw-press with her full weight.

*CRUNCH.*

The purple seal bit into the old vellum, deep, permanent, and eternal, sealing the covenant that had been made under the hemlock rafters while the sheriffs argued in the dark.

Corinne lifted the paper. She blew gently on the purple ink.

Then she walked to the green steel drawer labeled *1950–1955*, slid it open on its rumbling bearings, and placed the certificate into the very first slot, right at the front, wrapped in a fresh length of red cloth ribbon.

She slammed the drawer shut.

*Clack.*

She turned to Jesse. Her face was pale, but her eyes were clear, bright, and fierce with a light that Jesse had never seen before.

"Jesse," she said.

"Yes, Corinne?"

"Open that door," she commanded, pointing toward the hallway. "And let those people from Ohio in here."

### Chapter 39

The "Whetstone Test" was instituted on Monday morning at eight-thirty precisely.

It was not written on parchment; it was typed by Corinne Peake on the Underwood manual typewriter, using a fresh black-and-red ribbon that Toby had bought at an office supply store in Bowling Green, printed on four sheets of twenty-pound bond paper, and tacked to the outside of the Registry door with six brass carpet tacks.

It was titled:

*REGULATIONS CONCERNING APPLICANTS FOR SOLEMNIZATION UNDER THE CANOPY OF WHETSTONE COUNTY.*  
*Promulgated by the Registrar and the Presbytery-at-Large.*

> *Item 1: The Principle of Secular Labor. Matrimony being a state of mutual agricultural and domestic endurance, no couple shall be solemnized who has not demonstrated the capacity to perform six hours of manual labor on the public works of this county. Groom applicants shall report to Road Commissioner Gidley at the county barn at seven o'clock A.M. with an eight-pound sledgehammer, a round-point shovel, and a lunch bucket containing not less than two thick sandwiches of cured meat. Bride applicants shall report to Mrs. Lindy Gidley at the Extension Office to assist in the washing of milk testing bottles, the preparation of dried-apple schnitz, or the cleaning of the churchyard at Mount Zion.*  
> *Item 2: The Assessment of Temper. Any applicant who displays impatience, speaks sharply to a county official, inquires concerning the availability of cellular telephone service, or requests an alteration in the weather shall be dismissed immediately and referred to the commercial facilities in the State of Tennessee.*  
> *Item 3: The Canonical Offering. The Presbytery accepts no legal tender, paper currency, bank drafts, or electronic transfers. Consideration must consist of a useful domestic commodity produced by the labor of the parties, including but not limited to: ten bushels of clean winter potatoes; four gallons of sorghum molasses; two cured middlings of pork; or the complete mechanical overhaul of an eight-horsepower horizontal-shaft engine.*  
> *Item 4: The Weather. Ceremonies are conducted under the sky. If it rains, the parties shall get wet. If it sleets, the parties shall shiver. The Presbytery does not provide umbrellas, patio heaters, or dry-ice effects.*

The effect of the Whetstone Test upon the tourist population was instantaneous and miraculous.

By nine-thirty on Monday morning, forty-two of the sixty couples waiting in the hallway had packed their rucksacks, climbed into their Subarus, and were heading back toward the blue-grass parkway at sixty-five miles an hour.

The young man with the oiled beard from Bloomington had taken one look at Toby Gidley’s eight-pound sledgehammer and had announced that his personal chiropractor forbade repetitive concussive impacts, after which Toby had offered to use the sledgehammer to adjust his alignment for nothing.

The girl with the wildflower wreath had burst into tears when Lindy handed her a three-gallon bucket of lye soap and an iron scraper and pointed her toward the churchyard gate.

By noon, only three couples remained.

The first was a young fellow named Caleb Weaver, twenty-two years old, from a dirt farm up in Powell County, and his girl, Sarah, who had driven down in a nineteen-ninety-four Ford Ranger with a wooden rack on the bed. Caleb had spent four hours in the ditch on Route 18 with Toby, swinging a bush-hook through sumac sprouts until his hands were blistered, while Sarah had helped Lindy peel three bushels of winter apples without saying five words.

The second was an older couple from Louisville—a retired machinist from the naval ordnance station named Frank, sixty-four, and a woman named Mary who had worked thirty years in a laundry—who had arrived in an old station wagon with two five-gallon cans of kerosene and a freshly rebore cylinder for an old International tractor that Frank had turned on his own lathe in his garage.

The third was Liam and Meadow—the boy from Bexley who had high-centered his Tesla on Jesse’s culvert.

Julian had not gone back to Ohio.

He was currently standing in the gravel pit behind Toby’s maintenance barn, wearing an old pair of Toby’s work trousers that were four inches too short and three inches too wide, his cashmere beanie replaced by an orange lime-quarry cap, loading number-two limestone into the back of a dump truck with a square-point shovel.

His hands were bleeding through his yellow gloves, his beige running shoes were entirely encased in grey limestone mud, and his face was streaked with sweat and rock dust.

Beside him was Chloe.

Her cream-colored sweater was tied around her waist by the sleeves; she was wearing an old denim shirt that Lindy had lent her, her yellow suede boots were ruined forever, and she was currently using a steel wire brush to scrape the rust off the trip-springs of Billy Cates’s salt blade.

Jesse walked up to the edge of the pit.

He stood with his hands in his pockets, watching Julian heave a shovel-full of stone into the high steel bed with a rhythmic, grunting effort that shook his thin shoulders.

"Julian," Jesse called down.

Julian stopped his shovel. He leaned on the ash handle, wiped his forehead with his forearm, and looked up through the dust.

"Yes, Mr. Callow?"

"The culvert is fixed," Jesse said.

Julian looked at his shovel. Then he looked at Chloe, who had two streaks of black grease across her forehead and a smudge of iron rust on her nose.

"Mr. Callow," Julian said, his voice hoarse from limestone dust, "we don't want the sound-bowls anymore."

"You don't?"

"No, sir," Julian said, taking a deep breath of the cold March air. "We just want the two verses. And if you’ve got an extra pair of socks in that truck, I’d be obliged to borrow them."

Jesse smiled. He took off his knit cap, looked down into the pit, and nodded his head.

"Toby!" Jesse shouted toward the maintenance shed.

"Yeah, Jesse?" Toby called back from under the hood of a dump truck.

"Get Billy," Jesse said. "And tell Lindy to put the kettle on. We’ve got three weddings at the pavilion at four o'clock."

### Chapter 40

The three weddings were performed together, just as the sun was beginning to drop behind the dark cedar knobs to the west.

The sky had cleared—not to a soft, summer blue, but to that sharp, cold, pale turquoise that belongs to early spring in the uplands, when the air is so clear you can see the dead chestnut snags on the top of the ridge five miles away.

The new green steel roof of the pavilion caught the low, yellow rays of the sun, glowing like an emerald against the grey limestone gravel.

Tied to the four cedar posts, the four strips of white linen fluttered in the evening breeze with a soft, dry flutter that sounded like the rustle of dry fescue.

Standing around the edge of the stone were sixty people from the county:
- Donnie Cleary and his wife, with their boys running through the dead clover;
- Darrell Sneed, holding his baby;
- Virgil Poteet and Bernice, Virgil wearing his good suit and Bernice holding a jar of pickled watermelon rinds for the wedding dinner;
- Lyle Claggett, who had driven over from Jackson County in his work boots, holding the hand of his fiancée from Clark County; and
- Sheriff Wade Gidley, leaning against his cruiser, drinking coffee from his thermos.

Corinne Peake sat in her oak rocker at the front of the pavilion.

She was wearing her dark green cardigan with the acorn buttons, her purple stamp in her lap, her black leather folio open on her knees.

Jesse Callow stood on the limestone slab beneath the green steel gable.

He wore his blue suit, his boots shined with black paste, with the 1928 Book of Common Shelter open in his hands.

Before him stood the three couples:
- Caleb and Sarah, Caleb holding his hat against his thigh with his blistered hands, Sarah with three sprigs of cedar pinned to her coat;
- Frank and Mary, Frank with his machinist’s square sticking out of his coat pocket, Mary holding a small bunch of dried lavender from her garden; and
- Julian and Chloe, Julian wearing his orange quarry cap and Toby’s high-water work trousers, Chloe with her hair tied back with green baling twine, her fingers locked into Julian’s with a grip that turned her knuckles white.

Jesse looked out over them.

He saw the blistered hands; he saw the stone dust; he saw the seventy years of memory that had been buried in the zinc box beneath the foundation stones.

He raised his voice.

"Friends," Jesse said.

The wind on the hill died away. The creek below the road murmured over its yellow rocks, clear, cool, and steady, running down toward the river.

"In this world," Jesse said, "there are people who will tell you that a promise is only as good as the paper it’s written on. They will tell you that you need an office in an industrial concourse, an insurance policy from Nashville, and a seal that was bought from a court in Indiana."

He shook his head slowly.

"They are wrong," Jesse said. "A promise is like an eight-day clock. It’s made of common iron, shaped by hard hands, and set level on the rock. It doesn't need a golden case, and it doesn't need an electric cord. It only needs two people who have the courage to take the weight of the years, to catch it on the stones, and to let it tick through the frost, through the flood, and through the long, dark nights until the weights hit the bottom of the case."

He looked at Caleb and Sarah. He looked at Frank and Mary. He looked at Julian and Chloe.

"Do you take each other," Jesse asked, "under the wide sky above us, in the presence of these witnesses and before the records of this county, to have and to hold, to labor and to endure, so long as the seasons return and the creek runs to the river?"

"We do," the three men said together.

"We do," the three women answered, their voices clear and strong against the twilight.

Jesse closed the book.

*Crunch.*

### Chapter 41

The evening ended as all good upland evenings do: with the smell of woodsmoke, the music of Arthur’s mandolin drifting across the fescue, and forty people sitting on the tailgates of trucks eating ham biscuits and dried-apple stack cake under the stars.

At ten o'clock, Jesse Callow walked back up the road to his house.

Jesse walked into his workshop.

There was the Ansonia mantle clock, with its new phosphor-bronze bushing; there was the German cuckoo clock that had lost its weights; there was the Gothic cathedral clock that Billy Cates had brought from the flood; and high in the corner was an old Seth Thomas drop-octagon that his father had built in nineteen-sixty-two.

They did not tick together.

One struck fast and light; one struck slow and deep; two ticked with a dry, irregular chatter like cicadas in an August hayfield.

Together, they made a great, continuous, rhythmic tumble of sound that filled the low room, measuring out the seconds, the minutes, the hours, and the generations with that patient, unhurried certainty that outlasts all the courts, all the synods, and all the receivers on earth.

Jesse reached into his coat pocket.

He pulled out his green marbled notebook—the one in which he had recorded the chain-saw serial numbers, the cider mill gears, and the four hundred and two marriages.

He opened it to the last page.

With his blue ballpoint pen, he wrote three lines below the entry for Billy Cates:

*March 17, 2025.*  
*Solemnized at the Pavilion: Caleb & Sarah; Frank & Mary; Julian & Chloe.*  
*Consideration: Six hours with the sledge; two cans of oil; and one clean culvert.*  
*Status: Established under the High Canopy.*  
*Total to date: 405.*

Jesse closed the notebook. He laid it on the shelf between the can of number-four cotter pins and the jar of peach brandy that Virgil Poteet had given him.

Then he walked to the back window, pulled the green roller-shade up to the top of the frame, and looked out into the night.

The moon had risen over the knobs, large, round, and silver, casting a clean, white light across the five acres of limestone pasture, illuminating the old oak trees along the fence, the dark rectangle of the garden, and the quiet line of the creek winding through the hollow.

Above it all, stretching from the river to the mountains, deep, silent, and eternal, was the sky.

It had no walls. It had no rafters. It had no quarterly reports to file, and it had never asked a single man for twenty-two dollars to prove that he was standing under it.

It was enough.

Jesse reached out his hand, blew softly across the top of the lantern chimney, and let the darkness settle over the room.

The clocks ticked on into the night.

3. A five-pound coffee can filled with miscellaneous brass clock gears that Jesse had swept up from the floor of a jeweler’s shop in Meacham that had burned in nineteen-seventy-eight.

Jesse smiled beneath his loupe.

"In Whetstone County, Julian," Jesse said, "we’re a recoil. We’ve been pushed backward forty times by the high water, by the state road department, and by the people in Indiana. But every time the weight pulls on the cable, we pick up the beat and we tick on."

He laid down his pick, took off his loupes, and pointed to the rusted Briggs & Stratton engine.

### Chapter 43

The ten-forty-five Greyhound from Louisville stopped at the Sunoco station in Meacham on the second Monday of May.

### Chapter 46

The Fourth of July was a day that Whetstone County would talk about until the children who were in arms were carried to the cemetery on the hill.

The morning broke cool and clear, with a light fog rising from Whetstone Creek that burned away by eight o'clock, leaving the limestone hills sharp and green against a sky of pure, unbroken cobalt.

By ten o'clock, forty acres of fescue bottomland on the Gidley farm were filled with vehicles:
- Four hundred and eight pickup trucks, cars, and station wagons, arranged in sixteen neat rows by Billy Cates and the highway crew;
- The road department’s lowboy trailer, parked beneath the walnut trees, carrying Sheriff Wade Gidley’s overturned rowboat, in which Mrs. Wade Gidley was sitting comfortably on her green vinyl cushions under a beach umbrella, holding a plate of fried chicken and waving to her cousins;
- A stage built of heavy oak bridge planks laid over twenty round bales of straw, where Miss Eleanor Vance and thirteen upland fiddlers were playing *Soldier’s Joy* with such fury that two hound dogs were howling in rhythm behind the water tank; and
- A trench fifty feet long and four feet deep, where four whole hogs had been smoking over hickory coals since three o'clock on Thursday afternoon under the direction of Darrell Vance and three men from the lime quarry.

At noon, Jesse Callow climbed the steps of the straw stage.

He was wearing his blue suit, his boots shined, carrying his green marbled notebook and the 1928 Book of Common Shelter.

Beside him stood:
- Toby Gidley, holding his youngest girl on his shoulder;
- Lindy Gidley, holding the leather-bound 1874 survey map;
- Corinne Peake, sitting in an oak armchair that four road workers had carried up onto the straw bales; and
- Julian and Chloe, Julian holding a small brass clock movement that he had finished that morning, ticking steadily in the sunlight.

Eight hundred and twenty people stood in a wide, dense semicircle around the stage.

They were all there:
- Donnie Cleary and his wife, with their twin boys now big enough to carry four-pound bags of ice;
- Darrell Sneed and Brenda, their three children eating watermelon slices on the grass;
- Virgil Poteet and Bernice, Virgil wearing his wedding shoes (which were now scuffed at the toes from working in the garden) and Bernice holding a grand-baby that had been born in May;
- Lonnie and Arlene Fray, with seven children now, Uncle Creed sitting beside them on an overturned mineral tub;
- Frank and Mary, the machinist from Louisville, who had brought six re-ground crankshafts for the county tractors as their anniversary tithe;
- Caleb and Sarah from Powell County, their old Ford Ranger loaded with twenty bushels of clean new potatoes for the food pantry; and
- The couple who had been married on the limestone kiln, the couple from the apple orchard, the couple from the combine cab, and the boy who had dropped the ring down the intake vent, who was now thirty years old and had brought his own nine-year-old son to see the place where the ring had fallen.

The sun was high overhead, warm, golden, and vast, casting no shadows on the straw.

From the town of Meacham, three miles down the valley, came the deep, bronze roar of the Seth Thomas tower clock striking twelve:

*BONG.*  
*BONG.*  
*BONG.*

The sound drifted over the creek, slow, magnificent, and sweet, carried on the warm summer wind through the willow tops and across the clover pastures.

Jesse opened the green notebook.

"Friends," he said.

The fiddles stopped. The children went quiet. Even the hound dogs beneath the water tank laid their heads on their paws and listened.

"Thirteen years ago," Jesse said, "I took an ordination because a boy and a girl wanted to be married and didn't have anywhere else to go."

He looked at Arlene and Lonnie in the front row.

"I didn't have a church," Jesse said. "I didn't have a robe, and I didn't have a choir. I had five acres of briars, a set of clock wrenches, and an old Dodge truck with a loose tie-rod."

A quiet laugh rippled through the crowd.

"The people in Indiana," Jesse said, holding up the black book, "told me that if I didn't have two deacons and four pieces of linen, the marriages didn't exist. They told me that the words were irregular. They told me that the sky wasn't a church."

He closed the notebook. He set it on the straw bale beside Corinne’s chair.

He raised both hands toward the wide, blue heavens that reached from horizon to horizon, unbroken, untaxed, and free.

"Look up," Jesse said.

Eight hundred and twenty people tilted their heads back and looked into the high, blue bowl of the sky.

"There is your roof," Jesse said, his voice rising clear and strong over the bottomland. "It was here when Captain Trumbo dropped his telegraph wire in the rocks. It was here when Silas Sells hid his still in the sinkhole. It was here when Donald Vance put his ring on Corinne Peake’s finger while the sheriffs were shouting in the road."

He looked down at them, his eyes bright with that quiet, deep happiness that only comes to a man who has done his work with his own hands and seen that it was good.

"It has held up your fathers," Jesse said. "It has held up your mothers. It has held up four hundred and eight of your covenants through the frost, through the flood, and through the hard years when the corn was short."

He paused, his voice dropping into that gentle, unhurried rhythm that had comforted eighty different mothers in eighty different pastures.

"Hold to each other," Jesse said. "Keep your oil clean. Keep your weights level. And never let any man tell you that a promise made under God’s open air is anything less than eternal."

For three seconds, the valley was as still as a churchyard at dawn.

Then Corinne Peake stood up from her chair.

She did not use her cane. She stood straight and tall in her lilac cardigan, her white hair shining like silver in the noon sun, and raised her right hand.

"Amen!" Corinne shouted in a voice that reached all forty acres. "Now blow that whistle, Toby, and let the people eat!"

Toby Gidley reached into the cab of the International, grabbed the lanyard of the dual air horns, and pulled it down with all his might.

*BLARE.*

The horns roared; the crowd cheered—a great, thunderous, rolling roar of eight hundred voices that echoed off the limestone knobs, bounced across the river, and rolled all the way to Jackson County—and the fiddles struck up *The Eighth of January* as three hundred people surged toward the barbecue trench with paper plates in their hands.

### Chapter 47

At four o'clock in the afternoon, while the young people were playing horseshoes behind the pavilion and the old men were sitting on the running boards of the trucks talking about the price of feeder calves, a small ceremony took place in the shade of the walnut trees.

Corinne Peake sat at a folding table that had been covered with a white linen tablecloth from her mother’s chest.

On the table sat:

- The large leather ledger of Marriage Book 41;
- The square bottle of purple ink; and
- A clean, new commission certificate printed on heavy cream bond paper by the state printer in Frankfort.

Standing before the table was Lindy Gidley, wearing a clean blue cotton dress with a white collar, her dark hair combed and pinned with two silver combs that Toby had given her for their fifth anniversary.

Lindy touched the smooth, worn plastic of the machine. Her eyes were bright with tears, but her chin was high and steady.

"Yes, Aunt Corinne," she whispered.

Corinne looked at Harlan Grote, who was standing behind Lindy with his black catalog case under his arm, his spectacles shining in the green shade of the walnuts.

"And Harlan," Corinne said.

Grote stepped forward. "Yes, Mrs. Vance?"

"The key to the vault," she said, pulling a heavy brass key from her pocket and laying it beside the eraser. "It has to be turned twice to the right and lifted slightly on the latch, or the bolt will bind. You keep it in your right-hand pocket, and don't you ever let anybody from the Department of Transportation take it to look at the easement maps while I'm at my lunch."

Grote looked down at the brass key. He took it with two fingers, his face as pale, reverent, and solemn as a priest receiving the vessels of the altar.

"Madam," Grote said, his voice trembling with a quiet, boundless pride, "the records will be maintained in eternal order."

### Chapter 48

At seven o'clock that evening, while the sun was beginning to drop behind the western hills in a long, blazing blaze of orange, crimson, and gold, Jesse Callow walked with Julian up to the high pasture behind his workshop.

The air was cooling down, carrying the crisp, sharp scent of evening dew falling on warm limestone, and the sound of the celebration down in the bottom was becoming distant, muffled by the timber, like the sound of an old radio playing in another room.

They sat down on the flat limestone ledge where Captain Trumbo’s white oak stump had rotted away.

Jesse reached into his coat pocket.

He pulled out a small wooden box made of seasoned walnut, with brass corner brackets and a sliding lid, about six inches long and three inches wide.

He handed it to Julian.

Julian took the box. He slid the walnut lid back.

Inside, resting on a bed of dry cotton fleece, was:
1. A pair of antique steel hairspring calipers, with ivory tips, made in Sheffield in 1860;
2. A small, three-cornered scraper ground down from an old file, with a polished apple-wood handle; and
3. A small, pocket-sized notebook bound in black oilcloth, on the first page of which Jesse had written in his neat, clockmaker’s hand:  
   *THE CANONICAL WORDS OF THE OPEN CANOPY.*  
   *As recited from memory in the fields of Whetstone County.*

Julian looked at the scraper. He touched the sharp, polished steel with the tip of his thumb.

"Jesse," Julian said, looking out over the valley where the lights were beginning to appear in the windows of twenty different farms. "I’m from Ohio. I grew up in a subdivision where the houses had three garages and the lawns were sprayed with green dye every Thursday morning."

Jesse looked out over the knobs.

"A man can't help where he was born, Julian," Jesse said mildly. "My grandfather was born in a shanty boat on the Ohio River during the panic of ninety-three. The only thing that matters is where you put your feet down when the frost comes."

Julian closed the walnut box. He slid the lid shut with a clean, tight *click*.

"The words, Jesse," Julian said. "What if I forget them?"

Jesse smiled. He reached out and tapped Julian’s chest, right over the pocket where his work gloves were tucked.

"You won't forget them, son," Jesse said. "The words aren't complicated. You tell them that the grass withers and the flower fades, but the promise they make to each other is made of the same iron that holds the stars in their tracks. You tell them to be patient with the broken bolts, to keep the dirt out of the bearings, and never to let the sun go down while they're still holding a grudge over a line fence."

He stood up.

Below them, in the dark valley, the red taillights of the trucks were beginning to move slowly down the lane, their headlights sweeping across the fescue like long, golden fingers, heading home toward sixty different hollows under the warm summer night.

High above the town of Meacham, the Seth Thomas clock struck nine.

*BONG.*

The sound drifted up the hill, slow, magnificent, and sure, measuring out the seconds, the hours, the seasons, and the generations with that patient, unhurried certainty that outlasts all the courts, all the synods, and all the receivers on earth.

Jesse turned to Julian.

"Come on, Julian," Jesse said, pulling his coat around his shoulders against the rising dew. "We’ve got that Ansonia clock to deliver to the Miller girl’s grandmother in the morning, and if we don't get those weights balanced tonight, she’ll give that five-pound bucket of country lard to her cousin."

They walked down the slope together, their boots making a soft, swishing sound in the dark clover, their shadows stretching out before them under the pale light of the rising moon, heading toward the small, warm light that shone through the window of the workshop on Old Salt Road.

Above them, stretching from the river to the ridge, from the limestone bottoms to the high knobs, from the beginning of memory to the end of time, the great, open, unbroken canopy of heaven held up the world, silent and sufficient forever.
### Chapter 49

On Sunday morning, the fog in Lime Kiln Hollow was so thick that the tops of the walnut trees looked like islands floating in a lake of warm buttermilk.

Jesse and Julian drove up the branch road in Julian’s flatbed Ford. The granny low gear growled with a deep, content, mechanical purr as the four-wheel drive pulled through the yellow limestone mud of the creek crossings, where the water was clear enough to see the green water-moss trailing from the submerged rocks like horse manes.

On the passenger seat between them, wrapped in an old wool saddle blanket and tied with two leather bootlaces to keep the pendulum from swinging against the crutch, was Mrs. Odelia Miller’s Gothic cathedral clock.

Julian drove with both hands on the top of the wheel, his shoulders down, steering around the sharp limestone ledges with that slow, instinctive caution that a man only learns after he has spent three hours under a truck with a grease gun and a hammer.

"Jesse," Julian said, watching the ditch.

"Yeah, Julian?"

"Mrs. Miller’s ninety-four?"

"Ninety-four in May," Jesse said, looking out the side window at the blackberry brambles. "Her father was old man Absalom Miller, who had the lime kiln on the ridge. They crossed the third ford and pulled up into a yard that had been swept clean with a dogwood broom.

The house was a two-room log pen that had been weather-boarded with white oak in nineteen-twenty-six, with a tin roof that had weathered to a dark, velvet plum and a front porch supported by four cedar posts that still had their red bark on.

Sitting on the porch, in an arm-rocker made of split white-oak bark, was Mrs. Odelia Miller.

She was not a large woman; she was small, dried-down, and tough as a piece of cured hickory, wearing a dark calico dress that reached her shoe-tops, a white apron that had been starched and ironed until the folds were as sharp as knife creases, and a pair of black high-top shoes that had been shined with mutton tallow.

On the porch bench beside her sat:
- A five-pound lard bucket with a wire handle, filled to the brim with snow-white country lard that smelled faintly of hickory cracklings;
- Two two-quart Mason jars filled with preserved blackberries so dark and thick they looked like crude oil, sealed with red rubber rings and zinc screw-caps; and
- A three-layer dried-apple stack cake wrapped in a clean flour sack.

When the Ford stopped by the well-house, Mrs. Miller did not stand up. She adjusted her wire-rimmed spectacles, which were tied behind her ears with black silk thread, and watched Julian carry the saddle-blanket bundle up the steps with both arms.

"Don't bang it against the casing, young man," she said. Her voice was not weak; it was dry, clear, and carried that high, piping resonance of upland women who had spent seventy years calling men in from the timber for dinner. "That casing was hewn out of yellow poplar by my uncle Silas, and if you chip the bead, I’ll take a peach-tree switch to your trousers."

"Yes, ma'am," Julian said, his face flushing red under his cap as he carried the clock into the front room.

The front room smelled of dry cedar wood, beeswax, lavender, and ninety years of woodsmoke that had cured the poplar rafters until they shone like smoked bacon.

Above the stone fireplace was a mantle made of an eight-by-eight hemlock timber, hand-planed on three sides.

In the center of the mantle was a clean, bare rectangle in the dust, precisely twelve inches wide and eight inches deep, where the cathedral clock had sat until the great flood of nineteen-thirty-seven.

Jesse carried in the toolbox.

Together, they unwrapped the saddle blanket.

The clock stood on the hemlock timber, its cast-brass spires gleaming in the soft morning light from the door, its glass door clean, its pendulum bob hanging true and plumb between the two wooden uprights.

Mrs. Miller walked in from the porch, leaning on an ash-wood stick with a rubber tip, her small, hard fingers touching the cast-iron base.

"Absalom brought it from Louisville," she whispered, her voice dropping into the quiet twilight of memory. "In eighteen-ninety-eight. Carried it sixty miles on the pommel of his saddle through two snowstorms. When the flood took the lower house in thirty-seven, my husband Arthur rowed a skiff right through the parlor window, reached down into four feet of yellow water, and pulled this clock off the shelf by the steeple."

She looked up at Jesse.

"Does it strike, Jesse?"

Jesse reached into his pocket and pulled out the brass key.

He fitted it to the square winding arbor on the right.

He turned it six half-turns. The heavy steel spring took the torque with a firm, solid, ratchet-like *clack... clack... clack... clack*.

He fitted the key to the left arbor and wound the time train.

Then he reached out with his right index finger, pushed the minute hand to the figure twelve, and stepped back.

Inside the case, the warning lever lifted.

The great brass wheel released.

*TANG.*

The hammer struck the coiled wire gong with a deep, bronze, cathedral ring that filled the small log room, vibrating through the poplar floorboards, shaking the tin cups on the dresser, and rolling out through the open door into the quiet fog of the hollow.

*TANG.*  
*TANG.*  
*TANG.*

Ten times it struck.

When the last chime died away, the dead-beat escapement caught the tooth with a clean, sharp, magnificent snap:

*Tick... tock... tick... tock.*

Mrs. Miller stood very still.

Her small, dry hand reached out and touched the hemlock mantle. She closed her eyes.

"Arthur stood right there," she said, pointing to the hearthstone where Julian was standing in his boots. "On the tenth of May, nineteen-forty-two. Three days before he went to the Navy. Brother Luther Callow stood by the woodbox with his Bible. There wasn't any flowers, and there wasn't any cake. Arthur had on his work shirt, and I had on my sister’s Sunday shoes that pinched my toes."

She opened her eyes, looking straight into Jesse’s face.

"Arthur’s been dead twenty-two years, Jesse," she said. "And for twenty-two years, this room has been as quiet as a cellar. But that clock was ticking when he put the ring on my finger, and it’s ticking now."

She turned to Julian.

She picked up the five-pound bucket of lard by its wire handle, picked up the two quarts of blackberries, and pressed them into Julian’s arms with a strength that nearly took his breath away.

"You take these, son," Mrs. Miller said. "And tell that girl of yours that if she doesn't use two tablespoons of that lard in her pie crust, she’s not fit to live on the creek."

Julian looked down at the lard bucket. Then he looked at Jesse.

"Yes, ma'am," Julian said, his eyes bright behind his cap. "I’ll tell her."

### Chapter 50

On the second Monday of September, a long black sedan bearing the state seal of Kentucky on the door pulled into the gravel square of Meacham.

From it stepped Dr. Alistair MacIntyre, Chief Archivist of the Commonwealth of Kentucky, and Miss Eleanor Vance, who had spent three weeks in Frankfort consulting with the State Historical Society on the preservation of early upland legal indices.

They were accompanied by Cecil Flatt, who was wearing his good suit with a clean blue tie, his glass eye tracking the pigeons on the courthouse roof while his good eye surveyed the town square with immense, proprietary satisfaction.

- Every deed from 1888 to the present, cross-indexed by grantor, grantee, and boundary monument;

"Dr. MacIntyre," Corinne said, not blinking. "Wipe your feet on that mat by the door; the boys from the road department were in here this morning with mud on their heels."

> *BE IT KNOWN TO ALL MEN:*

> *The solemnizations recorded therein under the Open Canopy of Heaven are declared to be monumental acts of public record, immune from foreign audit, sequestration, or administrative challenge forever.*

Dr. MacIntyre looked at Harlan Grote.

"Mr. Grote," the archivist said. "I have examined the sample transcripts you transmitted to the capital. In forty years of archival administration, I have never seen a more rigorous, elegant, and flawless application of the nineteen-thirty-one Library of Congress descriptive canons."

Harlan Grote’s face turned the pale, translucent pink of a dogwood blossom in May.

He stood up from his cane-seated chair, bowed with a stiff, precise, German-Lutheran inclination of the head, and touched his spectacles with his thumb.

"Thank you, sir," Grote whispered. "The records were somewhat irregular when I arrived. Certain hound dogs had been entered without proper serial classification. But with Mrs. Vance’s assistance, the indices have been brought into canonical alignment."

Corinne took a mint lozenge from her tin.

"He does good work," Corinne conceded, popping the lozenge into her mouth. "A little slow with the mucilage, but he doesn't waste the paper fasteners, which is more than I can say for the magistrates."

"Cecil," she said.

"Yes, Corinne?"

"Did you bring the bronze plate?"

Cecil Flatt reached into his briefcase and produced a heavy bronze plaque, eighteen inches wide and twelve inches high, cast by a foundry in Louisville, reading:

*THE OPEN CANOPY OF THE WESTERN WATERS.*  
*Registered Registry Archive No. 1.*  
*Established under the Sky, 1780.*  
*Confirmed by the County Court, 2025.*  
*"The grass withers and the timber rots, but the covenant stands."*

"Toby’s got the masonry drill down in the truck," Corinne said, pointing toward the window. "Tell him to bolt it to the limestone pillar right beside the front door, four feet off the ground, where every lawyer who comes up those steps will have to scrape his sleeve against it."

### Chapter 51

On the third Saturday of October, one year to the day after the blue plastic envelope had arrived from Terre Haute, Jesse Callow did not go to the pavilion.

He stayed in his workshop on Old Salt Road.

It was four o'clock in the afternoon. The upland autumn had returned in all its magnificent, golden, iron-scented glory: the leaves on the white oaks were a deep, dark red like old wine; the fescue pastures were pale yellow; and the smoke from three woodstoves drifted across the creek in long, blue, horizontal ribbons that smelled of hickory and dry leaves.

Jesse sat on his plywood-topped milk can, wearing his clean denim shirt, his reading glasses on his nose, carefully cleaning the pivot holes of an eighteen-eighty-four Ansonia movement with a piece of dry elderberry pith.

The door opened.

It was not Toby. It was not Corinne.

It was young Billy Cates, wearing his Sunday suit, his hair slicked down with rosewater tonic, holding a white pasteboard box tied with pink paper ribbon.

"Morning, Billy," Jesse said, not looking up from his pith. "How’s the bride?"

"She’s fine," Billy said, grinning until his ears moved. "She’s up at her mother’s making chow-chow. She sent you this piece of coconut cake from the church supper."

Across the valley, three miles away, the green steel roof of the pavilion caught the low autumn sun, glowing like an emerald on the yellow pasture terrace.

Through the clear, thin autumn air, he could see the four white strips of linen fluttering gently from the cedar posts.

He could see the small cluster of people standing on the crushed limestone.

He could see Julian Vance-Holt, six feet tall, broad-shouldered now from six months of swinging the eight-pound sledge on the road crew, his orange quarry cap held behind his back, raising his right hand toward the high, blue bowl of the sky.

He could not hear the words; the distance was too great.

He didn't need to hear them.

He knew what Julian was saying:
- He was telling them that marriage was like an old eight-day clock;
- He was telling them that the iron was good and the weight was heavy;
- He was telling them that if they held level and kept the dust out of the escapement, the promise would tick on through the frost, through the flood, and through the long, dark nights until the weights hit the bottom of the case.

Jesse reached into his pocket.

He pulled out a small, flat brass key—the spare winding key for the courthouse tower clock that his father had given him when he was eighteen years old.

He turned it between his calloused fingers. The brass was cold, smooth, and heavy with fifty years of memory.

A gentle breeze came through the workshop window, bringing the smell of woodsmoke, wet limestone, and sweet dry clover from the hills.

From the town of Meacham, the Seth Thomas clock struck five:

*BONG.*  
*BONG.*  
*BONG.*  
*BONG.*  
*BONG.*

The sound drifted over the knobs, magnificent, sweet, and eternal.

Jesse smiled.

He laid the brass key on the shelf beside his jar of cotter pins, picked up his three-cornered scraper, and went back to work on his clocks.

### Chapter 52

The binding of *The Book of the Field* was completed on a rainy Friday in November, in the Registry of Deeds at Meacham.

The rain was not a flood; it was that quiet, persistent autumn rain that soaks into the pastures and fills the cisterns for the winter, pattering against the high leaded-glass windows of the courthouse with a soft, steady rhythm.

Spread across the slate counter were twelve gatherings of heavy, seventy-pound rag vellum, each gathering containing sixteen pages, printed by an artisanal letterpress printer in Lexington using hand-set Caslon type.

It was titled:

*THE PERPETUAL REGISTER OF THE WESTERN WATERS.*  
*Containing the Records of Four Hundred and Eight Marriages Solemnized Under the Open Sky in the County of Whetstone, Commonwealth of Kentucky, from July 14, 2012, to the Present Date.*  
*Compiled by Presbyter Jesse L. Callow, Registrar M. Corinne Vance, and Archivist Harlan Grote.*

Lindy Gidley stood at the counter, wearing an apron of clean grey linen.

In her hands was an ivory bone-folder and a long needle threaded with four strands of unbleached Irish linen thread that had been drawn through a cake of raw beeswax from Uncle Creed Fray’s hives.

She was sewing the gatherings to four heavy split-leather bands mounted on a sewing frame that Julian had built for her out of seasoned black walnut.

*Punch... pull... tighten.*

With every stitch, the needle passed through the fold of the vellum, looped around the leather band with a crisp, rhythmic *snick*, and pulled the paper into a solid, flexible, indestructible spine that would open flat on any table and never crack through three hundred years of turning.

Beside her stood Harlan Grote, holding the paste pot and a heavy press-board weighted with two twenty-pound pigs of lead.

"The tension on the kettle-stitch, Mrs. Gidley," Grote murmured with anxious, professional devotion. "Ensure that the kettle-stitch does not draw the head too tight, or the tail will flare when the boards are laced."

"I know how to sew a book, Harlan," Lindy said gently, her needle flashing in the warm yellow light of the lamp. "My grandmother sewed five quilts that went through three floods and two house fires, and there isn't a seam in one of them that has parted yet."

Corinne Peake sat in her rocker, watching the needle.

She had her dark green cardigan on, her feet resting on her foot-warmer, eating a slice of dried-apple stack cake that Chloe had brought her from the ridge.

"Make sure you lace the bands through both boards, Lindy," Corinne said. "The book they bound in nineteen-twelve only had three bands laced, and when old man Silas Fray dropped it on the floor during the road hearing, the front cover fell off and we had to hold it together with friction tape until the next election."

"I’m lacing all four, Aunt Corinne," Lindy said.

At the other end of the counter, Jesse Callow was preparing the covers.

They were not made of modern cardboard; they were two slabs of quarter-sawn white oak, three-eighths of an inch thick, planed smooth by Julian from a rafter taken from the ruins of old man Sells’s barn.

The wood was dark, dense, with that deep, golden-brown patina that only comes from seventy years of hanging over fescue hay in an upland draft.

Jesse was covering the wood with a single skin of dark brown vegetable-tanned calfskin that had been cured in oak bark at an old tannery on the Red River.

He smoothed the leather over the oak with his palms, his grease-stained thumbs pressing the turn-ins around the edges with that slow, perfect touch that he used when seating a cylinder head.

In the center of the front board, pressed deep into the leather with a brass die heated over an alcohol lamp, were four words:

*THE CANOPY IS OPEN.*

Lindy finished the last stitch. She knotted the linen thread with three half-hitches, trimmed the ends with Corinne’s shears, and laid the sewn book block into Jesse’s hands.

The paper felt heavy, cool, and alive, like a piece of freshly quarried limestone before the sun hits it.

Jesse laced the four leather bands through the oak boards, wedged them with small pegs of dry cedar, and brought the heavy screw-press down upon the binding with three firm turns of the iron wheel.

*Creak... grok... clack.*

The oak boards met the vellum; the calfskin settled into the grain; the four hundred and eight covenants became one single, permanent, indestructible monument of the upland country.

Corinne stood up from her chair.

She walked to the press, her purple stamp in her hand.

She did not stamp a page inside; she reached into her pocket, pulled out the ancient, circular steel embosser of the Whetstone County Court—the one her grandfather had used in 1888—and placed the heavy jaw over the lower corner of the front leather board.

She leaned on the lever.

*CRUNCH.*

The circular seal of Whetstone County sank into the dark calfskin, cutting a permanent, raised circle of laurel leaves and justice scales into the leather.

"There," Corinne said, stepping back and wiping her fingers on her apron. "That goes into the top shelf of the vault. Right beside Deed Book One. And if anybody from Frankfort, from Indiana, or from the United States Senate wants to know where our people are, you tell them to open that book and look at the names."

### Chapter 53

The winter returned to Whetstone County in the third week of December, but it was a quiet, gentle winter that laid four inches of dry, powdery snow over the limestone hills like an unbleached linen sheet.

On Christmas Eve, the sun went down behind the cedar knobs at five o'clock in a blaze of pale amethyst and rose, leaving the sky over the valley deep, velvet-blue, dusted with thousands of cold, sharp stars that sparkled in the frosty air like chips of broken glass.

The square in Meacham was dark, save for the warm yellow light that shone through the high arched windows of Courtroom 1, where the town choir was singing *Silent Night* to the accompaniment of Arthur’s mandolin, and the soft, green glow of the Christmas tree on the courthouse lawn.

Jesse Callow walked up Old Salt Road toward his house.

He was wearing his heavy canvas coat, his boots crunching in the dry snow with a crisp, rhythmic *squeak... squeak... squeak*.

He didn't walk fast; he took his time, looking at the dark shapes of the cedar trees along the fence, at the frozen pools of the creek shining like black mirrors between their banks of snow, and at the gentle swell of the pastures rising toward the ridge.

When he reached the top of the rise, he stopped.

Down in the hollow, the small frame cottage of Toby and Lindy Gidley sat with yellow light pouring from all four windows, smoke rising straight and true from the stone chimney into the cold, still air.

Beside the cottage, the new green steel pavilion stood in the snow, its six cedar posts dark and solid, its four strips of white linen pinned tight against the winter wind, waiting for the spring.

Beyond it, on the north ridge, a single light shone from the window of Julian and Chloe’s house, where a new eight-day clock was ticking on the mantle and a young man was learning that the world could be mended if you had the patience to hold the iron level.

Jesse took off his glove.

He touched the cold wire of the fence line, feeling the vibration of the wind humming through the strands like an ancient harp played by the winter hills.

He looked up.

Above him, vast, unbroken, and eternal, was the sky.

It had no denomination. It had no synodical assize. It had no quarterly quota, and it had never asked a single soul for twenty-two dollars to prove that it was there.

It was the same sky that had looked down on Captain Trumbo’s broken chain; on Donald Vance’s blue sweater; on four hundred pairs of young people holding hands in the clover; and on an honest man who had stood in a field with grease on his fingers and promised them that the covenant would stand.

The Seth Thomas clock in the courthouse tower struck six.

*BONG.*

He pulled his glove back on, tucked his hands deep into his pockets, and walked through the gate toward the warm light of the shop, while above him, through the long, quiet night, the high, open canopy of heaven held up the world.

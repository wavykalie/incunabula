# Chapter 30: Corvus at the Bridgehead

The Great Hall of the First Surveys sat in the heart of High Carrow, built directly over the living granite bedrock of the mountain.

It was an immense vaulted chamber of dressed limestone, fifty paces long and thirty paces wide, supported by twelve octagonal pillars whose capitals were carved with the compass roses and surveying instruments of four centuries. The walls were not hung with tapestries; they were lined with hundreds of engraved copperplates bolted directly into the stone, each plate recording a township, a valley, a river, or a mine of the interior realm.

In the center of the hall, upon a raised dais of dark serpentine marble, stood the First Benchmark: an eight-foot column of polished granite bearing the bronze master compass of the Founders.

Around the benchmark sat twenty elders of High Carrow, dressed in their ceremonial robes of green and scarlet wool.

Before them, on a broad trestle table of split oak, lay the two halves of the kingdom's geography.

On the left side lay Master Corvus's Admiralty Portfolio: forty sheets of coastal charts, soundings of the harbour, and the low-water contours of the South Headland, sealed with the Chancellor's modern mark.

On the right side lay the ancient copperplates of High Carrow: the mountain traverses, the silver veins, the high passes, and the *Via Alta Carrovensis*, preserved in the dry mountain air for two hundred years.

Between the two portfolios sat Maren Vane.

She had washed the stone dust from her face and hands; she wore a clean wool gown of mountain homespun that Elder Martha had given her, but her high surveyor's boots remained upon her feet.

Beside her sat Master Corvus Althaus.

The Chief Warden had taken off his steel breastplate and his cavalry boots. He sat in an oak armchair beside the benchmark, his grey head bowed over an engraved copperplate that Elder Martha had placed before him.

It was the matching plate to his own harbour master.

Where his plate ended at the mouth of the Stone Chasm with a line of heavy hatching and the word *Inaccessibilis*, the mountain plate began with the four arches of the Bridge of the Chasm, continuing northward through the terraced valleys to the gates of High Carrow.

The two copperplates had identical screw-holes along their adjoining edges.

They had been engraved by the same burin in the winter of 1440.

Elder Martha stepped forward, her ashwood staff clicking on the serpentine marble. She was seventy-two, her face lined with a thousand wrinkles of mountain sun and wind, her blue eyes sharp and clear beneath her white hair.

"Master Warden," Martha spoke, her voice ringing through the vaulted hall like the chime of an iron bell. "For forty years your Guild has told the harbour merchants that our town was an empty wilderness. You burned our maps in Harbour Square. You forbade your copyists to write our name. You told your children that the earth ended at the high weir."

She pointed her staff toward the two copperplates on the table.

"Tell me, Master Althaus: does the metal lie?"

Corvus did not look up immediately.

He reached out his scarred right hand. With his forefinger, he traced the severed line where the coastal copper met the mountain plate.

The registration was absolute. The two plates fit together with the precision of a master clockmaker's gears.

"The metal does not lie," Corvus said, his voice dropping into a register of profound, broken sorrow. "The metal tells the truth."

He lifted his head, looking around the circle of mountain elders, his dark eyes red with unshed tears.

"My predecessor, Chancellor Vane, believed that peace could be manufactured by silence. He believed that if he erased the mountain from the parchment, the harbour dukes could rule their trade without fear of competition. And I... I was his faithful hound. I thought that by burning every discordant line, I was holding the world steady against shipwreck."

He turned to Maren, his gaze resting upon her face with an expression of humble, fatherly wonder that she had never seen in the high tower.

"Your father Thomas told me the truth twenty years ago in this very hall, Maren. He stood where you are standing and told me that a map is not a wall to keep men apart, but a road to bring them together. I would not listen. I had him cast into the common ward because I was afraid of the fire."

He stood up from his chair.

He reached into his leather pouch, drew out the heavy brass Chancellor's seal of the Guild, and laid it upon the table between the two copperplates.

Beside the seal, he laid his heavy brass compass--the twenty-inch dividers he had tapped upon his drafting desk through thirty years of lonely nights.

"Maren Vane," Corvus declared, his voice rising in authoritative clarity that filled the cavernous hall. "By the power vested in me as Chief Warden of the Guild Charter, I declare your apprenticeship concluded. I declare your journeyman grade dissolved."

He turned to Elder Martha and the assembled elders.

"I nominate Maren Vane to be admitted as Master Cartographer of this realm, with jurisdiction over both the coast and the interior, authorized to unite the copperplates, to rewrite the Great Charter, and to seal the living map for all time."

Elder Martha smiled, her white head nodding in solemn assent.

"High Carrow confirms the appointment," the elder woman spoke.

A shout of approval rose from the elders and the workmen who packed the gallery, their voices ringing against the ancient stone arches.

Maren stood before the marble dais.

She picked up the brass compass. She picked up the Chancellor's seal.

And as she took the instruments into her hands, she looked down at the living vellum that lay unfurled between the copperplates.

The parchment was at rest.

The black lines of the coast, the river, the bridge, and the mountain highway were steady, deep, and indelible.

The earth had remembered.

In the Great Hall of High Carrow, the twenty elders rose from their chairs as Master Corvus laid his brass dividers upon the serpentine marble dais.

Elder Martha walked forward, holding a silver chalice of hot mountain wine.

She offered the chalice to Corvus.

"Drink, Master Cartographer of the Harbour," the elder woman said, her voice rich with ancient solemnity. "The mountain welcomes the coast. We drink to the memory of Johannes Althaus, who laid the stone, and to Thomas Vane, who remembered where it lay."

Corvus took the silver chalice with both hands.

His scarred fingers were steady. He looked into the elder woman's clear blue eyes, then turned to Maren Vane.

"To Thomas Vane," Corvus spoke, his voice ringing through the vaulted arches. "The truest cartographer who ever held an ashwood staff."

He drank the spiced wine, then passed the chalice to Maren.

She drank, the sweet, warm wine tasting of cloves, honey, and mountain rain, feeling the long war of two centuries dissolve forever in the warmth of the hall.

The map had answered.

And for the first time in six years, Maren Vane looked through the high arched windows of the hall toward the rising sun and felt no fear of the fire.

# Chapter 39: Return to the Harbour

The Red Sandstone Gate of Oakhaven stood wide open under the pale autumn sun.

The armed guard that Warden Brandt had posted four days before had vanished from the artillery towers; the iron portcullis hung high in its stone grooves, crusted with dry rust, while three thousand citizens of the town packed the roadway from the landward ditch to the central market square.

Word of the caravan's approach had reached the city at dawn.

The river boatmen had brought the news down from the weir: the bridge in the chasm was closed; Master Corvus had signed the treaty; twelve wagons of mountain rye, wool, and silver were rolling down the north road with an escort of royal archers.

When the lead wagon, driven by Kellan Reed with Maren Vane at his side, passed beneath the great red sandstone archway, a roar rose from the assembled crowd that made the gulls take flight from the harbour jetties.

Teamsters cheered from the tops of their hay wains; fishwives threw sprigs of dried rosemary into the wagon beds; merchant clerks in velvet coats ran beside the wheels, shouting the price of mountain wool and offering jugs of dark cider to the thirsty teamsters.

At the head of the High Street, outside the Town Hall, stood the Grand Council of Oakhaven.

Warden Malor and Warden Brandt stood on the mounting block in their scarlet gowns of office, their faces pale, their hands trembling as they looked at the twelve massive wagons of silver and grain that were rolling through their forbidden gate.

Beside them stood Archivist Sibyl Thorne.

The old woman was dressed in a clean black woolen gown, her single blue eye gleaming with fierce, triumphant joy beneath her black silk patch, her ring of fifty brass keys held silent in her hands.

Maren climbed down from the wagon seat.

She carried the heavy calfskin cylinder of the Great Charter of Reconciliation in her right hand, and in her left, wrapped in a square of clean linen, a six-pound block of white crystalline limestone chiseled from the keystone of the Bridge of the Chasm.

She walked through the cheering crowd, her surveyor's boots ringing against the cobblestones of Harbour Square, and stopped before the council mounting block.

She did not look at Malor. She did not look at Brandt.

She walked directly to Archivist Sibyl Thorne.

She knelt upon the flagstones and laid the white limestone block in the old woman's lap.

"I brought the mountain stone, Mistress Sibyl," Maren said, her voice clear and ringing in the quiet that fell upon the square. "As I promised in the salt cellars."

Sibyl's withered, ink-stained hands touched the cool, crystalline face of the stone.

Her single blue eye filled with tears that spilled down her wrinkled cheeks, washing forty years of cellar dust from her face.

"The stone is true, child," the Archivist whispered, her voice trembling with an emotion that shook her frail frame. "The stone is whole once more."

Maren rose to her feet.

She turned to face the three thousand citizens of Oakhaven and the stunned wardens of the Council.

She unbuckled the brass straps of the calfskin cylinder.

She drew out the four sheets of the Great Charter of Reconciliation and held them high above her head in the bright sea light.

"Citizens of Oakhaven!" Maren proclaimed, her voice carrying to the farthest corners of the square, echoing off the slate roofs of the merchant houses. "The Great Charter of 1492 is dissolved! Article XII is stricken from the register! The highway to High Carrow is open, free, and sealed under the royal covenant!"

She pointed toward the bronze brazier in the center of the square--the brazier that had consumed her father's work twelve years ago.

"From this day forth, no map shall be burned in this city! No road shall be declared non-existent! The Guild shall draw the earth as it is, and the King's peace shall walk upon the stone!"

A cheer erupted from the square that shook the leaded panes of the Guild Tower.

Carters embraced dockers; merchant clerks threw their hats into the air; children danced around the bronze brazier, heaping it with sprigs of mountain pine and dried gorse.

Warden Brandt stepped down from the mounting block, his head bowed, and handed his gold chain of office to the Mayor of the town.

Warden Malor turned and shuffled into the dark portal of the tower without uttering a single word.

Kellan Reed stepped up beside Maren, his ashwood sighting staff held high, his hazel eyes crinkling with proud, triumphant joy.

"You brought them home, Maren," he said, his voice rich with wonder.

"We brought each other home, Kellan," she answered, looking at the great wagons rolling toward the grain storehouses.

The trade caravan rolled down the final limestone shelf into the coastal plain as the sun reached the meridian.

The air grew warmer, tasting of salt, kelp, and the coal smoke of the harbor forges. Along the ditches of the north road, the gorse bushes were beginning to show their yellow winter blossoms, nodding in the sea breeze that blew off the bay.

Kellan pointed his whip toward the high stone wall of Oakhaven, half a league ahead.

"Look at the tower, Maren," he said, his voice full of pride.

Above the grey slate roofs of the town, the high spire of the Guild Tower rose into the blue sky.

And from the summit of the tower, flying from the staff that had carried only the Duke's private banner for forty years, flew the Great Standard of the Unified Realm: the open compass, the stone mason's chisel, and the sighting staff, painted in scarlet and green upon forty yards of white sea canvas.

The citizens had raised the banner at dawn.

As the wains approached the Red Sandstone Gate, the iron carillon of Saint Jude began to chime the ancient hymn of the First Founders--a melody that had not been played on the bells since the winter of the Great Cataclysm.

The teamsters cracked their whips, the oxen leaned into their collars, and the caravan rolled into the city between two roaring walls of cheering citizens who threw flowers, bread, and flagons of wine into the wagon beds as the mountain silver entered the market square.

The roar of the three thousand citizens in Harbour Square was like the thunder of the ocean breaking upon the South Headland.

Along the stone jetties of the King's Quay, eighty merchant captains had climbed into the rigging of their ships, waving their hats and firing three small signal cannons in salute as the twelve mountain wains rolled into the customs yard.

The white smoke of the cannons drifted across the bay, smelling of saltpeter and celebration.

Maren walked between Kellan Reed and Archivist Sibyl Thorne, her leather satchel resting on her hip, holding the Great Charter high for all the city to see.

Beside the bronze brazier, forty young apprentices from the high scriptorium had gathered in their grey woolen aprons, their ink-stained hands clapping in wild, unrestrained joy.

Among them stood young Orson, his head held high, his cap restored to his curls, his eyes shining with tears as he looked at the four sheets of sealed vellum.

Maren stopped before the young cartographer.

She reached into her satchel, drew out the charred fragment of his survey that she had rescued from the ash bin four days before, and laid it in his open palm.

"Your road is finished, Orson," she said, her voice clear and gentle in the roar of the square.

Orson looked at the blackened scrap, then at the twelve massive wains of mountain silver standing in the yard, and wept with the joy of a man who had seen his honor restored to the light.

The twelve mountain wains were parked in two parallel lines in the center of Harbour Square, their teams of oxen munching sweet mountain hay from canvas nosebags.

Around the wagons, five hundred merchants and shopkeepers of Oakhaven were inspecting the cargo: feeling the density of the mountain wool, testing the purity of the silver pigs against testing touchstones, and marveling at the rich, golden loaves of winter rye that the carters were handing out to the children.

At the base of the Guild Tower, where the bronze brazier had stood for two centuries, Master Varek and three mountain masons were already at work with crowbars and sledges.

They had unbolted the iron dolphins from the cobblestones; they were hauling the black bronze grate away to the blacksmith's scrap heap.

In its place, upon the very flagstones where Thomas Vane's sketchbooks had been burned twelve years before, Varek was setting a six-foot pedestal of dressed white limestone.

Upon that pedestal, surrounded by four carved stone waymarkers, would stand the First Benchmark of Oakhaven: an eight-inch brass circumferentor pointing true north toward the Bridge of the Chasm and the mountain gates of High Carrow.

Maren stood beside the pedestal, her hand resting upon the cool limestone.

She looked up at the high tower window where she had spent six years in the cold shadow of the scriptorium.

The window was open to the sea breeze.

The light was shining through the leaded panes, bright, clean, and gold, illuminating the new world that had waited four hundred years to be drawn.

She looked up at the high tower spire.

The iron caravel was pointing south-southwest into the clean salt breeze of the bay, its copper sails steady, peaceful, and at rest in the afternoon light.

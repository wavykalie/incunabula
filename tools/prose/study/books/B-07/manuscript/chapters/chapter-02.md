# Chapter 2: The Pyre in the Square

The bronze brazier in the center of Harbour Square was shaped like an inverted ship's hull supported by four iron dolphins.

Twice each month, on the morning after the spring tide, the Guild Bailiffs dragged the brazier into the middle of the cobblestones, filled its belly with dry birch kindling and sulfur cakes, and set an iron kettle of pitch upon the grate. By noon, the smoke hung thick between the salt-warehouse gables, smelling of tar, resin, and old wool.

The crowd gathered along the market ropes: fishwives with baskets of wet cod under their arms, tallow-chandlers, coal-heavers from the coal quays, and merchant clerks carrying balance books tucked into their belts. They did not come for blood. No heads fell on cartography days. They came to watch the paper burn. In Oakhaven, the burning of bad charts had the festive, sour gravity of a church excommunication.

Maren stood by the fish-landing arch, her hands tucked deep into the pockets of her grey apprentice apron. Her shoulders were stiff. The cold November wind drove grey spits of salt drizzle off the basin, but around the brazier the heat wavered in yellow ripples.

Master Corvus Althaus stood on the stone mounting block beside the brazier. He wore the formal scarlet robe of the Chief Warden of the Guild Charter, heavy beaver fur lining the collar, the bronze key of the Master Chest hanging from a silver chain against his chest. His face looked carved from grey salt-pork: broad jaw, skin pitted by old smallpox, dark eyes set deep under bristling iron-grey brows. He held a birchwood rod in his right hand.

Beside him stood young Orson, a second-year journeyman from the river survey house. Orson's cap had been pulled from his head. His eyes were red, staring down at the flagstones as if searching for a dropped silver groat.

"Hear the judgment of the Grand Wardens," Corvus called out. His voice was a heavy, resonant bell that cut cleanly through the squawk of gulls and the clatter of dray wagons. "Orson of the North Gate Scriptorium, journeyman of the third class, did on the fourth day of October take private measurement of the Upper Reach beyond the High Weir. On five sheets of rag paper, without license, without warrant, and without the presence of a sworn royal surveyor, he did draw three bends of the river, seven farmsteads, and a stone track leading into the eastern hills."

A murmur ran through the fishwives. A woman selling pickled herrings spat into the gutter.

"What harm in a farm track?" a young docker near Maren muttered under his breath. "The carters bring apples down that way every harvest."

"The harm is an unverified line," an older merchant clerk snapped back, tapping his ink-horn with a quill. "A carter knows his rut. A shipmaster reading an unsealed chart runs his keel upon a mudbank and drowns sixty tons of dried fleece. The Guild draws what the King warrants. Nothing more."

Corvus raised the birch rod. Silence settled across the square.

"The Guild Charter of 1492 is the boundary of this realm," Corvus spoke, his dark eyes sweeping over the crowd until they seemed to rest for a fraction of a second upon Maren herself. "The earth is wild. The earth shifts with every winter gale and every mountain torrent. Rivers silt their beds. Sandspits creep across the bay like thieves in the night. If every man with a pen and a compass were free to draw the land as his eyes fancied it, order would dissolve into chaos. A charter is not a drawing of the dirt. A charter is the King's peace written in ink."

He gestured to the two bailiffs. One of them stepped forward carrying a wicker basket filled with rolls of heavy rag paper.

"Orson," Corvus said, his voice dropping into cold, formal sadness. "Do you recant the lines you drew beyond the High Weir?"

"I recant them, Master," Orson whispered. His chin trembled. "I took the sighting by eye. I used no standard chain."

"Then let the fire take the error," Corvus said.

The bailiff reached into the basket, drew out the first roll, and unfurled it before the crowd. Maren leaned forward against the wet rope.

It was a beautiful sketch. The paper was rough rag, but Orson had a delicate, fluid hand. She could see the grey river curving between two steep ridges of pine wood, the hatching of the slopes rendered in tiny parallel strokes of black carbon, and along the eastern bank, a double dotted line tracing an old cart road winding north toward the limestone hills.

The bailiff touched the corner of the sheet to the brazier's glowing grate.

The paper curled. A circle of orange fire blossomed in the center of the pine ridge. The black carbon ink hissed as the heat met the gum sizing. For two heartbeats, the dotted cart road seemed to glow like a thread of red silk in the heart of the flame. Then the paper blackened, crinkled into frail carbon gauze, and broke into fluttering flakes that vanished up the stone flue of the chimney.

One by one, the five sheets were fed to the grate.

Corvus did not look at the fire. His gaze remained fixed upon the apprentices standing in the second row.

"Let every apprentice mark the lesson," Corvus proclaimed, his words ringing against the stone fronts of the merchant houses. "The eye deceives. The hand falters. Only the standard rod, sealed by the Council and preserved in the dry vaults of this tower, tells the truth of the world. What is not in the Register does not exist. What is not sealed by the Guild shall not be named."

He struck the mounting block with his birch rod. The ceremony was concluded.

The crowd began to disperse into the side alleys. The fishwives picked up their wet baskets, shouting the price of herring. The merchant clerks hurried back to the customs dock with their balance books. Orson was led away through the postern gate by his master, his head buried in his rough wool sleeves.

Maren did not move from the fish-landing arch.

The heat from the brazier was dying down, but a bitter, greasy smell lingered in the damp sea air. She knew that smell. She had smelled it when she was twelve years old, sitting on the bottom step of the water fountain while four bailiffs in leather jerkins hauled her father's cedar drafting chest down the stairs of their lodgings. Her father had shouted at them from the window, his hair wild, his hands stained with malachite green and walnut oil: *The road is there! You can walk it on your own boots, you blind fools! You can touch the milestone with your own hands!*

They had not touched the milestone. They had burned the cedar chest in Harbour Square, and three months later her father was dead of the lung-rot in the debtor's ward of the town gaol.

Maren looked down at her right sleeve.

A single flake of charred paper had drifted across the rope barrier and settled upon the grey wool of her cuff. It was no larger than a copper farthing. The edge was scalloped with black soot, but in the center, preserved by a speck of unburned clay sizing, lay three tiny dots of black ink. The tail of Orson's cart road.

Her fingers closed over the scrap. The burnt sheepskin crumbled slightly, cold and brittle against her skin.

Across the clearing square, Master Corvus had stepped down from the stone block. He was handing his birch rod to an attendant, unfastening the beaver-lined collar of his scarlet robe. As he turned toward the tower portal, his head lifted, and his sharp, hooded eyes met hers across forty paces of wet cobblestones.

The smoke drifted higher, eddying around the carved stone lions that guarded the entrance to the Customs Weigh-House. The crowd watched the embers with the quiet, bovine satisfaction of men who had seen a neighbor brought low. In the third row of spectators, two elderly wool carters leaned against their wagon tongues, their clay pipes glowing dull red under the grey sky.

"They burned my brother's pasture platt twenty years back," the older carter muttered through his teeth, squinting through the smoke. "Claimed he had drawn two rods of municipal ditch onto his freehold. Sent three bailiffs with an iron rod to knock his stone boundary markers into the marsh. A platt is like a bailiff's warrant: it tells you where you may walk and where you must take off your bonnet."

"Aye," his companion replied, spitting tobacco juice onto the wet cobblestones. "And when the winter frost heaves the mud and the ditch fills with gravel, who digs it out? The bailiff? Not him. The bailiff stays in his warm counting room while the sheep drown in the reeds."

Corvus heard the murmur. His head did not turn, but the birch rod in his right hand twitched against his scarlet skirts. He knew what the carters whispered in the ale-houses. He knew that outside the stone wall of Oakhaven, where the King's highway dwindled into muddy sheep-runs, the parish folk made their own tallies on hazel sticks, scorning the sealed vellum of the Guild. To Corvus, every hazel tally was an act of rebellion, every private milestone a challenge to the sovereign order that kept sixty galleys safe in the roadstead.

The bailiffs stacked the charred ash-cakes into two iron tubs, ready to be carried to the harbor mudflats where the tide would wash the black residue into the bay. In the crowd, an old compass-smith named Gervaise shook his head, spitting a splinter of tooth-pick onto the flagstones.

"They burn the paper," Gervaise whispered to the carter beside him, "because paper doesn't bleed. When I was a lad in the apprentice shop, Chancellor Vane burned forty rolls of the west road. My master wept over his walnut bench for three days. But the next winter, when the storm drove the herring away from the headland, those very bailiffs were buying mountain pork from smugglers who came down the unchartered road by night. The belly recognizes the road even when the charter denies it."

Corvus had stepped down from the block, but his ears caught the smith's whisper. His jaw tightened, the grey skin over his cheekbones stretching like dry parchment over a frame. He did not order Gervaise arrested; he knew that to arrest an old smith for speaking of bread would turn the silent crowd into a riot. Instead, he signaled to his halberdiers to clear the market lane, his boots striking the wet stones with cold, measured fury.

He did not scowl. He did not gesture. He simply looked at her with the cold, immovable weight of an iron anchor lying sixty fathoms deep in silt.

Maren lowered her eyes immediately. She tucked her hands back into the pockets of her apron, fingers pressing the charred flake against her thigh, and walked swiftly toward the scriptorium steps.

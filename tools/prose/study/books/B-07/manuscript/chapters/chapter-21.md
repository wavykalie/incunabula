# Chapter 21: Beyond the Land Gate

Dawn broke over the northern foothills in a long, cold band of lemon-yellow light.

Maren walked three paces behind Kellan's mule, her boots coated to the ankles in grey clay. The road here was not the rough ditch she had anticipated from the Guild's imperial decrees. Once they climbed beyond the salt marsh marshes and the customs cordon, the path resolved into an ancient, well-drained trackway paved with sunken cobbles of river quartz, bordered on either side by low walls of dry limestone masonry.

The valley opened before them like an unrolled scroll.

To the east, terraced vineyards climbed the south-facing slopes in neat green tiers, their vines pruned close against the winter frost. In the hollows between the ridges, stone farmsteads sent thin plumes of blue oak smoke into the still morning air. A watermill turned on the lower fork of the Black River, its broad wooden paddle-wheel throwing silver sheets of spray that caught the rising sun.

Maren stopped on the brow of the hill. She rested her hands on her ashwood sighting staff, staring down into the valley.

"Where is the wilderness?" she asked aloud.

Kellan pulled the mule to a halt. He leaned against the pack-saddle, an ironic grin tugging at the corners of his mouth.

"You're standing in it, Journeyman," he said, waving his arm toward a stone farmhouse where an old woman in a scarlet wool apron was scattering grain to a flock of white geese. "Terrifying wasteland, isn't it? Full of savage beasts and bottomless pits, just like Master Corvus wrote in the Grand Register."

"The Guild Charter says the land north of the chasm has no inhabitants," Maren said, her voice shaking slightly with cold and exhaustion. "It says there are no settlements, no cleared fields, and no permanent roads."

"The Charter says what suits the harbour dukes," Kellan replied, spitting into the gorse bush beside the track. "If the Duke acknowledged that five thousand people live in this valley, he'd have to explain why his customs collectors take three pence on every pound of salt that comes through the water-gate while providing neither roads, bridges, nor parish protection. So he writes 'empty wasteland' on his calfskin and sits in his high tower counting his silver florins."

He touched the mule's flank with his staff, leading the beast down the gentle slope toward a stone bridge that crossed a tributary brook.

Maren walked beside him, her surveyor's eye taking in the landscape with instinctive professional curiosity.

The agricultural engineering was astonishing. The terraces were not peasant scratch-plots; they were graded works of masonry, equipped with stone drainage channels and silt traps designed to prevent soil erosion during the autumn downpours. The retaining walls were built of dressed limestone blocks, battered back at an angle of one inch per foot to resist the thrust of the mountain soil.

"Who laid these walls?" she asked, running her fingertips along the cool, chisel-dressed face of a corner block.

"Our great-grandfathers," Kellan said. "Before the Guild closed the market. In those days, a surveyor was a man of the parish who carried a compass and an iron chain to settle the boundaries between neighbours. He wasn't a clerk with a red seal who burned his neighbour's work in Harbour Square."

A young boy carrying an iron milk pail ran out from the lane beside the watermill. He stopped when he saw Kellan, his face lighting up with recognition.

"Waymarker!" the boy called out in the broad, vowel-heavy mountain vernacular. "Is the stone coming down from the quarry? My uncle said the timber trucks are held at the lower weir."

"The stone is moving, Peter," Kellan answered, tossing the boy a dried fig from his pocket. "Tell your uncle the bridge span will be closed before the snow reaches the valley floor. Keep the cider dry in the cellar."

The boy caught the fig with a grin, turned, and bolted back toward the mill, his bare feet slapping against the quartz cobbles.

Maren looked up toward the northern horizon.

The gentle green slopes of the agricultural valley were ending. Two miles ahead, the ground rose abruptly into a formidable rampart of dark limestone crags that climbed into the low clouds like an iron wall.

In the center of the wall, cutting through the rock in a sheer vertical chasm three hundred paces deep, was the mouth of the Stone Gorge.

The wind from the chasm blew cold and steady, carrying the roar of the Black River and the distant, rhythmic clink of iron hammers on stone.

"That's the work," Kellan said, his voice dropping into quiet, reverent solemnity. "Eighty men have been sleeping in the quarry huts since Michaelmas. We've got eighteen voussoirs cut for the bridge arch, but the river is rising with the mountain rains. If we don't set the center-line today, the wooden scaffold will wash down to the bay before midnight."

Maren reached into her jerkin, feeling the hard brass cylinder of Sibyl's travelling case resting against her ribs.

"Give me the chain," she said, her voice tightening with resolve. "Let us see what your grandfathers left for us to finish."

The road through the lower valley crossed a stone bridge of two segmental arches that spanned the Black River four furlongs above the weir.

Maren stopped on the crown of the span, looking down into the clear, green water.

Below the bridge, three large timber barges were tied to the river bank, loaded with crates of winter apples, smoked hams, and casks of mountain cider. The boatmen were sitting on the cargo crates, smoking clay pipes and listening to a young man playing a wooden pipe.

They were not fearful. They were not hiding in the willow scrub like outlaws.

They were waiting for the word from the chasm.

"They've been holding their cargo here since Michaelmas," Kellan said, resting his hand on the bridge parapet. "Waiting for the bridge to open so they can carry their goods to the high towns before the snow locks the pass. If the Guild burns the bridge, these men will have to dump their apples in the river and slaughter their oxen for tallow."

Maren looked at the boatmen's faces: weathered, patient, lined with the hard dignity of honest labor.

"We will not let them burn it, Kellan," she said.

Kellan unhooked the wicker basket from the pack-saddle and handed her the sixty-six-foot chain.

Together, they turned onto the rising mountain track and marched into the shadow of the great limestone gorge.

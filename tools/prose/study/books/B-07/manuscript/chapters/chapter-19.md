# Chapter 19: The Wet Season Approach

The weather vane on the high spire of the Guild Tower was an iron caravel with copper sails.

For three weeks it had pointed steadily to the southwest, bringing the warm, damp sea-fogs of the autumn trade winds. But on the third night of November, as Maren climbed the steep gravel track along the Black River gorge two miles ahead of Kellan's mule, the iron ship swung ninety degrees with a shrill, screaming screech that sounded like an owl caught in a trap.

The wind had shifted to the north-northeast.

It was the mountain wind: dry, razor-sharp, smelling of frozen pine needles, crushed granite, and the immense, clean cold of the high peaks.

Maren pulled the sheepskin hood of her cloak tighter beneath her chin.

The path here was nothing more than an ancient sheep-run, clinging to the dizzy verge of the limestone chasm eighty fathoms above the roaring water. Below in the dark, invisible beneath a boiling ribbon of white foam, the Black River smashed through the limestone gorge with a sound like continuous thunder.

Kellan walked behind her, leading his pack-mule by a rope halter. The mule was a sturdy mountain beast, short-legged and broad-chested, carrying two wooden crates containing Maren's plane table, sighting alidade, and three sixty-link surveyor chains, along with two kegs of salt pork and forty pounds of dry oatmeal for the road menders.

"Snow before midnight," Kellan shouted over the roar of the water.

He pointed his ashwood staff toward the dark saw-toothed horizon of the mountains.

Above the black silhouette of the peaks, the stars were vanishing behind a vast, dark wall of cloud that rose like an iron shutter across the sky. The first few flakes of snow drifted down through the cold air--not the wet, slushy flakes of the coast, but tiny, hard grains of dry ice that stung the skin like sand thrown from a shovel.

"How far to the bridge?" Maren asked, turning her face from the wind.

"Three miles of climbing to the limestone shelf," Kellan answered, his breath coming in white clouds from beneath his wool scarf. "Then half a league through the pine scree to the bridgehead. If the wind stays below thirty knots, we'll reach the quarry camp before the pass freezes."

"And if it doesn't?"

Kellan spat onto the scree. "If the blizzard locks the Stone Chasm before we set the keystone, no man walks this path until the spring thaw in May. The snow drifts thirty feet deep in the gorge cuts. High Carrow will have to eat its seed-corn to see the summer."

Maren reached inside her jerkin.

Her fingers touched the cedar pencil box tucked against her ribs.

The living vellum was burning against her skin.

It was no longer a gentle, pleasant warmth. It felt like an iron tile heated in a bakery oven. Through the cedar wood and the oiled silk, she could feel the frantic, rapid vibration of the hide, beating with a wild, erratic cadence that matched the rising roar of the wind in the gorge.

The map knew the storm was coming.

The parchment was counting down the hours.

On the living vellum, the three black arcs of the bridge were drawn in bold, heavy carbon strokes, but the central keystone remained open--a wedge-shaped gap of white vellum, two millimeters wide, waiting for the stone to be laid.

Around the gap, tiny, frantic hairline hatchings were spreading outward like frost crystals across a winter windowpane. The ink was racing against the temperature, throwing out emergency spurs and drainage contours, seeking to record every rock shelter and dry cave along the gorge before the cold paralyzed the land.

"Look down," Kellan said, grabbing her elbow with his gloved hand.

They had reached the crest of the lower bluff.

Behind them, four leagues to the south across the rolling black hills, the harbour city of Oakhaven lay spread along the grey water of the bay like a handful of scattered embers.

The high lantern of the Guild Tower was burning bright and red--the alarm signal of the Grand Wardens.

And winding out from the Red Sandstone Gate onto the north road, like a glittering serpent of fire, moved a line of twenty torches.

"Corvus's horse archers," Kellan muttered, his jaw setting hard. "The old warden didn't waste any time saddling his mounts. He's bringing the heavy carbines."

"They have to cross the river flats," Maren said, calculating the travel times with her surveyor's mind. "The mud on the lower road will take them three hours in the dark. A horse can't gallop on the limestone scree."

"Aye, but they've got bloodhounds from the customs yard," Kellan said grimly. "A hound doesn't care about the mud. He follows the scent of the sheepskin."

He struck the mule's rump with his ash staff.

The beast gave a low grunt, tucked its head down into the gale, and began scrambling up the steep limestone path, its iron-shod hooves throwing sparks from the flint stones.

The sleet turned to rain as the evening bell tolled for seven from the tower spire.

Maren stood by the stable arch in the lower quadrangle, wrapped in her wool traveling cloak, watching the grooms saddle four heavy pack-mules with leather panniers. The air smelled of wet horse dung, ammonia, and the bitter coal smoke of the smith's hearth.

She checked the contents of her satchel for the third time:

Her brass dividers, her ivory standard rule, her sight-alidade, the vial of indelible carbon ink, three goose quills cut for hairline hatching, and her father's pencil box containing the living vellum.

Behind the stable wall, two grooms were whispering in the dark.

"The Chief Warden ordered twenty horses shod with frost nails at noon," one groom muttered, tossing an iron hoof-pick into a bucket. "He's riding for the north gate before midnight. Bringing carbines and powder chests."

"Who's he hunting in the hills in a sleet storm?" the other asked.

"A girl from the scriptorium," the first answered. "They say she stole the master charter and ran for the mountain."

Maren drew her wool hood forward, stepped out of the shadow of the stable wall, and slipped silently through the postern gate into the rainy darkness of the river quays.

Maren turned her back on the burning red lantern of Oakhaven.

She leaned into the rising gale, her surveyor's boots biting into the gravel, and climbed toward the dark wall of the mountains.

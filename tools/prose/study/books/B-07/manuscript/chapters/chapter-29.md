# Chapter 29: The Snowline Descends

The ascent from the Bridge of the Chasm into the high cirque of High Carrow was a march through a world of white silence.

By three o'clock in the afternoon, the temperature had dropped ten degrees below freezing. The mountain air was thin and dry, catching in the throat like needles of glass, each breath burning the lungs with clean, icy fire.

The company moved in a single, resolute line along the newly cleared highway.

At the front walked Kellan Reed and Maren Vane, their ashwood staff and surveyor's rod probing the snowdrifts ahead to verify the solid limestone flags beneath the powder. Behind them marched Master Varek's fifty quarrymen, their crowbars and sledgehammers resting on their shoulders, followed by Master Corvus Althaus and his eighteen mounted archers, who had dismounted to lead their steaming horses by the bridle reins.

The road was thirty feet wide, carved directly out of the dolomite cliffs.

Where the precipice was sheer, ancient retaining walls of cyclopean masonry held the roadbed against the abyss; where the slope was gentle, the highway wound through dense forests of mountain pine whose branches groaned under the weight of the snow.

Maren pulled the hood of her traveling cloak tight around her ears.

Every thousand paces, they passed another granite waymarker, its carved numeral clear and sharp beneath the brushed snow: *League IV... League V... League VI*.

The living vellum in her satchel had grown completely calm.

It was no longer burning with feverish heat. The parchment had settled into a steady, pleasant warmth that felt like a loaf of fresh bread wrapped in a wool towel.

On the sheepskin, the entire highway from Oakhaven Harbour to the mountain crest was drawn in rich, deep carbon black, without a single gap or wavering stroke. Beside the road, the contours of the limestone ridges, the alpine torrents, and the terraced pastures of High Carrow sat in absolute, harmonious equilibrium.

The map had finished its work.

It had guided the tools to the stone; it had brought the Chief Warden to the road; it had closed the circuit of memory between the coast and the mountain.

"Look up, Maren," Kellan said, stopping on the brow of the high pass.

They had reached the timberline.

Above them, the pine forests ended abruptly at the edge of an immense amphitheater of towering limestone crags that climbed into the pale blue evening sky like the spires of a cathedral.

In the center of the amphitheater, perched upon a natural terrace of grey granite surrounded by three tiers of fortified stone walls, sat the mountain city of High Carrow.

It was not a village of starving miners.

It was an ancient, majestic bastion of grey stone, its slate roofs dusted with white snow, its twelve round artillery towers rising above the ramparts like chess pieces of dark basalt. From fifty stone chimneys, blue smoke drifted peacefully into the clear air; along the lower terraces, three massive watermills turned in the foaming torrent of the upper river, their iron-banded wheels grinding the winter grain.

And on the central keep, floating proudly against the snowy peaks, flew the ancient banner of the mountain: an open compass and a stone mason's chisel upon a field of green wool.

A great bronze bell began to chime from the town hall tower.

Three deep, resonant strokes that echoed off the limestone cliffs with the joy of a city welcoming its sons home from an exile of forty years.

The heavy oak gates of the south portal swung wide upon their iron hinges.

Down the paved highway, their torches throwing a warm golden radiance across the snowy road, streamed two hundred citizens of High Carrow: weavers in wool mantles, miners with iron lamps, children running in the drifts, and the town elders dressed in ancient gowns of scarlet cloth trimmed with marten fur.

At their head walked an old woman with hair as white as the mountain snow, leaning upon an ashwood staff carved with a surveyor's rose.

"Elder Martha," Kellan whispered, his eyes filling with tears of relief and triumph. "She was my grandfather's surveyor."

Corvus stepped up beside Maren, his cavalry boots crunching in the snow.

The Chief Warden looked at the great walled city rising before him, then down at the master survey roll he held in his hands.

"Forty years," Corvus murmured, his voice lost in the chime of the mountain bells. "God forgive us. We called them non-existent."

He folded the roll, unbuckled his sword belt, and handed his saber to one of his mounted sergeants.

The wind on the high pass blew the snow into swirling dunes that drifted across the thirty-foot roadbed like frozen ocean waves.

Maren walked beside Kellan, her hand resting on the ashwood sighting staff.

Ahead of them, the road was completely clear of brush and briars. Every hundred paces, they passed small stone heaps marking the culvert drains where the mountain runoff had been channeled safely beneath the limestone pavement.

"Varek's boys worked night and day to clear this stretch," Kellan said, his breath freezing on his woolen scarf. "They hauled forty tons of shale out of the road cut with hand barrows. When a man works that hard for a road, Maren, he doesn't forget where it leads."

She looked at the mountain peaks rising into the clear cobalt sky.

The fear that had haunted her for six years--the smell of burning paper in Harbour Square, the cold shadow of Master Corvus's compass, the terror of her father's heretic blood--had vanished completely.

She was not a heretic.

She was the daughter of Thomas Vane, and she had brought the highway back to the light.

Along the verges of the mountain road, the stone gutters were already running with cold meltwater from the upper slopes.

Maren checked the drainage gradient with her ivory pocket level.

The ancient masons had cut the gutters with a half-inch fall every ten paces, ensuring that the water drained into natural rock fissures before ice could form across the carriageway. Even under six inches of fresh powder, the center of the road remained crowned and dry.

"Johannes Althaus spent two months testing the water flow in the autumn of 1437," Kellan said, kicking a drift of snow from a stone drain. "He poured barrels of red dye into the ditches to see where the current ran. He was an obstinate man, but he knew that water is the only enemy a stone road has."

"We enter the city on foot, gentlemen," Corvus commanded his men. "We come as guests of the mountain, not as conquerors."

Together, the cartographers of the harbour and the masons of the mountain walked down the snowy road into the welcoming arms of High Carrow.

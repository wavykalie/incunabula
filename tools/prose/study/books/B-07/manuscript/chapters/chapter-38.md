# Chapter 38: The Winter Treaty

The descent from High Carrow was a triumphant progress through the thawed mountain valleys.

Along the thirty-foot highway, the twelve heavy mountain wains rolled smoothly on their iron-banded wheels, their teams of four oxen leaning into their leather collars with steady, powerful strides. Beside the wains walked forty mountain carters, their whips singing in the crisp air, singing old ballads of the silver mines that had not been heard in the lowlands for two generations.

Maren rode on the seat of the lead wagon beside Kellan Reed, her satchel resting between her boots, her hands tucked into the fleece lining of her cloak.

Behind them rode the eighteen mounted sergeants of the Guild Guard, their cavalry sabers sheathed, their carbines slung across their shoulders, laughing and exchanging tobacco with the mountain teamsters.

By noon of the second day, the caravan reached the Bridge of the Chasm.

The four-ton limestone keystone stood firm and immaculate in the center of the span, its carved crest catching the bright afternoon sun. The mortar had cured into hard, crystalline stone, binding the voussoirs into an unbroken monolith of compression that showed not a single hairline fracture under the weight of the heavy ore wagons.

As the lead wain crossed the central arch, Kellan pulled the oxen to a halt.

He jumped down from the wagon seat, walked to the granite parapet, and looked eighty fathoms down into the roaring flume of the Black River.

Maren stepped down beside him, resting her hands upon the cold stone coping.

"Three months ago," Kellan said, looking at her with his hazel eyes crinkling in the mountain light, "I was a timber smuggler dodging your master's bailiffs in the salt ditch. If someone had told me I'd be driving twelve wagons of mountain silver across this chasm with eighteen royal archers as an escort, I would have thrown him into the harbour mud."

"Three months ago," Maren replied with a quiet laugh, "I was scraping calfskin with fine pumice in the high tower, terrified that if my quill deviated by half a millimeter I would be burned in Harbour Square."

She looked down at the granite parapet.

Beneath the coping, preserved in the sheltered stone recess of the pier, lay the ancient waymarker Kellan had cleaned three days before: *IOHANNES ALTHAUS, MAGISTER CARTOGRAPHUS: ANNO DOMINI 1438*.

"We didn't invent anything, Kellan," she said softly. "We only picked up the chain where they dropped it."

"Aye," Kellan said, his hand resting upon her shoulder. "But you had the eyes to see where the chain lay, Maren. That's the difference between an apprentice who copies lines and a master who makes them."

He reached into his pocket and withdrew a small, heavy object wrapped in oilcloth.

He placed it in her palm.

It was an iron mason's chisel, four inches long, its square head battered by thousands of hammer blows, its tempered cutting edge honed to razor sharpness.

"My great-grandfather's chisel," Kellan said. "He used it to dress the first abutment block on this bridge in 1438. Keep it in your travelling case, Master Cartographer. When you sit at your fancy drafting desk in the harbour tower, let the iron remind you of the men who cut the stone."

Maren closed her fingers over the cold, scarred steel.

"I will never forget the stone, Kellan," she said, looking him in the eyes. "As long as I hold a pen, the mountain and the harbour will walk the same road."

Kellan smiled, turned toward the teamsters, and swung his arm forward.

As the wains rolled off the bridge onto the south shelf road, Kellan pointed his whip toward the ruined tower of the Quarry Post.

On the summit of the tower, where the ancient stone lintel had fallen sixty years before, three young quarrymen had raised an ashwood sighting staff banded in black and white lead paint.

From the top of the staff fluttered a pennant of green mountain wool.

"The first waystation is re-established," Kellan said proudly. "By midwinter, we'll have a permanent smithy and a relay stable for forty horses at the quarry. A traveler will be able to ride from Harbour Square to High Carrow in fifteen hours without changing his mount."

Maren touched the ancient iron chisel in her pocket, its scarred steel warm against her thigh.

"Fifteen hours," she whispered. "Instead of forty years in the dark."

The wains rumbled across the southern shelf of the gorge, their iron wheel-tires throwing up showers of white limestone grit that settled upon the gorse bushes like frost.

Beside the road, a mountain shepherd boy in a fleece vest was driving a flock of twenty black-faced sheep toward the newly opened bridge pasture.

The boy took off his woolen bonnet as the lead wagon passed, waving with both hands.

"Where are you taking the wool, Waymarker?" the boy shouted above the rumble of the wheels.

"To Harbour Square, lad!" Kellan shouted back, cracking his whip over the oxen. "To the great weigh-house by the sea!"

The boy's face lit up with wide, joyful wonder. He turned and shouted to his sheep in the mountain tongue, his shrill calls echoing off the limestone cliffs like birdsong.

Maren looked at Kellan, then down at the ancient iron chisel resting in her palm.

The road was alive with life once more. The sheep were crossing the chasm; the wagons were rolling toward the sea; and the silence of forty years was broken forever by the sound of children's laughter.

The sun was warm upon Maren's face as the lead wagon crossed the lower boundary stone that marked the entrance to the coastal plains.

She took out her father's pencil box and laid her finger upon the sliding lid.

The wood was smooth, dark with the grease of twenty years of handling. For six years, she had kept the box hidden under her straw mattress, terrified that the sight of its carved compass rose would bring the bailiffs to her door.

Now the box sat openly upon the wagon seat in the bright afternoon light.

"We don't have to hide the box anymore, Father," she whispered.

"Wains forward!" he bellowed. "To Oakhaven and the sea!"

The oxen leaned into their yokes, the iron wheels rumbled across the limestone span, and the trade caravan rolled off the bridge onto the south shelf road, descending toward the agricultural valley and the distant red walls of the city.

# Chapter 20: The Theft of the Seal

The memories of her last hour in the Guild Tower came back to Maren as she climbed the dark gorge, every detail etched into her mind with the permanent bite of acid on a copperplate.

It had been four o'clock on the afternoon of her flight.

The bell for evening chapel had been ringing across the quadrangle, calling the copyists and wardens to the cathedral close. The high scriptorium was empty; the fires had been banked with wet peat, the drafting tables cleared of quills and inkstands.

Maren had slipped down the spiral stairs not to her garret, but to the Archivist's private drafting cabinet behind the library vaults.

She needed the seal.

Without an authentic impression of the Chancellor's Survey Seal upon heavy parchment, any map she carried beyond the chasm was legal contraband that justified her summary hanging by the first patrol of Town Wardens she encountered.

She knew where the master seal was kept.

It lay in a brass-bound mahogany casket on the upper shelf of the charter cupboard, secured by a four-tumbler brass lock whose key hung from Archivist Sibyl Thorne's iron belt hoop.

Maren carried two instruments in her pocket: a lump of softened beeswax wrapped in oiled silk, and a fine steel pick she had fashioned from a broken compass leg.

The lock on the cupboard was old. She knelt before the mahogany doors, inserted the steel pick into the keyway, and lifted the first tumbler.

The iron gave a dry, soft click.

She lifted the second tumbler.

"The third tumbler has a double notch, child," a dry, whistling voice whispered from the dark alcove behind her. "You have to turn the pick five degrees to the left before you lift, or the ward springs back and locks the bolt."

Maren froze. Her blood turned to ice water in her veins.

She slowly withdrew the steel pick, rose to her feet, and turned around.

Archivist Sibyl Thorne was sitting in an ancient high-backed oak chair in the shadow of the window drapery, wrapped in a black woolen shawl that smelled of dried lavender and vinegar. She held no keys. Her hands were folded peacefully in her lap, her single milky blue eye watching Maren with the quiet, unblinking amusement of a cat upon a wall.

"Mistress Sibyl," Maren whispered, her hand instinctively dropping to her side.

"Do not make excuses, Maren," the old woman said softly, her breath rustling like dry parchment. "You are thirty years too late to deceive me. Your father stood in that very spot with a piece of bees' wax in his pocket on the night he took the 1440 traverse."

She reached beneath her wool shawl.

She did not draw out a brass key. She withdrew an object wrapped in heavy, waterproof oilskin, roughly fourteen inches long and three inches in diameter, secured by two brass buckles.

She held it out across the dark space between them.

"Take it," Sibyl ordered.

Maren took two hesitant steps forward and received the cylinder.

It was heavy, made of thick, seasoned calfskin boiled in wax and tallow, completely impervious to water, snow, or cellar damp. On the brass end-cap was engraved the ancient crest: the open compass, the stone mason's chisel, and the sighting staff.

"Open the cap," Sibyl said.

Maren unscrewed the brass fitting.

Inside lay three sheets of pristine, heavy vellum, already stamped in the lower right-hand corner with the Great Seal of the Guild Chancellor in deep red wax. The date lines were blank; the surveyor's name line was blank; the cartouche was blank.

Beside the vellum lay a flat brass case holding six fine steel etching burins and a small vial of indelible carbon black ink mixed with gum mastic.

"My own travelling case," Sibyl whispered, her hand touching her black eye-patch. "Forty years ago, before the acid vessel burst in the laboratory, I was the Senior Surveyor of the Northern Reach. I drew the first three leagues of the chasm road for Chancellor Althaus--Corvus's uncle. When the Council decided to burn the maps, they told me to bring my sketchbooks to Harbour Square."

Her single eye flared with a cold, terrifying fire that had burned for four decades in the dark.

"I gave them five dummy rolls filled with blank soundings. I hid the true plates in the salt cellars, behind the foundation stones of the north pier. When Corvus found out, he didn't dare hang me--the Guild laws protect a sworn archivist from the cord. He simply took my laboratory keys and locked me in the cellars for forty years."

She leaned forward, her crooked fingers catching Maren's wrist with an iron grip that belied her frail body.

"Corvus thinks he is strong because he has the town halberds and the bronze brazier. But he is a terrified old man, Maren. He is terrified of his dead brother, terrified of the sea, and terrified of the earth that refuses to stay dead."

She released Maren's wrist and pointed toward the narrow postern door behind the filing presses.

"The postern leads to the salt ditch behind the timber wharf. Kellan Reed is waiting at the coal jetty. Go to the chasm, Maren. Lay the keystone across the gorge. And when the road is finished, bring me a piece of mountain stone and lay it upon my drafting table, so that I may know that an honest line was drawn in this kingdom before I die."

"I will bring it, Mistress Sibyl," Maren whispered, tears burning her eyes for the first time in six years.

Sibyl nodded once, leaned back into the shadows of her high-backed chair, and closed her single blue eye.

Sibyl's hand on Maren's wrist was cold, but the grip was like an iron clamp.

"Listen to me, Apprentice," the old Archivist whispered, her single blue eye boring into Maren's face through the gloom of the salt vault. "When you reach the chasm, do not let Varek lay the keystone by eye. A mason builds by the weight of the stone, but an arch is held aloft by its angle. If the radius is out by two lines, the compression will split the limestone voussoirs when the first frost bites the mortar."

She reached into her black apron and drew out a small ivory tablet covered with microscopic figures.

"Here are the trigonometric tangents of Johannes Althaus," Sibyl said, pressing the tablet into Maren's hand. "He calculated the radius of the chasm bridge in the winter of 1438. The radius is sixty-two feet and four inches. Keep that number in your mind, Maren. If the arch matches that radius, the bridge will stand for five hundred winters."

Maren closed her fingers over the ivory tablet.

"I will remember the radius, Mistress Sibyl."

"Go now," the old woman said, releasing her wrist. "And do not look back at this tower. A cartographer who looks back turns into a pillar of salt."

"Go now," she whispered into the darkness. "The tide does not wait for apprentices, and neither does the King's fire."

Maren had turned, unlatched the postern door, and stepped out into the cold sea fog of the harbor.

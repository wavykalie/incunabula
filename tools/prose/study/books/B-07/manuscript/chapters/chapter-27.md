# Chapter 27: The Keystone of the Arch

The south abutment of the Bridge of the Chasm was an arena of timber, iron, and stone.

On the rock platform, eighty paces above the boiling foam of the river, the four-ton keystone of High Carrow hung suspended from the oak shear-legs. It was a massive wedge of crystalline white limestone, carved by Master Varek's own hands, its face chiseled with the deep, bold relief of the founding crest: the compass, the chisel, and the sighting staff.

Four heavy hemp cables ran from the iron lifting-dogs in the stone to the drums of two double-purchase timber winches anchored to the rock by iron holdfasts.

Around the winches stood twelve masons, their bare arms corded with muscle, their wool shirts soaked with sweat despite the freezing mountain air.

"Hold the fall-tackle!" Master Varek bellowed, standing upon the timber falsework five feet above the abyss. "Journeyman Vane! Check the bearing!"

Maren knelt on the icy timber staging at the very edge of the central span.

The sixty-foot gulf fell away beneath her boots. Eighty fathoms down, the Black River smashed against the granite cutwaters in a roar of white fury that sent cold spray flying past her face like frozen hail.

She brought her brass sight-alidade to her eye.

Across the gap, on the north abutment, young Peter held the ashwood sighting rod steady against the centerline anchor mark.

Through the narrow slit, Maren saw that the wooden falsework had settled half an inch under the morning frost.

"Slack the eastern winch by two notches!" she shouted, her voice ringing clear and authoritative across the chasm. "Heave on the west! Bring the stone forward three inches to clear the spring-joint!"

The iron pawls on the winches clicked with heavy, metallic snaps: *clack-clack-clack*.

The great limestone wedge swung out over the abyss.

It hung suspended between the grey sky and the roaring river, turning slowly in the cold drafts that tore through the gorge. The hemp cables groaned under the four-ton load, stretching until droplets of water squeezed from the twisted fibers like tears.

"Lower away!" Varek shouted, his iron trowel raised above his head.

"Steady!" Maren commanded, her eyes fixed on the level bubble of her alidade. "Down three inches... down two... hold!"

The keystone entered the central notch between the two curved limestone arms of the arch.

The joint was an engineering masterpiece: two inches of clearance on either side, waiting for the bed of hot slaked lime and crushed quartz mortar that two masons were troweling across the stone faces with furious speed.

A sudden shout echoed from the pine woods behind the south abutment.

"Halt in the King's name!"

Maren whipped her head around.

Master Corvus Althaus had broken through the snowy trees at the head of his twenty horsemen. His black stallion skidded to a halt on the icy gravel fifty paces from the winches.

The Chief Warden's face was white with fury, his scarlet cloak flying behind him like the wings of a vulture. Behind him, twelve horse archers were already swinging their carbines from their saddle holsters, blowing on their wheel-lock matches.

"Varek!" Corvus roared, drawing his cavalry saber with a screech of steel on iron. "Step away from those winches! By the authority of the Grand Council, this bridge is condemned! Any man who touches that stone will be hanged for high treason!"

The twelve masons at the winches froze, their hands gripping the oak brake levers, their eyes darting between the leveled carbines and their master.

Varek did not move from the falsework. He stood above the abyss, his heavy iron hammer resting on his shoulder, his grey beard bristling in the wind.

"The stone belongs to the mountain, Warden," Varek called back, his voice steady as granite. "The King's law stops at the river."

Corvus turned his black stallion directly toward the timber winches.

"Archers!" he shouted. "Level your weapons! Prepare to--"

"Wait!"

Maren's voice rang across the platform like a cracked whip.

She stepped off the timber staging, walked past the twelve trembling masons, and stopped ten paces in front of Corvus's horse.

She held no weapon. She carried only the brass cylinder of Sibyl's travelling case in her left hand, and in her right, unfurled against the freezing wind, the living vellum scrap.

Corvus pulled his stallion's head back with a sharp jerk of the reins.

His dark eyes dropped to her face, then to the vellum she held before his chest.

"Maren," he whispered, his voice cracking with a terrible, grief-stricken rage. "You stole the master chains. You broke the apprentice oath. You joined with mountain rebels to destroy the peace I spent thirty years building."

"Look at the map, Master," she said.

Her voice was completely calm, devoid of fear, devoid of hatred.

She stepped forward until the edge of the sheepskin was two feet from his horse's nose.

The black stallion gave a low snort, its nostrils flaring as it caught the scent of the hide.

Corvus looked down.

He could not help himself. A cartographer who had spent forty years examining lines of ink could no more refuse to look at a map than a drowning sailor could refuse to look at a thrown rope.

What he saw upon the sheepskin made the sword in his right hand drop by three inches.

The ink was alive.

Before his very eyes, under the pale, cold light of the mountain morning, the black carbon lines were moving across the hide.

The three arcs of the bridge were complete. The four-ton keystone was drawn in the center, hovering two inches above the spring-line, vibrating in perfect harmony with the limestone block that hung from the oak shear-legs thirty feet behind her.

And in the lower corner of the vellum, drawn in the deep, indelible red of ancient cinnabar, sat his own family crest.

Not the modern Guild seal of the closed anchor.

The ancient founding seal of Chancellor Althaus, his great-uncle: the compass, the chisel, and the sighting staff.

"This is... impossible," Corvus choked out. His knuckles turned white on his saber hilt. "The 1440 plates were melted down forty years ago."

"They weren't melted, Master," Maren said softly. "They were hidden in the salt cellars by Archivist Sibyl. And the stone was preserved in the mountain by Master Varek."

She pointed her finger toward the suspended keystone.

"Look at your boots, Master Althaus. You are standing on cut limestone laid by your own ancestors. Do you intend to burn the mountain, or only the skin that names it?"

Corvus stared at her. His chest heaved beneath his steel breastplate.

For thirty seconds, the only sound in the gorge was the roar of the Black River and the groaning of the hemp cables in the wind.

The horse archers behind him sat motionless, their carbines lowered, their eyes fixed upon their commander's pale, trembling face.

Maren did not look at the soldiers. She turned her head toward Master Varek on the staging.

"Drop the stone, Master Varek!" she commanded.

Varek swung his iron hammer.

With a single, thunderous blow, he struck the trip-pawl of the main winch.

The hemp cables screamed through the sheaves.

The four-ton limestone keystone dropped into the central notch.

It met the mortar bed with a deep, hollow, bone-jarring *THUMP* that shook the rock platform beneath Corvus's horse's hooves.

The mortar squeezed from the joints in grey ribbons.

The oak wedges of the timber centering groaned under the sudden, colossal transfer of weight, cracked with the sound of snapping bone, and dropped three inches clear of the masonry.

The arch was closed.

Sixty feet of solid limestone spanned the chasm, holding itself aloft across the eighty-fathom drop by the pure, mathematically perfect geometry of compression.

And as the stone settled into the granite piers, Maren felt an astonishing sensation through the soles of her surveyor's boots.

A wave of deep, vibrating warmth surged through the living rock of the abutment, through the leather of her boots, through her bones, and struck into the living vellum in her hands.

On the sheepskin, the wedge-shaped gap of white vellum vanished.

The keystone drew itself in deep, velvety carbon black, locked forever into the ancient road.

The timber shear-legs groaned under the colossal weight of the suspended keystone.

The oak poles were eighteen inches thick, hewn from ancient mountain oaks that had grown in the high valleys for two centuries. Iron reinforcing hoops bound the ends to prevent splitting; four-fold tackle of two-inch hemp cable ran through iron snatch-blocks greased with mutton tallow.

Maren stood on the timber centering five feet above the abyss, her surveyor's level held in both hands.

The river spray was freezing on her eyebrows; the cold wind from the gorge tore at her wool skirts, threatening to pull her into the eighty-fathom drop below.

"Hold the winches!" she shouted, her voice cutting through the water's roar. "Master Varek! The west face is tight by three lines!"

Varek swung his iron cold chisel, chipping a quarter-inch flake of limestone from the mortar bed of the northern spring-block. The white dust blew into the abyss like snow.

"Try the drop now, lass!" Varek called back, wiping the sweat and stone dust from his brow.

The twelve masons at the winches leaned their weight against the oak brake levers.

The great four-ton wedge descended inch by inch.

The clearance was terrifyingly small: an inch of space on either side, filled with hot slaked lime mortar that smelled of fire and slaked stone. If the keystone jammed between the voussoirs before it reached the bed, the arch would be ruined, and forty men's labor for six months would be lost in the gorge.

"Lower!" Maren cried, her eyes locked on the level bubble. "Lower two inches... one inch... hold!"

The keystone settled into the mortar with a deep, shuddering impact that rattled the teeth in her jaw.

The compression struck through the arch like a thunderbolt.

The two halves of the stone span, which had leaned outward upon the timber scaffolding for eighty years, locked together in an unbroken ring of living limestone. The timber falsework groaned, the oak wedges cracked under the transfer of weight, and the bridge stood aloft upon its own masonry.

The iron winches screamed as the twelve masons hauled on the oak brake levers.

The four-ton keystone hung suspended twenty feet above the central gap, swaying in the freezing drafts that tore through the gorge like a pendulum of white limestone.

Corvus had halted his black stallion fifty paces away on the snowy shelf, his drawn saber resting across his pommel, his twenty horse archers watching the immense stone with wide, terrified eyes.

"Althaus!" Master Varek shouted across the platform, his voice carrying over the thunder of the river. "You carry the King's commission! Look at that stone! That limestone was cut from the quarry your own great-uncle opened in 1438! The crest on that keystone was carved by my grandfather for Johannes Althaus! Are you going to shoot the men who are putting your own family's seal back where it belongs?"

Corvus did not answer immediately.

His eyes were locked on the carved face of the keystone.

Through the mist of the river and the flying snow, he saw the deep, bold relief of the open compass, the stone mason's chisel, and the sighting staff--the very crest that was engraved upon the silver watch in his waistcoat pocket.

His saber hand began to tremble.

"The Charter..." Corvus whispered, his voice cracking with agony. "The Charter of 1492..."

"The Charter of 1492 was a lie, Master!" Maren cried out, stepping between his horse and the winches. "You know it was a lie! Your brother Lucian didn't die because of this road! He died because the charts were false! Will you kill another twenty men to protect a piece of stamped calfskin?"

Corvus looked down at her.

He saw the fire in her eyes, the charcoal on her fingers, and the living vellum she held before his face like a mirror of truth.

The saber slipped from his fingers, falling into the slush with a dull clatter.

Corvus dropped his cavalry saber.

The steel blade clattered against the limestone flags, ringing twice before coming to rest in the cold mountain slush at Maren's feet.

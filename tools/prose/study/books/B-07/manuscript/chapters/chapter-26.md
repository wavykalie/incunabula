# Chapter 26: The Wardens' Horsemen

The blizzard ceased three hours before dawn, leaving the mountain gorge wrapped in an immense, breathless silence.

The limestone crags were plastered with six inches of fresh powder that glittered like diamond dust under the cold stars. Every pine branch carried a heavy bolster of white; the roar of the Black River was muffled, subdued by the thick shelf of ice that had crept out from both banks overnight.

Maren walked beside Kellan at the head of a supply convoy of six pack mules.

They were moving down the narrow shelf road toward the bridgehead, carrying the last crates of forged iron cramps, two kegs of slaked lime mortar, and the heavy ashwood shear-legs needed for the keystone hoist.

Kellan stopped suddenly. He raised his hand, his head tilted toward the southern reaches of the gorge.

In the dead, frozen air, carrying across a league of snow-choked pine woods, came a sound that made every mule twitch its ears: the sharp, rhythmic jingle of iron bit-rings and the muffled thud of galloping hooves on wet snow.

"Corvus," Kellan breathed, his jaw tightening under his wool scarf. "He didn't wait for the daylight. He rode through the blizzard."

He scrambled up a rock spur beside the path, keeping his body low against the snowdrifts, and peered through his brass field glass toward the lower valley.

Maren climbed up beside him, her boots sinking to the shins in the powder.

A mile below, where the river track emerged from the pine forest onto the lower limestone flats, twenty horsemen in steel caps and scarlet coats were advancing at a steady trot.

At their head rode Master Corvus on his black stallion, his scarlet cloak thrown back to reveal his steel breastplate, an unlit branding torch resting in his leather stirrup-boot. Behind him, two pack-horses carried long wooden chests bound with iron bands: black powder.

"They'll be at the bridge in twenty minutes," Kellan muttered, lowering his glass. "We can't get the mortar wagons across the open shelf without them sighting us from the lower bend."

"We don't need the open shelf," Maren said.

She drew the living vellum scrap from the breast of her coat.

She spread it upon the flat crust of a snowdrift, pointing with her lead stylus to a faint, dotted black line that ran along the eastern face of the bluff fifty paces above their heads.

"What is that?" Kellan asked, squinting at the drawing. "There's no path up there. That's the Devil's Chimney--sheer dolomite rock."

"Look at the contour intervals," Maren insisted, her finger tracing the black dots. "The rock is not sheer. There is a dry gully behind the outer face, an ancient watercourse carved by the glacier. It runs behind the pinnacles and comes out directly upon the south abutment sixty feet above the horsemen's line of sight."

Kellan stared at the map, then up at the towering wall of white rock above them.

Through the snow-laden pines, half-concealed by an overhanging shoulder of limestone, he saw a narrow cleft in the rock, four paces wide, choked with dry scrub oak and scrub pine.

"A goat couldn't take a loaded mule through that cleft," he said dubiously.

"An apprentice who spent six years studying terrain geometry can," Maren retorted. She caught the bridle of the lead mule, turned the beast from the main track, and plunged directly into the snowy scrub.

For fifteen agonizing minutes, the small convoy fought its way through the hidden gully.

The snow in the cleft was waist-deep in places, powdery and loose. The men worked in absolute silence, heaving the mules forward by their halters, their shoulders braced against the wooden pack-boxes, their breath coming in ragged, steaming gasps. Not a man spoke; not a mule brayed.

Twenty feet below them, through the gaps between the rock pinnacles, they could see the main shelf road.

The Guild troop rode past beneath them like ghosts in the morning light.

Corvus rode with his chin dropped onto his steel gorget, his dark eyes scanning the snowy track ahead for footprints. His face was grey with fatigue and bitter cold, his iron-grey eyebrows crusted with white frost.

Beside him rode Warden Brandt, shivering in his beaver coat, clutching his saddle pommel with terrified, blue fingers.

"The track is clean, Master Althaus," Brandt croaked, his voice carrying clearly up to the hidden gully. "There are no wagon ruts. Perhaps the miners abandoned the bridge when the snow began."

"A mountain miner does not abandon stone, Brandt," Corvus replied, his voice heavy, hollow, and implacable as an iron bell. "He is waiting for us at the chasm. Look to your carbine flints. If a man raises a hammer on that bridge, shoot him through the heart."

The troop trotted past, their horses' hooves churning the pristine snow of the main road into grey slush, disappearing around the final granite shoulder toward the bridgehead.

Maren let out the breath she had been holding for three minutes.

Her fingers were frozen around the mule's leather reins, but beneath her ribs, her heart was beating with calm, cold precision.

She turned to Kellan.

"The gully exits fifty paces ahead, right above the crane staging," she whispered.

Kellan grinned, wiped the sweat and snow from his forehead with his sleeve, and gave her a sharp, admiring nod.

The dry gully wound behind the limestone pinnacles like a secret passage through a ruined fortress.

Maren led the third mule by its halter, her boots finding purchase on the icy rock shelf. Above them, the overhanging brow of dolomite rock sheltered the path from the falling snow, creating a dry, sheltered corridor fifty paces long that smelled of ancient bat dung and dry moss.

Kellan stopped at a narrow embrasure in the outer rock face.

He beckoned to Maren.

She crept to his side, peering through the gap between two natural limestone pillars.

Twenty feet below, on the open roadbed, Master Corvus was checking his horse's girth. The Chief Warden was standing in the snow, his cavalry saber resting against his thigh, his dark eyes scanning the gorge ahead.

Beside him, two sergeants were unlashing the wooden powder chests from the pack-horses.

"Set the powder kegs beneath the timber falsework on the south abutment," Corvus was commanding, his voice carrying clearly up the rock chimney. "If the miners resist, light the fuses and blow the staging into the gorge."

Maren felt a cold shudder run down her arms.

Corvus was not bluffing. He carried the black powder; he carried the match. If they did not drop the keystone within the hour, the bridge would vanish into a cloud of sulfur and shattered timber.

"We move now," Kellan whispered in her ear, his breath hot against her cheek. "Take the gully to the crane staging."

"Lead on, Cartographer," he whispered back. "Let's show your Master how we drop a stone."

They hauled the mules through the last drift of snow and stepped out onto the high granite platform of the south abutment, three minutes ahead of the Chief Warden's horsemen.

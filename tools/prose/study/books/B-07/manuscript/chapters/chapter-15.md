# Chapter 15: Kellan at the Tavern

The sign of the Salt Basin Tavern was an iron hoop hung with dried salt cod that rattled like wooden shingles in the night wind.

The tavern sat below the high-water line on the mudflats between the timber wharves and the evaporation pans. Its floor was thick oak planking crusted with dried sea salt and spilled ale; its ceiling was so low that a tall carter had to stoop to avoid the blackened rafters hung with smoked herrings and oil-flasks.

The room was hot, noisy, and thick with the bitter smoke of peat fires and cheap tobacco.

Maren sat on a corner bench behind an empty cider cask, her wool hood drawn forward over her face. She had traded her grey apprentice apron for a rough wool jerkin she had bought from a slop-seller on the quay, but she had kept her surveyor's boots.

At the table across from her sat Kellan Reed.

He had laid his ashwood sighting staff against the wall, but his mason's hammer and cold chisel were on the rough oak table before him, beside an earthenware pitcher of dark mountain ale.

He was using the point of his belt knife to carve a small, five-pointed compass star into the soft pine of the tabletop. With every cut of the blade, a tiny curl of pale wood peeled away from the grain.

"You took your time getting here, Journeyman Vane," Kellan said without looking up from his carving. "I've been sitting on this bench since the harbor bells rang for six."

"The gate was closed by Warden Brandt," Maren said, her voice low and tight. "The archers are searching every wagon on the north road."

Kellan grunted. He blew a sliver of wood from the carved star.

"Brandt is an old woman with a gold chain. Let him search the hay wains. A wagon doesn't need the King's road when there's an ancient bridle path running through the dry salt ditch three furlongs west of the gatehouse."

He drove the tip of his knife into the center of the compass star, leaving the blade quivering in the wood, then leaned forward across the table. His hazel eyes were bloodshot from wind and forge smoke, but their focus was sharp and steady.

"Why did you ask for me at the timber yard, Maren?"

Maren looked around the room.

Two tables away, three coal-heavers were arguing over a game of bone dice. By the bar, an old sailor with a wooden peg leg was singing a mournful ballad about a ship lost off the western rocks. No one was watching them.

She reached into the breast of her wool jerkin.

She withdrew the living vellum scrap, wrapped in a square of oiled silk, and laid it on the beer-stained wood between them.

Kellan looked at the silk. "What's this? Another piece of your master's parchment to tell me my boots are in the wrong county?"

"Open it," she said.

Kellan hesitated. His thick, scarred fingers unfolded the oiled silk.

When his eyes fell upon the sheepskin, his face went completely still.

The cynicism that had curled his lip vanished. His heavy shoulders dropped. He stared at the black lines drawn across the vellum, his calloused thumb hovering half an inch above the surface as if he were afraid the ink would burn his flesh.

"Where did you get this?" he whispered.

"I didn't get it from anywhere," Maren answered, leaning closer so her lips were three inches from his ear. "I prepared the vellum in the scriptorium six days ago. It had only the harbour soundings on it. The road drew itself."

Kellan's gaze snapped to her face.

"Don't talk fairy-tales to me, lass. Ink doesn't move on paper."

"It isn't on the paper," Maren said. "It is inside the skin. Look at the chasm entrance. Look at the dolomite shelf. Look at the northern spur."

Kellan picked up the scrap. He held it close to the tallow candle on the table, his eyes narrowing as he followed the hair-thin black lines.

His thumb moved to the cross and the letter *K* at the ruined tower.

"That's my mark," he murmured, his voice suddenly rough with wonder. "I chiseled that cross into the lintel of the Quarry Post yesterday at noon, when we cleared the fallen archway. How could you know I carved a cross on the lintel?"

"I didn't know," Maren said. "The map knew."

Kellan sat back against the rough timber wall of the tavern, the vellum resting upon his knee. He rubbed his jaw with his sleeve, staring at her through the peat smoke.

"My grandfather," he said softly, "used to tell a story by the winter hearth in High Carrow. He said that when the ancient kings built the road through the gorge, they didn't just lay cut limestone on the dirt. They laid down an oath with the earth. They had masters of the craft who knew how to bind the stone, the river, and the parchment together with iron vitriol and mountain water, so that what was built in the rock was recorded in the archives, and what was drawn in the archives was held steady by the stone."

He looked down at the parchment again.

"He said that when the harbour dukes burned the maps forty years ago, they thought they were just burning calfskin. But the road didn't die. It just went to sleep. Waiting for someone who had the hands to wake it."

"The Guild is sending horse archers to the chasm tomorrow," Maren said. "Warden Brandt has orders to arrest every mason and blow the rock shelf with powder."

Kellan let out a dark, savage curse.

He reached out and snatched his belt knife from the tabletop, sliding it back into its leather sheath with a sharp click.

"They won't blow the shelf," he said. "The dolomite is three fathoms thick. A keg of black powder would only chip the face."

He looked at her, his eyes assessing her from boots to hood.

"We need a surveyor, Maren."

"I am a copyist," she said.

"You're a cartographer," Kellan said, his voice hard as his mason's hammer. "You know the plane table. You know how to take sights with the alidade. The bridge over the chasm is down. The four central arches fell sixty years ago when the ice choked the gorge. We've cut the limestone voussoirs for the new span, but we don't have the center-line. If we hoist the keystone half an inch out of true, the arch will buckle under its own weight when the timber centering is struck."

He pointed his thick finger at the vellum.

"The ancient span is on this sheepskin. The radius of the arch, the spring-line, the bearing of the piers. If you come with me to the gorge tonight, you can lay the sight-line across the chasm with your brass instruments. We can have the keystone set before Corvus's horsemen reach the river."

Maren stared at him.

Her heart was pounding so hard she could hear the pulse in her temples.

If she left the city tonight, there would be no return. Her name would be struck from the journeyman register. Her tools would be broken upon the anvil in Harbour Square. She would be an outlaw, hunted across the province with an price on her head.

She thought of Master Corvus's grey, severe face, his iron compass clicking in the high cabinet, his dead brother Lucian lying on the shingle with seaweed in his hair.

Corvus thought he was saving the world from shipwreck.

He was only drowning it in silence.

Maren reached out and picked up the living vellum scrap. She folded it carefully into its oiled silk wrap and tucked it deep inside the lining of her wool jerkin.

"I have my brass dividers, my alidade, and my compass box," she said, looking Kellan squarely in the hazel eyes. "Where is the boat?"

Kellan smiled. It was the first time she had seen him smile, and it transformed his rough, weathered face, revealing the stubborn, unyielding pride of the mountain folk.

The fire in the tavern hearth popped, throwing a shower of bright peat sparks across the wet oak floorboards.

A woman carrying an iron tray of smoked herrings pushed past their table, her wooden clogs clattering against the planking. Kellan reached out with his left hand, caught a pewter mug of cider from her tray, tossed a copper groat onto her apron, and slid the mug to Maren.

"Drink it, lass," he said. "Your lips are blue as lead shot. Scriptorium air thins a person's blood."

Maren took the mug. The hot cider smelled of cinnamon, cloves, and sour apples; the warmth of the pewter seeped through her cold fingertips, loosening the stiffness in her wrists.

"How many masons are in the gorge?" she asked, setting the mug down.

"Eighty stonecutters from the High Carrow quarries," Kellan answered, leaning across the table. "Forty carters with ox teams. We've got six blacksmiths forging iron cramps at the lower weir, and twenty timbermen cutting oak centering in the high pine woods. We've spent three thousand silver florins on this work since Midsummer."

"Where did the mountain folk get three thousand florins?"

Kellan gave a short, hard snort that rattled his ale mug.

"We sold silver ore to the northern dukes across the pass," he said, his hazel eyes flashing with defiance. "The northern provinces don't have an Admiralty Guild with scarlet robes to tell them whose silver is legal and whose silver is contraband. They pay fifty silver pennies on the pound for our grey tin and our lead. We brought the coin back in pack saddles over the high snowfields while your Master Corvus was sitting in his cabinet tracing the South Headland for the three thousandth time."

He leaned closer, his voice dropping into a harsh, conspiratorial whisper.

"The mountain people don't want a war with Oakhaven, Maren. We want to trade. We want your harbour salt to cure our mutton; we want your imported oil for our wool combs; we want your iron from the Swedish ketches. But we won't pay the Duke three pence on every pound of rye just because his grandfather signed a piece of sheepskin that called our valley a wasteland."

Kellan reached into his rough coat pocket and pulled out a worn buckskin pouch tied with a sinew cord.

He opened the pouch on the table between them.

Inside lay three small coins of refined silver, roughly octagonal, stamped on one side with the image of an eagle and on the other with a Latin inscription: *CIVITAS CARROVIENSIS: ANNO 1560*.

Maren picked up one of the coins.

The metal was heavy, cold, and extraordinarily pure. It lacked the dull, grey lead cast of the debased shillings that the Duke's mint issued in Oakhaven. The silver was bright as fresh snow, ringing with a clear, high pitch when she tapped it against the oak tabletop.

"The mountain mines are producing twenty thousand ounces of this silver every summer," Kellan said, his voice quiet and proud. "The veins in the high cirque are four feet wide, running straight through the grey slate into the heart of the mountain. We have four water-driven stamping mills crushing the ore night and day."

He looked at her with his hazel eyes flashing in the candle flame.

"The Duke wants that silver, Maren. He wants to force our miners to bring the ore down the river barges so his collectors can take forty ounces out of every hundred for the royal treasury. That is why he burned the road. He would rather see the silver stay buried in the rock than let the mountain folk trade it as free men."

Maren laid the silver coin back in the buckskin pouch.

"The road will carry the silver, Kellan," she said. "And the Guild will seal the wagons."

"Tied under the coal jetty," he said. "Two oars and no rowlocks. We'll be three leagues past the water-gate before the watchman rings for midnight."

He picked up his mason's hammer, dropped two copper pennies upon the tabletop for the ale, and walked toward the rear door of the tavern.

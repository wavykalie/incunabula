# Chapter 17: Corvus's Suspicion

The rain began at midnight, drumming a heavy, steady tattoo against the leaded windows of Master Corvus's private cabinet.

Corvus did not sleep. At fifty-eight, sleep was a fragile thing that visited him only in the small hours between three and five, and even then his dreams were filled with the sound of surf breaking upon the Black Shoals and the white face of his brother Lucian floating beneath the foam.

He sat at his cherrywood drafting desk in his dressing gown of dark blue wool, a single wax taper burning in a silver candlestick beside his right hand.

Before him lay the Master Survey Register of Oakhaven Harbour.

He turned the leaves slowly, his scarred fingers pausing on every entry.

*Sheet 41: The South Spit. Copyist: Anselm. Error: half a fathom on the low-water spit. Corrected by scraping.*

*Sheet 42: The Basin Jetties. Copyist: Orson. Disqualified for cartographic heresy.*

*Sheet 43: The Harbour Channel. Copyist: Maren Vane. Flawless.*

He closed the register. The leather boards met with a dull, heavy thump.

He picked up his brass dividers and tapped the joint against his cheekbone.

Something was wrong in the tower.

He had felt it two days ago in Harbour Square, when he looked down from the mounting block and met Maren Vane's eyes across the smoking brazier. There had been no fear in her face. An apprentice who had seen her father's sketches burned twelve years ago should have looked at the fire with terror, or at least with the sullen resentment of a beaten hound.

Maren had looked at the flames with the cold, assessing eye of a surveyor measuring a baseline.

Then there was Archivist Sibyl Thorne.

Sibyl had spent forty years in the salt cellars without uttering three sentences together to any living soul in the tower. Yet yesterday, when the inventory of the 1540 tidal master was concluded, the old woman had mentioned Chancellor Vane twice in the council corridor, whispering about mountain boundaries and broken locks.

Corvus stood up.

He did not light a second candle. He took the silver candlestick in his left hand, picked up his master keys from the cedar tray, and walked out of the cabinet onto the spiral stairs.

He descended two flights to the fifth floor, where the newly admitted journeymen had their drafting cells.

The corridor was silent, smelling of damp lime and cold slate.

He stopped outside cell number four. A narrow strip of yellow light showed beneath the door frame.

Corvus did not knock. Under Article IX of the Guild Ordinance, the Chief Warden had the right of unannounced visitation at any hour of the day or night to inspect the worktables of sworn copyists.

He turned his master key in the brass lock, pushed the door open, and stepped across the threshold.

The cell was empty.

The narrow straw pallet in the corner had not been slept in; the wool blankets were folded neatly at the foot, the pillow untouched.

On the small pine drafting table by the dormer window stood an unlit horn lantern, an earthenware inkpot sealed with its wax cork, and a clean slate with an iron stylus resting upon it.

Beside the slate lay her portfolio of coastal charts, closed and weighted with four bronze lions.

Corvus walked to the table. His eyes scanned the room with the practiced efficiency of an inquisitor.

The window latch was secure. The peg on the wall was bare; her winter traveling cloak was missing.

He looked at the floor beneath the bed.

The dust was disturbed. In the corner behind the cedar chest, half-concealed beneath a wisp of loose straw, lay a tiny scrap of blackened rag paper no larger than a thumb-joint.

Corvus knelt, his heavy knees cracking in the silence.

He picked up the scrap. He brought it close to the flame of his silver taper.

It was charred around the edges, smelling of sulfur and wet birch ash. But on the pale surface of the unburned center, drawn in dilute carbon wash with loose, fluid hatching, were two tiny rocky bluffs labeled in a delicate hand:

*The Twin Sisters.*

Corvus's breath stopped in his throat.

He knew that hand. He had watched that hand burn in Harbour Square three days ago.

It was young Orson's heretic survey of the Upper Reach.

The bailiff had sworn that all five sheets had been burned to white ash in the bronze brazier. And here, in the private cell of the safest pair of hands in Oakhaven, lay a severed fragment of the forbidden map.

Corvus rose slowly to his feet, his fingers crushing the charred scrap into black soot against his palm.

He looked at the drafting table.

He picked up the lead stylus that lay beside the slate.

Beneath the slate, etched faintly into the pine grain of the table by the pressure of a hurried hand, was an impression. It was not a coastline. It was an angle: *north-northeast, forty-two degrees*.

And beneath the angle, written in Maren's neat, microscopic Chancery script:

*Pons Chasmensis.*

The Bridge of the Chasm.

Corvus's face turned the color of cold tallow.

"Thomas," he whispered into the empty room, his voice shaking with a terrible, grief-stricken fury that sounded like tearing sailcloth. "You taught your girl well, didn't you? You died in the gaol, and you left your poison in her blood."

Corvus had left the drafting cell door ajar, the draft from the stone stairwell fluttering the edges of Maren's coastal charts.

She stood motionless in the center of the room, her heart pounding against her ribs like a trapped hawk.

Under the stack of harbour depth soundings on her table, the living vellum was burning with feverish intensity. She could smell the warm hide, the sharp, pungent scent of cured sheepskin and fresh carbon ink rising through the cold room.

She walked to the table with silent, deliberate steps.

She lifted the depth soundings.

The living scrap lay beneath, its black lines pulsing with rapid, frantic energy.

At the entrance to the Stone Chasm, where the road approached the ruined bridge, the ink was drawing a series of tiny, urgent crosses--the surveyor's mark for an emergency triangulation station.

Corvus was coming.

The map knew that the Chief Warden had discovered the missing scrap. It was warning her that the hours of safety in the high tower were ended, and that the only sanctuary lay in the high limestone gorge where the masons held the stone.

He turned on his heel, strode out into the corridor, and pulled the cell door shut with a crash that echoed through the tower.

"Guard!" he roared down the stairwell, his voice ringing against the granite steps like an iron bell. "Turn out the watch! Saddle the horses at the North Gate! The apprentice has broken the charter!

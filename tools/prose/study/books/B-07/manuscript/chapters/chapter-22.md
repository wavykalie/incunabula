# Chapter 22: The Sighting at the Gorge

The chasm was a wound in the earth eighty fathoms deep.

At the narrowest point of the gorge, where the limestone walls converged until the sky above was reduced to a ribbon of grey mist, the Black River smashed through a rocky flume of dark basalt. The noise was absolute, a crushing, physical roar that vibrated through the soles of Maren's boots and filled the damp air with clouds of cold spray.

Across this abyss, four hundred years ago, the engineers of High Carrow had thrown a bridge of ashlar stone.

Four massive triangular piers of dressed granite still stood in the riverbed, their upstream cutwaters splitting the boiling foam like the prows of war galleys. But the timber roadbed was gone, and the central stone arch had fallen eighty years before, leaving a yawning gap of sixty feet between the two middle piers.

On the south abutment, where Maren stood, an ancient Roman-style pier of cyclopean masonry rose thirty feet above the rock shelf.

Around the abutment, forty men in wool caps and leather aprons were at work. Great shear-legs of oak timber leaned out over the chasm, rigged with four-fold tackle of thick hemp cable and iron snatch-blocks. Below the shear-legs, resting upon rollers of peeled pine logs, lay eighteen wedge-shaped voussoir blocks of hard white limestone, each block five feet long and two feet thick, weighing more than two tons.

A tall, grey-bearded man in a smith's leather apron walked toward them, wiping stone dust from his eyes with the back of a hairy forearm.

"You brought the surveyor, Reed?" the old man barked over the roar of the water.

"Journeyman Vane," Kellan said, gesturing to Maren. "Master Varek, chief mason of the High Carrow quarry."

Varek looked at Maren's slender frame, her grease-stained canvas apron, and the wooden case she held under her arm. His dark eyes were skeptical and hard as flint.

"A girl from the harbour tower," Varek spat, shaking his head. "What does a town copyist know about three tons of live limestone hanging sixty feet above a boiling river?"

"I know that your southern pier is out of plumb by two inches," Maren replied immediately, her voice cutting through the water's roar with the cold authority of the high scriptorium.

Varek stopped. His jaw dropped slightly.

"What did you say?"

Maren walked to the edge of the granite abutment, unlatched the legs of her plane table tripod, and planted the steel shoes firmly into the crevices between the stones.

"Look at the water-line on the cutwater," she said, unclamping her brass sight-alidade and setting it upon the walnut board. "The river has scoured the foundation of the second pier on the downstream corner. Your timber falsework is leaning three degrees to the northeast. If you drop the keystone with that centering, the thrust will push the third arch into the gorge the moment you strike the wedges."

Varek stared at her for three seconds. He turned to his lead journeyman, an enormous man with shoulders like an ox.

"Fetch the lead bob," Varek commanded.

Two men dropped a forty-pound cone of lead on a chalked cord over the edge of the pier. The cord hung five feet down the face of the stone, then drifted two inches clear of the masonry at the water-line.

The pier was out of plumb. Exactly as she had sighted.

Varek turned back to Maren, his rough, scarred face flushing beneath the stone dust. He pulled his wool cap from his head with a gesture of gruff, reluctant respect.

"My apologies, Journeyman," the master mason said. "My eyes were looking at your apron instead of your work. What do you need from us?"

"I need six men on the iron chain," Maren said, opening her instrument case and taking out her magnetic compass box and sighting pins. "I need an anchor pin set in the north abutment across the gorge, and I need absolute silence along the line while I take the trigonometrical baseline."

"You'll have it," Varek said.

For the next two hours, Maren ruled the bridgehead with the precision of a master shipwright.

She measured the sixty-six-foot baseline along the south shelf three times with the master chain, checking the tension against Kellan's spring scale at exactly twenty pounds. She aligned the plane table against the magnetic meridian, leveled the walnut board with her glass spirit tube, and brought her eye to the pinhole of the brass alidade.

Across the sixty-foot gap, Kellan stood on the ruined north pier with an ashwood sighting staff held vertical against his sternum.

Through the narrow slit of the alidade vane, Maren aligned the horsehair with the black-and-white bands of the staff.

The calculations flowed from her memory with effortless certainty: tangent of forty-three degrees, angle of deflection, allowance for the curvature of the arch at one in four.

She took up her lead stylus and drew the geometric diagram directly upon the clean sheet of drawing paper on the plane table.

The center-line of the bridge was not straight. It carried a subtle, magnificent camber of four inches toward the crown to counteract the downstream pressure of the winter gales that tore through the gorge.

She laid her living vellum scrap beside her calculations.

The black ink on the sheepskin agreed with her pencil down to the twelfth part of an inch.

She called out the adjustments to Varek's carpenters:

"Slack the north guy-rope by three turns! Drive the oak wedges beneath the timber falsework on the downstream cutwater until the bubble centers! Bring the third voussoir forward on the rollers!"

The carpenters leaped to the ropes. The iron winches groaned as the hemp cables tightened across the chasm. The heavy timber scaffold shifted, creaked under the immense weight of the limestone blocks, and settled into the true spring-line of the ancient arch.

Varek watched the level bubble in Maren's hand come to rest squarely between the two black marks.

He looked down at Maren, his rough eyes shining with tears of relief.

"You've given us the road back, lass," the old mason whispered.

"The road was always here, Master Varek," Maren said, wiping the cold river spray from her brass alidade with her sleeve. "We are only putting the stone where the earth remembers it."

The roar of the Black River below the bridgehead was like the grinding of ten thousand millstones.

Maren knelt upon the granite coping of the south abutment, her bone folder pressed against the damp drawing paper on her plane table.

The engineering of the ancient Roman span was revealed in every dressed block of the foundation. The builders had not used mortar in the lower three courses. They had cut the granite blocks with interlocking tenons and mortises, each block weighing five tons, fitted together so closely that a knife blade could not be forced between the joints.

"Look at the lead keyways," Master Varek said, kneeling beside her and pointing his iron chisel at a T-shaped channel cut into the granite surface. "They poured molten lead into these channels after the stones were set. The lead cushioned the granite against the frost heave. When the ice expanded in the winter, the lead squeezed like cheese without fracturing the stone."

Maren touched the grey metal that still filled the ancient groove.

"Who taught them this geometry?" she asked, her voice filled with professional wonder.

"The First Guild," Varek answered, his weathered hand resting upon the stone. "Four hundred years ago, before the ocean broke into the bay. In those days, a cartographer was also a master mason. He knew how to cut a stone arch as well as he knew how to grind Aleppo galls. He didn't sit in an attic copying coastlines that never moved. He walked the mountains with his compass, his plumb line, and his iron chisel, building the roads he drew upon his charts."

Maren unrolled her plane table paper, weighting the corners with four iron rivets from Varek's forge.

She took out her magnetic needle box, leveled the walnut head on the ashwood tripod, and brought the sight-alidade to her eye.

The trigonometry of the chasm crossing was a magnificent challenge of spatial geometry.

The south abutment stood at an elevation of four hundred and twenty feet above sea level; the north abutment stood at four hundred and twenty-eight feet. The span between the two piers was sixty-six feet and four inches. To ensure that the roadway remained level across the crossing, the builders had designed the southern arch with a spring-line two feet higher than the north, compensating for the natural dip of the dolomite strata.

"Look through the vanes, Master Varek," she invited, stepping back from the table.

The old master mason leaned forward, his heavy, scarred face squinting through the ivory pinhole of the alidade.

He saw the vertical horsehair split the black-and-white sighting staff held by Kellan on the far side of the abyss.

"What does it tell you, lass?" Varek asked, his voice hushed with awe.

"It tells me that your wooden centering is leaning four inches toward the river," she answered, pointing her lead stylus at the timber struts below the span. "If you drop the four-ton keystone with that lean, the south voussoirs will pinch the timber before the mortar sets, and the arch will break its back the moment you remove the shores."

Varek straightened up, his face pale beneath the stone dust.

"God above," he muttered, crossing himself with a grey, calloused hand. "We would have lost the span."

He turned to his lead journeymen, his voice roaring over the thunder of the water:

"Heave on the south guy-cables! Bring the timber falsework true to the surveyor's mark! We don't lay an ounce of lime until Journeyman Vane gives the word!"

Maren unrolled the trigonometric table of chords she had copied from the Guild's private library in the third year of her apprenticeship.

The table had been compiled in 1480 by Brother Lucas of the cathedral chapter, using an astrolabe and a twenty-foot quadrant to calculate the angles of the sun at the equinoxes. Lucas had recorded the natural sines and tangents to six sexagesimal places in neat columns of red and black ink.

She set her ivory dividers to the tangent of forty-three degrees.

The transverse measure was three inches, four lines, and one quarter.

"Look at the south abutment footing, Kellan," she said, pointing with her ashwood sighting rod across the foaming water. "The ancient masons didn't lay their foundation on the limestone scree. They cut two fathoms into the living basalt until they reached the horizontal bedding plane. They anchored the first course with iron dowels set in molten sulfur."

Kellan knelt on the edge of the granite coping, looking down through the spray.

"The sulfur dowels are still there," he shouted over the roar of the river. "My grandfather told me that when they drained the mill race thirty years ago, he saw the yellow sulfur rings around the iron pins, untouched by sixty winters of mountain flood. The First Guild knew how to marry iron to stone."

"They knew how to marry geometry to the earth," Maren corrected, adjusting the leveling screws of her plane table until the amber bubble rested motionless in the center of the spirit tube.

She looked up at the sky.

The lemon-yellow light of dawn had vanished behind a dense canopy of leaden grey clouds. High above the gorge, the pine trees on the mountain crest were beginning to toss their branches in the rising blizzard.

# Chapter 11: The Salt Cellars

The descent into the Cold Repository began behind an iron-strapped oak portal in the lower quadrangle.

Archivist Sibyl Thorne led the way down forty-eight spiral steps of dressed basalt. The air grew damp, tasting of old brine, tallow grease, and the dry, alkaline dust of slaked lime. Water seeped through the mortar joints of the foundation, trickling down the curved stones in black ribbons to collect in shallow gutters cut along the flagstones.

Sibyl carried a square copper lantern with horn panes. In the yellow light, her ring of fifty brass keys clicked against her hip like the teeth of a mechanical clock.

"Mind the tenth step, Apprentice," Sibyl whispered, her voice rustling like dried reeds in a pond. "The sea tide backs up through the lower drain at full moon. The slime will throw you on your skull if your boots lack nails."

Maren stepped carefully, her hand skimming the greasy granite wall. Her high surveyor's boots were heavy, but against the calf of her left leg, wrapped inside her wool sock, she felt the stiff, comforting curve of the two rolled maps: Orson's scorched scrap and her own living sheepskin.

At the foot of the stairs lay a vaulted gallery fifty paces long, supported by six squat octagonal pillars of green sandstone.

This was the Cold Repository, where the Guild preserved its pre-charter archives and master tidal registers. The ceiling hung low, crusted with white stalactites of salt efflorescence that dripped cold drops onto Maren's wool shoulders. Along both walls stood ranks of heavy iron-bound cedar chests, each sealed with a padlock of cast bronze and stamped with the personal mark of an ancient Chancellor.

Sibyl stopped before chest number forty-two, inscribed with the date *Anno Domini 1540*.

"Master Corvus wants the tidal master for the South Channel," the Archivist said, selecting an angular brass key from her ring. "The Harbour Wardens claim the silting rate on the inner bar has doubled since the autumn gales. They want to compare the soundings of fifty years ago with last week's register."

She inserted the key into the heavy padlock. The iron tumblers threw a sharp double click that echoed off the wet granite.

Maren knelt on the damp flags and helped the old woman lift the massive cedar lid. Inside lay forty cylinders of heavy calfskin vellum, each wrapped in waxed linen and tied with green silk ribbons. The scent of horn oil and dried lavender rose from the cedar shavings packing the seams.

"Take out the third roll," Sibyl directed, holding the copper lantern low.

Maren reached into the chest. Her fingers closed over the cylinder labeled *Tidal Inquest: The Inner Bar and Shoals*. The parchment was thick, stiff with forty years of cold storage, but the grain remained supple under the wax wrap.

She laid the roll upon the sorting bench beside the pillar.

As she stepped back, her eye caught the chest immediately to the left. It was older than the others, its oak boards blackened by cellar damp, its iron corner bands pitted with orange rust. Unlike the neat bronze plates on the tidal chests, this chest bore an inscription cut directly into the wood with a gouge: *Charta Antiqua et Fines Montium: Anno 1440*.

The Ancient Charter and the Mountain Boundaries.

Maren's breath caught in her throat. She looked at Sibyl.

The old archivist was standing by the salt gutter, her back turned, holding a horn hydrometer to a pool of seepage water to measure the cellar damp. Her black silk eye-patch was turned toward the wall, but her good blue eye seemed fixed on the wet stones at her feet.

"You have your father's neck, Maren," Sibyl said without turning around. "Thomas used to crane his chin at that very chest when he was a boy in the second class."

Maren froze, her fingers resting on the linen ribbon of the tidal roll.

"My father worked in this vault?"

"For two winters," Sibyl murmured, shaking the water from her glass tube. "Before he decided the sky was bigger than the cellar. He was thirty years old when he asked Chancellor Vane why the 1440 survey carried the names of seven towns that were never copied into the Great Charter."

"What did the Chancellor tell him?"

Sibyl turned slowly, the horn lantern throwing long, cage-like shadows across her wrinkled cheeks.

"He told him that when a kingdom changes its mind about where its borders lie, an honest cartographer changes his ink. Your father told him that an honest cartographer records the earth as God made it, and the Chancellor called the bailiffs."

She walked to the sorting bench, her keys chiming against her apron. She looked at Maren, her single eye glittering in the yellow lantern light.

"The lock on that chest is broken, Maren. Has been broken for thirty years. The spring rusted through in the winter of the great frost."

She laid the tidal roll in Maren's hands, then pointed toward the spiral stairs.

"Take the 1540 roll up to Master Corvus's cabinet. He is waiting by the window with his dividers. When you have delivered it, come back down with two dry tallow candles and the salt mop. I have inventory work in the north vault that will take me until the noon chimes."

She turned and shuffled down the gallery toward the dark archway of the north cellars, the click of her keys fading into the cold, dripping silence.

Maren stood beside the ancient chest.

Sibyl led her deeper into the north vault, where the ceiling dropped so low that Maren had to duck her head beneath the salt-crusted granite arches.

The smell of cellar salt was overwhelming here, catching in the back of the throat like powdered alum. Along the wall stood three iron-bound chests sealed with lead wire, their identification tags written in archaic Chancery Gothic that had faded into grey shadows.

"The secret archive," Sibyl whispered, her brass keys clicking softly against her aprons. "Here lie the surveys that were too dangerous even for the fire. The charts of the drowned western coast, the ancient road registers, and the original grant of Chancellor Althaus."

She stopped before a chest marked with an iron cross.

"Your father spent forty nights in this vault, Maren," the old woman said, turning her single good eye upon her. "He sat on that very salt tub with a horn lantern between his knees, tracing the coordinates of High Carrow onto scrap paper. When Corvus found out, he didn't call the Town Guard immediately. He came down these stairs alone. He pleaded with Thomas. He fell on his knees on these very flagstones and begged him to burn the sketches."

"Why would Corvus beg?" Maren asked, stunned by the revelation.

"Because Corvus loved your father," Sibyl answered softly. "They were boys together in the scriptorium. They had shared a drafting bench for seven years. Corvus knew that if the Council saw Thomas's sketches, they would demand his blood. When Thomas refused to burn his work, Corvus had to choose between his friend and his Guild. He chose the Guild. And he has spent thirty years trying to burn away the memory of that choice."

Her fingers touched the cold, rusty iron hasp. She lifted the latch by a fraction of an inch; the broken spring gave way without a sound.

She did not open it yet. She pulled the tidal roll against her ribs, turned toward the basalt stairs, and hurried upward toward the light.

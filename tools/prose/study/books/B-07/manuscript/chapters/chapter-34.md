# Chapter 34: The Gates of High Carrow

The following morning dawned under a sky of brilliant, cloudless cobalt.

The blizzard had passed completely, leaving the mountain world washed in dazzling, crystalline light. The temperature had risen five degrees as a warm southwest current began to breathe over the high passes, setting the snow melting from the eaves of High Carrow in a musical chorus of dripping water.

At the South Gate of the city, fifty pack mules and twelve heavy mountain wains were drawn up in double rank along the paved highway.

The wagons were loaded with the harvest of the mountain: sacks of hard winter rye, barrels of salted mutton, crates of fine mountain wool dyed in rich madder and woad, and forty pigs of refined silver ore stamped with the town seal.

Around the wagons stood the citizens of High Carrow, their wool caps adorned with sprigs of mountain pine, shouting cheerful farewells to the teamsters.

Maren walked along the line of wagons, her leather satchel slung across her shoulder, accompanied by Kellan Reed and Master Corvus.

Corvus was dressed in his formal scarlet Chief Warden's robe, but he had removed the beaver-lined collar of monopoly, replacing it with a simple border of mountain pine green. At his belt hung his master keys, alongside the newly engraved seal of the Unified Realm.

"How long to the coast with loaded wains?" Corvus asked Kellan, watching a teamster tighten the hemp ropes around a crate of silver pigs.

"Two days of steady hauling," Kellan answered, checking the iron lynch-pins of the lead wagon's axle. "The bridge is solid as living rock. The gradient through the gorge is one in twelve; four sturdy mountain oxen can walk the wain from the quarry to the river weir between sunrise and dusk."

"And the harbour tolls?" Kellan added, glancing sidelong at Corvus with his characteristic mountain smirk.

Corvus let out a short, dry chuckle that surprised even himself.

"Article XII has been repealed, Waymarker Reed. By decree of the Joint Council, the mountain wains will enter the King's Quay under the open charter. A duty of one half-penny on the hundredweight of silver to maintain the bridge piers, and not a single brass farthing on food or wool."

"A fair toll," Kellan said, offering his calloused hand. "We can live with an honest half-penny."

Corvus took the younger man's hand, his grip firm and sincere.

"The mountain has kept the road for forty years, Kellan. It is the harbour's turn to keep the gates open."

Elder Martha walked forward from the gatehouse, accompanied by four town councilors carrying an iron-bound chest of mountain cedar.

She stopped before Maren.

"Journeyman Vane," the elder woman said, her voice rich with solemn affection. "The town of High Carrow has neither gold medals nor royal titles to bestow upon an apprentice of Oakhaven. But we have the tools that built our city."

She opened the cedar chest.

Inside lay an ancient surveying instrument: an eight-inch circumferentor of solid brass, its compass box lined with silver, its sighting sights engraved with the crest of Johannes Althaus. It was the instrument the Founder had used to sight the first baseline of the mountain road in 1438.

"This belonged to your great-uncle, Master Althaus," Martha said, turning to Corvus. "We have kept it polished in our town treasury for four generations. We return it now to the hand that completed his work."

She lifted the brass instrument and placed it in Maren's hands.

The metal was heavy, perfectly balanced, cold and smooth as polished river jade.

Maren looked at the silver compass rose, then up at the white-haired elder.

"I will carry it to the sea, Elder Martha," Maren said, her throat tight with emotion. "And I will use it to draw every road that connects this mountain to the world."

The great bronze bell of the town hall began to chime, its deep notes rolling across the snowy amphitheater like thunder.

The caravan moved past the outer artillery towers of High Carrow, the iron wheels of the ore wagons singing against the limestone flags.

Along the roadside, twenty mountain children were tossing snowballs, their laughter echoing off the granite crags like church bells.

Kellan looked down at Maren from the wagon seat, his eyes full of warmth.

"Will you come back to the mountain, Maren?" he asked.

"I will come back every spring, Kellan," she answered, holding the brass circumferentor in her lap. "We have forty leagues of the Northern Reach to survey before the next winter. We have to map the silver mines, the high passes, and the road to the western ocean."

"A lifetime of work," Kellan said with a wide mountain grin.

"An honest lifetime," she replied, looking ahead toward the open gates of the sunny valley.

The mountain air tasted of clean melting snow, wild thyme, and cedar sawdust.

As the wains filed through the fortified stone gateway, Maren looked back at the north wall of the city.

The masonry was a lesson in geometric fortification. The round towers were set at intervals of sixty paces, ensuring that every foot of the curtain wall could be swept by crossbow fire from the arrow-slits. The stone was hard grey granite, cut in heavy rustic ashlar with drafted margins that caught the morning light like cut gems.

"My father worked on the third tower when he was nineteen," Kellan said, pointing his ashwood staff toward the western bastion. "He spent an entire summer dressing those granite quoins with an iron chisel. When he finished, the master mason gave him an silver shilling and a surveyor's compass."

Maren smiled, holding the Founder's circumferentor in her hands.

"He would be proud of the road today, Kellan."

"He's watching us from the high rocks, lass," Kellan said softly, his voice full of quiet conviction. "The mountain doesn't forget a man who gave his sweat to its stones."

The lead teamster cracked his long leather whip over the backs of his oxen.

With a creak of seasoned oak timber and the heavy rattle of iron-shod wheels on limestone flags, the first trade caravan in forty years moved out through the South Gate of High Carrow, descending the sunlit highway toward the Bridge of the Chasm and the distant, sparkling waters of the bay.

# Chapter 6: The Gall Oak Ink

At five in the morning, before the scriptorium fire was lit, the laboratory in the tower basement was colder than an icehouse.

The room was vaulted in rough granite, its walls lined with shelves of stone jars, earthenware jugs, and glass retorts coated with chemical dust. Great sacks of dried Aleppo galls from the Levant leaned against the mortar basin, smelling of tannins, mould, and dry forest leaves. On the central slate bench sat four copper cauldrons used for boiling vitriol and gum arabic.

Maren stood at the small testing table by the air shaft, a wool shawl pinned tight around her neck.

On the slate table before her sat six small ceramic saucers, a bottle of distilled rainwater, a vial of stale white wine, an iron pestle, and her brass magnifying loupe. Beside the saucers lay the scrap of living vellum, weighted down with two lead cubes.

She had to know what the ink was.

If the mark had been made by an intruder in her garret, it would be composed of the standard materials available in the tower: iron vitriol, gall oak tannins, and gum arabic. If it were standard ink, it would react to the chemical reagents.

She took up an iron spatula and chipped a flake of dry gall nut from the mortar. She ground it into a fine yellow-brown powder with the pestle, transferred the dust into the first saucer, and added three drops of warm rainwater. The water turned a pale amber, releasing the dry, bitter scent of oak bark.

In the second saucer, she dissolved three crystals of green copperas--ferrous sulfate--imported from the copper mines of the western hills. The crystals dissolved into a pale, sea-green liquor.

With a glass rod, she transferred a single droplet of the copperas solution into the oak gall extract.

Instantly, the two pale liquids struck together. A cloud of velvety, purplish-black precipitate blossomed in the saucer, swirling like smoke in water before settling into deep, opaque ink. It was the standard reaction: the iron salt binding to the gallic acid to form insoluble iron tannate.

Maren cleaned the glass rod on a linen rag.

Now came the test on the vellum itself.

She leaned over the parchment scrap with the magnifying loupe pressed to her right eye. She dipped the clean tip of an ivory stylus into a droplet of distilled water. With a hand as steady as a stone mason's plumb line, she brought the stylus down until the tiny sphere of water touched the very tail of the mysterious black line where it met the High Weir.

She held the drop there for ten seconds.

If the ink were ordinary iron gall, the water would soften the gum arabic binder within twenty seconds, causing a faint brown halo of soluble tannin to leach outward into the surrounding sheepskin fibres.

Nothing happened.

The droplet of water sat upon the black line like a bead of mercury on an oiled steel plate. The ink did not soften. It did not bleed. The boundary between the black trace and the cream vellum remained as sharp as a razor cut.

Maren blotted the water drop away with a scrap of clean linen.

Next, she tested with stale white wine. The acetic acid in the wine was an effective solvent for carbon blacks bound with animal glue or egg white. She applied the drop with the stylus, watching through the loupe.

Again, the line remained completely inert.

She took up a steel scraping needle, the finest tool in her case, its point honed to a microscopic wedge. She gently probed the surface of the black line at forty-fold raking light.

What she saw through the glass made her breath catch in her throat.

The black pigment was not resting on top of the parchment.

When an apprentice drew a line with a quill, the ink sat upon the upper surface of the skin, held in place by the dried glaze of gum arabic and cuttlefish bone sizing. If you scraped it with a needle, the ink flaked away, revealing the white scraped sheepskin beneath.

This ink was inside the skin.

The microscopic carbon particles were embedded directly within the dermal bundles of the collagen fibres, three layers below the surface. It was as though the ink had not been applied from above by a pen, but had seeped upward from the interior flesh of the hide itself, aligning along the natural grain of the skin like iron filings around a lodestone.

"It wasn't drawn," she whispered aloud.

The word echoed softly off the granite vaulting.

The hide had been cured three months ago in the salt pits of the South Ward. The lamb had grazed on the pastures of the Upper Valley before it was slaughtered for the winter market. The skin had lived in that valley. It had walked upon the hills, breathed the salt fog, and drunk from the mountain streams that tumbled down from the Stone Chasm.

The parchment was not recording an imaginary map.

The parchment was remembering the ground it had walked upon.

She heard a footstep on the stone stairs outside the laboratory door.

Maren's movements were instant and silent. In three seconds, the cedar case was closed, the vellum hidden within its velvet lining, and the testing saucers dumped into the waste bucket of slaked lime. She took up the iron pestle and began grinding Aleppo galls with rhythmic, vigorous strokes.

The heavy oak door swung open. Master Corvus stood in the entrance, his dark woolen cloak dusted with morning frost, his brass dividers tucked into his belt.

"You are early at the bench, Apprentice Vane," Corvus said, his sharp eyes taking in the mortar, the pestle, and the pile of ground gall dust.

"The second-year stock of iron gall was running low, Master," Maren said, keeping her head bowed over the mortar. "I wanted to grind forty ounces before the drafting bell."

Corvus walked to the slate table. He picked up a pinch of the yellow gall dust between his scarred thumb and forefinger, sniffed it, and nodded once.

"A good apprentice anticipates the need," he said. He looked down at her worktable, his gaze lingering for a second upon the clean drop of wine she had not yet wiped from the corner of the slate. "Wash the slate before you go up to the hall. The Council meets at ten. You will carry the master survey chain to the quay."

"Yes, Master," she said.

Corvus turned and climbed back up the granite steps, his heavy boots thudding against the stone.

Maren wiped the drop of wine away with the palm of her hand. Her skin was cold, but beneath her ribs, her heart was beating like an iron hammer on an anvil.

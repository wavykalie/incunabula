# Chapter 24: The Guild Inquest

In Oakhaven Harbour, the great alarm bell of Saint Jude had been tolling since four in the morning.

The iron clapper struck the bronze lip with a heavy, frantic cadence that rolled across the dark roofs of the town, waking the merchant houses, the dock taverns, and the barracks of the Town Watch. In the cobblestone square outside the Guild Tower, two hundred citizens had gathered in the freezing sleet, their lanterns throwing jagged yellow beams across the wet mud.

Inside the Great Hall of the Cartographers, the Council of Wardens was in an uproar.

Thirty senior members sat in their tiered stalls, their scarlet and velvet gowns thrown hurriedly over their nightshirts, their faces pale with rage and confusion.

At the center of the dais stood Master Corvus Althaus.

He had not slept. He wore his heavy buff-leather riding coat, his steel gorget buckled around his neck, his long cavalry boots spattered with mud from the stables. On the mahogany table before him lay three objects: Maren Vane's empty portfolio of coastal charts, the charred scrap of Orson's heretic survey he had found in her cell, and the Great Seal of the Guild Chancellor, its brass face turned upward like an unblinking eye.

Warden Malor was on his feet, his wig crooked, his flabby hands shaking as he gripped his ivory stylus.

"Treason!" Malor shouted, his voice cracking with hysteria. "Treason within the very heart of the scriptorium! The girl was Thomas Vane's daughter! I warned you, Althaus! I told this Council three days ago that she carried the heretic blood!"

"Hold your tongue, Malor," Corvus said, his voice dropping into a register of glacial, lethal authority that cut through the shouting like a razor through sheepskin.

Malor choked on his words and sank back into his velvet stall.

Corvus stepped to the front of the dais, his dark eyes sweeping over the assembled wardens.

"Journeyman Maren Vane fled the tower four hours before midnight," Corvus spoke, every syllable falling like a lead sinker into deep water. "She did not go alone. She was met at the coal jetty by Waymarker Reed of the mountain parishes. They took a timber dinghy through the water-gate, carrying plane tables, sighting alidades, and three standard survey chains stolen from the municipal storehouse."

A murmur of horror and disbelief ran through the merchants on the left stalls.

"The chasm," Warden Brandt of the Customs whispered, clutching his gold chain of office with sweating fingers. "They are heading for the Stone Chasm."

"They are already in the chasm," Corvus replied coldly. "My scouts report that eighty miners from High Carrow have spent three months rebuilding the ancient bridge over the Black River. If they close the arch, the mountain wagons will bypass our water-gate before the winter ice sets in. In six weeks, half the silver and wool trade of this realm will be flowing down an unchartered highway, beyond the reach of our tariffs, our courts, and our King's peace."

A merchant duke in a sable-lined cloak rose from the front bench.

"Then call out the garrison!" the duke cried, slamming his fist onto the rail. "Send two companies of foot archers! Tear down the bridge! Hang every mason from the rocks!"

"A foot company would take four days to drag their artillery through the mountain mud," Corvus said, his voice flat and practical. "The blizzard has already begun in the upper passes. In twenty-four hours, the snow will close the valley to infantry."

He turned to the Captain of the Town Watch, who stood by the portal with twenty mounted sergeants in steel cuirasses.

"Captain," Corvus commanded. "We ride with twenty horse archers. We carry light carbines, ten kegs of black powder, and iron branding torches. We take the river track at the gallop. We do not negotiate with the miners. We do not take prisoners at the bridgehead."

He picked up the heavy brass Chancellor's seal from the table and dropped it into the leather pouch at his belt.

"We will burn the wooden falsework beneath their arch. We will drop their limestone voussoirs into the river. And when we have destroyed the stone, we will take Maren Vane and her stolen vellum, and we will burn them together in Harbour Square so that no man in this province will ever dare draw an unchartered line again."

He turned and strode down the central aisle of the hall, his cavalry saber clinking against his spurs.

Behind him, the twenty horse sergeants followed in double file, their iron breastplates gleaming in the candlelight, their boots striking the stone flagstones with the steady, unyielding rhythm of an army marching to war.

In the Great Hall of Oakhaven, the merchant dukes were surrounding the Council table, their voices rising in panic and fury.

"If the mountain wagons reach the bay," the Duke of the South Spit shouted, his jeweled fingers clutching the arm of his chair, "my grain rents will fall by half! The mountain rye will undersell our coastal tenant crops in every market in the province!"

"And our customs duties!" Warden Brandt cried, his flabby cheeks quivering with terror. "We took four thousand gold florins on the water-gate tolls last season! If the road opens, the merchants will drive their pack trains directly across the mountain passes to the northern provinces without paying a single farthing to the town treasury!"

Corvus stood before them, his face dark and impassive as a granite cliff.

He looked at the trembling dukes with cold, unutterable contempt.

He had spent thirty years defending these men. He had burned the work of his closest friend, he had condemned young apprentices to the common ward, and he had lived with the ghost of his drowned brother, all to preserve the velvet robes and the gold chains of merchants who panicked at the mention of a stone road.

"You speak of silver, gentlemen," Corvus said, his voice cutting through the hall like a winter frost. "I speak of the King's peace. If the road is an error, I will burn it with powder and iron. But if the road is the work of our own founders, I will not burn my own heritage to save your grain rents."

He turned on his heel and walked out to his horse without waiting for their answer.

As Corvus passed beneath the archway into the courtyard, he looked up at the high tower spire.

The iron caravel was pointing due north into the freezing gale, its copper sails shivering in the dark.

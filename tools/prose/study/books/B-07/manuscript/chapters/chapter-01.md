# Chapter 1: The Scraped Sheepskin

The calfskin smelled of dried river silt, stale lime, and the greasy fat of young mutton.

Maren pinned the hide to the sloping pine worktable with four iron weights shaped like couchant lions. The grain was stubborn. A grey patch of rough epidermal fleece remained along the flank where the salt pit had failed to bite. She took up her scraping blade--a crescent of curved Swedish steel with a boxwood haft smoothed by six years of apprentice sweat--and tested the edge against the ball of her thumb. It shaved the fine hair cleanly without catching.

She began at the neck. The iron blade rasped against the dried collagen with a sound like dry grass dragged over paving stones.

"Work the flank with pumice before the blade," Master Corvus had told her on her first winter morning in the high tower, thirty leagues of salt spray lashing the leaded panes behind his shoulder. "If you hurry the scraper, you cut the grain. If you cut the grain, the iron vitriol pools in the flesh side like sheep blood in a ditch. A pooled line is an uncertain sound, and an uncertain sound loses ships."

She did not hurry. Her strokes were short, rhythmic, and angled at twenty degrees across the stretch lines. A pale snow of parchment dust drifted over her wool sleeves and settled in the creases of her knuckles. She blew it away in steady breaths, counting the scrapes in sets of fifty. The work was slow, cold, and mindless. That was why she clung to it. In the scriptorium, six inches of scraped vellum stood between a copyist and the freezing cobbles of Harbour Square.

Her father had never counted his strokes. Thomas Vane had worked by candlelight in damp roadside taverns, his knives nicked from carving ashwood survey pegs, his paper foxed with beer stains and woodsmoke. When the Guild Wardens came to Harbour Square twelve years ago, they had not bothered to read his hinterland traverses. They had dumped three wicker hampers of his sketchbooks into the bronze brazier and stirred the embers with an iron poker until nothing remained except white flakes of ash rising toward the gulls.

Maren scraped harder. The boxwood haft bit into her palm callus.

She was twenty-four. For six years she had drawn only what Master Corvus placed before her eyes: the South Headland, the Outer Shoals, the three-fathom channel between the red buoy and the basalt pillar, and the stone jetties of Oakhaven Harbour. She had traced the South Headland three thousand times. The headland was grey granite capped with gorse. It did not move. The tides washed its base twice a day, depositing kelp and barnacles, but the high-water contour remained exactly where the Guild Founders had fixed it with their bronze sighting rules two hundred years before.

She laid down the crescent blade and reached for the second-grade pumice. The stone was volcanic, porous, and grey as sea foam, imported from the western islands in kegs of dry sawdust. She rubbed the chalky block across the scraped hide in tight, overlapping circles. The friction warmed the skin under her fingers. The rough greasy spots flattened, yielding to a cool, velvet smoothness that felt like the inner petal of a water lily.

Next came the cuttlefish bone. She rubbed the dry white calcified shield along the margin where the title cartouche would sit. The bone sealed the pores, closing the microscopic channels between the dried flesh fibres so that no stray hairline of ink could bleed sideways into the grain.

The scriptorium was silent except for the cry of terns wheeling past the stone gargoyles outside and the steady drip of the water clock by the north window. Across the long hall, seven other apprentices sat at their sloped desks under the high oak rafters, their heads bowed, their bone folders smoothing vellum, their quills dipping into earthenware inkstands. None of them spoke. Speech in the high scriptorium was forbidden during morning drafting hours under Article IV of the Guild Ordinance.

Maren wiped the table surface with a square of dry lambswool. Not a single grain of grit remained.

She reached for her iron drafting square and her brass dividers. The dividers were seven inches long, forged by master clockmakers in the capital, their tapered legs terminating in needles of tempered steel. She set the needle points against the ivory standard rule embedded in the edge of her table. Three inches and four tenths: the exact measure from the harbour lighthouse to the western breakwater.

She opened the small drawer beneath her bench. Inside sat her tools, arranged in parallel rows according to their function: three goose-feather quills cut for fine hairline hatching, two reed pens for bold border titles, a vial of refined ox-gall to break surface tension in the ink, an agate burnisher, and a small block of gum arabic wrapped in oiled silk.

Beside the tools lay her private practice sheet--a scrap of trimmed calfskin no larger than her palm, cut from the waste edge of last week's Admiralty portfolio.

Maren did not touch the scrap. She did not even look at it directly. But beneath her wool bodice, her heart gave a single, tight contraction against her ribs.

Last night, when she had laid the practice scrap upon the cedar chest beside her cot in the attic garret, it had contained only her practice attempts at compass roses and three standard soundings of the harbour channel. When she woke at dawn to the chime of the harbour bells, a new mark had appeared between the mouth of the Oakhaven River and the foot of the limestone cliffs. It was a faint, hair-thin arc of carbon black, no thicker than an eyelash, curving inland toward the north.

She had not drawn it. Her inkpot had been stoppered with its cork. Her quills had stood clean and dry in their pewter holder.

Between the scraping and the pumicing lay the washing. Maren dipped a linen sponge into an earthenware crock of cold rainwater mixed with slaked lime. The lime water neutralized the residual tallow in the pores of the sheepskin, turning the translucent fat into a chalky curd that could be lifted with a horsehair brush. She wiped the skin from the spine to the belly in long, even strokes, watching the water bead on the surface before sinking into the grain.

Her father had taught her the three virtues of parchment when she was eight years old, sitting on a three-legged stool in his portable drafting tent. *A good skin has tooth, Maren,* he had told her, holding a scrap of sheepskin up to the candle flame. *It must have enough tooth to hold the carbon, enough grease to resist the damp, and enough memory to remember the beast that wore it. If you scrape it until it is slick as window glass, the ink will sit on the skin like grease on an iron kettle, and the first sea-fog will wash your coastlines into the bilge.*

She had never forgotten his voice. In the high scriptorium, Master Corvus forbade all talk of the skin's memory. To Corvus, parchment was an inert mathematical surface, a blank void of bleached protein upon which the authority of the Council could be stamped without argument. If the skin remembered the hillside where the lamb had grazed, Corvus considered that memory a defect to be planed away with pumice.

Maren drew in a slow breath of cold sea air through the window embrasure. She picked up her penknife, a sliver of honed razor steel with an ebony scale, and pared a quarter-millimeter from the slit of her goose quill.

She laid the clean parchment squarely against the drafting board, weighted its corners with the bronze lions, and uncorked the black earthenware pot of iron gall ink.

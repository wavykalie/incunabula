# Chapter 28: The Burnt Waymarker

The silence that fell upon the bridgehead was broken only by the drip of melting snow from the pine branches.

Master Corvus sat on his black stallion, his hands resting limp upon the saddle pommel, staring at the newly closed limestone arch as though a mountain had fallen from the sky.

His horse archers had dismounted. They stood in the slush beside their mounts, their wheel-lock carbines held loosely at their sides, their eyes wide as they looked at the massive granite span that bridged the eighty-fathom chasm.

Kellan Reed walked to the southern abutment of the bridge, carrying an iron cold chisel and a stiff wire brush.

Beside the stone parapet stood a rectangular pillar of grey granite, six feet high and two feet square, half-buried in a mound of frozen shale and dead gorse bushes.

The face of the pillar was scarred with deep, violent gouges where cold chisels had hacked away the original carving decades before. Black soot from old brush fires crusted the stone, turning the granite into a featureless black tooth.

Kellan knelt in the snow.

He set his chisel against the soot crust, tapped the haft with his mason's hammer, and began chipping away the fire scale.

Flake by flake, the blackened crust fell away onto the snow.

Maren walked to his side, bringing her tin flask of dilute vinegar and a clean linen rag. She poured the liquid over the freshly exposed stone, scrubbing the surface with the rag until the grey crystalline texture of the granite emerged.

Beneath the hacked chisel marks, preserved by the depth of the original relief, the carving reappeared.

It was an inscription in formal Roman capitals, three inches high, flanked by two carved pilasters:

*ITER CARROVIENSE: MUNICIPIUM OAKHAVEN*

*ANNO DOMINI 1438*

*IOHANNES ALTHAUS, MAGISTER CARTOGRAPHUS*

The High Road of Carrow: The Liberty of Oakhaven.

Anno Domini 1438.

Johannes Althaus, Master Cartographer.

Maren looked up at Corvus.

The Chief Warden had dismounted from his horse. He walked forward like an old man who had lost his sight, his heavy cavalry boots dragging in the slush, his scarlet cloak trailing in the mud.

He stopped before the granite pillar.

His large, scarred hand reached out, his trembling fingers tracing the carved letters of his great-uncle's name.

"Johannes," Corvus whispered, his voice cracking with thirty years of buried grief. "Johannes laid this stone."

"He laid it with his own hands, Master," Kellan said, rising to his feet and wiping the stone dust from his chisel. "My great-grandfather was his master mason. They spent three summers in this gorge, cutting the piers and paving the approach. Johannes Althaus didn't build this bridge to bypass the harbour tolls. He built it so the mountain children would have bread when the sea fisheries failed."

Maren drew a sheet of clean drawing paper from Sibyl's travelling case.

She laid the paper against the damp granite inscription, took a stick of soft birch charcoal from her pocket, and rubbed the charcoal across the surface in broad, rhythmic strokes.

The white letters emerged from the black field: *IOHANNES ALTHAUS, MAGISTER CARTOGRAPHUS*.

She lifted the paper.

She brought it to Corvus and placed it in his cold, gloved hands.

Beside the charcoal rubbing, she laid the 1440 survey roll she had retrieved from chest sixteen in the salt cellars.

The two inscriptions were identical. The same Chancery Latin. The same Roman capitals. The same founding seal: the compass, the chisel, and the sighting staff.

"The Guild did not begin in Harbour Square, Master Corvus," Maren said gently, her voice steady in the cold mountain air. "It began here. On this stone. With an honest chain and a mason's hammer. The founders did not burn maps. They drew the world so men could find their way home."

Corvus looked at the charcoal rubbing in his hands.

A tear ran down the deep furrows of his smallpox-scarred cheek, freezing on his iron-grey mustache before falling onto the paper.

He looked at the eighteen horsemen behind him, then at the eighty mountain masons who stood on the bridge with their tools in their hands.

"I spent forty years," Corvus whispered, his shoulders shaking beneath his steel breastplate. "Forty years burning the truth because I thought... I thought if a line moved, Lucian would drown again."

"Lucian didn't drown because the line moved, Master," Maren said, touching his leather sleeve with her hand. "He drowned because the chart was old and the reef was real. When you freeze a map in ink, you don't save the ship. You only blind the captain."

Corvus bowed his head.

He turned toward his twenty horsemen, his voice returning with a quiet, broken dignity that had none of the Chief Warden's terror.

"Put up your carbines," he ordered. "Unharness the pack-horses. Unload the powder kegs and stack them under the rock shelter."

He looked across the newly closed stone span toward the towering snow-covered peaks that rose into the clear morning sky.

Maren knelt beside the ancient waymarker, running her fingertips over the carved letters of Johannes Althaus's name.

The granite was cold, but beneath the crystalline surface she felt the incredible permanence of the stone.

She drew out her ivory tablet of trigonometric tangents and compared the numbers with the carved inscription on the side of the pillar:

*RADIUS ARCUS: LXII PEDES ET IV POLICES.*

Radius of the Arch: Sixty-two feet and four inches.

It was the exact radius she had calculated on the North Cliff battery two days before.

Johannes Althaus had carved his calculations directly into the granite waymarker four hundred years ago, so that any future cartographer who possessed an iron chain and an alidade could rebuild the bridge if the river washed the timber away.

The Founder had anticipated everything: the frost, the flood, the political amnesia of the Council, and the long exile of his mountain children.

He had left his geometry written in stone where no brazier could reach it.

"We have two leagues of climbing before the snowdrifts close the upper pass," Corvus said, looking at Kellan and Maren. "Lead the way, Waymarker. It is time the Guild saw the town it forgot."

Kellan smiled, shouldered his ashwood sighting staff, and led the combined company onto the solid limestone arches of the Bridge of the Chasm.

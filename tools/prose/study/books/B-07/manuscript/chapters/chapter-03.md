# Chapter 3: Master Corvus's Compass

Master Corvus's cabinet sat at the very top of the Guild Tower, six flights of spiraling granite steps above the street.

The room smelled of beeswax, stale pipe tobacco, and the sharp, vinegar tang of acid mordant used for etching copperplates. Three walls were lined with cedar chests fitted with double iron locks, each chest bearing an engraved brass plate: *Soundings of 1520*, *Harbour Mouth and Jetties*, *Outer Banks and Spit*. The fourth wall was entirely taken up by a broad bay window overlooking the harbour basin. Below, the masts of fifty merchant vessels rocked in the grey swell like reeds in a pond.

Maren stood beside the long cherrywood table, her portfolio of coastal charts open beneath two lead paperweights.

Corvus did not look at her when she entered. He was standing by the window with a pair of brass dividers in his right hand. The dividers were twenty inches long, heavy and dark with patina, their joint secured by an iron wing nut that allowed adjustments down to the breadth of a hair. He turned the nut with his thumb, the iron threads giving off a dry, rhythmic clicking.

"Bring the chart of the South Headland," he said without turning.

Maren stepped forward, lifted the top sheet of scraped vellum from her portfolio, and laid it upon the green baize table cover.

The ink was dry. She had spent four days tracing the master plate: the jagged promontory of dark slate, the sounding marks indicating seven fathoms at half-flood, and the tiny anchor symbol marking the safe roadstead behind the spit. Her hand had been steady. The hairline hatching along the cliff face looked as though it had been printed from copper rather than drawn with a split goose feather.

Corvus walked to the table. His scarlet robe was gone, replaced by a working coat of dark grey broadcloth with horn buttons. His hands were large, square, and scarred across the knuckles from old acid burns.

He placed the fixed point of his brass dividers upon the basalt pillar at the harbour mouth. He swung the second point in a wide, sweeping arc toward the outer reef.

"Three inches, two lines," Corvus murmured, his voice flat.

He reached for the ivory standard rule that lay in a velvet-lined groove in the table. He laid the dividers against the markings.

"Three inches, two lines," he repeated. He glanced up at her from under his heavy grey brows. "Your eye is good, Apprentice Vane. Most of the boys in the third year give the reef three inches and three lines because their quill wobbles when they turn the curve. A line too wide makes the channel appear broader than it is. A shipmaster reading three lines of clearance runs his bilge onto the sunken teeth."

"I used the ivory rule twice each hour, Master," Maren said, keeping her chin level and her hands clasped behind her back.

"Good." Corvus set the dividers down. The brass legs clicked against the cherrywood. "Accuracy is not a virtue of the spirit, Maren. It is an exercise of mechanical discipline. The earth does not care what you feel. The granite reef does not soften because a boy on a timber ketch has a mother waiting in the weavers' quarter."

He turned back to the window, staring out toward the grey line of the horizon where the open sea met the low autumn clouds.

"Twenty years ago," he said, his voice dropping into a register so low and rough it sounded like stones rolling in the surf, "I took the survey ship *Gullwing* past the Black Shoals. I was thirty-eight. I thought I knew the water better than the old wardens who sat in this room before me. I believed the local fishermen when they told me the shoal had shifted three hundred paces to the east after the winter gale."

Maren held her breath. She had heard whispers of the *Gullwing* in the tavern beneath the quay, but no apprentice had ever dared mention the name in the tower.

"I altered the plate," Corvus whispered. His hand reached out to the table, his thumb unconsciously tracing the edge of Maren's chart, his fingernail pressing into the vellum margin until the pale cuticle turned red and white. "I altered the copperplate with my own burin. On the seventeenth of November, the merchant flagship *Sea Horse* came running before a southwest squall with eighty men aboard, including my brother Lucian. He was nineteen. He was wearing his first officer's coat of blue velvet with gilt buttons."

Corvus stopped. The wind rattled the leaded panes of the window with a hollow, dry chatter.

"The *Sea Horse* struck the reef exactly where the old chart said the reef sat," Corvus said. "She broke her spine in six minutes. When I reached the beach at dawn, the surf was tossing pieces of salted pork and broken pine spars onto the shingle. Lucian was lying on the sand with seaweed tangled in his curls. His velvet coat was full of wet sand."

He turned his head slowly, looking directly into Maren's eyes.

"That is why we burn the unverified paper, Apprentice Vane. That is why your father died in the common ward, and that is why young Orson will spend three years scraping hides in the tannery before he touches a quill again. When a man draws a line upon a chart, he writes the life or death of everyone who follows it."

"Yes, Master," Maren said. Her throat was tight as dry sheepskin.

Corvus nodded once. The vulnerability that had shadowed his face vanished behind the rigid, severe mask of the Chief Warden.

"Take the chart to the drying rack," he ordered, his voice returning to its crisp, authoritative snap. "Prepare three skins of heavy calf for the winter soundings. The Council convenes on Friday for your journeyman review. I have told Warden Malor that you have the steadiest hand in the scriptorium."

He picked up his brass dividers and tapped the heavy hinge once against the desk.

"Do not make me regret my words, Maren."

Corvus opened the small mahogany cabinet behind his chair. Inside sat his working standards: four brass rulers calibrated by the Royal Astrologer, a balance scale with weights of silver and lead, and six bone folders of varying curvature.

He lifted a bone folder made from the rib of a sperm whale, its polished surface yellowed with sixty years of handling.

"Your father believed that a line was an impression of the soul," Corvus said, turning the bone tool in his scarred fingers. "He told me once that when he drew the high contour of the Black Gorge, his hand trembled because the rock was grand. I told him that if his hand trembled, he should lay down his quill and hire a carter to carry his bags. The mountain is neither grand nor humble, Apprentice. The mountain is an obstacle of stone. If you draw it with reverence, you draw it too wide, and the timber wagons tip into the chasm because your reverence stole two feet from the roadway."

Maren watched his hand. The cuticle of his thumb was raw, bitten down to the quick where he had worried the skin against the brass screws of his dividers.

"We do not honour the world by flattering it," Corvus whispered. "We honour the world by holding it still. Look at this portolan. The three-fathom channel between the basalt pillar and the red buoy is sixteen perches wide. It has been sixteen perches wide since the Great Charter of 1492. As long as that number stands in the Register, sixteen merchantmen can run through the narrows in a single tide without fouling their spars. That is not coldness, Maren. That is eighty lives preserved against the rocks."

"I will not, Master," she said.

She gathered her vellum sheets, bowed her head, and stepped backward toward the door. As she pulled the heavy oak portal shut behind her, she heard the iron wing nut of Corvus's compass begin its slow, mechanical clicking once again in the silence of the high cabinet.

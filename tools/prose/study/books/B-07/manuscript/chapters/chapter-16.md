# Chapter 16: The Third Branch

The small timber dinghy moved through the black water of the Oakhaven River like a water-beetle.

Kellan rowed with muffled oars, their shafts wrapped in greased rags to deaden the squeak against the thole pins. The night was cold and starless, a heavy river mist rising from the salt marshes on either bank, smelling of rotting reeds, cold mud, and the distant woodsmoke of charcoal kilns.

Maren sat in the stern sheets, her cloak drawn around her, her feet resting upon the wooden cases holding her plane table and sighting instruments.

Above them, on the high stone embankment to the left, the dark mass of the town wall drifted past in the fog. Every two hundred paces, the yellow beacon of a watchtower glowed through the mist, the halberds of the night sentries silhouetted against the brazier smoke.

They passed beneath the water-gate without challenge.

The iron portcullis had been raised six feet to allow the tidal current to scour the mud from the sluices. The boat glided under the black dripping spikes of the grate, the shadow of the stone arch falling across them like the roof of a tomb, and then they were through, out upon the wide, dark reaches of the Upper River where the jurisdiction of the Port Warden ended and the empty silence of the hinterland began.

Two hours later, Kellan pulled the boat into a sheltered reed bed below the High Weir.

"We rest thirty minutes," he whispered, shipping the oars and securing the painter to an overhanging willow root. "The river current turns against us at the gorge mouth. We have to walk the towpath from here."

He stepped ashore into the mud, his cowhide boots sinking to the ankles, and disappeared into the willow scrub to scout the track.

Maren remained in the boat.

She reached inside her wool jerkin and drew out the cedar pencil box.

She did not light a candle. A spark on the open river would bring the mounted patrols down from the road in ten minutes. But the moon had begun to break through the scudding clouds, throwing a cold, silver radiance across the bottom of the boat.

She unrolled the living vellum across her knees.

Under the moonlight, she saw that the map had transformed once again.

The main black line had advanced past the Quarry Post, climbing the steep northern wall of the chasm toward the limestone cliffs of the upper gorge.

And there, two leagues beyond the quarry, the road branched for the third time.

It was not a gradual fork. The line split into three distinct, parallel traces that converged upon a narrow, black cleft in the rock labeled with a single word in archaic Chancery lettering:

*PONS CHASMENSIS*

The Bridge of the Chasm.

Maren leaned her face close to the vellum, her breath misting the dry skin.

She watched in real time as the ink moved.

It was not like watching an artist draw. An artist's quill deposited ink in a continuous, flowing line that followed the movement of the hand.

This was different.

Across the cream surface of the sheepskin, three tiny pinpoints of carbon black appeared simultaneously along the lines of the three arches. They were like seeds of soot dropped into water.

Slowly, with the agonizing, rhythmic patience of moss growing across a tombstone, the black pigment bled outward along the dry collagen fibres of the hide. The fibres themselves seemed to guide the ink, drawing the carbon particles along the microscopic channels of the dermal grain until the three dots lengthened into arcs.

The arcs had the exact curvature of Roman-style segmental arches: four to one ratio of span to rise, supported by two massive triangular cutwaters set into the river bed.

The map was redrawing the ruined bridge.

And as she watched, she realized why the line had branched into three.

The central trace was the main carriageway, thirty feet wide, paved with limestone flags for four-horse wains. The left trace was the raised pedestrian footway for shepherds and pack-mules. The right trace was the drainage gutter and water-channel that carried the snowmelt from the mountain peaks down to the cisterns of the lower valley.

The engineers who had built the bridge four hundred years ago had understood everything: hydraulic scour, wind-load across the gorge, the expansion of stone under summer heat, and the shattering frost of mountain winters.

They had built an eternity of stone across an abyss of air.

And Master Corvus had called it an impassable ruin.

A heavy hand touched the gunwale of the boat.

Maren looked up. Kellan was standing in the wet reeds, his ashwood sighting staff in his left hand, water dripping from the brim of his wool cap.

His eyes were shining in the moonlight.

"Path is clear," he said in his low mountain drawl. "The bailiff patrol turned back at the second milepost. We've got four hours before dawn to reach the quarry."

He looked down at the vellum on her knees, noting the three silver-black arcs spanning the chasm cleft.

"She's ready for us, isn't she?" he asked softly.

Maren rolled the vellum, slid it into its oiled silk wrap, and handed him her instrument case.

The moonlight through the dormer casement was so bright that Maren could read the engraved numbers on her ivory standard rule without lighting her candle.

She sat on her narrow cot, her knees pulled up to her chest, watching the sheepskin resting on her lap.

The movement of the ink was fascinating and terrifying.

It did not move in a straight, mechanical line like a pen guided by a ruler. It moved with the organic, branching rhythm of a river finding its way through gravel. The black carbon particles would pause at a dense cluster of collagen fibers, gather in a tiny dark pool no larger than a flea, and then suddenly shoot forward along a microscopic channel in the hide, branching into two or three delicate hairlines that wrapped around the contour of the valley.

The skin was alive with geometry.

She felt the cold night wind rattling the slate tiles above her head, but within the small room, the air seemed warm, smelling of summer clover, dried lime, and the clean stone dust of the mountain quarries.

"The arches are true, Kellan," she said, stepping over the gunwale into the cold mountain mud. "Take me to the bridge."

He caught her hand, his calloused palm warm and hard as a granite cobble, and pulled her up the muddy bank toward the shadow of the mountains.

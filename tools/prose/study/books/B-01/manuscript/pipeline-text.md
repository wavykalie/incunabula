# Chapter 01: Station 42

The station peg sat in a dry socket of hard gray dolomite, three centimeters above the mud floor. Mara Lin set the brass tip of the tape against the center punch mark and held it firm until Linh pulled the fiberglass line taut from forty meters down the gallery. The line gave no flutter. In the dead air of the Lower Siphon gallery, fifty meters beneath the jungle limestone cap, nothing moved except the cold condensation dripping off the brim of her Petzl helmet.

"Forty point two eight," Linh called out. Her voice arrived flat, stripped of treble by the soft silt banks flanking the passage. "Inclination minus three point five. Azimuth two hundred fourteen degrees."

Mara checked the twin optical windows of the tandem instrument. She closed her left eye, letting the illuminated grid settle against the reflective red target pinned to Linh's chest rig. The black reticle stabilized on two hundred fourteen. The vertical degree dial settled on negative three point five. She rotated the dial barrel twice, letting the damp brass click against the index pin, then sighted again. There was no drift. Grade Five BCRA standard required two independent sights on every leg exceeding thirty meters, with a maximum allowable discrepancy of zero point two degrees on azimuth and zero point one degrees on inclination.

"Confirmed," Mara said. She did not raise her voice. Sound travelled poorly through the phreatic tube, dampened by wet limestone scallops that lined the ceiling like inverted egg cartons. "Station forty-two recorded. Tie-in matches the secondary traverse from Tuesday."

She took the orange Rite in the Rain notebook from her breast pocket. The paper was stiff with subterranean moisture, thick with synthetic sizing that resisted water and grease. With a 3H mechanical pencil, sharpened to a needle point on a scrap of carborundum stone, she lettered the coordinates into the matrix. The lead was hard enough to dent the waterproof paper without smudging. Her handwriting remained upright, small, and strictly box-ruled. Each numeral occupied exactly two millimeters of vertical grid.

Behind her, at the base of the flowstone cascade, Dr. Julian Vance knelt in a pool of clear drip-water. His yellow waterproof Pelican case was open on a flat limestone slab, displaying rows of narrow glass sample vials and the digital readout of an electrical conductivity probe. The cable vanished into a dark fissure where a trickle of seep water emerged from an unmapped bedding plane.

"Conductivity is climbing, Mara," Julian said, without lifting his gaze from the LCD screen. "Four hundred twenty microSiemens per centimeter. It was three hundred ten when we dropped through the entrance sinkhole thirty-six hours ago."

"The base level aquifer carries dissolved carbonates from the surface soils," Mara replied, capping the pencil and seating it in the spiral binding. "Normal seasonal runoff."

"It is not runoff from the surface dolines," Julian said. He dipped a plastic syringe into the pool, drawing ten milliliters of liquid and expelling it through an inline cellulose filter. "Surface runoff carries organic acids from decayed foliage. That drops the pH below seven. This water is sitting at seven point eight. It is saturated with calcite. It has been held under hydrostatic pressure deep inside the phreatic zone for months, and now something is squeezing it upward through the floor fissures."

Rik Sen clattered down from the boulder choke twenty meters upstream. He carried two coiled dynamic safety ropes over his broad shoulders, his red Cordura oversuit caked in stiff, drying mud. The metal gates of six steel carabiners clicked against his Petzl Stop descender with every stride. He dropped the ropes onto a dry gravel ledge with a heavy, wet thud.

"Rigging is clear up to Station thirty-eight," Rik said, wiping a streak of red cave clay from his forehead with the back of his neoprene glove. "I replaced the rusted hanger on the traverse pitch. The expansion bolt held twenty kilonewtons on the torque wrench. But the air in the upper slot smells stale. Wet silt and dead leaves."

"The entrance shaft is two kilometers north," Mara said, pointing the pencil tip at the survey sketch. "The barometric gradient usually draws fresh air down the pitch during the afternoon heat."

"Usually," Rik grunted. He unclipped his water bottle, took a short swallow, and shook the canteen to judge the remaining volume. "We have eight kilometers of horizontal crawlways between here and the sunlight. If we finish the loop to Passage Blue tomorrow morning, we can begin the haul out by noon. Julian, how long will your water assays take?"

"Fifteen minutes for the titration," Julian said. He capped his vial and snapped it into the foam rack. "The calcium carbonate saturation index is plus zero point four. Calcite is actively precipitating in the fissure mouth right now. That requires an immense hydrostatic head driving water from the deep phreatic reservoir."

Mara picked up the laser distance meter resting on her instrument case. The small gray LCD screen displayed distance, battery reserve, and ambient barometric pressure. The pressure reading showed one thousand twelve millibars.

She stared at the numbers. Two hours ago, when they established Station forty-one at the siphon bypass, the ambient pressure had measured one thousand twenty millibars. In a deep subterranean karst system, surrounded by hundreds of thousands of tons of impermeable limestone, atmospheric pressure changed slowly, damped by the narrow friction of long cave passages. An eight-millibar drop in one hundred twenty minutes was impossible under normal diurnal weather patterns.

"The monsoon front was forecast for Thursday," Linh said, stepping up from the survey station with the target staff tucked under her arm. Her youthful face was pale under the cool glare of her LED headlamp. She looked at Mara, waiting for the dismissal that usually followed any breach of schedule. "Today is only Tuesday."

Mara did not answer immediately. She knelt beside Station forty-two, running her bare fingers across the cold dolomite bedding plane. The rock was smooth, cold, and solid. For twenty years she had worked subterranean systems from the French Alps to the tropical towers of Southeast Asia. She had never lost a survey station, and never lost a team member. Her safety record was built on a single unshakeable premise: the cave was a mechanical structure governed by mathematics. If you recorded every angle, respected every tensile limit, and maintained strict geometric closure on every traverse loop, the rock remained predictable.

She placed her palm flat against the floor. Beneath the cold surface of the stone, she felt a faint, rhythmic pulse. It was not the tremor of a falling rock or the vibration of their own boots. It was the low-frequency hydraulic thump of water moving under immense hydrostatic pressure through submerged conduits far below their feet.

"Pack the survey gear," Mara said calmly, closing the orange notebook and tucking it into her zipped inner pocket. "Julian, pack the titration kit. Rik, do not derig the traverse lines. We are moving our bivouac up to the dry terrace above Section Nine immediately."

Linh hesitated. "What about the survey leg into Passage Blue? We only need three more stations to tie the loop into the master map. If we do not finish it now, the season closes."

"The loop waits," Mara said. She strapped the tandem clinometer into its padded Cordura holster at her hip. "The air pressure dropped eight millibars because a massive tropical low-pressure cell has stalled directly over the mountain. The monsoon has arrived forty-eight hours early, and the upper sinkholes are already taking water."

Rik stood motionless, his heavy jaw set. He looked down the dark black throat of the Lower Siphon gallery, where the pool reflected the pale beam of his headlamp like a sheet of black obsidian. "If the sinkholes take water now, the lower siphon fills in six hours. Section Nine has two wet crawls that drop below the base flood line."

"Which is why we move now," Mara said, snapping the lid of the Pelican case shut. "We take forty minutes of personal gear, the primary ropes, and the emergency medical kit. The rock drill and the heavy core samples stay on the high ledge at Station forty."

Julian stood up slowly, pressing both hands against the small of his back to ease the stiffness of five hours spent kneeling on wet gravel. He looked at the bubbling fissure in the floor. A thin film of brown foam was forming along the rim of the pool, spinning slowly in the eddy.

"Look at the foam," Julian whispered. "Saponified organic matter. Soil wash from the summit dolines. It has already traveled through the vertical vadose shafts."

"Five kilometers in less than an hour," Linh said, her voice trembling slightly. "The vertical karst chimneys function like storm drains."

"They are storm drains," Mara said, hoisting her yellow PVC tackle bag onto her shoulders. She adjusted the chest strap until the nylon webbing bit into her collarbone. "This entire massif is an open hydraulic valve. Rik, take the lead with the rigging sack. Linh, stay on my heel with the instruments. Julian, bring up the rear with the medical kit. We maintain three paces of separation on the climbs and five paces through the breakdown blocks. Nobody unclips from a safety line until both cowstails are locked into the next anchor."

Mara knelt on the damp gravel bench to calibrate the Suunto optical clinometer against the plumb line.

The brass transit casing was cold, its sapphire bearing floating in damp damping fluid that resisted the four-degree chill of the portal draft. To maintain British Cave Research Association Grade 6 standard, every foresight required an immediate backsight verification within zero point two degrees of arc. She sighted through the rubber eyepiece toward the carbide station mark on the travertine boss, holding the crosshairs steady while her ribcage expanded and contracted. A deviation of two tenths of a millimeter at twenty meters would compound across forty survey legs into a five-meter positional error at the terminal sump.

"Check the station mark," Mara said.

Julian adjusted the reflective prism on the survey rod. "Prism is level. Bubble is centered."

The limestone here was massive biohermal sparite, bedded in horizontal layers sixty centimeters thick that dipped two degrees toward the western syncline. Water seeped between the bedding planes, depositing paper-thin crusts of calcium carbonate that cracked like eggshells under the soles of their Vibram boots. Above the bench, the ceiling arched into a parabolic pressure dome carved by ancient phreatic water under twenty atmospheres of hydrostatic pressure. Every square meter of stone bore the shallow, scallop-shaped hollows of rapid turbulent flow, their steep upstream faces recording a current that had drained the upper plateau during the middle Pleistocene.

Mara cross-checked the magnetic declination offset against the regional geological quadrangle map.

In this quadrant of the Shan plateau, the geomagnetic field drifted three degrees and fourteen minutes west of true geographic north, requiring a permanent correction factor dialed into the optical sighting prism. A failure to apply the declination offset would rotate the entire surveyed polygon clockwise, introducing a forty-meter lateral displacement where the subterranean conduit approached the perimeter fault. She used the brass adjustment screw on the compass sideplate to lock the declination ring, verifying the setting against a solar azimuth shot taken at the portal veranda before dawn.

"Declination is locked at three point two degrees west," Mara said.

Julian recorded the calibration constant in the station header. "Transit zero is verified."

The entrance portal breathed with the steady, pulsing rhythm of a deep karst aquifer. Every four seconds, a gentle surge of cool, subterranean air spilled across the threshold, condensing into wisps of white vapor as it met the warm, saturated atmosphere of the jungle valley outside. The temperature differential was seven degrees, generating a continuous convective draft that kept the rock walls dry for the first fifty meters before the phreatic zone commenced.

Mara paused at the station marker to record the ambient air humidity on the survey card.

The sling psychrometer registered one hundred percent relative humidity, with the wet bulb and dry bulb thermometers reading identical values of eleven point eight degrees Celsius. In fully saturated cave atmospheres, human perspiration could not evaporate from the skin, severely impairing the body's natural thermoregulatory cooling during heavy physical exertion and accelerating heat exhaustion during gear hauls. She noted the psychrometric values in the station header alongside the barometric pressure, providing a baseline environmental profile for the entrance zone.

Mara sealed the primary survey log in its heavy vinyl sleeve, clipping the waterproof pouch to her chest sling D-ring with an aluminum screw gate. The damp limestone air carried the clean, cold scent of condensation and ancient calcite, a constant reminder of the geological mass resting above their helmets.

The entrance survey established the geodetic datum for the entire exploration. Mara verified the benchmark coordinates against the national triangulation network, ensuring that every subterranean vector tied back to the primary benchmark on the ridge.

Mara checked the alignment of the tripods on the station platform, verifying the plumb line against the bronze index mark. The optical transit required continuous recalibration as the morning temperature rose, shifting the refractive index of the glass prisms by microscopic fractions.

"Backsight is stable within three arc-seconds," Julian called out from the portal threshold.

The structural geology of the entrance arch exhibited classic phreatic features from the early Tertiary. Massive bioherms of fossilized coral and calcareous algae formed the upper lintel, their porous matrices impregnated with crystalline sparite that sparkled in the oblique morning light. Below the fossil bed, the rock transitioned into thin-bedded micritic limestone, easily fractured along argillaceous partings. She recorded the geological facies boundary in the station logbook, sketching the stratigraphic contact to assist regional geologists in correlating the subterranean cave network with the surface escarpment.

The four headlamps swept across the damp, fluted walls of Station forty-two one final time. Mara took a strip of reflective survey tape, peeled the backing, and pressed it firmly over the brass station peg in the dolomite floor. The silver chevron gleamed brightly in the darkness, marking the exact point where their surveyed world ended and the rising water began.
# Chapter 02: The Sound in the Floor

The bivouac at Section Nine smelled of wet wool, damp silt, and spent carbide sludge. Dr. Julian Vance sat on a flat sheet of closed-cell foam with his boots unlaced, pressing the steel sensor of his acoustic stethoscope against the polished surface of the bedding plane. The instrument was an industrial geophone, modified with a brass transducer cone to pick up micro-vibrations in dense rock.

Mara knelt beside him, wiping the optical lenses of the Disto with a microfiber square. "What does the frequency spectrum look like?"

"Eighty hertz," Julian said, his eyes closed beneath the dark brim of his wool watch cap. "Broadband harmonic tremor. This is not surface rainfall hitting the sinkholes. When raindrops hit a forest floor or funnel into an open doline, they create high-frequency noise, above two hundred hertz. The signal here is low, rhythmic, and continuous."

"Bedload movement," Mara suggested, checking the battery contacts on her spare lamp. "Gravel rolling along the subterranean stream bed."

"No," Julian said, taking off the headset and handing it to her. "Put this on. Listen to the phase interval."

Mara set the binaural headset over her ears and adjusted the knurled volume dial. Through the cold metal tips, the voice of the mountain entered her skull. It was not a roar or a splash. The vibration resembled the deep, subterranean thrum of a diesel turbine idling inside a drydock. Every six seconds, a heavier surge struck the bedrock, vibrating through the bones of her jaw.

"That is hydraulic pounding," Mara said, lowering the headset. "The lower conduit operates under open-channel flow, but the air space above the water is compressing."

"Look at the direction of the wave front," Julian pointed toward the black arch of the lower passage, where the survey line ran back toward Station forty. "The siphon is two kilometers downstream. The gradient drops twelve meters between here and the sump. Under normal drainage, water accelerates as it falls down the incline. But the geophone shows the shock waves propagate upstream. From the siphon, toward us."

Linh looked up from her sleeping mat where she was re-rolling survey tape. "How can a shock wave travel upstream?"

"When a subterranean sump completely fills with water, it ceases to function as an open drain," Julian explained, speaking in the quiet, methodical cadence he used in university lecture halls. "The conduit becomes a closed pipe under pressure. If water continues to pour into the system from the vadose shafts above, the hydrostatic head increases rapidly. The water cannot escape through the flooded restriction fast enough, so it backs up into the dry galleries. The conduit reverses its apparent current. The lower sump is no longer draining the cave. It is flooding backward into Section Nine."

Mara stood up and walked to the lip of the ledge. Below their camp, the gravel floor of the gallery remained dry. Pale white cave crickets crawled across the dry rimstone pools, their long antennae sweeping the still air. There was no visible water yet. Through the soles of her rubber caving boots, she felt the rock trembling. The limestone transmitted the immense hydraulic strain of millions of cubic meters of water trapped in the mountain below.

"How much time before the gravel floor takes water?" Mara asked.

"If the inflow at the summit sinkholes matches the barometric drop we recorded," Julian said, checking his waterproof digital stopwatch, "the hydrostatic head will overcome the friction in the lower crawls within three hours. When that happens, the gravel floor will not flood from the ceiling. It will fountain up through the drainage cracks beneath our feet."

Rik walked over from his gear depot, holding a torque wrench and a pouch of stainless steel anchor bolts. "Section Nine crawlway is two hundred meters long. The ceiling clearance is forty-five centimeters. If the gravel floor fountains while a surveyor is inside that tube, there is nowhere to turn around. You cannot back out of a forty-five-centimeter pinch with a tackle bag on your foot."

"We move through Section Nine immediately," Mara said. "We do not wait for morning. We haul Julian's hydro kits and the survey lines through to the Upper Gallery before the floor cracks open."

Julian smiled faintly, though his knuckles were white where he gripped his Pelican case. "A prudent calculation, team leader. I would prefer not to have my dissertation samples converted into an impromptu gravel slurry."

Mara checked the watch pinned to her chest pocket. "It is twenty-three hundred hours. Check all lamp batteries. Pack the bivouac. Rik, you take the first haul rope into the crawlway. I want an anchor bolt placed in the solid limestone above the pinch before anyone enters the squeeze."

Linh reached for her tackle sack, her movements quick and nervous. "Mara, what about the gear depot we left at Station thirty-nine? The spare climbing ropes and the heavy surveying tripod?"

"The tripod stays," Mara said without turning around. "The mountain will keep it safe until the dry season, or it will bury it in silt. We travel with survey logs, medical supplies, primary climbing line, and personal lights. Everything else is secondary mass."

Rik shouldered his heavy canvas bag, testing the balance of the hammer drill. "Two hundred meters of belly crawl on cold limestone. Let us hope the rock remembers its manners for another hour."

Mara secured the waterproof notebook in her internal pocket. She felt the chill of the dolomite seep through her gloves as she strapped on her helmet. The air in the gallery had turned heavy, saturated with aerosolized moisture that tasted faintly of pulverized calcite. Beneath her feet, the pulse of the geophone seemed to beat in direct synchronization with the deep, blind pressure of the flooded massif.

"Take fifty meters of dynamic line as a tow line, Rik," Mara added, inspecting his carabiners. "Clip the lead bag behind your heels. If you hit standing water before the ninety-meter bend, halt and signal with three tugs."

Linh timed the drip intervals with the mechanical stopwatch strapped to her forearm.

The drops fell from an elongated soda-straw stalactite three meters above the mud bank, striking a flat chert slab with the precision of a metronome. At fifteen minutes past eight, the interval had been forty-two seconds. By nine o'clock, the period had contracted to nineteen seconds, each splash throwing tiny beads of calcite-saturated water across the rubber toe caps of her boots. An acceleration of that magnitude meant the epikarst perched aquifer on the surface ridge was fully saturated, passing hydrostatic head directly down the vertical fracture network.

"The infiltration delay is gone," Linh said.

Rik looked up from coiling the dynamic traverse rope. "How much water is coming down?"

Mara took out her water-testing kit and dipped a test strip into the pooled runoff. The electrical conductivity read four hundred and twenty micro-siemens per centimeter, showing high dissolved calcium bicarbonate from fresh meteoric water rather than stagnant phreatic storage. Fresh rain was punching straight through the limestone cap, bypassing the diffuse matrix porosity through open solution rifts. If the surface storm maintained its intensity for another four hours, the conduit below Station 14 would transition from free-surface flow to full pipe pressure.

Mara adjusted the sensitivity ring on the acoustic geophone probe pressed against the rock floor.

The sensor was an electromagnetic transducer tuned to pick up low-frequency vibrations transmitted through the solid limestone strata. Through the foam-padded headphones, the subterranean world hummed with microscopic mechanical activity: the steady, rhythmic thrum of falling water drops in distant chimneys, the faint scraping of gravel shifting in submerged bedding planes, and the distant, subsonic roar of the trunk stream grinding through the deep canyon.

"The bedrock frequency is rising," Mara noted.

Linh watched the analog needle twitch across the dial. "The aquifer is loading."

When water filled the unsaturated fracture network of a karst massif, the added mass altered the resonant acoustic properties of the rock towers. Dry limestone exhibited a fundamental resonant frequency around twenty-two hertz under ambient atmospheric pressure; as the pore volume saturated with water, the acoustic velocity through the stone increased from three thousand meters per second to nearly four thousand five hundred meters per second, shifting the resonant harmonics upward.

Linh tapped the glass face of the aneroid barometer mounted on her instrument panel.

The needle swung three tenths of a millibar to the left, indicating that the atmospheric pressure inside the gallery was falling in synchronization with the advancing surface storm front. Even through eighty meters of solid limestone overburden, barometric pressure fluctuations penetrated the cave system almost instantaneously through micro-fractures and bedding joints, altering the boiling point of water and influencing the degassing rate of dissolved carbon dioxide from dripping seepage water.

Linh recorded the barometric differential in the station margin, noting that the ambient air density had shifted by two percent since entering the upper trunk conduit. In closed phreatic systems, even microscopic density shifts altered acoustic propagation and air exchange rates across narrow constrictions.

The hydrograph curve showed a steepening recession limb as the epikarst drained into the lower fractures. Each millimeter of surface rainfall translated into thousands of liters of pressurized water seeking egress through the narrow cave conduits.

Rik grunted in assent, adjusting his knee pads. "If I hit standing water at ninety meters, the bend is already sumped. Keep your whistles handy."
# Chapter 03: The Squeeze at Section Nine

The mouth of Section Nine was a horizontal slit in a bedding plane of dense, black limestone, thirty-eight centimeters high and barely wide enough for a human chest without a tackle bag. Cold, stagnant air pooled around the entrance, smelling of wet clay and sulfur. Mara knelt at the lip of the slot, shining her narrow-beam spot lamp into the darkness. The passage ran straight for twenty meters over a bed of polished chert gravel before turning sharply to the left around a calcified bedrock rib.

"Tackle bags go ahead of you on drag lanyards," Mara instructed, her voice steady and procedural. "No personal packs on your back. If your chest jams, exhale completely and push with your boot toes. Do not thrash. If you thrash against the limestone teeth, you will wedge your pelvic bones."

Rik had already stripped off his outer technical gear, leaving only his minimal webbing sit rig and knee pads. He clipped a thirty-meter haul cord to the grommet of his heavy rigging sack and pushed it into the hole. "I will set the directional anchor at the bend. Once I call clear, Julian goes second, then Linh. Mara, you bring the master survey case."

"Understood," Mara said. "Julian, keep your limbs extended. If the ceiling drops below thirty-five centimeters, call out the measurement."

Rik dropped onto his stomach and slid into the slot with a practiced, snakelike wriggle. The sound of his Cordura suit scraping over the gravel echoed back through the limestone tube, rhythmic and dry. For ten minutes, only the rasp of fabric and the occasional muffled grunt marked his progress. Then his voice drifted out from the bend, sounding small and hollow, as if speaking through a lead pipe.

"Station thirty-seven junction reached. The passage widens to half a meter past the calc rib. The rock here is solid micrite. Anchor set. Send Julian."

Julian pulled his neoprene knee sleeves tight and adjusted his helmet chin strap. He looked at the narrow dark slot with an expression of clinical detachment. For all his academic brilliance in subterranean fluid dynamics, he lacked the wiry, compact build of a lifetime caver. His shoulders were wide, his frame long and angular. He lay flat on the cold gravel, pushed his small instrument pack ahead of him, and pulled himself into the squeeze using the strength of his forearms.

Mara watched his boots vanish into the black slit. She kept her fingers resting against the rock at the entrance, feeling the vibration. Two meters inside, Julian's breathing grew shallow and rapid, amplified by the tight stone walls.

"Pinch point at eight meters," Julian called back, his voice strained. "My ribcage is scraping both ceiling and floor. Exhaling."

"Take your time, Julian," Mara said calmly into the opening. "Work with the rhythm of your lungs. Two centimeters per push. Do not lift your chin."

The rasp of Julian's boots continued. Slowly, inch by inch, his frame moved deeper into the bedding plane. Linh stood beside Mara, her hands clenched around the strap of the survey target staff, her knuckles pale under the glare of her headlamp. She stared into the dark throat of the rock as if expecting the mountain to clamp shut before her eyes.

"He is moving too slowly, Mara," Linh whispered, her voice tightening with dread. "Listen to the drip in the sump gallery behind us. The water is rising in the floor cracks."

"Focus on your gear, Linh," Mara said softly, without breaking her gaze from the crawlway. "Count your carabiners. Check the locking sleeves. Panic is simply energy wasted on unmeasured outcomes."

Then the sound changed.

A sharp, explosive crack echoed through the bedding plane, like a dry timber snapping under heavy snow. A muffled thud shook the limestone floor, followed by an immediate, choked gasp from deep inside the crawlway. A puff of dry, calcified dust blew out of the slot, coating Mara's eyelashes in fine white powder.

"Julian!" Mara shouted into the slot. "Status!"

There was a long, suffocating silence. The only sound was the steady drip of condensation from the ceiling and the frantic, ragged gasps of a man trying not to scream.

"Boulder dropped," Julian's voice came back, thin and fractured with pain. "Ceiling block at twelve meters. A breakdown slab from the false bedding. It sheared off when my shoulder brushed the base."

"Did it strike you?" Mara demanded, dropping onto her belly and shining her light straight into the crawlway.

In the narrow beam, fifty feet into the black slit, she saw a jagged slab of gray limestone resting diagonally across the passage. Beneath the bottom edge of the stone, Julian's right leg was pinned flat against the gravel bed. His upper body was twisted onto his side, his hands clawing at the gravel to take the crushing weight off his limb.

"My femur," Julian gasped, his voice cracking into a high, breathless register. "The edge caught me midway down the shaft. I felt the bone shear. Mara, I cannot move the slab. It is fifty kilograms of solid rock."

Rik's voice boomed from the far end of the crawlway, sharp with alarm. "Julian! Can you reach my haul cord?"

"No," Julian groaned. "My torso is through the constriction, but my hips are jammed. The rock has me pinned to the floor."

"Rik, stay where you are!" Mara ordered immediately. Her mind snapped into the cold, rigid geometry of mountain rescue protocol. "Do not pull from that side. If you pull on his shoulders while his leg is wedged under a shear slab, you will compound the fracture and sever the femoral artery."

She turned her head back toward the entrance ledge. "Linh! Bring the titanium crowbar, the Petzl Rocpec drill, and the sam splint from the medical sack. Move!"

Linh was already diving for the gear bags. Within thirty seconds, she laid the tools on the gravel beside Mara's boots. Her hands were shaking violently, but her jaw was clenched tight. "What do you need me to do, Mara?"

"You are smaller than I am," Mara said, measuring Linh's chest depth with an experienced eye. "You have thirty-two centimeters across the shoulders. But you cannot lift that slab alone. I am going in with the crowbar. You stay at the entrance and feed the rescue line. If I call for the tackle, you haul on the mechanical advantage pulley."

Mara took a deep breath, hooked the flat edge of the titanium pry bar into her waist belt, and pushed herself into the squeeze.

The limestone pressed down upon her immediately, heavy, cold, and utterly indifferent. The ceiling hung four inches above the bridge of her nose. To move forward, she had to turn her head completely sideways, dragging her cheek through the damp silt, digging her toes into the gravel bed to generate forward momentum. The air smelled of powdered calcite and cold sweat. Her chest compressed against the floor with every exhale, and she felt the terrible, primitive weight of five hundred feet of solid mountain pressing down upon her shoulder blades.

She did not allow herself to think about the barometric drop. She did not allow herself to think about the rising water backing up through the lower siphon. She counted her pushes. One. Two. Three. Four.

At ten meters, her helmet lamp illuminated the breakdown slab. It was an oblong block of crystalline limestone, sixty centimeters long and twenty thick, which had dropped from an unstable mud seam in the roof. The sharp lower corner rested directly across Julian's right thigh. The fabric of his Cordura suit was torn open, exposing blood-soaked thermal underwear.

Julian lay on his side, his face gray beneath the dirt, his teeth digging into his lower lip so hard that blood dripped from his chin. His eyes were wide, dilated with agony and shock.

"Do not look at the leg, Julian," Mara whispered, her face inches from his. "Look at my helmet lamp."

"Compound fracture," Julian whispered, his breath hitching violently. "Mid-shaft. Bone ends are grinding against the rock. Mara, you cannot lever this slab in a forty-centimeter space. There is no fulcrum."

"I do not need a fulcrum," Mara said coldly. "I have twenty years of structural geometry."

She surveyed the slab. The limestone block was not pinned by the ceiling; it had slid down from a lateral pocket and wedged against a floor rib. If she applied vertical force, the rock would bind tighter against the limestone teeth. But if she drove the wedge horizontally into the gravel bed beneath the block, the angle of the bedding plane would cause the stone to slide down and away from the bone.

She set the chisel edge of the titanium bar against the base of the slab. "Julian, this is going to hurt more than the fracture. When I strike the bar, the rock will shift two centimeters lateral."

"Do it," Julian rasped, closing his eyes. "Do it before I pass out."

Mara pulled her small steel rock hammer from her belt loop. In the suffocating, horizontal slot, she had less than twenty centimeters of swing. She locked her elbow, using the rotation of her wrist to deliver sharp, rapid blows to the striking cap of the crowbar.

The metallic clang of steel on titanium rang out like a gunshot inside the narrow crawlway, deafening and harsh. On the fourth strike, the chisel bit deep into the gravel seam. Mara threw her entire upper body weight across the bar, using her shoulder as a cantilever.

The slab groaned, shifted, and rolled sideways into the mud trench.

Julian let out a single, strangled cry as the pressure released, then his head fell limp against the gravel bed. He had lost consciousness from the sudden release of shock.

"Rik!" Mara yelled toward the far end. "The slab is clear! Feed the haul line to his chest rig! Gently! Maintain absolute alignment on his spine!"

From the far end, Rik's muscular arms reached through the wider section of the tube. Working with delicate, agonizing precision, Mara supported Julian's fractured limb, aligning the jagged bone ends as best she could while Rik slowly, centimeter by centimeter, drew his limp body through the remainder of the constriction into the wider chamber beyond.

Mara crawled out behind them, her oversuit torn at both elbows, her chest heaving with exertion. She knelt on the floor of the upper chamber, her hands covered in cold mud and Julian's blood. Rik was already wrapping an aluminum emergency bivvy sheet around Julian's torso, while Linh knelt beside his leg with the sam splint and rolls of elastic bandage.

Julian's eyes fluttered open. He looked up at the black vaulted ceiling of the upper chamber, then down at his immobilized leg.

Mara pressed her chest flat against the cold gravel of the stream bed to sight the low passage.

# Chapter 40: The Closed Polygon

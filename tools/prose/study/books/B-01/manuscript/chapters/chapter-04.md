# Chapter 04: Splint and Sump

The cold in the upper chamber had the density of lead. Mara molded the pliable aluminum sam splint around Julian's right lower limb, securing it with strips of wide adhesive tape. She did not look at his face. To look at his face was to see the white, pinched skin around his nostrils and the blue tint of his lips that signaled the onset of hypothermic shock.

"Capillary refill is four seconds in the right foot," Mara said, her voice dropping into the flat, clinical cadence of field first aid. "The pulse in the posterior tibial artery is weak, but present. The fracture is contained. We need dry insulation."

Linh unzipped her personal tackle sack and pulled out a fleece pullover, still dry inside its heavy ziplock bag. She bunched it beneath Julian's foot to keep the cold limestone floor from draining his body heat. "I have two chemical heat packs left. Should I activate them?"

"Put one in his groin and one under his left armpit," Mara directed. "Keep them away from the splint. We cannot afford vasodilation in the injured leg until we control the internal hematoma."

Julian pulled his head back against a coil of rope, his breath misting faintly in the beam of Linh's headlamp. He reached down with a trembling hand and caught Mara by the sleeve of her muddy oversuit.

"Mara," Julian said, his voice a low, raspy whisper that barely carried over the steady dripping of the chamber walls. "Look at the logistics. Be a surveyor for thirty seconds."

"I am being a surveyor," Mara said, tightening the last elastic bandage around his knee. "I am calculating the load."

"No, you are protecting your record," Julian said softly. The words were not spoken in anger; they carried the dry, analytical weight of an academic paper. "A compound femur fracture cannot be hauled through vertical pitches without a rigid titanium Stokes litter. We do not have a litter. We have sixty meters of dynamic rope, four ascenders, and a box of expansion bolts. If you haul me back through the lower siphons, it will take forty-eight hours of vertical hauling. The water will catch you at Section Nine before you clear the first pitch."

Mara did not answer. She checked the tension on the splint straps, ensuring the foam padding prevented the aluminum edge from biting into his swollen flesh.

"Leave me on the dry terrace above the station," Julian continued, his dark eyes fixed on hers. "Give me the geophone, the extra carbide canisters, and the survey radio beacon. The beacon has an emergency low-frequency transmitter. If a rescue team reaches the entrance doline, they can track the signal through the rock."

"The limestone cap above this chamber is two hundred meters of dense dolomite," Mara said without pausing. "A low-frequency beacon will not penetrate fifty meters of water-saturated rock. Leaving you behind amounts to abandonment, Julian, not an evacuation protocol."

"It preserves three lives instead of losing four," Julian insisted, a flare of desperate intensity breaking through his exhaustion. "You have never lost a member of your survey party. Twenty years of pristine Grade Five surveys. Every journal in Europe cites your safety record. If you drag my broken carcass through an active flood surge and drown the whole team in a flooded crawlway, what does that record mean?"

Rik stepped forward from the gear rack, his face dark with fury. He knelt down beside Julian, his massive frame blotting out the light from the passage. "Shut your mouth, Vance. We do not leave divers in a sump, and we do not leave surveyors on a ledge. If we have to carry you across our shoulders like a sack of drill steel, you are going up the line."

"Listen to the water, Rik!" Julian yelled, the effort draining the last vestige of color from his cheeks. "Listen to it!"

They fell silent.

From the far end of the chamber, where the lower survey line led back toward Section Nine, came a new sound. A high, wet, sucking gurgle rose above the silence, followed by the violent crash of water pouring over a lip of stone.

Mara stood up and walked to the edge of the descent slot.

Ten meters below, the narrow crawlway they had just negotiated was gone. In its place, a violent torrent of brown, silt-laden water was erupting from the mouth of the bedding plane like water blasting from a ruptured municipal main. Foam, yellow with organic scum from the surface jungle, boiled against the limestone ceiling, churning through the restriction with a deafening, continuous roar.

The lower crawlway was completely sumped. The route back to the entrance was permanently closed.

"Look at the discharge velocity," Mara observed, her voice cold and level as she watched the boiling mud. "Two thousand liters per second. The hydrostatic head from the upper vadose shafts has fully coupled with the siphon."

Rik stood beside her, staring down into the drowned slot. "Nobody swims that. Not even with full trimix and twin cylinders."

Julian held the graduated measuring rod against the roof of the siphon arch.

The clearance was thirty-five centimeters. Beneath the arch, the dark water was glassy and still, reflecting their headlamp beams in shimmering yellow ovals on the drowned ceiling. There was no sound except for the rhythmic lap of tiny shock waves against the limestone walls, generated by water pulses arriving from the unseen trunk river kilometers upstream. A single millimeter rise in the hydrostatic level would seal the air space completely, converting the arch into a terminal siphon.

"We have twenty minutes of air space," Julian said.

Mara checked the buoyancy seals on the Pelican instrument case. "We push the tackle bags through first."

The limestone framing the arch was dense micritic calcilutite, extremely fine-grained and impermeable, which forced all subterranean groundwater into this single constricted bedding joint. When water flowed through an orifice of these dimensions under high pressure, the Venturi effect created a localized drop in fluid pressure that could pull floating debris into the choke point with immense force. If the water crested the ceiling while they were navigating the slot, the suction would make swimming backward against the current physically impossible.

Mara checked the O-ring seals on the waterproof instrumentation container with a tube of silicone grease.

The case housed the team's primary optical instruments: two spare compasses, the laser rangefinder, the digital anemometer, and the master survey log. A single grain of calcite sand trapped on the neoprene gasket would compromise the water seal under three meters of hydrostatic head, flooding the delicate electronics with mineralized water. She inspected the sealing groove under her headlamp beam, wiped the channel clean with a lint-free cotton swab, and applied a uniform film of grease before clamping the twin latches shut.

"Case pressure is equalized," Mara said.

Rik strapped the yellow container to his chest webbing. "It floats with three kilos of reserve buoyancy."

In flooded cave passages, equipment buoyancy was a critical safety factor. A neutral or slightly positive buoyancy prevented heavy tackle sacks from sinking into deep sediment pools if dropped during a swim, while allowing the divers to push the gear ahead through low air bells without expending metabolic energy fighting dead weight. Rik clipped a two-meter floating tow line to the handle, securing the free end to his gear loops with a screw gate carabiner.

Mara measured the water clearance under the arch with her calibrated fiberglass survey pole.

The distance between the swirling water surface and the lowest ceiling pendant was thirty-two centimeters, down three centimeters from the reading taken ten minutes earlier. She marked the water level on the limestone wall with a grease pencil, noting the time and the rate of rise. If the inflow velocity continued to accelerate, the siphon would seal completely before the second gear tackle sack could be towed through the constriction.

Mara checked the buoyancy balance of the sealed Pelican cases, ensuring that the heavy stainless steel drill kits were centered between the flotation bladders. An unbalanced tackle bag could roll in turbulent siphon water, snagging on submerged rock horns and trapping the divers in the choke.

The air space in the siphon arch continued to vent fine mist as the hydrostatic pressure increased upstream. Every member of the crew kept their emergency breathing regulators ready on their chest webbing in case of sudden submersion.

"No," Mara said, turning back toward the camp. "The debate is finished. We move up."

# Chapter 35: The Updraft

The silence in the ascending flue was sudden and absolute.

Behind them, the roar of the subterranean river receded into a muffled, low-frequency hum, its acoustic energy swallowed by a dogleg bend in the limestone conduit. For the first time in sixteen hours, Mara did not have to shout to make herself understood. The air here was strangely stagnant, trapped in a vertical fissure four meters wide that ascended at an angle of seventy degrees toward the northwest.

Julian sat propped between two smooth pillars of calc-spar, sipping the last mouthful of iodine-treated water from Mara's canteen. His breathing was shallow, but the bleeding in his lower leg had stopped beneath the pressure bandage.

"The temperature is changing," Julian observed, wiping sweat from his eyebrows.

Mara took the digital thermo-hygrometer from her chest pocket. The liquid crystal display showed 11.4 degrees Celsius at the entrance to the flue. But as she held the probe three paces higher up the scree slope, the numbers began to climb rapidly: 14.2 degrees, 17.8 degrees, 21.5 degrees.

"Subterranean air in this karst block is stabilized at fourteen degrees year-round," Mara said, checking the calibration. "The rock holds the mean annual surface temperature of the plateau. It does not vary by more than half a degree between winter and monsoon."

"Then why does this air feel like a greenhouse?" Rik asked, pulling off his neoprene balaclava. His hair was plastered to his skull with mud, his beard stiff with dry silt.

"A chimney effect," Mara replied. "Tropical karst towers act as natural thermal engines. During the day, the solar radiation warms the surface limestone plateaus, creating an enormous reservoir of hot, humid air. But when an intense monsoon downpour rolls in, the sudden barometric drop at the surface reverses the convection currents."

She pointed to the fine threads of ancient spider webs clinging to the ceiling above them.

The silk strands were not hanging straight down. They were fluttering horizontally, pulled upward toward the throat of the shaft by an invisible convective draft.

Then the draft reversed.

A violent, warm gale blasted down the fissure from above, sweeping through the narrow corridor with the force of an industrial blower. It did not carry the damp, mineral chill of the underworld. It smelled of wet teak bark, crushed jungle ferns, rotting bamboo shoots, and ozone.

It was the smell of the surface world in the grip of a tropical storm.

"Do you smell that?" Linh whispered, her face turned upward into the warm wind. Her eyes filled with tears that cut clean channels through the dried mud on her cheeks. "That is earth. Fresh leaf mold. Not cave mud."

"Air velocity is six meters per second," Mara measured, watching the anemometer wheel spin on her handheld unit. "That volume of air cannot pass through micro-fissures or porous gravel beds. It requires an open aperture at least two square meters in area."

Julian touched the splint on his leg, leaning forward with renewed focus. "How much vertical distance?"

Mara took out her brass compass and sighted up the throat of the fissure along the trajectory of the wind.

The ceiling sloped upward in a series of stepped limestone terraces, flanked by massive collapsed slabs of breakdown that had tumbled from the roof in some prehistoric ceiling collapse. The angle was steep, thirty-five to forty degrees, but it did not require technical rope climbing. It could be scrambled on hands and knees.

"Seventy vertical meters," Mara calculated, checking the altimeter reading against the station benchmark from the morning's survey log. "If the surface topography matches the geological quadrangle map, this flue terminates in a doline on the western ridge of the plateau."

"Can we carry Julian up that incline?" Rik asked, testing the stability of the scree slope with his boot heel.

"We do not carry him," Julian answered, pulling himself up onto his right foot with the aid of two aluminum survey poles used as crutches. "You assist my balance. I have one good leg, two good arms, and I am not spending another hour inside this rock."

Mara smiled for the first time in forty-eight hours, her face cracking through a mask of dry clay.

Mara held her anemometer aloft as the convective gale tore through the narrow throat of the upper flue.

The wind speed registered eight point four meters per second, an astonishing velocity for an underground passage that indicated an immense pressure differential between the subterranean atmosphere and the outside storm. As the warm, tropical air blasted downward, it met the cold, saturated air of the phreatic passages, creating a dense condensation fog that rolled through the corridor like steam escaping from a locomotive boiler. The humidity was ninety-nine percent, but the air was clean, devoid of sulfurous mud odors and rich in fresh oxygen.

"The barometric gradient is thirty millibars," Mara announced.

Julian filled his lungs with the warm air. "The storm outside is a major monsoon depression."

A barometric drop of thirty millibars at the surface was typical of severe tropical cyclonic systems, which created powerful low-pressure cells that sucked air out of karst chimney networks with enormous force. By reversing the normal thermal updraft, the storm was acting as an enormous natural ventilation pump, driving surface air deep into the upper fissure and providing the trapped surveyors with the oxygen they needed to make the final ascent.

Julian monitored the barometric pressure reading on his wrist altimeter as the warm updraft surged.

The pressure read nine hundred and eighty-two hectopascals, climbing at a rate of two hectopascals every fifteen minutes as the surface cyclonic depression passed over the plateau. The altimeter display showed an elevation of four hundred and twelve meters above sea level, indicating that the team had ascended ninety vertical meters from the lower phreatic gallery since leaving Station 28. If the surface quadrangle map was accurate, the rim of the western doline sat at an elevation of four hundred and sixty meters, leaving less than fifty vertical meters to the forest floor.

"We are at four hundred and twelve meters elevation," Julian called out.

Mara verified the contour lines on her topographical sketch. "Forty-eight vertical meters to the surface rim."

The barometric altimeter was an indispensable navigation tool in complex vertical karst systems. While cave surveying measured relative distances between stations, an accurate barometric altimeter provided an absolute vertical datum that tied the underground traverse directly to the surface topography. Knowing their exact vertical distance from the jungle canopy gave the exhausted team the psychological boost they needed to press on through the final choke.

Julian recorded the wind chill factor inside the ascending chimney using his field computer.

With an ambient temperature of twenty-two degrees Celsius and a wind velocity of eight meters per second, the convective heat transfer rate was moderate, providing refreshing cooling for the climbing surveyors without inducing hypothermia. The high air exchange rate kept the atmosphere completely fresh, venting all moisture, carbon dioxide, and rock dust out through the surface fissure.

Julian welcomed the warm, saturated breeze of the monsoon updraft, letting the clean tropical air fill his exhausted lungs. The barometric pressure differential was ventilating the entire fissure, clearing all moisture and providing optimal breathing conditions.

"Pack the gear," Mara commanded. "Every piece of scrap steel, every battery, every carabiner. We leave nothing behind but our survey tags."

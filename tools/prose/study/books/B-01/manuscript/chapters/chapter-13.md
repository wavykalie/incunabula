# Chapter 13: The Haul System

The vertical rift above Station B-nine rose twelve meters through fractured dolomite before narrowing into an ascending horizontal tube. The rock on either side was sheer, polished by ancient water, offering neither natural handholds nor safe footholds for a free climb.

Rik stood at the base of the pitch, inspecting his gear rack. He laid out four Petzl spits, two expansion bolts, a steel hammer, and a mechanical advantage pulley system.

"We cannot carry him up on a shoulder carry," Rik said, looking over at Julian. "The pitch is eighty degrees vertical. The walls are slick with wet clay. If I lose my footing with Julian on my back, we both take a twelve-meter drop onto breakdown blocks."

"We build an overhead counterweight haul," Mara decided, inspecting the anchor points along the base of the rift. "Rik, you free-climb the crack with the drill kit and set a twin-bolt bombproof anchor at the top of the pitch. Rig a directional pulley with a progress-capture progress-capture ascender. We use forty meters of static rope to haul Julian vertically while I guide his splint from below."

"And the counterweight?" Rik asked.

"Linh and I will provide the haul mass," Mara said. "We clip our gear loops to the hauling strand. When we step down the boulder slope, our body weight lifts Julian off the floor. Three-to-one mechanical advantage gives us full control of the ascent rate."

Rik grunted, tightening the chin strap of his helmet. He racked his hammer and spits into his gear sling, clipped a lead line to his sit rig, and stepped into the base of the fissure.

Watching Rik climb a vertical rift was like watching a hydraulic machine at work. He was not fast, but every placement was absolute. He jammed his heavy rubber boots into the flared seams of the rock, chimneying his broad shoulders against the opposing wall, pausing every two meters to test the stability of the limestone flakes with sharp taps of his hammer.

At six meters, a hollow thud echoed through the rift. Rik stopped instantly, freezing in place. A flake of rotten dolomite, thirty centimeters wide, detached under his left hand and plunged down the shaft, shattering into sharp gravel against the boulder floor.

"Rotten rock," Rik called down, his breath coming in heavy, rhythmic grunts. "The bedding plane is fractured by ancient frost wedging. I have to traverse two meters west to find solid micrite."

He moved laterally with agonizing care, finding a solid pocket of gray stone. For ten minutes, the sharp, rhythmic ring of his hammer driver echoed through the chamber as he drove two eight-millimeter self-drilling anchors into the rock. When the bolts were set, he torqued the hanger plates until the metal bit into the stone face, then rigged the Petzl Pro Traxion pulley.

"Anchor set!" Rik called down. "Drop the haul line!"

Mara caught the descending end of the rope and clipped it into Julian's central chest sling with two locking steel carabiners. She adjusted the tubular webbing supporting his splinted leg, ensuring the aluminum frame remained perfectly rigid from hip to ankle.

"Julian," Mara said, checking his pupil dilation with her secondary light. "Keep your arms inside the sling. When you reach the mid-pitch pinch, use your left hand to fend off the wall. Do not let the splint catch on the rock ribs."

Julian nodded, his jaw clamped shut, his face slick with sweat. He did not speak. He took a wooden tongue depressor from the medical kit, wrapped it in a clean gauze square, and bit down on it between his molars.

"Hauling on three," Mara signaled to Linh. "One. Two. Three. Step."

Mara and Linh threw their combined weight onto the haul line, backing down the gentle slope of the boulder floor. The rope twanged with tension, vibrating like a cello string under the heavy load. Above them, the Pro Traxion clicked rhythmically as the cam teeth gripped the sheath.

Julian lifted off the gravel floor, ascending smoothly into the vertical rift. Rik guided the haul strand from above, his muscular arms pulling in the slack with steady, mechanical precision.

At eight meters, Julian's right foot brushed against an overhanging calc-spar tooth. The impact jarred the aluminum splint. The lower strap slipped two centimeters down his calf, allowing the fractured bone ends to shift beneath the bandage.

Julian's body went rigid. A violent tremor racked his frame, his knuckles turning dead white where he gripped the chest sling. He did not scream. He bit down on the gauze-wrapped wooden stick so hard that the wood cracked with a sharp snap between his teeth, blood welling over his lower lip as his eyes rolled back in agony.

"Hold the line!" Mara shouted, locking her boots against the boulder floor. "Rik, slack ten centimeters! The splint caught a spur!"

Rik eased the tension with millimetric control. Mara scrambled up the lower flakes of the rift, reaching Julian's boots. With both hands, she lifted the splint clear of the limestone tooth, reseating the foam padding and pulling the Velcro straps taut.

"Clear," Mara called, panting. "Haul away!"

Rik hauled with desperate, concentrated fury. Three more pulls brought Julian to the lip of the pitch. Rik reached down with one massive arm, grabbed the haul ring of Julian's sling, and dragged his limp frame onto the flat limestone terrace above.

Mara followed up the static line on her ascenders, her arms burning with lactic acid. She hauled herself over the lip and dropped to her knees beside Julian.

He lay on his back on the flat stone, his chest heaving, blood dripping from his chin onto his torn collar. His teeth were still clenched, but his eyes were open, focused on Mara with terrifying clarity.

He spat out the splintered fragments of the wooden stick.

Mara used two aluminum survey rods and three rolls of waterproof adhesive tape to immobilize Julian's fractured leg.

She laid the two lightweight rods along the medial and lateral aspects of his lower leg, extending the splint from above the knee joint to below the ankle to prevent rotation of the fractured tibia fragments. Julian's face was chalk-white in the lamp light, beads of cold sweat running through the dried mud on his forehead as she pulled the tape tight around the cotton padding. Without immediate rigid immobilization, any involuntary contraction of the calf muscles would drive the jagged bone ends through the surrounding muscle tissue.

"Hold the foot at ninety degrees," Mara told Rik.

Rik supported Julian's heel with both hands. "Pulse in the foot is strong."

Julian leaned his head back against the cold limestone wall, his hands clenched into fists in his lap. Pain in a remote underground setting was a critical psychological stressor that could induce systemic shock, lowering blood pressure and accelerating hypothermia. Mara opened her personal medical pouch, found two five-hundred-milligram tablets of acetaminophen and an anti-inflammatory capsule, and gave them to Julian with a swallow of warm water from her thermos.

Rik cut four strips of closed-cell foam padding from his sleeping mat to cushion Julian's splint.

He placed the foam between the rigid aluminum survey rods and Julian's swollen calf, securing the layers with elastic crepe bandages wrapped in an overlapping spiral. The padding prevented pressure necrosis over the bony prominences of the tibia and fibula, distributing the compressive force of the tape evenly across the lower leg. Julian watched the procedure with stoic concentration, his breathing steady despite the sharp pangs radiating from the fracture site.

"Pressure is even," Rik said, testing the tension.

Julian nodded slowly. "Good support. The ankle does not move."

Wilderness medicine in remote cave environments required improvisation using expedition equipment. Modern aluminum survey rods, designed to be rigid and lightweight, made excellent structural splints when paired with closed-cell sleeping pads and duct tape. By stabilizing the fracture above and below the knee and ankle joints, the team ensured that Julian could be transported through narrow slots without risking further displacement of the bone fragments.

Julian felt for the pedal pulse on the dorsum of his injured foot, confirming blood flow.

The pulse was distinct and rhythmic, beating at seventy-six beats per minute, reassuring him that the splinting rods were not compressing the anterior tibial artery. He tightened the upper Velcro strap on the aluminum frame, taking up two millimeters of slack to ensure that the knee joint remained completely locked against accidental flexion during the upcoming tackle haul.

Mara checked the tightness of the compression bandage over Julian's splint, confirming that the capillary refill in his nail beds remained under two seconds. The rigid aluminum survey rods held the tibial fragments in stable anatomical alignment, preventing bone displacement.

Julian tested the rigidity of the aluminum splint by gently flexing his calf muscles against the tape. The immobilization was absolute, eliminating all shearing motion at the fracture site and keeping the pain manageable.

"Station B-ten reached," Julian whispered, his voice a raw, shredded rasp. "You still have your zero-lost record, Mara. Keep moving."

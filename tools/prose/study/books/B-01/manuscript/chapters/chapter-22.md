# Chapter 22: Air Quality

They rested on the upper terrace of the Great Hall, two hundred meters beyond the constriction. The ceiling here dropped into an undulating sequence of low phreatic bells, trapping stagnant pockets of air beneath the limestone roof.

Mara sat beside the survey pack, checking the flame safety lamp they had carried as a secondary atmospheric backup. It was an antique brass Koehler mining lamp, retrofitted with an internal wire gauze chimney designed to detect methane and oxygen deficiency in confined spaces.

"The air feels heavy," Linh said, rubbing her temples. She was breathing through her mouth, her chest rising and falling in rapid, shallow cycles. "My vision has a gray halo around the edges."

"Carbon dioxide accumulation," Julian diagnosed from his bivouac mat. He reached into his pocket and produced a chemical Drager gas detection tube, snapping the glass tips with his pliers. He attached the glass cylinder to a rubber bellows pump and drew ten measured strokes of cave air through the reagent.

The pale white crystals inside the tube turned deep purple, indicating the concentration of carbon dioxide in the chamber.

"One point eight percent," Julian read, holding the tube close to his headlamp. "Normal surface air is zero point zero four percent. At one point eight percent, your respiratory center is stimulated to double its ventilation rate. At three percent, you suffer blinding headaches and nausea. At five percent, you lose consciousness within twenty minutes."

"And the oxygen concentration?" Mara asked, watching the yellow flame inside the brass safety lamp.

The flame was burning low, barely five millimeters high, its bright yellow cone shrinking into a pale, sickly blue halo. As Mara watched, the blue cone began to flutter, starving for oxygen molecules in the dense, carbon dioxide-rich atmosphere.

"Oxygen is down to sixteen percent," Julian said, checking the chemical scale. "The flood water rising in the lower system is acting like a giant liquid piston. As millions of cubic meters of water enter the lower galleries, the water pushes the stagnant, heavy carbon dioxide ahead of it, forcing the bad air into these upper pockets."

Rik stood up, checking his pack straps. "If the air is pooling in this chamber, we cannot sleep here. If we fall asleep with carbon dioxide at two percent, the concentration will climb while our respiration slows down. We will never wake up."

As Rik spoke, a faint gust of dead air drifted down from the ceiling bells.

Inside the brass safety lamp, the pale blue flame flickered once, guttered, and died completely. A thin wisp of gray smoke rose from the charred wick, curling against the cold brass bonnet.

Silence settled over the ledge, absolute and terrifying. In mining and caving tradition, the death of a safety flame is the ultimate, non-negotiable directive: the air cannot support human combustion.

Mara watched the small blue flame of the safety lamp flicker and lean inside its wire gauze cage.

The Davy safety lamp was an ancient piece of mining technology, but its mechanical reliability in detecting oxygen-deficient atmospheres was superior to modern electronic sensors that could fail in saturated moisture. When ambient oxygen levels dropped below seventeen percent, the flame elongated and turned a pale, watery yellow; at fourteen percent, it sputtered and died completely, warning the miners of imminent asphyxiation before carbon dioxide poisoning caused loss of consciousness.

"Oxygen is dropping," Mara reported.

Julian checked the breathing rate of the team. "We are hyperventilating to compensate."

In stagnant cave passages where organic matter decomposed in deep water pools, carbon dioxide accumulated near the floor because it was one point five times heavier than normal air. At four percent concentration, carbon dioxide induced severe headaches, rapid pulse, and mental confusion, impairing a surveyor's ability to calculate simple trigonometric angles. Mara ordered the team to keep their heads as high as possible, climbing onto elevated breakdown boulders where convective drafts maintained higher oxygen levels.

Mara took a Draeger chemical gas detection tube from its sealed plastic case.

She broke the glass tips off both ends of the tube with the built-in clipper on the hand pump, inserted the tube into the intake port, and executed ten full strokes of the bellows. The reagent inside the glass tube—a mixture of hydrazine and crystal violet dye—began to change color from white to deep violet as it absorbed carbon dioxide from the ambient air. She read the graduation at the color boundary: zero point eight percent carbon dioxide, comfortably below the OSHA immediate danger threshold of three percent.

"Carbon dioxide is zero point eight percent," Mara announced.

Julian checked his pulse rate against his watch. "Seventy beats per minute. Air is safe."

Regular gas monitoring was essential when navigating unventilated phreatic bypasses. In flooded cave systems, organic matter carried underground by previous floods settled into stagnant water pockets, where anaerobic bacteria consumed dissolved oxygen and generated large volumes of carbon dioxide, methane, and hydrogen sulfide. A team caught in an unventilated dead end could slip into unconsciousness within minutes without smelling or seeing the toxic gas pocket.

Mara tested the ambient air velocity near the ceiling of the stagnant air pocket.

The digital anemometer showed zero point zero meters per second, confirming that the pocket was completely unventilated and cut off from the main barometric draft of the gallery. In stagnant pockets, heavy gases like carbon dioxide settled by gravity, while lighter gases like methane accumulated near the ceiling. She instructed the team to maintain their breathing level between one and one point five meters from the floor, avoiding both the toxic floor pool and the upper pocket.

Mara recorded the chemical detector tube reading in the environmental log, confirming that carbon dioxide levels remained within safe physiological margins. Regular gas monitoring ensured that the team could rest on the elevated ledges without risk of asphyxiation.

"Pack everything," Mara ordered, her voice cutting through the sluggish fog of carbon dioxide lethargy. "No rest stop. We do not stop until we find active ventilation. Julian, keep your chin up. Linh, stay on my belt line. Move."

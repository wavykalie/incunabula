# Chapter 05: Watermark

Mara stood on the limestone ledge, watching the brown water rise. She took her wax crayon from her survey pouch, knelt at the water's edge, and drew a thick yellow line across the gray flowstone at the precise level of the current. Beside the mark, she lettered the time: 01:15.

The water was thick with suspended mountain sediment, opaque as coffee, carrying tiny fragments of decayed bamboo and shredded teak leaves from the surface sinkholes. Every thirty seconds, a surge of air bubbled up from the flooded crawlway below, throwing plumes of cold spray against her face.

"Julian," Mara called back over the roar of the water. "What was the historical flood rise rate in the Tham Nam Sawan system during the 2018 monsoon?"

Julian was propped against the boulder wall, his injured leg resting straight on two padded tackle bags. He took a sip of electrolyte water from Linh's canteen before answering. "The speleological survey logged eighty centimeters per hour during the peak storm event in October. That was considered the fifty-year maximum."

Mara waited. She kept her eyes fixed on the yellow wax mark. The cold mountain water crept over the line, covering the pigment in less than four minutes. She measured the vertical difference against her pocket steel rule.

"It has risen twelve centimeters in six minutes," Mara said. Her voice remained even, stripped of panic, stating the measurement as if recording a routine backsight reading. "That is one hundred twenty centimeters per hour. It is rising three times faster than your fifty-year historical model."

Julian closed his eyes, his head resting against the cold limestone. "The upper dolines must have suffered a catastrophic debris dam failure. When heavy monsoon rain pools behind a natural dam of fallen timber and mud in a jungle ravine, the pressure eventually blows out the plug. Tens of thousands of tons of pooled water dump into the primary sinkhole in a single pulse. A hydraulic bore."

Linh walked to the edge of the ledge, shining her headlamp down into the rising pool. The beam revealed swirling eddies where the water was already licking at the lower rungs of the wire caving ladder they had left rigged to the drop. "The terrace we are standing on is only four meters above the current water level. If it rises at over a meter an hour, this chamber will be flooded to the roof before dawn."

"The roof here is sealed," Rik said, pointing his beam upward into the vaulted ceiling. The stone above was a smooth, arched dome of solid micrite, completely devoid of openings or chimneys. "It is a phreatic cupola. If the water reaches the ceiling, it forms a dead-end air trap. The air will compress into a stagnant pocket, and the water will rise until the pneumatic pressure equals the hydrostatic head."

"And the air pocket will turn to carbon dioxide within twenty-four hours," Mara added. She turned away from the rising water, facing her team. "We cannot stay on this terrace. We cannot wait for a surface rescue team. A surface team will arrive at the entrance sinkhole, find the main stream passage completely submerged under thirty meters of water, and declare the cave inaccessible until the wet season breaks in November."

"Four months from now," Linh whispered.

"We have two options," Rik said, crossing his arms over his chest. "We either force the survey route back through the upper overflow passages, or we find high ground."

"There is no upper overflow passage on the mapped route," Mara said, pulling out the master survey map. She unrolled the heavy plasticized sheet over a flat boulder, weighting the four corners with clean limestone pebbles. "Look at the profile. The entire known cave system is a single linear phreatic conduit running from the summit sinkhole down to the resurgence spring in the river valley. Every surveyed bypass connects back to the main trunk below the base flood line. If the trunk is flooded, the bypasses are siphons."

Rik pointed a thick, mud-stained finger at a faint, dashed pencil line branching off the northern wall of the chamber, three hundred meters upstream from their current position. "What about this? Passage Blue. You scouted it during the preliminary expedition in April."

Mara looked at the dashed line. Her fingers tightened around the edge of the map. "Passage Blue remains an uncompleted survey, an unverified lead rather than an escape route."

"It goes up," Rik said, his voice rising, carrying a harsh edge of demand. "The survey notes say inclination plus fourteen degrees over the first seventy meters. That climbs toward the upper karst tower."

"It climbs into an unmapped fault zone," Mara replied, her tone hard and unyielding. "The survey ended because we reached a vertical constriction and ran out of exploration rope. We have no closure on the polygon. We do not know if it connects to the surface, if it drops into an impassable vertical shaft, or if it pinches out into solid stone fifty meters in. In cave survey protocol, an unverified passage is treated as a hazard, not a path."

Julian let out a dry, rasping laugh from his bivouac corner. "Protocol, Mara. Always the protocol. Look at your yellow wax mark on the wall."

Mara glanced down. The yellow line was already submerged under twenty centimeters of swirling brown water. The edge of the lake had advanced another meter across the gravel terrace, lapping at the base of their tackle bags.

"The known route is a drowned tomb," Julian said quietly. "Your protocol is measuring our burial depth to the nearest millimeter."

Rik inspected the tip of the hardened steel masonry drill bit under his headlamp.

The tungsten carbide cutting insert was chipped along its outer edge, worn down by contact with an embedded nodule of black chert. In pure limestone, a hand drill could bite five centimeters in ten minutes of rhythmic hammer strikes, but chert nodules measured seven on the Mohs scale, harder than tool steel and completely resistant to mechanical cleavage. To set an expansion bolt in this wall, he had to search for a clean pocket of crystalline calcite free of siliceous impurities.

"The stone is full of flint nodules," Rik said.

Julian shifted his weight against the rock wall. "Move sixty centimeters to the left."

The geological contact between the massive limestone and the cherty member was marked by a distinct horizontal stylo-lite, a serrated suture line where insoluble clay minerals had concentrated during millions of years of chemical compaction. Above the suture, the limestone was white, pure, and easily worked with cold chisels; below it, the rock was a dark, brittle conglomerate of silica lenses that shattered into razor-sharp shards under the hammer. Rik tapped the wall with the steel pick, listening for the clear, resonant ring of pure calcium carbonate.

Julian picked up a freshly fractured chip of the black chert to examine its conchoidal cleavage under his hand lens.

The fracture surfaces were glassy and smooth, curving in concentric ripples that met at razor-sharp edges capable of slicing through neoprene gloves like surgical scalpels. Chert formed as diagenetic nodules within marine limestone, where siliceous sponge spicules and radiolarian skeletons dissolved and reprecipitated as microcrystalline quartz in irregular pods and bands. Because quartz lacked cleavage planes, it fractured unpredictably when struck by steel chisels, posing an eye hazard to anyone working without safety goggles.

"The flint is pure silica," Julian said.

Rik examined the chipped edge of his cold chisel. "The tool steel cannot cut it."

To bypass the chert layer, Rik shifted his drilling angle forty degrees upward, targeting a pocket of porous calcarenite where the rock was composed of cemented shell fragments and ooids. In calcarenite, the drill bit crushed the soft calcite cement without resistance, penetrating three centimeters in less than five minutes of rhythmic rotation and percussion. Julian cleared the stone dust from the hole with a rubber blow tube, keeping the borehole clean for the expansion anchor.

Rik used a steel wire brush to scour the pulverized rock powder from the drill hole.

In rotary-percussion drilling, accumulated stone dust cushioned the hammer blows, absorbing up to fifty percent of the impact energy and preventing the carbide chisel tips from fracturing the underlying rock. By clearing the borehole with the wire brush and a rubber squeeze bulb, he ensured that every strike of the hammer delivered maximum kinetic energy directly to the solid sparite bedrock.

Rik ran his thumb across the polished surface of the calcarenite core, inspecting the tiny cemented ooids under his headlamp beam. The rock was porous and uniform, providing the ideal mechanical substrate for a permanent expansion anchor that could withstand dynamic shock loads.

The contact zone between the chert nodule and the surrounding limestone showed intense pressure solution features. Microscopic stylolites zigzagged across the rock face, concentrating dark insoluble residues along the boundary.

Mara stood silent for three beats. The sound of the rising water filled the chamber with a rhythmic, pulsing roar that vibrated through the rock beneath her feet. "Pack the camp. We haul Julian to the entrance of Passage Blue."

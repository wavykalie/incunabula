# Chapter 37: Cutting Through

Could two dive knives and a geological hammer carve an escape route through two centuries of calcified timber?

The first thirty minutes yielded five centimeters of wet sawdust and torn palms.

The wood of the ancient teak was iron-hard, seasoned by decades of subterranean draft and impregnated with microcrystalline calcite that dulled the serrated steel blades after fifty strokes. Rik worked overhead, standing on Mara's shoulders with his boots wedged into the vertical fissure, sawing in furious thirty-second bursts until the muscles in his forearms locked in violent cramps.

Sawdust showered down in sticky, damp flakes, sticking to their sweaty necks and burning their eyes with astringent wood oil.

"Rotate!" Mara yelled, bracing her neck as Rik climbed down onto the scree.

Linh stepped into the gap, taking the dive knife with blistered, bleeding fingers. She did not hesitate. Reaching overhead into the cramped space, she attacked a horizontal root branch eight centimeters thick, using the serrated edge like a drawknife to strip the outer cambium layer.

Beneath the wood, the air was changing.

The temperature in the fissure surged to twenty-four degrees Celsius. Puffs of warm, tropical vapor swirled around their heads, condensing into heavy droplets on the cold limestone walls. Rain was falling in torrents on the plateau above, sending dirty trickles of surface water running through the root ball into their faces.

Julian pulled himself up onto the intermediate boulder, his splinted leg dragging behind him on the loose stone. In his right hand, he held the hardened steel geological hammer.

"Give me the chisel end," Julian said, his voice hoarse from sawdust and exertion.

"You cannot stand up there," Mara said, wiping sweat and clay from her forehead.

"I do not need to stand," Julian replied. "Hoist me up to the shelf. I have thirty years of stonecutting in my wrists. You are sawing with the grain. You need to fracture the calcified nodes across the growth rings."

Rik and Mara hoisted Julian by his armpits, seating him on a narrow ledge seventy centimeters below the root ball. He braced his good right leg against the wall, hooked his left elbow over a limestone projection for stability, and swung the hammer.

Clack.

The hardened pick of the geological hammer struck a football-sized nodule of calcified clay wedged between two master roots. A shower of white sparks lit the dark as the steel impacted a chert cobble.

Julian struck again, aiming for the stress joint where the root had grown around the stone.

The calcified crust fractured with a sharp, hollow crack. A ten-kilogram chunk of cemented gravel fell into the void, bouncing down the scree slope into the dark below.

Through the newly opened aperture, a blast of warm, saturated rain slammed into Julian's face.

"Sky!" Julian choked out, spitting rain and sawdust. "I can see the canopy!"

The aperture was thirty centimeters wide, fringed by the jagged stubs of severed roots. Above the fissure, thirty meters into the gray gloom of dawn, the crowns of towering Dipterocarpus trees thrashed wildly in the monsoon gale. Sheets of torrential tropical rain cascaded down through the branches, soaking the forest floor in endless sheets of gray water.

"Enlarge the slot!" Mara ordered, scrambling up beside Julian with the second knife.

Together, working with mechanical desperation, they hacked at the remaining root collars. Rik drove the hammer pick into the soft cambium, prying the fibers apart while Mara and Linh sawed through the tensioned wood strands. The smell of fresh teak sawdust filled the throat of the chimney, mingling with the clean, metallic odor of falling rain.

With a final, splintering groan, the central root core broke free.

Rik shoved the wooden mass upward with both palms, tumbling it outward into the dense undergrowth of the forest floor above.

An opening sixty centimeters wide and ninety centimeters long yawned open to the sky.

Mara leaned into the root choke, her dive knife sawing through the iron-hard fibers of the teak taproot.

The wood was dense and saturated with mineral-rich sap, producing a thick, bitter sawdust that coated her tongue and burned the lining of her nostrils. Teak was naturally resistant to rot and water damage due to high concentrations of natural tectoquinones and silica, making it one of the most durable timbers in the tropical world. To cut through a root thirty centimeters in diameter required hundreds of repetitive sawing strokes with the serrated edge, working the blade back and forth until the friction heated the steel until it was hot to the touch.

"The grain is splitting," Mara called out.

Julian swung the geological hammer with surgical accuracy. "Hit the wedge now, Rik!"

Rik drove the cold chisel into the kerf Mara had cut, striking the steel head with the two-pound sledgehammer. The sound was a dull, wooden thud that vibrated through the root mass. With a sharp, splintering crack, the tensioned wood fibers ruptured along the growth rings, releasing the central root core from the limestone socket and opening a ragged window through the forest floor.

Rik cleared the wet wood chips from the kerf using a narrow aluminum scraper.

Every stroke of the dive knife generated dense, fibrous sawdust that clogged the saw teeth, reducing cutting efficiency by half if not cleared continuously. He worked with calm, methodical persistence, alternating between the serrated knife blade to cut the outer wood fibers and the geological hammer pick to shatter the brittle, calcified core of the root ball. Behind him, Linh and Mara carried the severed root segments down the scree slope, keeping the working stance clear of debris.

"The kerf is clean," Rik reported.

Julian passed the second knife up to Mara. "Keep the cut straight across the grain."

The physical labor of breaching a timber choke in a narrow vertical chimney was punishing on the human body. Standing on an inclined scree slope in ninety-nine percent humidity, working overhead with heavy steel tools, required immense physical stamina and mental determination. The surveyors rotated every ten minutes, maintaining a continuous, rhythmic attack on the wooden barrier that chipped away at the obstacle centimeter by centimeter.

Rik tested the prying force of the steel crowbar wedged behind the central root core.

By applying his full body weight to the one-meter steel bar, he generated over three thousand newtons of prying force against the calcified timber, slowly shearing the fibrous root attachments from the rock socket. With a deep, groaning crack, the final structural root detached from the limestone wall, tumbling outward into the sunlight and rain of the forest above.

Rik cleared the final wooden debris from the severed root collar, pushing the severed timber outward into the wet undergrowth. Through the newly opened slot, the gray light of dawn poured into the chimney, illuminating their triumphant faces in the mist.

Above them lay freedom. Below them, forty meters down the chimney shaft, the first brown waves of the rising flood crested the lower lip of the scree slope, hissing as they swallowed the dry stone.

# Chapter 06: The Known Way

Rik kicked a limestone cobble into the rising water. The splash was swallowed instantly by the continuous, hollow roar of the drowned crawlway. He turned on Mara, his broad frame tense, his neoprene gloves dripping dirty cave water onto the gravel between them.

"We force the known way," Rik said, his voice cutting through the damp air like a dull saw. "We know the geometry of the main trunk. We know every bolt placement, every traverse line, every rebelay between here and the entrance pitch. If we rig a guide line along the ceiling of Section Nine, we can pull Julian through on a haul drag before the water reaches the roof."

"Section Nine is already completely submerged, Rik," Mara said, standing her ground without flinching. "Look at the hydraulic gradient. The water is discharging under pressure. You cannot swim against a pressurized hydraulic bore through a thirty-eight-centimeter slot."

"I am not talking about swimming," Rik snapped, stepping closer, his face inches from Mara's helmet lamp. "I have two hundred meters of nine-millimeter semi-static line in the haul bags. I have six Petzl expansion bolts and an impact driver. I can dive through the flooded restriction with a lead line, anchor it on the far side at the Station thirty-six ledge, and set up a three-to-one mechanical advantage haul system. We put Julian in a neoprene dry suit, give him a five-liter pony bottle, and pull him through like a torpedo."

Julian coughed, a dry, painful spasm that shook his fractured leg. "A pony bottle gives twenty minutes of air at shallow depth, Rik. If my splint catches on the breakdown teeth inside that flooded crawlway, twenty minutes is just enough time to watch the pressure gauge drop to zero while you pull my arms out of their sockets from thirty meters away."

"You want to sit here and wait for the lake to drown us?" Rik shouted, turning on Julian. "You want to trust her pencil sketches into Passage Blue? She ran seventy meters of line into a blind crack six months ago and walked away because she didn't want to get her ropes muddy! That blind crack is a dead-end chimney!"

"The line in Passage Blue was stopped because of safety margins, not convenience," Mara said, her voice dropping into a register of icy, absolute authority that made Rik pause. "We had four hours of battery life remaining and zero backup anchors. Grade Five protocol forbids extending an exploratory survey into an unmapped vertical fault without a verified secondary exit."

"And your protocol has brought us here," Rik snarled, pointing down at the water that was now inching across the toes of his boots. "Stuck on a four-meter ledge with a broken man and seventy meters of fantasy!"

Linh stepped between them, holding her yellow waterproof survey tablet like a shield. "Stop it. Both of you. Look at the hydro-telemetry from Station thirty-five."

Mara took the tablet from Linh's hands. The screen displayed a simplified cross-section of the Tham Nam Sawan system, populated by the automated pressure loggers Julian had installed at four critical siphons during the initial expedition. The red line representing the water table at Siphon One, the primary exit point near the entrance shaft, had spiked into a vertical cliff.

"The hydro-telemetry confirms it," Mara said, holding the screen toward Rik. "Siphon One took eighty thousand liters per minute starting at twenty-two hundred hours. The water level at the entrance pitch is currently eleven meters above the lip of the crawlway. Even if you survived diving through Section Nine, the entire main trunk is a continuous, water-filled pipe for three kilometers. There is no air space. There are no ledges. It is a solid hydraulic plug."

Rik stared at the red line on the screen. The muscles in his neck worked as he swallowed hard. His aggressive stance slowly dissolved, replaced by the hollow, vacant expression of an experienced technical rigger who has suddenly seen the load limit on his primary cable drop to zero.

"Eleven meters over the lip," Rik whispered. "The whole lower cave is drowned."

"Yes," Mara said, turning off the tablet screen to preserve the lithium battery. "The known way has vanished until December. If we attempt to force the lower route, we will drown within forty meters. No safe alternatives exist on this ledge, Rik. The alternative to certain drowning on the surveyed line is entering an unmapped chimney that might give us access to the upper karst towers."

Julian let out a slow, controlled breath through his teeth. "The karst tower above Passage Blue is called the Dragon's Spine. It rises three hundred meters above the jungle floor. It is full of ancient fossil caves, abandoned phreatic galleries formed millions of years ago when the river flowed at a much higher elevation. If Passage Blue cuts through the fault plane, it might intersect one of those fossil galleries."

"Or it might be an isolated blind pocket that pinches out into solid limestone twenty meters up," Rik said, his voice flat, stripped of its earlier fire.

"Yes," Mara admitted, looking Rik directly in the eyes. "It might dead-end. It might pinch to ten centimeters. It might be completely blocked by calcified flowstone. But it is seventy meters higher than the water line right now, and it is moving upward. That is the only fact that matters."

Linh knelt beside the survey case, repacking the backup optical prisms into their foam cutouts. "Mara, what happens if the fault intersects an active vadose stream higher in the tower? If the summit sinkholes are dumping flood water, won't that water drain down through the same fault?"

"The geological strike runs at a steep angle to the summit drainage basin," Mara answered, unrolling the tectonic profile map. "Julian's catchment models showed that the summit sinkholes discharge predominantly to the south, into the main river sink. Passage Blue lies in the northern fault block. If the fault has active drainage, it should be localized percolation, not a main streamway."

"Should be," Rik muttered, testing the gate of a steel screwlock carabiner. "A geologist's 'should be' has buried more divers than bad gas."

"We are out of options that carry guarantees, Rik," Mara said, tightening her chest strap. "We take two dynamic ropes, the drill, the medical kit, and forty meters of static line. We leave the heavy survey tripod and the water titration bottles. Every kilogram we haul into that chimney must contribute directly to vertical ascent or basic survival."

Julian pushed himself up onto his elbows. "Leave my water conductivity logger on the ledge, Mara. Set it to log at five-minute intervals. If the water reaches the roof, the internal flash memory will record the hydrostatic pressure curve until the casing crushes. If someone recovers our gear next season, at least the hydrology department will have accurate flood surge data."

Mara looked at him for two seconds, her expression unreadable. "The logger stays in your pocket, Julian. You can write your dissertation chapter on hydrostatic pressure curves from a hospital bed in Chiang Mai."

Mara tested the core fibers of the nine-millimeter static haul rope under her headlamp beam.

Three meters from the working end, the braided polyamide sheath had been abraded by a sharp limestone rib during the equipment haul, exposing the white core bundles to the damp air. Modern caving ropes relied on the inner parallel core fibers for eighty percent of their tensile strength, while the outer woven sheath protected the load-bearing strands from grit and mechanical abrasion. With the sheath severed, fine particles of calcite sand had penetrated between the nylon filaments, where each sharp crystal acted as an abrasive knife edge under cyclic tension.

"Cut the damaged section," Mara ordered.

Rik opened his dive knife and sliced through the rope with a single clean cut. "We lose four meters of working length."

He pulled the newly cut end through the flame of his butane lighter, melting the frayed nylon strands into a solid mushroom of hardened plastic that would prevent the core from slipping inside the sheath. In single-rope technique, an unraveled rope end could slip through an ascender cam during a changeover, dropping the climber into the void. He tied a figure-eight on a bight with twenty centimeters of tail, seating the coils until the knot was dressed flat and symmetrical.

Linh inspected the sewing on the load-bearing loops of her caving sit rig.

The webbing was forty-five-millimeter high-tenacity polyester, stitched with contrasting orange nylon thread in a three-bar box pattern designed to withstand twenty-two kilonewtons of dynamic arrest force. She checked every stitch with the tip of her fingernail, feeling for broken threads or abrasion from limestone flakes. In single-rope technique, the sit rig was the climber's primary structural connection to the vertical rope system; any failure in the central maillon or the leg loop stitching would result in an immediate, catastrophic fall.

"Webbing is intact," Linh reported.

Mara double-checked the locking collar on Linh's central steel demi-rond. "Collar is torqued shut."

Unlike mountaineering gear, caving sit rigs were designed without padding to prevent moisture retention and mud absorption in wet crawlways. The low-profile webbing sat tightly around the pelvis, positioning the central attachment point as low as possible to maximize the mechanical stroke length of the chest ascender during rope climbing. Every centimeter of stroke efficiency reduced metabolic energy expenditure over hundreds of meters of vertical ascension.

Mara examined the central locking pin of the rescue haul pulley under her headlamp beam.

The stainless steel axle was fitted with sealed cartridge bearings designed to exclude abrasive limestone silt and mud slurry during wet cave operations. She spun the aluminum sheave with her thumb, feeling for grit or resistance in the race. A jammed pulley wheel would turn a rolling rope system into a high-friction slide, multiplying the haul team's physical effort and generating friction heat that could melt the nylon rope sheath under load.

Linh checked the gate clearance on her primary steel carabiner, spinning the knurled locking sleeve until the brass index mark aligned with the barrel notch. Disciplined hardware inspection was second nature to the survey crew, a simple mechanical habit that prevented unclipped gear accidents.

The cut rope end was inspected for core herniation, confirming that all twelve core bundles terminated evenly within the melted sheath. Rik coiled the remaining thirty meters of static line into a tight mountain coil for rapid deployment.

Rik picked up his heavy rigging sack, slinging the thick webbing straps over both shoulders. "Show me the survey notes for Passage Blue. If we have to climb into the dark, I want to know what size expansion bolts we need."

# Chapter 08: The Unfinished Line

Mara laid the field notebook from the April preliminary expedition flat on her knee. The waterproof page was smudged with red cave silt from six months ago, but the pencil lines remained sharp.

"Station B-zero to Station B-seven," Mara read aloud, tracing the traverse line with the blunt tip of her pencil. "Total surveyed length, seventy-two point four meters. Average inclination, plus sixteen point two degrees. Azimuth zero-three-eight degrees magnetic."

Rik leaned over her shoulder, his headlamp beam glaring off the laminated page. "Why did you stop at B-seven? The notes say 'Passage continues upward over breakdown. Airflow detectable. Survey terminated due to equipment limits.'"

"We did not run out of food, and we did not run out of battery power," Mara said, keeping her voice flat. "We ran out of rope. Station B-seven sits at the lip of a vertical fissure eight meters high. In April, we were operating as a two-person reconnaissance team. We carried only one sixty-meter rope for the lower siphon drop. I refused to free-climb an unverified limestone pitch without an anchor bolt and a belay."

"You walked away from an open climbing pitch because you didn't have a piece of dynamic rope?" Rik asked, his voice thick with disbelief. "A seven-meter scramble?"

"Climbing an unverified vertical rift in fractured limestone invites disaster," Mara said firmly, looking straight at him. "A loose block on a vertical lead can crush a climber or sever an unprotected line in seconds. Grade Five protocol states that an exploration lead is paused until proper rigging equipment can be staged. That protocol is the reason both members of that April team returned to the surface alive."

Julian leaned forward from his foam pad, his face pale in the shadow of his hood. "Look at the sketch beside Station B-four, Mara. You noted a measurement discrepancy."

Mara turned the page. Below the coordinate table for Station B-four was a short marginal note written in her upright, small handwriting: *DistoX2 backsight shows azimuth discrepancy of plus four point five degrees against Suunto tandem. Local magnetic anomaly suspected. Rock sampled: siderite-bearing limestone.*

"Iron mineralization," Julian said, his eyes narrowing as he read the note. "The fault zone contains siderite and hematite deposits. If there is local iron mineralization along the fault plane, a magnetic compass will give false readings."

"The DistoX2 uses triaxial magnetic sensors," Mara acknowledged. "It detected a four-degree deflection near the fissure wall. That means the compass bearings in Passage Blue are distorted by the mineralized rock."

Linh looked up from packing the primary survey tripod. "If the compass bearings are distorted, how do we know Passage Blue climbs toward the Dragon's Spine tower? What if the fault turns east, back toward the main river gorge?"

"We don't," Mara said simply. "We have to rely on optical backsights and manual clinometer inclinations. In an iron-rich fault, geometry is our only reliable instrument. We survey every station by sighting both forward and backward, calculating the internal angles without trusting magnetic north."

Rik rubbed his stubbled chin, his muddy gloves leaving dark streaks across his jaw. "How wide is the fissure at Station B-seven?"

"One point two meters at the base, narrowing to sixty centimeters near the lip," Mara answered, closing the notebook and sealing it in its double ziplock pouch. "Wide enough to chimney with back and feet, but narrow enough that a falling climber would wedge into the constriction."

"And how do we get Julian up an eight-meter vertical chimney with a broken femur?" Rik asked, gesturing toward Julian's heavily splinted limb.

Julian looked down at his leg, then reached into his pocket and pulled out his field compass, turning the brass casing in his fingers. "You rig a haul system from the top of the pitch, Rik. You use a three-to-one mechanical advantage with a directional pulley and a chest sling. I can keep my fractured leg extended and push with my good foot against the back wall."

"It will be excruciating, Julian," Linh said softly, her voice trembling.

"Pain is a manageable physiological variable," Julian said, his voice hard, lacking any trace of self-pity. "Drowning in two meters of brown mud on this ledge is an absolute failure state. Pack the haul bags. Let us go see Mara's unfinished line."

Mara compared the magnetic azimuth with the backsight reading in the survey logbook.

The foresight from Station 21 to Station 22 read one hundred and eighty-four degrees, but the backsight from 22 to 21 read zero zero seven degrees, an angular discrepancy of three full degrees. In cave surveying, foresight and backsight readings must agree within one hundred and eighty degrees plus or minus zero point five degrees. Any error larger than that indicated either an unrecorded transcription blunder or severe localized magnetic anomaly from iron-rich sediments in the fault gouge.

"We have magnetic interference," Mara said.

Julian brought the aluminum survey pole closer to the wall. "Look at the clay band."

The fault zone was filled with a thick vein of dark red terra rossa, a residual clay rich in oxidized iron and manganese nodules weathered from the limestone plateau over geological epochs. When she moved the sighting compass within thirty centimeters of the clay vein, the damped compass dial drifted two degrees to the east, pulled by the remanent magnetization of the sediment. To obtain an uncorrupted bearing, she had to construct an offset survey station two meters into the stream bed, isolated from the mineralized wall.

Julian checked the leveling vial on the aluminum surveying tripod head.

The spirit level bubble was floating between the two black index lines, indicating that the transit head was horizontal within three arc-minutes. In high-precision speleological mapping, an unlevel instrument base introduced systematic errors into every horizontal azimuth measurement, skewing the coordinate grid across the entire traverse. He adjusted the three brass leveling thumbscrews, rotating the head ninety degrees to confirm the level across both perpendicular axes before clamping the center column.

"Transit is level," Julian called out.

Mara looked through the sighting prism. "Station target is aligned."

The survey team operated as a coordinated geometric engine, each member executing their specific task with silent, practiced discipline. Julian managed the tripod leveling and target centering, Linh recorded the raw numbers in the station logbook, Rik handled the physical tape tensioning, and Mara sighted the optical instruments. By maintaining this strict division of labor, the team could survey a twenty-meter station leg in less than four minutes without sacrificing precision.

Julian checked the magnetic compass needle for frictional drag against the sapphire pivot.

He tilted the compass casing twenty degrees to the side, allowing the needle to swing freely before returning the instrument to a level stance. The damped needle oscillated three times and settled precisely on the magnetic meridian, showing that the pivot jewel was free of debris and that the internal damping fluid had not developed bubbles in the cold cave air.

Julian adjusted the leveling bubble on the transit base, confirming that the horizontal circle was orthogonal to the local gravity vector within one minute of arc. Precision leveling was the foundation of all BCRA Grade 6 surveys, ensuring that azimuths remained true across vertical drops.

The cross-bearing from Station 22 confirmed that the local magnetic anomaly was confined to the terra rossa vein. By moving the transit two meters into the gravel bed, the sighting compass returned clean, reproducible azimuths.

Mara looked at the survey sketch once more before slipping it into her inner coat pocket. The line ended at Station B-seven with a small penciled question mark. For six months, that question mark had nagged at her sense of cartographic order. Now it was the only direction left to move.

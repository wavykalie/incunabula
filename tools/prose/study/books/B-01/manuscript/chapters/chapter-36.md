# Chapter 36: Daylight in the Fissure

At zero four hundred hours, forty-five minutes of steady scrambling brought them to the head of the breakdown slope.

The darkness did not dissolve gradually. It was pierced by a single, needle-thin beam of emerald light.

Mara stopped in her tracks, resting one gloved hand on a damp limestone boulder. She clicked off her headlamp. Beside her, Rik, Linh, and Julian followed suit, plunging the chamber into immediate, total blackout.

Except it was not black.

Ten meters above their heads, where the limestone walls narrowed into an acute wedge no wider than a surveyor's tripod, a crooked slit in the bedrock admitted the gray, diffused luminescence of early dawn. Filtered through thirty meters of dense tropical rainforest canopy, the light was the color of jade, illuminating floating dust motes and drifting water droplets with ethereal brilliance.

A single green leaf, torn from a wild fig tree by the monsoon wind, drifted down through the slit. It fluttered in the updraft, spinning like a sycamore seed before landing softly on the brim of Julian's safety helmet.

Julian took the leaf in his trembling fingers, rubbing the waxy green surface against his palm.

"Ficus microcarpa," Julian said softly, his voice cracking. "Live chlorophyll. It grew in the sun yesterday."

Rik climbed up onto a perched block of breakdown, his hands reaching upward into the slit. His fingers touched the rock walls, tracing the joint upward toward the light.

"We have a problem," Rik called down.

Mara climbed up beside him, hauling the tackle sack onto a stable rock shelf. She looked up into the throat of the fissure.

The opening was not clear.

Two meters above the ceiling of the cave, the bedrock throat was completely choked by a colossal root ball. The root system of an ancient teak tree, seeking moisture during the dry season, had penetrated the bedding planes of the limestone, expanding over centuries until it filled the entire aperture with an impenetrable wooden plug.

The roots were as thick as human thighs, interwoven in a dense, knotted matrix of iron-hard timber. Trapped between the roots were angular limestone boulders, washed down from the surface soil horizon during ancient floods, cemented together by a hard conglomerate of calcified clay and gravel.

Water trickled through the root ball in a dozen tiny rivulets, dripping into the chamber below with steady, rhythmic splashes.

"Can we squeeze around the edge?" Linh asked from below, holding Julian steady on his crutches.

Mara examined the contact margins with her flashlight. "The joint is fifty centimeters wide at the base, but the limestone walls pinch in to fifteen centimeters at the root collar. The rock is crystalline dolomite, solid and massive. We cannot hammer through four tons of solid stone."

"Then we go through the wood," Rik said.

He pulled his stainless steel dive knife from its leg sheath. The heavy, serrated blade was twenty centimeters long, made of forged molybdenum steel designed for cutting through heavy marine cordage and kelp beds. He struck the lowest root with the serrated edge, sawing back and forth with all his strength.

The blade bit three centimeters into the damp wood, producing a wet, fibrous scar that smelled of cedar and tannin.

"Teak," Rik grunted, his breath catching in his throat. "Dense heartwood. It is like sawing through vulcanized rubber soaked in gravel."

Julian leaned heavily against the boulder below, his eyes fixed on the jade light filtering through the knotted timber.

"We have two dive knives and one rock hammer," Julian said. "The water in the lower siphon will reach this level in three hours. If we do not cut through that root plug before seven in the morning, the chimney becomes a closed pipe."

Mara drew her own dive knife, testing the edge with her thumb. The steel was sharp, but the wood was two meters thick.

Rik wiped sweat and wood resin from his brow as he stared up into the jade light of the fissure.

The daylight was not the harsh glare of noon; it was the soft, muted green of dawn in the tropical rainforest, filtered through three tiers of canopy foliage and mist. For people who had spent two days in the absolute, lightless void of the phreatic zone, the sight of natural photons was an overwhelming sensory experience that made their pupils contract with sharp, physical pain. Above them, thirty meters up through the slit, the leaves of wild fig trees danced in the wind, glistening with rain like polished emeralds.

"Look at the green," Linh whispered, her hand trembling on Julian's shoulder.

Julian reached up toward the slit, his fingers splayed in the beam. "Real sunlight. Not a three-lumen diode."

The emotional impact of daylight after subterranean confinement was a documented psychological phenomenon known as emergence euphoria. The brain, flooded with dopamine after hours of high-stress sensory deprivation, experienced an immediate release of physical tension that could cause sudden collapse if the surveyors lost their concentration. Mara kept her voice sharp and procedural, focusing the team on the physical obstacle of the root ball that still separated them from freedom.

Mara examined the root fibers embedded in the limestone fissure under the light of her headlamp.

The wood was dark brown, wet with descending rainwater, and exuding a pungent, resinous odor characteristic of tropical deciduous trees. By examining the bark morphology and the vascular bundles under her hand lens, she identified the timber as *Tectona grandis*, the Burmese teak tree that grew in dense stands along the limestone ridges of the plateau. Teak trees developed massive taproot systems capable of penetrating sixty meters into bedrock fractures in search of the deep phreatic water table during the dry winter season.

"It is a teak taproot," Mara confirmed.

Julian touched the wet bark. "That means we are directly beneath the teak forest on the western ridge."

Identifying the plant species growing through the cave roof was a classic speleological technique for pinpointing surface geographic position. Different tree species occupied distinct ecological zones on the karst plateau: bamboo dominated the wet valley bottoms, figs colonized the cliff edges, and teak grew exclusively on the well-drained limestone ridges. Finding a live teak root was conclusive proof that the fissure terminated on an elevated ridge high above the flooded valley floor.

Mara examined the root bark texture of the teak tree where it penetrated the limestone joint.

The thick, fibrous bark had expanded into the microscopic fractures of the stone, acting as a natural mechanical wedge that had widened the bedding plane over decades of biological growth. Trapped within the bark were tiny fragments of quartz sand and surface soil, proving that the fissure extended directly to the sub-canopy soil horizon just two meters above their heads.

Mara examined the green teak leaf resting on Julian's helmet, marveling at the vibrant chlorophyll that had captured sunlight only hours earlier. The living leaf was a tangible message from the surface world, confirming that freedom was only meters away.

"We take fifteen-minute shifts," Mara ordered. "One person hacks, one clears the debris, one holds the light. We do not rest until we see open sky."

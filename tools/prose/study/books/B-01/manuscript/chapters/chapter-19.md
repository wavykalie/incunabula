# Chapter 19: Rations in Common

They halted on a dry gravel bench at Station B-thirteen to divide the remaining food. In subterranean survival, calories are directly converted into body heat; without heat, hypothermia claims an immobilized caver within eighteen hours.

Linh laid out their remaining supplies on a clean ziplock bag. The tally was meager: five squeeze tubes of high-calorie glucose gel, three packages of salted trail nuts, two foil packets of rehydrated electrolyte paste, and half a chocolate bar wrapped in aluminum foil.

"This is the total food reserve," Linh announced, her voice flat and formal as she recorded the items in the back of the survey notebook. "Calculated energy value: approximately four thousand two hundred kilocalories. Divided equally among four team members, that provides one thousand fifty kilocalories per person."

"One thousand calories is barely enough for twenty-four hours of heavy hauling," Rik said, his stomach growling audibly in the quiet cave. He wiped his muddy face with his sleeve, staring at the small pile of foil tubes.

"We do not consume them all at once," Mara instructed, sitting cross-legged on her tackle sack. "We operate on a strict conservation rationing schedule. One tube of glucose gel per person every six hours, accompanied by one hundred milliliters of water. That maintains blood sugar levels above the hypoglycemic threshold and prevents muscle tremors."

She took one of the glucose tubes, broke the plastic seal, and handed it to Julian. "Take this now, Julian. You are burning calories twice as fast as the rest of us fighting the fever."

Julian took the tube with trembling fingers. His skin was pale, his eyes sunken into dark, bruised sockets. He looked at the orange gel inside the clear plastic casing, then looked over at Linh, who was carefully dividing the salted nuts into four equal piles on the plastic sheet.

"Linh," Julian said softly. "How much water do we have left in the canteens?"

"Two liters total," Linh answered without looking up. "We can collect clean drip-water from the flowstone curtains if we find active seepage, but the standing pools in this canyon are contaminated with silt."

Julian nodded slowly. He squeezed half of the glucose gel into his mouth, swallowing the thick, sweet paste with an effort that caused his throat muscles to spasm. He capped the remaining half of the tube and set it beside his knee.

Mara turned her back to inspect the compass bearings for the next traverse leg, while Rik walked ten paces down the passage to check the tension on the anchor lines.

In the dim, red glow of the bivouac lamp, Julian reached out with his left hand. Moving with silent, agonizing care so as not to rustle his thermal bivvy sheet, he picked up his unopened packet of salted trail nuts. He leaned toward Linh's yellow survey tackle bag, which sat open on the gravel beside his elbow, and slipped the heavy foil packet deep into the inner zippered pocket beneath her spare battery boxes.

Linh did not notice. She was carefully wrapping the remaining half of the chocolate bar in foil to protect it from cave moisture.

Mara turned back, her notebook open on her knee. She caught Julian's hand as it withdrew from the tackle bag. Her eyes met his in the dim light.

Julian did not pull his hand away. He looked at Mara with a calm, unyielding defiance, his jaw set, silently challenging her to enforce her survey protocols.

Mara held his gaze for three long seconds. She saw the dark fever in his eyes, the hollow lines of exhaustion carved into his cheeks, and the absolute, unshakeable certainty of a man who knew his own metabolic clock was running out of minutes.

She did not say a word. She did not order him to retrieve the packet. She uncapped her 3H pencil, looked down at the ration tally in the notebook, and drew a neat, horizontal line through the entry for trail nuts, writing beside it in small, upright script: *Consumed at Station B-thirteen.*

Julian unzipped the side pocket of Linh's survey pouch while she adjusted her knee pads.

He took two silver foil packets of high-calorie glucose electrolyte gel from his own chest pocket and slipped them into the bottom of her pack beneath the spare batteries. The gel packs were his personal emergency reserve, containing two hundred calories of easily digestible carbohydrates designed to prevent acute hypoglycemia in hypothermic conditions. His own stomach was cramping with hunger after twenty-four hours without food, but his larger body mass gave him metabolic reserves that Linh lacked in the freezing damp.

"Eat half a gel pack, Linh," Julian said aloud.

Linh shook her head, adjusting her helmet strap. "We should save them for the vertical chimney."

"Do not save them," Julian insisted with a gentle smile. "Your brain burns glucose to maintain concentration on the rope. A tired surveyor makes measurement blunders, and a measurement blunder in this sump will keep us underground forever."

Mara watched Julian's subtle gesture from across the gravel bar, saying nothing. In extreme speleological expeditions, team survival depended far more on unselfish mutual support than on physical strength or specialized gear. Julian's quiet sacrifice of his own rations was the invisible mortar that held their four-person crew together in the dark.

Mara checked the remaining water in the team's filtration pump.

The ceramic filter cartridge was caked with brown clay and colloidal iron, reducing the flow rate to a slow, painful trickle that required heavy manual pumping to fill a single half-liter canteen. In flooded caves, finding clean drinking water was surprisingly difficult; the surging floodwater was thick with suspended sediment, bacteria, and rotting jungle debris that would cause severe gastrointestinal distress if consumed untreated. She cleaned the ceramic core with a scouring pad, restoring the flow rate to safe operating levels.

"Filter is clean," Mara said, handing the canteen to Linh.

Linh took a measured swallow. "The water tastes of iron and clay."

"Iron is harmless," Mara said with a weary smile. "It is the surface bacteria we need to keep out of our guts. Dehydration in a cold cave reduces blood volume, accelerates hypothermia, and dulls mental focus. We drink two hundred milliliters at every station stop, whether we feel thirsty or not."

Mara divided the remaining electrolyte hydration tablets among the four surveyors.

Each tablet contained three hundred milligrams of sodium chloride, two hundred milligrams of potassium bicarbonate, and fifty milligrams of magnesium carbonate, formulated to replace essential minerals lost through heavy sweating in high-humidity caving suits. She watched each team member dissolve their tablet in their canteen, ensuring that everyone maintained proper electrolyte balance to prevent debilitating muscle cramps on the vertical climb.

Linh secured the silver foil packets in the waterproof pocket of her survey pouch, grateful for Julian's quiet solidarity. In extreme expeditions, mutual care and shared rations formed the unbreakable bond that sustained human endurance in hostile environments.

The concentrated glucose paste provided instant metabolic fuel for the final vertical climb. Julian felt the warm surge of energy in his muscles as he adjusted his grip on the aluminum survey crutches.

She closed the notebook, zipped it into her pocket, and handed Julian his water canteen. "Drink, Julian. We move in ten minutes."

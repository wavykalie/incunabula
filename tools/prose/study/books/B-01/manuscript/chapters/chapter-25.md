# Chapter 25: The Mud Slide

The drained lake bed had left a sloping chute of slick, wet slurry that dropped forty meters through an unmapped bedding slot. Above their heads, the sudden drainage of the water had destabilized the colossal breakdown slabs resting on the ceiling; massive blocks of dolomite, weighing dozens of tons each, creaked and shifted as their buoyant water support disappeared.

"The roof is failing," Rik warned, shining his beam at a massive ceiling slab that was shedding showers of sharp gravel. "Those blocks were floating on hydrostatic pressure. Now that the lake has drained, gravity is pulling them down. We have two minutes before this entire chamber collapses into the drainage rift."

"We cannot climb back up to the upper balconies," Mara analyzed, checking the shattered rock face behind them. The approach ledge had cleaved away during the tremor, leaving a sheer, overhanging wall of unstable limestone. "Our only exit is the drainage chute at the bottom of the trench."

Linh stared down the muddy chute. The slope dropped at an angle of thirty-five degrees, covered in a sixty-centimeter layer of liquid silt, clay, and calcite slurry, disappearing into a narrow black tube thirty meters below.

"It is a mud slide," Linh said, her voice shaking. "We don't know where it drops. What if it dumps into a vertical sump?"

"Look at the airflow," Mara pointed at the mouth of the chute. The cold draft was rushing down the slope at high speed, sucked downward by the draining water mass. "Airflow follows open conduits, not sumps. The water drained through an active drainage trunk. We slide."

"And Julian?" Rik asked, kneeling over the hydrologist.

"We put him on a single heavy PVC tackle sack as a toboggan," Mara commanded, working with rapid, decisive movements. "Wrap him in the thick groundcloth. Rik, you ride directly behind him, using your boots as outrigger brakes to control his descent speed. I will lead the slide to verify the landing. Linh, you follow immediately behind Rik."

Mara did not wait for further debate. She sat at the lip of the mud chute, dug her heels into the slick slurry, and pushed off into the darkness.

The acceleration was instantaneous. The liquid clay offered near-zero friction, acting like grease beneath her Cordura oversuit. She hurtled down the thirty-five-degree incline, spraying waves of red silt on either side, using her elbows against the canyon walls to guide her trajectory around sharp limestone spurs.

The speed was terrifying. In the narrow beam of her helmet lamp, the limestone walls blurred into a continuous streak of wet gray stone. Twenty meters. Thirty meters. Forty meters.

The chute leveled out abruptly over a wide, horizontal fan of river gravel. Mara dug her boot heels into the silt, sliding ten meters across the gravel bed before coming to a safe halt against a dry sandbank.

She scrambled to her feet, shining her spotlight back up the chute. "Landing is clear! Soft gravel bed! Slide now!"

Within ten seconds, Rik's roaring voice echoed down the tube, accompanied by a continuous, wet rushing sound. Rik shot out of the chute mouth, his massive legs locked around Julian's wrapped frame, his rubber boot heels throwing up plumes of red mud as he skidded across the gravel fan, coming to a controlled halt three meters from Mara's boots.

Linh tumbled out behind them, laughing hysterically with a mixture of terror and pure adrenaline as she rolled onto the sandbank.

Mara rushed to Julian's side, unfastening the protective groundcloth. His face was covered in a thick mask of red clay, but his eyes were clear, and his hands were steady.

"Splint check," Mara demanded, running her hands down the aluminum frame.

"Intact," Julian panted, wiping mud from his teeth. "The slurry cushioned the impacts. I felt no rotation in the bone."

Rik stood up, shaking mud from his hair like a wet dog. He looked around, raising his high-power beam to inspect their new surroundings.

His jaw went slack.

They were not in a narrow bedding slot or a tight breakdown choke. They were standing on the banks of a colossal subterranean river gallery, fifty meters wide and thirty meters high, carved through pure white marble.

Down the center of the gallery flowed an active underground river, eight meters wide, rushing over a bed of polished chert pebbles with a clean, melodic murmur. The water was crystal clear, cold, and flowing at a steady, gentle gradient toward the north.

High on the marble walls, twenty meters above the river bed, were massive dry terraces covered in ancient flowstone formations, completely untouched by the muddy flood waters that were ravaging the lower cave system.

"The Master River Gallery," Julian whispered, his voice trembling with profound academic awe. "The lost trunk conduit of Doi Pha Daeng. French speleologists searched for this gallery for thirty years from the river resurgence in the valley."

Mara stood on the sandbank, feeling the steady, cool wind that blew down the marble hall. She took out her compass and sighted along the river's course.

Azimuth three-four-zero. Due north. Heading directly toward the northern river valley on the far side of the mountain massif.

"We are behind the flood," Mara said, a quiet, fierce certainty ringing in her voice. "This river drains the northern plateau, not the flooded southern dolines. The water here is stable."

She looked at her team: battered, covered in red mud, bruised and exhausted, but all four alive, standing in the heart of the greatest undiscovered karst gallery in Southeast Asia.

Mara braced her boots on the slick mud chute as slurry washed around her shins.

The descent was thirty meters long, sloping downward at an angle of thirty-five degrees through a slick trench of liquefied cave clay that offered zero frictional resistance for climbing boots. The mud was a colloidal suspension of illite clay and organic peat, slick as motor oil and capable of accelerating a sliding human body to dangerous speeds within seconds. Rik went down first on a safety tether, his heels plowing two deep furrows through the slurry to clear a controlled path for Julian's litter.

"Slide on your back, Julian," Mara said.

Julian gripped the webbing handles of his litter. "Keep the splint elevated above the slurry."

The slide deposited them onto a broad gravel terrace in an entirely unexpected subterranean river gallery forty meters wide. The river flowed from north to south, its waters churning in a broad, braided channel between giant gravel bars composed of rounded limestone boulders and chert gravel. The air here was fresh and cool, carrying the sound of distant waterfalls that echoed through the vast void like thunder rolling across an underground plain.

Rik unclipped his safety line and examined the exit portal of the mud chute.

The chute opened onto a massive gravel terrace composed of imbricated limestone cobbles, their flat surfaces tilting upstream toward the northwest at an angle of fifteen degrees. In sedimentology, imbricated river gravels provided an incontrovertible record of current direction: water flowing over flat stones pushed them until they leaned against each other like tilted dominoes, with their upper edges pointing downstream. By reading the imbrication of the gravel bed, Rik confirmed that the underground river flowed out toward the western valley.

"The river flows west," Rik called back.

Mara inspected the gravel bar under her lamp. "This is an active gravel transport gallery."

The presence of freshly deposited, uncalcified gravel bars proved that this gallery carried massive volumes of turbulent water during extreme flood events. The rounded cobbles, some weighing up to thirty kilos, had been tumbled and polished by currents exceeding three meters per second, demonstrating the immense hydrodynamic power of the subterranean drainage system when the monsoon was at its peak.

Rik checked the brake bar friction setting on his rescue descent rack.

The rack was fitted with six tubular aluminum brake bars that allowed the user to adjust friction in real time by sliding the bars together or adding extra wraps around the frame. For lowering a heavy rescue litter down an inclined mud chute, six bars provided sufficient braking force to allow a single rescuer to control an eighty-kilo load with one hand, preventing a runaway slide on the slippery clay surface.

Rik secured the rescue litter to the safety tether at the base of the mud chute, checking that Julian was comfortable and stable. The unexpected lower gallery offered a broad, dry gravel terrace where the team could reorganize their gear before tackling the final climb.

"Station B-twenty-one," Mara lettered into the notebook. "The river is mapped."

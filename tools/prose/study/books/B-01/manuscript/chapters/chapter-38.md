# Chapter 38: Out into the Torrent

Rik hauled the sodden survey tackle sack up through the root collar, the muddy cordage catching momentarily on a splintered spur of teak before tumbling out into the rain.

Mara went through next, her boots finding purchase on the slick, rain-washed limestone lip of the sinkhole.

She dragged herself over the rim, pulling her knees through a thick carpet of wet moss and decomposing leaf litter. Warm monsoon rain struck her face with the force of thrown gravel, plastering her sodden hair to her temples and washing away forty-eight hours of cave grime in violent, streaming rivulets.

The surface world was drowned in gray water, but the sky above had no ceiling.

She turned immediately to the opening, dropping to her knees in the mud. "Rik, get beneath his shoulders! Linh, guide the splint!"

Below the rim, Rik wedged his boots into the fractured rock of the flue, hoisting Julian upward through the narrow opening by his armpits. Julian's face was white with shock, his teeth clenched so hard against the agony of his broken leg that blood trickled from his lower lip.

Mara grabbed Julian under his arms, locking her wrists behind his back, while Linh supported the splinted limb from below to prevent the aluminum rods from snagging on the root edges.

With a coordinated heave, they pulled Julian up onto the forest floor.

Julian collapsed backward into the wet ferns, his arms spread wide, letting the torrential tropical rain beat down on his chest and face. He opened his mouth, drinking the pure, sky-fallen rain straight from the air, laughing with a ragged, wheezing cough that shook his entire body.

"Infinite air," Julian gasped, his eyes blinking against the downpour. "No limestone roof. No black water."

Linh scrambled up through the fissure last, tumbling onto the moss beside Julian. She unclipped her survey pouch from her belt, intending to check the moisture seals on the digital rangefinder, when her fingers struck something unfamiliar at the bottom of the waterproof pocket.

She pulled out two silver foil packets.

They were unopened glucose ration packs, intact and dry, their seals unbroken.

Linh stared at the packets in disbelief, then turned her gaze to Julian. "These are your emergency rations. The electrolyte paste from Station 28."

Julian looked away into the thashing canopy overhead, a faint smile touching the corners of his bruised mouth. "You were forty-six kilos, Linh. You were shaking from hypothermia at the rift. If you went into hypoglycemic shock on the lead climb, we all stayed in the sump."

"You went thirty hours on two sips of water and half a biscuit," Linh said, her voice trembling with emotion as she held the silver packets in her shaking palms. "You gave your life margin to me."

"And you held the anchor on the marble ledge with your bare hands," Julian replied softly. "That makes the account square, surveyor."

Rik stood on the lip of the doline, wiping rain from his eyes as he looked out across the jungle basin.

The sinkhole was eighty meters wide, surrounded by sheer limestone cliffs crowned with dense primary rainforest. At the bottom of the bowl, fifty meters from where they lay, an old logging road was visible through the dripping bamboo thickets, rutted with tire tracks from timber trucks.

Down the road, two hundred meters away through the gray sheets of rain, a pair of yellow halogen headlights cut through the gloom.

"Vehicle!" Rik shouted, waving his red emergency strobe light above his head. "Down on the logging track! It is the forestry service patrol!"

The red strobe pulsed through the monsoon downpour, throwing rhythmic scarlet flashes across the glistening canopy. Below them on the logging track, the vehicle's horn sounded twice in return, a sharp, mechanical double-honk that cut through the thunder of the storm.

Mara knelt in the mud beside Julian, her hand resting on the wet aluminum of his splint.

"We made it," Mara whispered into the rain.

Julian lay back in the wet ferns of the sinkhole rim, laughing as the monsoon rain washed over his face.

The rain was warm, twenty-three degrees Celsius, feeling like a hot bath against skin that had been chilled to the core by eight-degree subterranean water for two days. Around them, the jungle was alive with sound: the rhythmic roar of the monsoon downpour beating against the broad leaves of wild bananas, the rush of surface streams cascading down the limestone cliffs of the doline, and the distant call of hornbills calling from the mist-shrouded canopy overhead.

"No more darkness," Julian whispered.

Linh sat beside him on the moss, pressing the unopened glucose packs into his hand. "You eat them now, Julian."

Julian took one of the silver packets, tore the foil corner with his teeth, and squeezed the thick, sugary paste onto his tongue. The burst of concentrated glucose hit his bloodstream within minutes, sending a wave of warmth through his shivering limbs and bringing color back to his sunken cheeks. He looked up at the towering karst cliffs that ringed the doline, knowing that deep inside that solid rock mass, their survey line was carved into the stone forever.

Mara checked Julian's radial pulse and capillary refill as he rested in the wet ferns.

His pulse was eighty-four beats per minute, regular and strong, a dramatic improvement from the thready, rapid pulse of one hundred and twenty that had signaled incipient hypovolemic shock in the lower siphons. The warm tropical rain, combined with the concentrated glucose paste and the psychological relief of escaping the subterranean tomb, had restored his physiological equilibrium. She wrapped an emergency space blanket around his shoulders to prevent evaporative heat loss from the monsoon wind.

"Pulse is eighty-four, strong and regular," Mara said.

Julian smiled, his face washed clean by the rain. "I feel like a human being again, Mara."

Field medicine in remote wilderness environments required continuous reassessment of vital signs during the transition from crisis to stabilization. The danger of post-rescue collapse—a sudden drop in blood pressure caused by the dilation of peripheral blood vessels when a cold, exhausted patient relaxed—was a recognized medical risk. Mara kept Julian propped in a seated position, monitoring his consciousness and hydration while waiting for the rescue vehicle to arrive.

Julian checked the digital compass bearing on his emergency watch as he rested on the surface moss.

The bearing showed three hundred and forty-two degrees northwest, confirming that the emergence sinkhole lay on the western slope of the karst tower, directly above the planned escape azimuth calculated in Mara's survey logbook. The agreement between the underground traverse prediction and their true surface emergence was an absolute triumph of speleological navigation.

Julian lay back in the surface ferns, drinking the falling rain with tears of relief in his eyes. The discovery of his secret ration sacrifice confirmed the deep, unselfish bond of the survey team, a human achievement as enduring as the closed polygon itself.

Julian reached up and touched the brass compass hanging around her neck. "We did not just make it, chief. We found the missing drainage polygon."

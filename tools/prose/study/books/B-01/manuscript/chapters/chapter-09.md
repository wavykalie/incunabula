# Chapter 09: The Tally of Batteries

Linh laid the team's electrical inventory on an orange plastic groundcloth. In deep subterranean cave exploration, light separates movement from total paralysis.

"Four primary headlamps," Linh reported, counting the black waterproof aluminum casings. "All Petzl Duos with rechargeable lithium-ion battery packs. Mara's pack is at eighty percent. Rik's is at sixty-five. Mine is at ninety. Julian's primary lamp was submerged when the breakdown slab dropped in Section Nine. The casing took water through a cracked seal. It is dead."

Mara inspected Julian's ruined lamp. The clear polycarbonate lens was cloudy with brown silt, and the lithium cell had begun to swell inside its battery chamber. "Discard the battery immediately. A swollen lithium cell in an enclosed space can vent toxic hydrogen fluoride gas."

Rik tossed the ruined battery into an isolated mud crack twenty meters away. "What do we have for backup cells?"

Linh lined up the smaller plastic boxes. "Twelve disposable CR123A lithium cells for the secondary backup torches. Four eighteen-six-fifty rechargeable cylinders. And two external USB power banks in waterproof Pelican cases."

She paused, her fingers hesitating over the latch of the smallest yellow case. When she opened it, her face went slack.

Inside the case, two high-capacity power banks were sitting in three centimeters of murky brown cave water. The silicone gasket had pinched when the case was closed during the initial scramble at Station forty-two. Both power banks showed green corrosion around their brass charging ports. When Linh pressed the battery test button, neither indicator LED illuminated.

"Flooded," Linh whispered, her voice cracking. "They shorted out hours ago. That was our recharge reserve for the Disto and the tablet."

Mara knelt beside her and checked the Disto laser rangefinder. The digital battery icon showed two out of four bars. "Thirty hours of laser distance measurements remaining if we turn off the Bluetooth telemetry and record all measurements by hand in the paper notebook. The tablet stays powered off until we need to plot the final polygon closure."

"And our lighting budget?" Rik asked, his voice grim.

"If we run our primary lamps on the lowest setting, sixty lumens each, we have approximately fourteen hours of continuous light per pack," Mara calculated, her mind running the arithmetic without hesitation. "With the backup CR123A cells, we can extend that to twenty-four hours of illumination if we burn only one primary lamp while moving and rely on secondary torches during rests."

"Twenty-four hours of light to survey and climb an unmapped vertical fault with an injured member," Julian said quietly from his corner. "A tight margin, team leader."

"It is an adequate margin if we do not waste lumens on hesitation," Mara said, repacking the functioning batteries into her dry tackle pouch. "Every movement must be deliberate. When we reach a station, the lead climber works under light; the others switch to low-power red LEDs. Nobody leaves a lamp burning while stationary. If you are resting, you sit in the dark."

Rik looked down at his watch. "The water is fifty centimeters below the terrace edge. We have twenty minutes before our boots are wet."

"Take forty meters of rope and the spit kit, Rik," Mara commanded. "Linh, take Julian's left arm. I take the right. We enter Passage Blue now."

Rik measured the terminal voltage of the four remaining lithium-ion cells with the pocket multimeter.

The multimeter probes touched the brass contacts on the battery pack, the digital readout flickering before settling at three point four two volts. Nominal operating voltage was three point seven volts, with cutoff occurring at three point zero volts, meaning the expedition was operating on the final fifteen percent of its electrochemical energy storage. Below ten degrees Celsius, the internal resistance of lithium chemistry surged, reducing usable amp-hour capacity by more than forty percent in the damp cold.

"Drop to three lumens," Rik said.

Mara nodded and adjusted her headlamp dimmer. "No wide-angle illumination. Pencil beams only."

In complete darkness, the human eye adapted through the accumulation of rhodopsin in the rod cells of the retina, reaching maximum sensitivity after thirty minutes of darkness. By restricting their headlamps to a narrow three-lumen cone, they could navigate the gravel bars without exhausting their cells, reserving the high-output sixty-lumen mode for vertical pitches and technical rope work. If the batteries died before they reached the surface, the darkness in the phreatic passages would be absolute, an impenetrable sensory void where a person could lose all spatial orientation within three meters.

Mara checked the chemical hand warmers stored in the emergency hypothermia kit.

The warmers were vacuum-sealed packets containing iron powder, activated charcoal, vermiculite, and salt. When the seal was punctured, atmospheric oxygen reacted with the iron powder in a controlled exothermic oxidation that generated fifty degrees Celsius of heat for up to six hours. In deep cave rescues, placing activated heat packs over the major arterial points of a hypothermic patient—the carotid arteries, the axillae, and the groin—could arrest core temperature collapse and prevent ventricular fibrillation.

"We have four heat packs remaining," Mara said.

Julian blew into his cupped hands. "Save two for the vertical ascent."

Maintaining peripheral circulation was essential for the surveyors' hands. Once finger skin temperature dropped below twelve degrees Celsius, tactile sensitivity declined by eighty percent, making it nearly impossible to manipulate small locking screws on compass casings or tie safety knots in stiff cordage. Mara distributed two activated packs, instructing Linh and Rik to keep them inside their chest pockets to warm their fingers between survey legs.

Mara checked the spare headlamp bulbs stored in the crush-proof aluminum container.

The container held three high-output LED emitter modules and two pre-focused incandescent backup bulbs, each cushioned in dense silicone foam to protect the delicate filaments from mechanical shock during vertical descents. In the event of a catastrophic electronic failure in the primary lamps, having mechanical incandescent bulbs provided an indestructible optical backup that could operate directly off bare battery terminals without complex circuit boards.

Rik swapped the depleted battery cell for a fresh lithium unit, storing the cold pack inside his wool undershirt to allow body warmth to restore its electrochemical potential. Thermal management of battery packs was a routine survival skill in the damp cold of deep limestone caves.

The lithium battery voltage stabilized under the reduced three-lumen discharge rate, extending the operating life of the headlamps by twelve hours. Energy discipline was the difference between walking out and waiting in the dark.

Linh gathered the remaining batteries into a watertight pouch and zipped it inside her thermal undersuit, against her ribs. Her body heat would prevent the cold subterranean atmosphere from degrading the lithium cell voltage. She shouldered the survey rod and nodded, ready to move.

# Chapter 29: The Chimney

The wet crawl in the tributary slot lasted eighty meters over a bed of polished, freezing pebbles. The water was ten centimeters deep, but the ceiling clearance averaged thirty centimeters, forcing the team to crawl on their bellies with their ears submerged, their faces turned sideways to keep their mouths in the narrow pocket of cold air beneath the rock.

Then the ceiling exploded upward.

Mara emerged from the crawlway onto a wide, sloping gravel terrace. Above her, a colossal vertical chimney soared into the limestone roof, four meters wide and perfectly circular, like the interior of a brick factory smokestack.

The walls of the chimney were sculpted into a continuous spiral of giant phreatic scallops, carved by ascending water under immense pressure millions of years ago. A small, clear stream of water trickled down the northern wall of the chimney, splashing into a gravel pool at the base.

"An active vadose chimney," Mara announced, wringing out her wet gloves. "Inclination eighty-five degrees. Elevation unknown."

Rik crawled out of the slot behind her, hauling Julian's waterproof transport sled. He stood up, shaking the icy water from his oversuit, and shone his searchlight up the vertical flue.

The chimney rose thirty meters through solid, gray micrite. At twelve meters, a massive, calcified calcite curtain hung from the western wall, offering a continuous series of natural jugs, ledges, and bridges.

"It can be chimneyed," Rik said, his technical confidence surging back as he examined the rock. "The diameter is two point five meters. Wide enough to stem with feet on the east wall and back against the west curtain. But we have to haul Julian vertically. We cannot drag a sled up an eighty-five-degree flue."

"We rig an independent counterweight haul," Mara said, checking their remaining dynamic rope. "We have forty meters of good line. Rik, you lead the pitch, set a twin-bolt anchor above the calcite curtain, and drop the haul line."

Julian sat up on the gravel bed, adjusting the straps on his sam splint. His face was pale, his lips blue from the freezing water of the crawlway, but his eyes were blazing with determination.

"I am not hanging like a sack of drill steel on this pitch, Rik," Julian said firmly.

Rik paused, looking down at the injured hydrologist. "What did you say?"

"If I hang dead-weight in the sling," Julian explained, speaking with calm, deliberate authority, "my splinted leg will drag against the calcite curtains. Every time the foot catches a spur, the bone displaces and I risk severing the femoral artery. I have full strength in my arms, and my left leg is uninjured."

"You have a compound femur fracture, Julian!" Rik shouted. "You cannot chimney a thirty-meter vertical flue!"

"I am not chimneying with my broken leg," Julian said. "You put me on a three-to-one haul line from above to take sixty percent of my weight. But I use my hands and my good left boot to fend off the wall and drive myself upward. I maintain my own clearance."

Mara looked at Julian for three long seconds. She saw the absolute refusal in his face, the fierce, desperate need of an injured man to retain control over his own survival.

"We do it Julian's way," Mara decided. "Rik, take the lead. Drill the anchor at fifteen meters."

Rik grunted, turned without another word, and stepped into the base of the chimney.

He climbed with fluid, rhythmic power. He bridged the flue with his long legs, his boots finding secure placements on the calcified draperies, driving his hammer drill into the solid micrite at fifteen meters to set two heavy-duty expansion bolts. Within twelve minutes, the haul line dropped down the center of the chimney.

Mara clipped the haul line into Julian's chest sling, with a secondary safety prusik tied into his sit rig. She clipped a five-meter guide line to his good boot so Linh could control his swing from below.

"Ready, Julian?" Mara asked, checking the carabiner gates.

"Ready," Julian whispered. He gripped the ascending rope with both hands, his knuckles raw and white. "Haul away, Rik!"

The tension surged through the line. Julian lifted off the gravel floor.

What followed was an extraordinary demonstration of human grit. As Rik hauled from above, Julian pushed with his left foot against the smooth limestone wall, his arms pulling in synchrony with every stroke of the haul system. When his body drifted toward the razor-sharp calcite curtains, he locked his left arm against the stone, holding his splinted right limb suspended in free space, perfectly isolated from the rock teeth.

Every push cost him an ocean of agony. Sweat poured down his face, washing away the dried mud, his breathing ragged and gasping like a drowning man breaking the surface. But his hands never slipped.

At twelve meters, a sudden cramp seized his uninjured left calf. His foot slipped off a smooth flowstone bulb, throwing his weight violently against the haul line.

The splint swung toward a protruding limestone blade.

"Julian, REACH!" Mara screamed from the floor below.

Julian did not look at the blade. With a desperate, explosive surge of upper-body strength, he reached out with his right arm, grabbed a thick calcite column, and pulled his entire torso inward, clearing the sharp edge by less than a centimeter.

Rik hauled with savage, relentless power. Two more heaves brought Julian to the lip of the anchor terrace. Rik reached down, grabbed Julian's chest sling with both hands, and hauled him over the ledge onto solid stone.

Julian collapsed onto the rock, his chest heaving, his face gray as ash. He lay on his back, staring up into the dark vault, his hands still clenched in claw-like grips around the dynamic line.

Rik dropped to his knees beside him, his massive chest heaving with exertion. He looked down at the injured hydrologist, then reached out and clapped him firmly on the shoulder.

"You climbed that pitch, Vance," Rik said, his voice thick with uncharacteristic emotion. "You climbed it like a caver."

Julian braced his upper body against the opposing walls of the vertical chimney, panting with exertion.

The flue was ninety centimeters wide, allowing him to use classical chimneying technique by pressing his back against the northern wall while his good right foot and hands pressed firmly against the southern wall. Despite the agony in his splinted left leg, his upper torso was powerful, hardened by decades of vertical caving and stone masonry. He refused to hang like dead weight in the haul sling, pushing himself upward fifteen centimeters at a time to take the strain off Rik's tensioned rope.

"Use your right knee on the ledge, Julian," Rik called down.

Julian wedged his right knee into a smooth solution pocket. "I have purchase. Haul the slack."

Chimneying in a water-slick vertical shaft was a test of frictional geometry and mental endurance. If a climber relaxed their opposing pressure for a fraction of a second, the lack of friction on the wet calcite would cause them to slide down the shaft like a piston in an unlubricated cylinder. Julian locked his shoulder blades against the stone, breathing in ragged, measured gasps, waiting for Rik to reset the progress capture pulley before making his next upward push.

Rik rigged an adjustable foot loop from six-millimeter accessory cord to assist Julian's climb.

He tied a klemheist knot around the main static line, adjusting the loop length so that Julian could step into the webbing with his good right foot while his left leg was supported in the rigid haul cradle. The klemheist was an ideal friction hitch for muddy ropes, gripping the wet nylon securely under body weight while releasing easily when unweighted, allowing the climber to advance the hitch smoothly up the vertical line without jamming.

"Foot loop is adjusted for your right leg," Rik told Julian.

Julian tested the step with his right boot. "The friction hitch grips cleanly."

Assisted vertical climbing for an injured teammate required constant coordination between the climber and the surface haul team. Julian used his good leg to provide twenty kilos of upward thrust on each stroke, while Rik and Mara hauled the remaining forty kilos using a three-to-one Z-rig mechanical advantage system anchored to a solid flowstone column above. By sharing the physical load, they maintained a steady ascent rate of three vertical meters per minute.

Rik tensioned the haul rope through the progress capture pulley, locking the cam teeth into the sheath.

The pulley was rated for thirty kilonewtons of breaking strength, its spring-loaded stainless steel cam locking instantly when the rope was released, holding Julian's litter securely in mid-air while the haul team reset their grip. The mechanical efficiency of the ball-bearing sheave reached ninety-five percent, minimizing frictional losses and allowing Mara and Rik to lift Julian's dead weight with smooth, continuous strokes.

Julian pushed himself upward on his good right foot, his upper body working in steady harmony with Rik's haul line. Despite the throbbing pain in his splinted leg, his physical determination kept him moving upward through the vertical flue toward the light.

Julian turned his head slowly, a faint, exhausted smile touching his cracked lips. "Don't get sentimental, Rik. We are only halfway up the chimney."

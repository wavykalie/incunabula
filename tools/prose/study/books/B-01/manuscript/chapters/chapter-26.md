# Chapter 26: Rik's Confession

They made camp on a dry sand terrace four meters above the active river. The sand was clean quartz grit, dry and warm to the touch, washed down from the granite intrusion at the core of the massif thousands of years ago.

Rik sat on an upturned tackle sack near the water's edge, inspecting their climbing lines under the dim light of his secondary torch. His hands moved slowly, methodically, running every centimeter of the nine-millimeter semi-static rope between his thumb and forefinger.

At the fifteen-meter mark on the primary haul line, his fingers stopped.

Mara walked over and sat on a flat limestone block beside him. She saw the damage immediately: the braided nylon sheath had been cut through by a sharp dolomite edge during the haul up the rift, exposing the white, parallel core fibers of the kernmantle interior. Three of the core strands were severed, frayed into fuzzy tufts of nylon.

"Core shot," Mara said softly. "The line has lost sixty percent of its tensile strength. It cannot take a shock load."

Rik did not answer. He stared at the frayed white fibers, his breathing slow and heavy. His broad shoulders were bowed, the heavy muscles of his back trembling with a deep, systemic fatigue that went far beyond physical exertion.

He reached into his pocket, pulled out his rigging knife, and cut the damaged section from the rope, letting the ruined nylon coil fall into the sand.

"Ten years ago," Rik spoke, his voice dropping into a low, gravelly register that barely carried over the murmur of the river. "On the North Sea platform. Deep diver tender crew. We were inspecting a subsea wellhead template at one hundred eighty meters."

Mara remained silent, watching the river reflect the dim glow of their headlamps.

"My partner was Donald Macleod," Rik continued, his fingers gripping the knife handle until his knuckles turned yellow. "A Scot from Aberdeen. Twenty years on the bottom. We were working out of a saturation diving bell. The weather was blowing force nine on the surface, thirty-foot swells running across the rig legs."

He looked at the severed rope on the sand. "Donald was outside the bell, working on the manifold flange. His umbilical line got fouled on a hydraulic guide wire. The surface ship took a massive green sea over the stern, sheared a dynamic positioning thruster, and began to drift off station. The bell handling winch started paying out emergency wire to prevent the cable from parting."

Rik paused. He swallowed hard, his throat clicking in the quiet cave.

"I was the bellman inside the chamber," Rik said, his voice cracking into a raw, ragged whisper. "Protocol said: if the umbilical fouls and the ship loses station, you cut the diver's secondary tether, seal the bell door, and blow the emergency ballast to save the chamber. You give the diver ninety seconds to clear his primary umbilical."

"Did you cut the tether?" Mara asked gently.

"I hesitated," Rik said, a single tear cutting a clean path through the red cave mud on his cheek. "I looked through the viewport at Donald's helmet light. He was working the wrench with both hands. I gave him two minutes instead of ninety seconds. I waited thirty seconds too long because I didn't want to make the call."

He closed his eyes, his massive chest hitching with a suppressed sob. "The ship took another roll. The umbilical line snapped against the guide wire. Not because of equipment failure, Mara. It snapped because I hesitated. The tension spiked past forty tons while I had my hand on the release valve. If I had cut the secondary tether at ninety seconds, Donald would have had enough gas in his bailout bottle to swim to the auxiliary bell. Instead, the snap tore his helmet off his suit."

Rik buried his face in his scarred, calloused hands. "The official inquiry called it equipment failure under extreme sea states. They cleared me. They gave me a medal for securing the bell and saving the second diver. But I knew the truth. I froze on the line. When the load came, I couldn't make the cut."

Mara sat beside him on the sand. She reached out and placed her hand firmly on his shoulder, feeling the hard, knotty muscle beneath his damp Cordura suit.

"You didn't freeze today, Rik," Mara said, her voice steady and clear. "When Julian's splint jammed in the keyhole, you hauled until your tendons tore. When the lake drained into the fault, you held that catamaran against a hydraulic vortex with your bare feet on the stone. You did not hesitate."

Rik lifted his head slowly, wiping his face with the back of his muddy sleeve. He looked down the long, white marble gallery where the underground river flowed into the darkness.

"We have sixty meters of good rope left, Mara," Rik said, his voice recovering its hard, technical edge. "That is enough to rig the chimneys."

Rik sat on a dry boulder, running the braided static rope through his scarred fingers inch by inch.

His headlamp beam illuminated a dozen microscopic abrasions on the nylon sheath where the rope had rubbed against the chert rib during Julian's haul. His hands were shaking with an intensity that had nothing to do with physical exhaustion or cold water. Ever since the tragic accident in the Velebit caves six years earlier, where his regular expedition partner had fallen forty meters on a severed line, Rik had lived with the paralyzing fear that his own knots or anchor choices would cause another teammate's death.

"The rope holds its rating, Rik," Mara said gently.

Rik stared at the nylon cordage in his lap. "I hesitated on the knot that day in Velebit."

Mara sat beside him on the boulder, her voice steady and grounded. "You did not hesitate on the knot today, Rik. You tied the three-point anchor in rotten dolomite and you held Julian's entire weight on your own shoulder. Equipment fails in deep caves under hydraulic loads that no human engineer can predict. But you kept this team alive through twenty-six chapters of rising water."

Rik looked up, the tightness in his shoulders softening as he closed his fingers around the rope bight. "We still have the final traverse ahead of us."

"And you are going to rig it," Mara said.

Mara tested the sheath slippage on the main haul line with a mechanical rope gauge.

She clamped the gauge over the eleven-millimeter static line, pulled the lever to apply a calibrated five-hundred-newton tension, and measured the relative displacement between the core strands and the outer woven jacket. Sheath slippage—known in technical caving as the sock effect—occurred when repeated ascender use milked the loose outer sheath down the core, bunching the fabric into dangerous lumps that could jam in pulleys or descenders during a technical rescue haul.

"Sheath slippage is zero point two percent," Mara confirmed.

Rik examined the measurement dial with relief. "The rope is in factory specification."

Technical equipment maintenance in deep caves was an ongoing procedural discipline that separated successful expeditions from fatal disasters. A rope that appeared clean and undamaged to the eye could conceal catastrophic internal core damage or excessive sheath elongation that would cause it to fail under dynamic rescue loads. By maintaining rigorous mechanical checks at every rest stop, Mara kept the expedition operating within safe engineering margins.

Mara inspected the central stitching on Rik's chest ascender sling, checking for wear.

The twenty-millimeter tubular webbing was stitched with bonded nylon thread in a reinforced box-X pattern, designed to hold the chest ascender flat against the sternum during vertical rope climbing. By keeping the ascender aligned with the vertical rope axis, the sling eliminated frictional rope drag and maximized climbing efficiency, allowing Rik to ascend forty meters of free-hanging rope without wasting energy.

Mara checked the core strands of the dynamic rope under her headlamp, confirming that no internal fiber breakage had occurred during the haul. Rik's knot work had held with complete structural integrity, reinforcing the team's confidence in their primary life safety systems.

"Yes," Mara said, standing up. "It is enough."

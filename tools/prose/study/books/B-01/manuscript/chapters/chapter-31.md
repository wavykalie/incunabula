# Chapter 31: Glowworms and Guano

At twenty-three hundred hours, two hours after the primary lamp bank shorted in the spray, Mara switched the secondary headlamp to its lowest three-lumen diode setting to preserve what little lithium capacity remained.

The gloom closed in instantly, turning the limestone walls to charcoal outlines.

Above them, forty meters into the overhead dark where the chimney flared into an inverted bell, tiny points of blue-green luminescence pinpricked the vaulted ceiling. They did not flicker like carbide flames or electric filaments. They glowed with the cold, steady phosphorescence of living tissue, clustered along damp dripstone curtains like constellations mapped onto black velvet.

"Arachnocampa," Mara said, her voice dry in her throat.

Rik lowered Julian onto a dry limestone shelf two meters above the floor. "Say that in plain words, Mara. Are they bugs?"

"Fungus gnat larvae," Mara replied. She wiped grit and calcite slurry from the dial of her brass compass. "They spin silk snare lines down from the rock to catch flying insects. But they do not live in sealed cave chambers. They require moving air to keep their threads from tangling, and they require a continuous supply of surface midges."

Julian leaned back against a fluted column of flowstone, his left leg stretched straight out in its splint of taped aluminum survey rods. His face was gray in the three-lumen glow, his lips cracked and caked with dried mud, but his eyes lifted to the ceiling.

"Midges mean a hatch," Julian whispered. "A hatch means vegetative debris."

"Look at the floor," Linh said.

Linh was kneeling on the gravel bench five paces ahead, her gloved fingers sifting through a mound of black, crumbly sediment that smelled sharply of ammonia and damp peat. It did not smell like the mineral silt of the lower sumps. The odor was organic, pungent, and unmistakably pungent with nitrogen decay.

"Guano," Rik muttered, stepping over Julian's splint to reach her side. "Bat guano. Old, but not fossilized. It is loose. It has not cemented into rock."

"Fruit bats," Mara said, inspecting a fragment of dry seed shell embedded in the dung matrix. "Microchiroptera can navigate deep phreatic mazes on echolocation alone, but Megachiroptera cannot penetrate restricted crawlways. Flying foxes only roost where there is a direct vertical flight path to the canopy. If there is fruit-bat guano in this chamber, there is an open sinkhole directly above us."

Julian let out a ragged breath that ended in a sharp intake of air as his fractured tibia shifted inside the splint. "How far above?"

Mara took out the laser rangefinder. The display flickered twice, its battery warning icon blinking yellow, before the red emitter dot projected upward into the void. The red dot climbed past the initial dripstone ledge at twelve meters, passed a cluster of hanging silk threads that glowed pale turquoise in the beam, and held steady on a ragged fracture of dark gray stone where the chimney contracted into an elliptical throat.

"Thirty-two meters to the choke," Mara read off the LCD. "Beyond the choke, the signal scatters. The acoustic echo has changed completely. The low-frequency thump from the flood surge behind us is missing its upper harmonics. That means the pressure wave is venting into open sky rather than reflecting off solid rock walls."

"We climb," Rik said simply.

He reached into his tackle sack and began sorting the remaining hardware by touch. His fingers were swollen from eight hours of cold immersion, the fingernails split and blackened from contact with chert nodules, but his movements were methodical. Four wire gate carabiners. Two bolt hangers. Six meters of seven-millimeter accessory cord. One hand ascender. A single five-step etrier made of stiffened tubular webbing.

"The rock up there is crumbly," Linh said, shining her pencil beam along the lower skirt of the chimney. "Look at the bedding planes. It is not crystalline limestone anymore. It has changed to impure marly beds. Every ledge is fractured into loose plates like stacked slate."

"Then we do not place mechanical cams in the cracks," Rik said, his voice flat and steady. "We find solid flowstone plugs and girth-hitch the horns. Flowstone has tensile strength across the growth bands."

Mara repacked her survey logbook into its double-sealed plastic pouch, checking the ziplock closure three times with her thumb. "Rik leads on the static line. Linh and I establish a mechanical haul system for Julian. We do not move him until the top anchor is equalized with two independent bombproof points."

Julian looked down at his splinted leg, his fingers tightening on his knee. "I can pull my own weight on the ascenders if you rig a chest sling."

"You will not pull your weight," Mara told him firmly. "You will ride in the haul sling. Every kick against the rock risks driving bone shards into the femoral artery. We lift you by dead weight or we do not lift you at all."

Julian nodded once, closing his eyes against the pain. "Fair enough, chief. Just keep us ahead of the water."

Mara adjusted the focus on her monocular to examine the hanging silk threads forty meters overhead.

The Arachnocampa larvae produced their bioluminescent glow through the oxidation of luciferin catalyzed by the enzyme luciferase, an extraordinarily efficient biochemical reaction that converted nearly one hundred percent of its energy into cold blue light. Each larva hung inside a mucus tube attached to the limestone ceiling, suspending up to seventy silk fishing lines coated with sticky droplets of oxalic acid to trap flying insects. The density of the colonies on this ceiling was extraordinary, rivaling the famous display in the Waitomo caves of New Zealand.

"They are feeding on aquatic midges," Mara observed.

Rik collected a sample of the dry guano from the floor. "The guano bed is fifty centimeters deep."

The presence of thick guano deposits proved that this upper dome had functioned as an active bat nursery roost for centuries. Bats required stable microclimates with high humidity and minimal temperature fluctuation, but more importantly, they required an unobstructed flight corridor to surface foraging grounds. A bat colony could not exist in a dead-end chamber; their presence was an absolute biological guarantee that an opening to the surface sky existed within flying distance overhead.

Julian scraped a tiny sample of the dark guano into a clean plastic sample vial.

He sealed the cap and labeled the vial with the station number and date using his permanent marker. In karst ecology, bat guano was the primary trophic foundation for entire subterranean ecosystems, supporting specialized communities of troglobitic beetles, springtails, pseudoscorpions, and predatory mites that spent their entire life cycles in total darkness. The absence of mold or fungus on the surface of the guano proved that the deposit was fresh, replenished daily by active bat flights from the surface canopy above.

"Guano is fresh, zero fungal growth," Julian noted.

Rik looked up into the darkness above. "The bats are roosting in the upper chimney."

The presence of an active bat roost provided critical operational data for the survey team. Fruit bats—Megachiroptera—had a wingspan exceeding eighty centimeters, requiring an open flight corridor at least one point five meters wide to navigate without damaging their wing membranes. If fruit bats were flying through this chimney every evening to forage in the jungle outside, the passage was guaranteed to be unobstructed by narrow rock pinches or water sumps.

Julian inspected the soil texture of the guano sediment core taken from the upper terrace.

The upper five centimeters consisted of loose, dark brown granular fecal pellets, underlain by a compact layer of yellowish-brown phosphate mineralization where leaching rainwater had reacted with the limestone bedrock. The presence of secondary calcium phosphate minerals proved that the guano deposit had occupied this chamber for centuries, confirming the long-term structural stability of the upper ceiling dome.

Mara noted the biological density of the Arachnocampa colonies in the survey log, recording their presence as diagnostic bio-indicators of surface proximity. The combination of active glowworm colonies and fresh bat guano confirmed that an open sinkhole lay immediately above.

From the base of the chimney behind them, a low, guttural gurgle echoed through the lower siphon, followed by the wet slap of water rising three centimeters against the gravel bank.

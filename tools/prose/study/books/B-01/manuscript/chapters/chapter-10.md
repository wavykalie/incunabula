# Chapter 10: The Vote in the Dark

The entrance to Passage Blue was an irregular fissure in the north wall of the upper chamber, six meters above the gravel floor. To reach it, the team had to traverse along a narrow ledge of slippery flowstone that hovered directly above the rising lake.

Rik had already rigged a handline of eight-millimeter dynamic cord along the wall, anchoring it to three expansion bolts he had driven into the rock during the April expedition. Mara stood at the mouth of the fissure, looking back at the terrace.

The brown water had reached the bivouac site. The flat stone where Julian had set his geophone was completely submerged, and the yellow wax watermark Mara had drawn two hours ago was buried under nearly two meters of churning flood water. Silt-covered foam lapped against the bottom rungs of the access traverse, spinning in violent eddies as the hydraulic head from the lower siphon continued to pump water into the chamber.

Julian was secured to the traverse line by two dynamic cowstails clipped into his chest sling. He hung between Rik and Mara, his broken right leg suspended in a sling improvised from tubular nylon webbing. His face was covered in a sheen of cold sweat, his jaw set in a rigid line of suppressed agony. Every shift of the traverse line sent a jolt of pain through his splinted femur, but he had not made a sound for forty minutes.

"This is the threshold," Mara said, gathering the three of them into the narrow alcove at the mouth of Passage Blue. The fissure was less than a meter wide, sloping upward at a steep angle into total darkness. A faint, cool current of air touched her damp cheek, smelling of ancient dust and dry stone, completely distinct from the sulfurous, wet stench of the rising flood below.

"Before we pass Station B-zero, we make an irrevocable decision," Mara continued, her headlamp switched to its lowest power setting to conserve the battery. "Once we ascend this fault, we cannot retreat. The terrace below will be four meters underwater in less than three hours. If Passage Blue pinches out, or if we encounter an impassable vertical pitch that we cannot climb, we will be trapped in an ascending dead end with no water, no exit, and twenty-four hours of battery life."

She looked at each of them in turn. "Under International Speleological Union guidelines and my personal survey contract, entering an unverified high-risk lead requires unanimous consent from all team members. If any member votes to hold our position on the highest remaining ledge in the main chamber, we stay and prepare an emergency air pocket survival plan."

Rik scoffed, a short, sharp bark of laughter that echoed off the narrow walls. "An emergency plan on a four-meter ledge? Look at that water, Mara. It is rising at over a meter an hour. The ceiling in this chamber has no dry bells. Waiting inside an air pocket merely delays hypothermia. I cast my vote for the climb. We move into the fault."

Linh wiped a streak of muddy sweat from her cheek. Her hands were raw, the skin across her knuckles scraped from hauling Julian's tackle bags across the traverse. She looked down at the swirling brown lake below, then up into the black throat of Passage Blue.

"I agree with Rik," Linh said, her voice small but firm. "My brother drowned in Da Nang because he waited in a second-floor room for the water to stop rising. It didn't stop. We have to go up."

Mara turned to Julian. The hydrologist hung in his gear sling, his eyes half-closed, his breathing shallow and rapid.

"Julian," Mara said gently. "Your decision."

Julian lifted his head slowly. A faint, bitter smile touched the corners of his pale lips. "I already gave you my decision, Mara. I told you to leave me on the terrace with a radio beacon. You refused to accept an abandonment protocol because it would tarnish your pristine safety record."

"I refused because a surveyor does not leave team members behind while there is an open line on the map," Mara answered steadily. "What is your decision on Passage Blue?"

Julian looked at Rik, who was supporting half his weight with a massive arm looped under his shoulder, then at Linh, who was holding his splinted foot to prevent it from striking the rock.

"I say we climb," Julian whispered. "Not because I believe your map has an exit, Mara. I say we climb because Rik and Linh refuse to let me die alone, and I will not rob them of their chance to find the sky."

Mara nodded once, a sharp, decisive gesture. She pulled the orange Rite in the Rain notebook from her pocket, uncapped her 3H pencil, and turned to the blank page following Station B-seven.

At the top of the grid, she wrote the date, the time, and the station coordinates: *Passage Blue Traverse. Station B-zero. Unanimous ascent decision logged at 02:45.*

She capped the pencil, zipped the notebook into her chest pocket, and looked up into the darkness of the unmapped chimney.

Rik anchored the static descent rope around a massive flowstone column as thick as an ancient oak.

He wrapped thirty-millimeter tubular webbing three times around the calcified stem, equalizing the two loops with a water knot and clipping two locking steel carabiners into the master point. The pitch dropped twenty-two meters through a vertical solution shaft, its fluted walls slick with wet moonmilk that gleamed like polished lard in the headlamp beams. Below the lip, the rope hung clean of the rock for eighteen meters, a free-hanging vertical drop that eliminated the risk of rope abrasion against chert edges.

"Descent line is rigged," Rik said.

Mara checked Julian's sit rig and chest sling. "Julian descends second. Rik goes down first to set the bottom stance."

Single-rope technique on vertical pitches required absolute mechanical discipline. Rik clipped his bobbin descender into his front master link, threaded the eleven-millimeter static line through the two aluminum friction pulleys, and locked the rope off with a safety bight. He stepped backward over the abyss, his boots pressing lightly against the slick calcite wall as he controlled his descent speed with his gloved right hand, dropping smoothly into the black void beneath them.

Rik inspected the rebelay anchor placed five meters below the lip of the vertical shaft.

The rebelay was rigged with two eight-millimeter steel expansion bolts set forty centimeters apart in solid limestone, linked by an equalized Y-hang of eight-millimeter dynamic cord. By establishing intermediate rebelays down the vertical pitch, the rope was divided into short, manageable segments, preventing dangerous rope stretch under climber weight and eliminating rub points against abrasive chert ledges. He clipped his safety cowstail into the upper bolt hanger, weighted the loop to test the anchor, and adjusted the slack until both legs shared the load equally.

"Rebelay is equalized," Rik called up.

Mara monitored the rope angle from above. "The rope hangs completely clear."

The geometry of the Y-hang anchor required an internal angle of less than sixty degrees between the two anchor legs. If the angle exceeded ninety degrees, the force exerted on each individual bolt increased exponentially, multiplying the shear load during a dynamic fall. Rik kept the angle at forty degrees, ensuring that each bolt supported only fifty-three percent of the total hanging weight, providing a massive safety factor against rock failure.

Rik adjusted the tension on the lower rebelay loop with an overhand knot on a bight.

He ensured that the loop hung with sixty centimeters of slack below the anchor bolt, allowing a descending caver to clip their safety cowstail into the anchor hanger without unweighting the descender. Proper rebelay spacing prevented shock-loading the lower anchor in the event of an upper anchor failure, keeping the fall factor below zero point three throughout the vertical descent.

Mara inspected the equalized knot on the Y-hang anchor, checking that both loops of dynamic cord shared the static load equally. The braided fibers sat flat and dressed, distributing the tension across both expansion bolts with mechanical symmetry.

The pitch rope hung perfectly centered in the vertical shaft, twelve centimeters clear of the nearest chert protrusion. Rik tested the bottom anchor with his full body weight before unclipping his descender from the line.

"The survey is open," Mara said. "Rik, take the lead line. Linh, bring the survey staff. We move into the fault."

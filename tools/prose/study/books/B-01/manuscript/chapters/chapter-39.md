# Chapter 39: The Field Assessment

The pulsing amber flashers of the rescue truck lit the dripping bamboo in rhythmic waves of gold and shadow.

Two forestry paramedics in orange waterproof jumpsuits scrambled up the bank of the doline, carrying a folding titanium stretcher, a vacuum immobilizer, and two heavy trauma kits. Behind them came the regional ranger chief, his vinyl poncho glistening under the relentless downpour.

"Hold your position," the lead paramedic called out, sliding down into the mud beside Julian. "Do not move the extremity."

Mara stepped back, her knees trembling from exhaustion as she leaned against a mossy teak trunk. Her caving suit was torn at both elbows and knees, her knuckles raw and bleeding from the climb through the root choke.

The paramedic, a calm man named Sarit with silvering hair at his temples, knelt beside Julian's splint. His gloved hands moved with clinical speed, cutting away the muddy duct tape and aluminum survey rods with heavy medical shears.

"Closed fracture of the distal tibia," Sarit assessed, feeling the pulse behind Julian's medial malleolus. "Pedal pulse is palpable, two out of four. Capillary refill is under two seconds. The alignment is surprisingly stable. Who set this splint?"

"Julian set it himself," Mara said, wiping rain from her eyelashes. "Rik and Linh secured the rods."

Sarit looked up, nodding with professional respect. "Good work. Another twenty degrees of rotation and the bone shard would have punctured the anterior tibial artery. You saved his lower leg from amputation. But we need him in the vacuum splint and on IV antibiotics immediately. That mud carries Clostridium tetani and every fungal spore in the karst."

They slid Julian onto the titanium litter, inflating the vacuum mattress until it hardened into a rigid shell around his hips and legs. Julian let out a long sigh as the paramedic injected five milligrams of morphine into his saline port. His eyes drifted half-shut, his breathing deepening into rhythmic ease.

"Good gear," Julian murmured into the rain. "Better than aluminum survey rods."

The ranger chief stepped over to Mara, offering her a steaming thermos lid filled with black, sugary tea. Mara took it with both hands, her numb fingers soaking in the warmth of the metal before she drank. The hot liquid tasted of cardamom and smoke, cutting through the mineral taste of cave air that had coated her mouth for two days.

"We had you marked as trapped at Station 14," the ranger chief said, shining his tactical light toward the jagged fissure in the limestone floor. "The main river sink went under ten meters of water at midnight. The hydraulic engineers said no human team could survive that hydrostatic head."

"We did not stay in the main trunk," Mara replied. She pulled her double-wrapped waterproof logbook from her chest pocket. "We cut through the western fault system into an unmapped bypass conduit. Station 31 through 38."

The ranger chief took the logbook, shielding the pages with his poncho as he glanced at Mara's meticulously penciled azimuths, inclinations, and passage dimensions.

"This fissure links the central sinking stream to the western doline system," the ranger said, his eyes widening as he traced the survey vectors. "We have looked for this hydraulic relief overflow for twelve years. Every monsoon, the lower agricultural valley floods because we thought the subterranean river was a single blocked conduit. You found the high-level relief valve."

"It is not just a relief valve," Linh added, wrapping a dry foil emergency blanket around her shoulders as she sat on the truck's tailgate. "It is a dry-weather escape route for any expedition caught by flash floods in the lower canyon."

The rescue crew hoisted the litter, carrying Julian down the slick trail toward the four-wheel-drive ambulance idling on the logging road.

Mara stood alone at the rim of the sinkhole for a moment, looking down into the narrow slit where the root ball had been severed. Deep within the earth, forty meters down, the muffled roar of the rising flood was still audible, a distant bass rumble of pressurized water venting harmlessly into the limestone vastness.

The mountain had kept its secret for ten thousand years.

Sarit, the lead paramedic, cut away the last layer of soaked cotton from Julian's splinted leg.

The skin over the fracture was pale and bruised, swollen with localized edema, but there was no sign of arterial compromise or compartment syndrome. Sarit wrapped a padded inflatable cuff around the calf, pumping the pressure bladder to thirty millimeters of mercury to stabilize the bone fragments without restricting venous return. He swabbed a vein in Julian's left forearm with povidone-iodine, inserted an eighteen-gauge catheter, and initiated an infusion of lactated Ringer's solution to treat dehydration and hypovolemic shock.

"Vitals are stabilizing," Sarit told Mara.

The ranger chief stood beside Mara, reviewing the survey vectors in her field logbook. "This bypass changes our disaster maps."

The discovery of the western bypass fault solved a twelve-year hydrological puzzle for the forestry department. During every monsoon season, the agricultural valley to the south had suffered catastrophic flash floods that drowned crops and destroyed bridges because hydrological models had assumed that the central sinking stream had only one drainage outlet. Mara's survey proved that when the primary resurgence choked, the entire flood discharge diverted through this high-level fault into the western dolines, opening a new pathway for flood management.

Sarit, the lead paramedic, verified the immobilization of the vacuum splint with a digital manometer.

The pressure inside the rigid vinyl sleeve was maintained at negative zero point four atmospheres, molding the micro-bead filling into an unyielding cast that conformed perfectly to the contours of Julian's leg without exerting uneven pressure points. He checked the skin temperature of Julian's toes, finding them warm and pink, confirming excellent peripheral perfusion through the tibial artery. He recorded the initial field assessment on the medical transport manifest, signing the document with his badge number.

"Splint rigidity is verified," Sarit said to Mara.

The ranger chief handed Mara an official accident log sheet. "Sign the intake section, surveyor."

The transition from wilderness survival to bureaucratic emergency management was abrupt and surreal. In the cave, survival had depended on brass compasses, nylon knots, and raw human endurance; on the surface, it was governed by medical manifests, radio frequencies, and institutional liability protocols. Mara took the pen with steady fingers, signing her name as expedition leader beneath the coordinates of the newly discovered western bypass fault.

Sarit recorded the Glasgow Coma Scale score for all four surveyors on the emergency triage log.

All four team members scored a perfect fifteen out of fifteen, showing normal eye-opening, verbal responsiveness, and motor function despite forty-eight hours of extreme physical and psychological stress. The paramedic administered oral rehydration salts and prophylactic antibiotic broad-spectrum tablets to protect against tropical soil pathogens before loading the litter into the rescue transport.

Mara watched the rescue transport disappear down the muddy logging road, carrying Julian toward the regional hospital and complete recovery. The unmapped bypass was now officially integrated into the provincial emergency flood plan, protecting future generations.

Now it was measured, drawn, and pinned to the grid.

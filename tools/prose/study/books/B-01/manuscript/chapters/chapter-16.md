# Chapter 16: The Rift

Beyond the gallery of mud flutes, the passage terminated abruptly against a sheer vertical fault scarp. The rift cut across the limestone beds like an open wound, two meters wide and soaring upward into complete, impenetrable blackness.

Mara stood at the base of the rift, shining her hundred-lumen search beam into the cleft. The light travelled thirty meters up through fractured dolomite before diffusing into darkness. There were no flowstone cascades here, no gentle sloping ramps, and no calcified ledges. The walls were vertical sheets of dark micrite, slick with a greasy film of black manganese oxide.

"The fault continues straight up," Mara reported, checking the clinometer. "Eighty-eight degrees inclination. Azimuth zero-four-two. It tracks the primary tectonic fracture line."

Rik stepped to the base of the crack, leaning his head back until his helmet struck his collar. "The rock is totally sheared. Look at the slickensides."

Mara examined the stone. The surface of the rift was covered in deep, parallel grooves, ground into the limestone by tectonic friction when the fault slipped millions of years ago. The grooves offered zero horizontal shelves for boot placement.

"We need a lead climber on aid," Rik said, his voice flat with exhaustion. "Drill a hole every meter. Place a spit, hang an aid ladder, step up, drill the next hole. To climb thirty meters of aid on fractured micrite will take eight hours of continuous drilling. We do not have eight hours. My hammer drill battery is down to one bar."

"We cannot drill aid ladders on one battery bar," Mara said. "A lead climber must free-climb the crack using natural friction, setting protection bolts only at the belay stations."

Rik looked at his massive, scarred hands. His forearms were swollen, the tendons in his wrists creaking with tenosynovitis from hauling Julian up the previous pitch. He attempted to close his right hand into a fist, but his fingers seized midway, locked by muscle cramps.

"I cannot lead it, Mara," Rik admitted, the words tasting like poison on his tongue. He looked down at the mud floor, unable to meet her gaze. "My grip is gone. If I try to chimney an eighty-eight-degree slot with dead forearms, I will peel off ten meters up and take the gear rack with me."

Silence descended on the rift, heavy and suffocating. Without a lead climber to carry the haul line up the shaft, their ascent was dead. They were trapped on a ledge thirty meters above the flood, with twenty hours of battery life and a rising lake beneath their heels.

"I will climb it," a quiet voice said.

Mara turned. Linh was standing beside the survey gear, her helmet lamp switched to its lowest beam. Her small, thin frame looked fragile against the colossal dark rift. Her hands were tucked into the pockets of her oversuit, but her posture was upright, rigid as a plumb line.

"Linh," Mara said gently. "You have never led a vertical exploration pitch on natural friction."

"I have second-climbed every Grade Five pitch you have rigged in the last three years, Mara," Linh replied, her voice gaining strength with every word. "I weigh forty-eight kilograms. My boots are size thirty-six. I can fit my feet into the tectonic grooves where Rik's boots cannot even touch. I can stem this chimney using back and knees."

"Linh, look at the rock," Rik warned, stepping toward her. "It is coated in manganese slurry. If your foot slips on that grease, you will fall thirty meters onto breakdown blocks. A dynamic rope will stretch, but in a narrow slot you will strike every rib on the way down."

Linh looked at Rik, then down at Julian, who lay on his foam pad watching her with dark, unreadable eyes.

"When my brother went under the water in Da Nang," Linh said, her voice dropping into a register of cold, absolute calm, "I was standing on the concrete retaining wall three meters away. I was terrified of the current. I held onto the iron railing and I watched him drift toward the storm drain. I didn't jump. I stayed on the wall because I was afraid of the dark water."

She unzipped her oversuit, pulled out her lightweight caving sit rig, and stepped into the leg loops. She tightened the waist buckle with a sharp, decisive tug.

"I am not staying on the wall today," Linh said. "Give me the spit kit and forty meters of dynamic lead line."

Mara looked at her young assistant for five long seconds. In the quiet geometry of expedition command, there are moments where protocol must yield to the reality of human will. She saw no panic in Linh's eyes; she saw only the terrible, clear resolve of a survivor who has already lived through her worst nightmare.

"Racking the lead gear," Mara said, stepping to the equipment bag.

She handed Linh four eight-millimeter self-drilling expansion bolts, two light aluminum hangers, and a small steel hand-drill driver. She tied the end of the bright orange Beal dynamic rope into the central loop of Linh's sit rig using a double figure-eight knot with a long stopper.

"Listen to me, Linh," Mara instructed, her hands resting firmly on the younger woman's shoulders. "Stem with your back against the north wall and your boots against the south wall. Use opposed pressure. Keep your center of gravity low. Every four meters, look for a constriction to place a natural thread anchor. If you find solid rock at twenty meters, drill the belay bolts. Do not look down. Do not think about Julian. Think only about the three centimeters of rubber under your boot soles."

Linh nodded once. She blew on her cold fingers, set her hands against the slick, dark limestone of the rift, and stepped into the vertical throat of the mountain.

The climb was an exercise in terrifying, slow-motion precision. Linh stemmed the chimney like an insect, her small boots finding microscopic ripples in the tectonic slickensides. Her breathing drifted down through the slot, rapid, rhythmic, and shallow.

At ten meters, she paused, wedging her back firmly into an angle of the fault. Working with one hand while maintaining body tension with her knees, she threaded a piece of five-millimeter Dyneema cord through a natural rock eyelet, clipping the lead rope into a wiregate carabiner.

"First protection set," Linh called down, her voice steady. "Continuing ascent."

Mara paid out the rope through her Petzl GriGri belay device, inch by inch, her eyes tracking the tiny point of light ascending into the vault. Ten meters. Fifteen meters. Twenty meters.

At twenty-four meters, the light stopped.

"Mara!" Linh's voice echoed down, carrying a high, sharp edge of triumph. "Large horizontal terrace reached! The rift opens into an upper gallery! Solid limestone on both walls! Setting the belay bolts now!"

The metallic clink of the hand drill rang out through the rift, rhythmic and clear like a bell tolling in a cathedral. Ten minutes later, Linh's whistle blew three long blasts.

"Belay locked!" Linh shouted down. "Send the haul line!"

Rik let out a massive, shuddering breath, resting his forehead against the cold rock wall. Beside him, Julian closed his eyes and whispered a single word of thanks to the dark stone.

Rik inspected the vertical joint running up the center of the sheer limestone rift.

The fracture was fifteen meters high, narrowing from eighty centimeters at the base to a knife-edge crack at the top where the bedding planes converged. The rock surface was coated in slick moonmilk, a pasty, white suspension of microcrystalline calcite and water that offered zero friction for climbing boots. To ascend the pitch without expanding bolts, the lead climber had to use natural chert horns and delicate flowstone pockets for intermediate protection.

"The lead climb is technical," Rik muttered.

Linh stepped forward, her sit rig already adjusted. "I will take the lead rack."

Linh was thirty kilos lighter than Rik, allowing her to utilize delicate calcite protrusions that would snap under a heavier climber's weight. She tied into the dynamic lead rope with a double figure-eight, clipped four micro-camalots and six wire gate carabiners to her gear loops, and chalked her hands with dry limestone dust. With steady, deliberate foot placements, she worked her way up the vertical fissure, finding tiny finger pockets in the sparite crystal beds that had resisted dissolution.

Linh tapped her aluminum carabiner against the rock to test the resonance of the limestone flake.

The stone responded with a sharp, high-pitched ring that confirmed solid connection to the underlying bedrock. If the flake had been detached, the impact would have produced a hollow, thudding vibration, warning the climber that the hold would shear away under body weight. She placed a tiny wire cam into the horizontal seam behind the flake, tugged the sling sharply downward to set the lobes into the limestone, and clipped the dynamic rope into the gate with a smooth flick of her wrist.

"First protection point is set," Linh called down.

Rik kept the belay line tensioned through his ATC device. "You have six meters of rope out."

Leading on vertical limestone required total psychological discipline and acute sensory awareness. Every handhold had to be tested for stability, every foot placement examined for mud grease, and every piece of protection evaluated for pull-out resistance under fall vectors. Linh moved with fluid grace, her small frame distributing weight across three points of contact at all times, trusting the friction of her sticky rubber boots against the sharp calcite crystals.

Rik inspected the cam springs on his mechanical micro-camalots, oiling the pivots with silicone lubricant.

The four tiny aluminum lobes moved smoothly against the stainless steel axle, expanding instantly when the trigger wire was released. In narrow limestone seams, micro-cams provided the only reliable active protection against vertical falls, gripping the slick rock through logarithmic spiral geometry that converted downward pull into outward radial pressure against the fissure walls.

Rik tested the friction hold of his rubber climbing boots on the wet limestone crystals, shifting his weight across three points of contact. The coarse calcite spar provided excellent mechanical grip, allowing him to ascend the sheer rift without artificial aids.

Linh set a final micro-cam in the horizontal bedding seam at the top of the pitch, clipping the belay anchor with two locking carabiners. Her breathing was steady, her confidence fully restored by the successful lead climb.

Linh paused at the twenty-meter stance, anchoring her cowstail to a stout calcite horn that jutted from the eastern wall. Her hands were caked with white moonmilk slurry, but her pulse was steady and her breathing was controlled.

"Rope length is twenty-two meters," Linh called down to Rik.

The rift wall here was fluted with vertical solution rills known as rillenkarren, razor-sharp limestone fins carved by trickling condensation water over thousands of years. The rills provided positive edges for the rubber rands of her climbing boots, allowing her to bridge the narrow fissure with minimal physical exertion. She placed an equalized directional anchor using two wire chocks seated in a narrowing solution furrow, clipping the static line through a locking carabiner to keep the rope clear of the abrasive rock fins.

Mara clipped the haul line into Julian's chest sling and looked up into the black vertical rift. "Linh made the line," Mara said, her voice trembling slightly for the first time in thirty-six hours. "Prepare to haul."

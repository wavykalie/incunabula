# Chapter 40: The Closed Polygon

The tin roof of the survey station hut rattled under the diminishing patter of the morning squall.

Inside, the only illumination came from a single paraffin lamp hanging from a wire hook above the drafting table. The yellow flame cast long, steady shadows across sheets of gridded Mylar film, brass dividers, a pocket stereoscope, and three cups of lukewarm coffee that had gone cold hours ago.

Mara sat on a folding wooden stool, her bare feet propped on a clean rucksack to keep them off the damp concrete floor. Her skin was scrubbed raw, the smell of carbolic soap battling the lingering stench of cave clay that seemed permanently etched into the pores of her hands.

On the table before her lay the field logbook.

The pages were wrinkled, their edges stained with iron-rich sediment from the lower sumps, but the pencil notations remained sharp and legible. Forty stations. Two hundred and eighty-six individual survey shots. Azimuths, vertical angles, left-right-up-down cross-sectional dimensions, and the crucial offset vectors through the western bypass fault.

Beside the logbook sat a small yellow plastic disc, scuffed and abraded by gravel. It was Station Tag 42, the marker her mentor had hammered into the limestone ledge thirty years before his death in the lower siphons.

Mara picked up the scientific calculator, her thumb tapping out the trigonometric coordinates: Northing, Easting, Elevation.

`ΔN = L × cos(θ) × cos(φ)`
`ΔE = L × cos(θ) × sin(φ)`
`ΔZ = L × sin(θ)`

Station by station, she summed the vectors around the perimeter of the karst massif.

From Station 01 at the resurgence portal, deep into the Phreatic Gallery, through the siphon constriction where Julian had jammed his leg, up the vertical chimney where the glowworms had clustered, across the roaring high line where Rik had held the failing anchor with his own flesh, through the submerged rib where Linh had conquered her terror, and out through the severed root choke of the western doline.

From the doline, she entered the surface tie-in traverse surveyed by the forestry service an hour ago: four hundred meters across the teak ridge, tying back into the primary geodetic benchmark on the station veranda.

The loop was complete.

Mara entered the final sum into the closing equation:

Total polygon perimeter: four thousand seven hundred and twenty meters.
Misclosure vector in Northing: plus zero point zero one eight meters.
Misclosure vector in Easting: minus zero point zero two one meters.
Misclosure vector in Elevation: plus zero point zero zero nine meters.

Total three-dimensional error of closure: zero point zero two nine meters.

Twenty-nine millimeters.

Across nearly five kilometers of blind subterranean surveying, conducted in total darkness, through hypothermic water and collapsing dolomite, the survey polygon had closed within the thickness of a thumb.

It was a precision grade higher than British Cave Research Association Grade 6 standard.

Mara laid the drafting pencil down in the groove of the wooden table.

According to strict institutional survey protocol, the dataset was formally compromised. She had abandoned the standard foresight-backsight verification routine at Station 22 when the air began to thin. She had omitted three cross-sectional station sketches to haul Julian through the siphon before the water crested the shelf. The regulatory board in the capital would find minor procedural irregularities in the margins of her field notes.

She picked up the yellow survey tag and pressed her thumb against its embossed numerals.

She had broken the manual to save her crew.

Julian was asleep in the regional hospital ward forty kilometers to the south, his fractured tibia pinned with titanium screws, his lower extremity fully viable. Rik and Linh were asleep on cots in the adjoining room, their steady breathing audible through the thin plywood partition.

Mara took a bottle of black India ink, uncapped the glass dropper, and loaded the fine nib of her technical ruling pen.

With steady, deliberate strokes, she inked the final closing line across the gridded Mylar sheet, sealing the perimeter of the mountain on paper forever.

Mara dipped the technical ruling pen into the vial of black India ink, holding the nib against the Mylar film.

The drafting table in the forestry hut was illuminated by the steady yellow glow of the paraffin lamp, throwing her silhouette against the whitewashed wooden wall. With practiced, deliberate movements, she drew the closing line of the survey polygon from Station 40 back to Station 01 at the resurgence portal. The line was crisp, zero point two millimeters wide, joining forty survey stations into a continuous geometric loop that represented five kilometers of subterranean topography.

"Polygon error is twenty-nine millimeters," Mara said to the empty room.

She set the ruling pen down and looked at the yellow Station 42 tag resting beside her notebook.

Her mentor's tag was no longer an isolated marker lost in a drowned cavern beneath the mountain. It was an integral vertex of the closed polygon, its spatial coordinates fixed in three dimensions with millimeter precision. She had broken procedural protocol, abandoned secondary sketches, and sacrificed standard timing to pull her team through the rising flood, but the arithmetic of the earth was true and all four surveyors were alive to sign the final field report.

Mara entered the final closure figures into the official survey log for the National Speleological Archive.

The document was printed on acid-free archival parchment, requiring permanent black pigment ink for all station coordinates, passage cross-sections, and closure misclosures. She recorded the coordinates of all forty stations in chronological order, noting the instrumental methodology, the BCRA Grade 6 accuracy standards, and the precise geographic coordinates of the entrance portal and the emergence sinkhole. Across forty stations, the cumulative spatial error was zero point zero two nine meters, a triumph of mathematical and physical discipline under mortal stress.

"BCRA Grade 6 certification complete," Mara wrote in the summary box.

She looked out the window of the station hut at the clearing sky.

The monsoon squall had passed, leaving the jungle canopy glistening in the golden light of the afternoon sun. White clouds of moisture rose from the limestone towers, drifting toward the blue sky like smoke from an ancient altar. Below the surface of the green mountain, the water was still rising through the dark conduits, but its path was no longer an unmapped mystery. It was measured, closed, and understood.

Mara applied the institutional rubber stamp of the National Speleological Survey to the title block of the map.

The purple ink stamped the official certification across the parchment: *COMPLETED TRAVERSE - BCRA GRADE 6 - SEALED AND FILED*. She signed her name with black India ink, followed by the signatures of Julian, Rik, and Linh. The survey of the wet season was no longer an emergency escape line; it was an enduring scientific document, permanently registered in the national speleological archives for all generations of explorers to come.

Mara closed the field logbook, tied the protective cotton ribbons around the archival cover, and placed the yellow Station 42 tag on top of the document. The wet season survey was finished, the polygon was closed, and all four surveyors had returned to the light.

The wet season was over.

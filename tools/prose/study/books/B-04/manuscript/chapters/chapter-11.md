# Chapter 11 — Tea with the Registrar

At ten o'clock on Wednesday morning, Arthur Pendelton sat in a high-backed mahogany chair opposite Audrey Vance in the private inner office of the County Registry Office in Hereford.

The room smelled of floor wax, archival acid-free paper, and cold Earl Grey tea. Behind Mrs. Vance's desk stood four towering oak filing presses, their panelled doors locked with heavy brass escutcheons. On the desk itself sat three open volumes bound in dark blue cloth, two pairs of reading spectacles, an ebony ruler, and a brass date stamp whose mechanical digits had been freshly set to 11 NOV 2026.

Audrey Vance did not look up immediately when Arthur sat down. She was holding a sheet of heavy vellum against the desk lamp, scrutinizing the watermarked crown with rhythmic, clinical patience. She was fifty-three years old, dressed in a tailored charcoal wool suit with a high-necked cream blouse secured by a modest silver brooch shaped like a Tudor rose. Her iron-grey hair was pinned behind her ears with steel clips, and her sharp jawline suggested a woman who had never once yielded an argument to a borough surveyor.

"You have come about a commercial parking permit, Mr. Pendelton," she said evenly. Her voice had the crisp, unimpeachable cadence of a high court clerk reading an indictment.

"That was the initial thought, Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied, his voice small and polite. He had placed his brown woollen gloves neatly on his knees. "The municipal space behind the butter market... the signs are somewhat ambiguous regarding non-commercial light utilities."

Mrs. Vance laid the vellum sheet down upon a clean blotting square. She took off her reading spectacles with her right hand and adjusted them against the bridge of her nose with a sharp, rhythmic snap that echoed like a pistol shot in the quiet room.

"The parking regulations for the butter market are administered by the Department of Transport and Highways in Plough Lane," she stated, looking Arthur directly in the pupils. "You were informed of that fact yesterday morning at eleven-twelve by my junior assistant, Miss Cleary. And yet here you sit, Mr. Pendelton, in the office of Her Majesty's Superintendent Registrar, clutching a council tax demand and smelling faintly of peppermint."

Arthur swallowed. His throat felt dry. He reached instinctively into his cardigan pocket, but his fingers encountered only empty cellophane wrappers.

"Miss Cleary is a very efficient officer," Arthur murmured. "Most attentive to statutory boundaries."

"This entire department is attentive to statutory boundaries," Mrs. Vance said. She picked up a bone folder and smoothed the crease of an open certificate. "We have to be, Mr. Pendelton. The county of Herefordshire contains two hundred and sixty thousand citizens, seventy-four civil parishes, and eighty-two thousand registered households. If my clerks were to overlook a single defective baptismal declaration or accept an uncertified foreign dissolution, the property titles of half the Golden Valley would be tied up in Chancery proceedings before Michaelmas."

"Quite so," Arthur said, nodding frantically. "The integrity of the crown rolls. An essential bulwark against social confusion."

"Indeed," Mrs. Vance said. She opened her middle drawer, extracted a heavy cardboard file bound with red tape, and placed it squarely between them on the mahogany surface. The label on the folder read: IRREGULAR SOLEMNIZATIONS — WESTERN REGISTRATION DISTRICT.

Arthur felt the blood drain from his cheeks until his nose felt cold.

"Do you know what this is, Mr. Pendelton?" she asked softly.

"It... it appears to be an administrative portfolio, Mrs. Vance."

"It is a dossier of irregularities," she said. She untied the red tape with two deft movements of her fingers. "Over the past five years, my office has received thirty-seven separate enquiries from building societies, probate solicitors, and the Inland Revenue regarding marriages purported to have been solemnized in pastures, apple barns, and hop kilns across this county. In every single instance, the contracting parties produced an elaborate cream certificate bearing a gold seal, a quotation from the First Epistle to the Corinthians, and the signature of one Arthur Reginald Pendelton, describing himself as a Presbyter Licentiate of the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace."

Arthur said nothing. He stared at the brass inkwell on the corner of the desk.

"Under Section forty-four of the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine," Mrs. Vance continued, her voice dropping into a rhythmic, statutory cadence, "a religious marriage may be solemnized only in a registered building certified under the Act of eighteen-fifty-five. An open hayfield is not a registered building, Mr. Pendelton. A cider barn with unmortared rubble footings is not a registered building. And an unploughed clover pasture in Pembridge is merely an agricultural enclosure where sheep graze and thistles propagate."

"The intentions of the parties were honourable, Mrs. Vance," Arthur ventured, his voice trembling slightly with quiet passion. "They were decent people. They made sincere vows before their neighbours."

Audrey Vance removed her spectacles again and rubbed the bridge of her nose. For a fleeting fraction of a second, the statutory mask slipped, revealing a look of profound, exhausted weariness around her grey eyes.

"Do you imagine I take pleasure in this, Mr. Pendelton?" she asked in a low voice. "Do you think I enjoy summoning a woman whose husband has just died of tractor rollover to tell her that her pension rights are invalid because her marriage took place under an oak tree instead of beneath a statutory crest? Do you think I relish watching a probate solicitor seize a family orchard because the marriage certificate in the sideboard was printed on a home computer from Swindon?"

Arthur looked at her in surprise. "You have seen that happen?"

"Three times in five years," she said grimly. "The law does not possess a heart, Mr. Pendelton. It possesses only clauses and schedules. If a marriage is not inscribed in these books beneath my hand, it does not exist when the bailiffs arrive. It is merely theatrical loitering in the rain."

She stood up from her chair and walked to the window overlooking the cathedral green.

"The General Register Office has ordered a county-wide inspection of all independent officiants," she said, her back turned to him. "The Charity Commission is auditing Dr. Finch's fraudulent internet enterprise in Wiltshire. When that audit concludes, my statutory duty will require me to review every single union you have conducted in this county. If those records cannot be reconciled with the civil registers, I shall have no choice under Section seventy-five but to declare them null and void."

Arthur rose slowly to his feet. His hands were clasped in front of his tweed coat.

"There is a couple at Blackthorn Hollow, Mrs. Vance," Arthur said softly. "Leo and Clementine Marsh. They were married in an apple barn in July of twenty-sixteen. A formal certificate was signed. If that document can be produced... if the yellow duplicate is verified..."

Audrey Vance turned slowly from the window. She looked at Arthur with an expression of sharp, penetrating assessment.

"The Marsh farm in Vowchurch," she murmured. "I know of that property. The land registry queried their joint tenancy two years ago because their marital counter-foil could not be traced in our index. If an authentic statutory certificate exists for that solemnization, Mr. Pendelton, it would constitute an evidentiary cornerstone. It would demonstrate that your ministry maintained concurrent documentation under the nineteen-forty-nine Act."

She paused, stepping back behind her desk.

"You have until Friday afternoon at four-thirty, Mr. Pendelton," she said, her voice resuming its cold civil service authority. "At that hour, my quarterly returns to the Registrar General must be locked in the dispatch pouch. If the Marsh certificate is not on this desk by four-thirty, the audit notice will be gazetted on Monday morning."

Arthur bowed his head. "Thank you, Mrs. Vance."

"Good morning, Mr. Pendelton," she said, already reaching for her ebony pen. "And do mind the wet stones on the steps. They have not been salted since Monday."

Arthur walked out of the inner office. Behind him, the heavy mahogany door clicked shut with the precision of a bank vault.

Outside in the corridor, Arthur paused to catch his breath. The confrontation had shaken him to his marrow, yet beneath the administrative dread lay a faint, unexpected ray of hope. Audrey Vance was not a monster. She was an uncompromising guardian of public records who had seen real families ruined by shoddy paperwork. She had given him a deadline, but she had also named the key: if the Marsh document could be retrieved from Blackthorn Hollow, the county would acknowledge the validity of the ceremony under the historical exemptions of the Act.

Arthur stepped out into the damp Hereford air. His resolve had crystallized. He would drive into the Golden Valley, locate the reclusive couple, and find that yellow certificate before Friday afternoon.

Audrey Vance picked up a small brass paperweight shaped like an open book and weighed it thoughtfully in her palm. The metal caught the cold morning light from the cathedral green.

"When I entered this service as a junior copyist in nineteen-ninety-six, Mr. Pendelton," she said, her voice dropping into a quiet, reflective tone that Arthur had never heard from her before, "my predecessor, old Mr. Gwatkin, took me into the strongroom beneath the crypt. He pointed to thirty yards of vellum folios bound in pigskin. He told me: 'Audrey, governments fall, banks collapse, and wars sweep across the Marches, but so long as these names are inscribed in certified iron ink, an English family knows where its threshold stands.' I have lived by that creed for twenty-eight years. I have guarded those books against fraud, bigamy, and administrative negligence."

Arthur leaned forward, his hands resting on his knees. "I honour that service, Mrs. Vance. Truly, I do. But what of the people whose lives cannot be squared with a ruler? What of the couples who have no church and cannot bear a fluorescent ceiling? Does the law exist to protect their vows, or does it exist merely to punish them for lacking an approved north wall?"

Audrey looked at him for several seconds in total silence. Her grey eyes were steady, impenetrable, yet completely devoid of malice.

"The law exists to give human promises permanent form, Mr. Pendelton," she said softly. "A promise spoken into the wind is gone when the wind drops. The ink holds it against the winter. If you wish me to accept the Marsh ceremony, bring me that yellow counter-foil. Prove to me that an authentic record was made beneath the rafters of that barn, and I will find a way under the eighteen-thirty-six provisions to enter it into the county rolls."

She opened her drawer and retrieved her ebony fountain pen.

"Now, if you will excuse me, I have three death declarations from Bromyard that require certified marginal notation before noon."

Dr. Finch's thermal laminator gave off a smell reminiscent of scorched hair and overheated bakelite. He plugged it into the double socket behind the parish safe, unspooling an orange extension lead that snaked across the coconut matting like an intrusive tropical vine. To Finch, the entire history of the Church of England since the Reformation was merely an inefficient preamble to the creation of a centralized relational database. He clicked his ballpoint pen twice, entered a four-digit security code into his laptop, and watched the status bar crawl across the screen at three percent per minute.

Arthur stood up, bowed with genuine gratitude, and walked out of the office.

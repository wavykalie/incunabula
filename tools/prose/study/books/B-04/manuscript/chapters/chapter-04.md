# Chapter 4 — The Statutory Threat

The inkjet printer on the small bedside table in Arthur's spare bedroom sounded like an industrial loom attempting to weave barbed wire. It clunked, paused, gave a violent internal spasm that rattled the bedside lamp, and then slowly ejected page four of Dr. Finch's purple PDF document into the plastic output tray.

Arthur took the warm sheet by its corner. The purple border had printed out in an uneven shade of magenta, owing to a chronic shortage of cyan ink that Arthur had been ignoring since the previous spring.

He gathered all fourteen pages into a neat pile, squared the edges against his thighs, and carried them downstairs to the kitchen table. He retrieved his heavy brass reading glass from the sideboard drawer, settled himself into his Windsor chair, and began to scrutinize the small print at the foot of the annexes.

Arthur was not intimidated by statutory language. During his twenty-two years as a senior billing clerk with the municipal water board, he had spent entire afternoons dissecting the Water Industry Act 1991, searching for obscure statutory clauses that might exempt a struggling dairy farmer near Madley from paying commercial surface drainage tariffs on an open slurry pit. He understood the cautious, defensive cadence of administrative English: the deliberate use of the passive voice, the conditional clauses that delayed meaning until the third comma, and the ambushes hidden between parentheses where bureaucratic annihilation was formally pronounced.

He also remembered how the council had handled unlicensed water abstractions along the River Arrow in nineteen-ninety-eight. The enforcement officers had not arrived with sirens or bluster. They had arrived in grey saloon cars with buff folders, clipboards, and statutory notices under Section 24, quietly sealing borehole pumps with lead tags and leaving the landowners facing five-figure fines that took ten years to pay off. The law in rural England was slow, heavy, and completely devoid of sentiment.

What he uncovered in Dr. Finch's document caused the short hairs on his neck to prickle with cold apprehension.

The Universal Church of Perpetual Grace was not, in the eyes of the British state, an authentic ecclesiastical body. According to Annex E, titled CONSTITUTIONAL CHARTER AND STATUTORY DESIGNATION, the organization had been registered in England and Wales under the Companies Act 2006 as a Private Limited Company by Guarantee, bearing company registration number 08412991, with its registered corporate address situated in an industrial business park in Swindon.

Arthur squinted through the magnifying glass at clause 14(c).

"The Company hath claimed statutory exemption as a recognized religious denomination under Section 26 of the Marriages Act 1949, by virtue of maintaining certified pastoral congregations across three or more civil registration districts. Said exemption remains legally operable only so long as each appointed provincial celebrant submitteth sworn quarterly solemnization schedules to the central secretarial office, demonstrating an active, continuous, and non-commercial pastoral ministry within the gazetted territory."

Arthur turned to page seven. His thumb left a grey smudge of newsprint against the magenta border.

"In the event that an appointed officiant faileth to tender certified quarterly schedules for a period exceeding four consecutive registration quarters," clause 18 stated, "the Board of Trustees shall certify said default to the General Register Office and Her Majesty's Revenue and Customs. Upon receipt of said certificate of administrative default, the statutory exemption granted under Section 26 shall be deemed revoked in respect of the entire Western March province."

Arthur took off his reading glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"The entire province," he whispered to the empty room.

The Western March province was not an extensive clerical network. It consisted entirely of Arthur Pendelton, his Vauxhall Astra, and his collection of twenty homemade satin stoles. When Dr. Finch had created the Western March province in the spring of 2013, he had drawn a wobbly circle around Herefordshire, south Shropshire, and the edge of Powys on an AA road atlas with a black marker pen, rolled the map into a cardboard postal cylinder, and dispatched it to Leominster with a certificate of apostolic oversight.

If Arthur was disqualified, the Western March exemption collapsed immediately.

And if the exemption collapsed, what became of the four hundred marriages?

Arthur flipped back to Annex C. His index finger traced down the eight-point type until he reached the subsection governing retroactive invalidation.

"Where a marriage hath been solemnized under the purported authority of an exempted body whose statutory qualification hath lapsed, and where said solemnization hath not been registered in the statutory register books of the Superintendent Registrar within twelve calendar months of the event, the validity of said union shall fall to be determined under Section 49 of the Marriages Act 1949 (Marriage without due authority). If the parties did knowingly and wilfully consent to such solemnization without compliance with the statutory preliminaries, the marriage shall be void to all intents and purposes whatsoever."

Arthur stared at the words *knowingly and wilfully*.

Did his couples know? Of course they did not know. The people who asked Arthur to officiate were smallholders, carpenters, landscape gardeners, primary school teachers, and orchard workers. They had invited him because he had a gentle, unhurried voice and did not object to muddy work boots or howling sheep dogs. They had assumed that when Arthur put on his gold-braided stole and read from his little black prayer book, the sovereign majesty of the British constitution was standing behind him, smiling in calm agreement.

They had trusted his presence.

On the strength of those ceremonies, they had taken out joint agricultural mortgages with the building society. They had registered joint tenancies with the county smallholdings estate. They had claimed statutory allowances from the tax office. They had registered births where both names were inscribed as lawfully married parents. They had drawn up simple wills leaving their cider presses, tractors, and stone cottages to their surviving spouses.

If Audrey Vance, the Superintendent Registrar in Hereford, commenced an investigation into twelve years of uncertified ceremonies, every single one of those four hundred couples would receive an official recorded delivery notice on heavy council crest paper, informing them that their wedding was merely an informal outdoor gathering in an uncertified field.

Arthur read on through Annex G. It outlined the procedure for an on-site audit. Dr. Finch had empowered an ecclesiastical inspection committee, consisting of himself and an unnamed accounts auditor from Swindon, to requisition all original ceremonial logbooks, receipt counter-foils, and correspondence files held by provincial celebrants.

"Said celebrant shall produce all records within seventy-two hours of formal requisition. Failure to make discovery shall be reported to the Charity Commission under Section 46 of the Charities Act 2011 as suspected fraudulent concealment."

Arthur turned to Annex J, which was headed: CANONICAL DISPOSITION OF ASSETS AND ECCLESIASTICAL DISSOLUTION.

"In the event of statutory revocation by the civil registrar," the clause specified, "all provincial warrants, vestments, and ceremonial accoutrements purchased under the tax-exempt covenant shall become the immediate property of the central administration, to be liquidated at public auction for the satisfaction of outstanding corporation liabilities."

Arthur looked down at his slippers. They wanted his Astra. They wanted his twenty homemade stoles. They wanted to drag twelve years of kind words and damp confetti before the Charity Commission and the inland revenue auditors.

He reached for the final page. Annex L contained the sworn declaration form. It required the celebrant to affix a five-pound postal order, obtain the counter-signature of a Justice of the Peace, and certify that every wedding had been conducted within a permanent stone structure possessing a consecrated north wall.

"A consecrated north wall," Arthur said aloud, his voice cracking. "In an apple orchard."

He set the papers down. His chest felt heavy, as if a bag of wet grain had been laid across his sternum.

He walked out into the narrow hallway, opened the front door, and leaned his forehead against the damp wooden doorframe. The rain had ceased, leaving behind a cold, heavy fog that smelled of wet clay, diesel exhaust, and fallen cider apples.

A yellow delivery van clattered past along Bargates, its tyres throwing up arcs of brown water from the kerb.

Arthur stepped back inside, shut the front door, and turned both the brass Yale latch and the lower mortise bolt until the mechanisms engaged with a sharp click.

Arthur sat back in his Windsor chair and let his gaze drift to the water board long-service certificate framed on the wall. Twenty-two years of municipal revenue collection had taught him one fundamental truth: an administrative system never forgave an anomaly. When an irregularity was discovered in a public register, the machine did not pause to consider whether the human beings involved were kind, hardworking, or devoted to their children. It simply ground forward, issuing automated notices in windowed brown envelopes, calculating statutory interest, and revoking concessions with blind, mathematical indifference.

He picked up his brass magnifying glass once more and examined the small print of Annex H: FISCAL CONSEQUENCES OF STATUTORY REVOCATION.

Under Section 18 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, transfers of property between spouses were entirely exempt from tax. For twelve years, four hundred farming families in the Marches had relied on that statutory protection. Arthur personally knew eight elderly hill farmers who had executed mutual wills leaving their freeholds, their flocks of Welsh mountain sheep, and their vintage tractors to their surviving wives on the clear understanding that Arthur's meadow blessing had conferred full civil status.

If those marriages were declared void under Section 49 of the Marriages Act, every single one of those farms would be classified as an unexempt transfer between legal strangers. The revenue commissioners would demand forty per cent on everything above the nil-rate band. Families who had farmed the same red clay ridges along the Monnow valley since the Napoleonic wars would be forced to sell their pastures at public auction to satisfy back-dated capital levies.

Arthur felt a cold drop of sweat slide down his neck.

"Forty per cent," he whispered. "On four hundred homes."

And what of the children? He turned to Annex I, which dealt with parental responsibility under the Children Act 1989. Where parents were lawfully married at the time of birth, both acquired automatic joint custody. If the ceremony was deemed an informal gathering in an unapproved field, the legal status of hundreds of local school-children would be plunged into administrative chaos, requiring retrospective court declarations and statutory paternity assessments.

The weight of it pressed upon Arthur's chest like a stone slab. He was not merely a retired billing clerk who had spent twelve years putting on a purple stole to make village girls smile. He was the solitary keystone holding up an enormous, precarious vault of civil legitimacy across the entire county of Herefordshire. If his licence was stripped by Finch or struck down by Audrey Vance, the entire ceiling would collapse into the mud.

He looked at his watch. Quarter to three. The light outside was fading to the colour of dirty dishwater.

Audrey Vance did not take sugar. When Arthur offered the pewter bowl, she regarded the white cubes as though they represented an unregulated deduction from the parish building fund. Her fountain pen, a heavy brass instrument with an iridium point, remained poised three inches above her legal pad. Outside the vestry window, the parish jackdaws quarreled around the lead gutters of the bell tower. She did not look up. To Audrey Vance, church architecture was simply the uninsulated perimeter enclosing the real business of the church, which was the timely collection and transmission of statutory certificates. Every three years the General Synod produced another eighty pages of guidance notes on ecclesiastical boundaries, and every three years the rural deaneries lost another two dozen duplicate return envelopes behind their oak credences.

Arthur reached into his pocket, took out a peppermint humbug, and bit into it with a sharp, brittle crunch that echoed in his jaw.

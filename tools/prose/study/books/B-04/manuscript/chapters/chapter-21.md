# Chapter 21 — The Cider Pomace

The track descending from the ridge into Blackthorn Hollow was little more than a red clay trough, churned into deep liquid ruts by the passage of heavy timber sledges.

Arthur emerged from the dripping hazel coppice at half-past one that afternoon. He was coated in red mud from his knees to his waist; his Barbour jacket was soaked through at the shoulders, and his woollen cap hung limp and heavy over his ears. His leather briefcase was splattered with mud, but he held it tightly against his ribs like a mother protecting a newborn infant.

Before him opened the hollow. It was an amphitheatre of ancient perry orchards, sheltered from the mountain gales by towering sandstone bluffs. In the centre of the clearing stood the great eighteenth-century timber barn, its weathered oak weatherboarding stained the colour of dark tobacco, its roof of Welsh slate patched with corrugated iron sheets. Around it clustered several timber sheds, stacked cords of seasoned ash and oak, and rows of conical wooden apple crates filled with freshly harvested cider apples.

The air in the hollow was thick, warm, and pungent with the smell of crushed apples, wood smoke, wet sawdust, and fermenting perry pomace.

Arthur had taken three steps across the muddy yard when a sharp, metallic click sounded from behind an upturned cider vat.

"Stand right there," a voice commanded. The tone was low, rasping, and entirely devoid of hesitation. "Don't move your boots another inch into that pomace, or I'll set the lurchers on your shins."

Arthur froze.

Stepping out from behind the vat was Clementine Marsh. She was thirty-four years old, dressed in stained heavy canvas work trousers tucked into knee-high rubber boots, a faded flannel shirt with the sleeves hacked off at the elbows, and a leather pruning apron stuffed with whetstones and brass wire. Her dark hair was tied back in a careless knot with a piece of baler twine; her grey eyes were narrowed with lethal intensity, and in her right hand she held an antique billhook with a curved, razor-sharp ash blade.

"If you're from the planning inspectorate in Hereford," Clem said, taking a slow, balanced step toward him, "you can turn around and walk back over the ridge. We don't recognize your stop notices, we don't recognize your tree preservation orders, and we certainly don't pay commercial business rates on an agricultural drying shed."

"Clem," Arthur wheezed, pushing his misted spectacles up his nose with a muddy thumb. "Clem, my dear girl. It is Arthur. Arthur Pendelton."

Clementine paused. She lowered the billhook two inches, squinting through the rain at the soaked, middle-aged figure standing before her.

"Arthur?" she murmured, her voice softening in pure bewilderment. "Arthur Pendelton? Good God in heaven. What has happened to you? Did you fall out of an oak tree?"

"The ford was flooded," Arthur explained apologetically, trying to catch his breath. "The river Dore was four feet over the gate. I walked over the ridge from Turnastone. My umbrella... unfortunately, the ribs succumbed to a gust near the sheep pen."

From the open doorway of the timber workshop came the high-pitched whine of a circular saw dying down to a low drone. A man stepped out into the daylight, wiping fine yellow sawdust from his forehead with the back of an arm.

It was Leo Marsh. He had grown broader in the shoulders over ten years, his dark beard streaked with grey at the jawline, but his eyes held the same quiet, anxious gentleness that Arthur remembered from the wedding. He was holding a heavy wooden mallet in one hand and a tenon saw in the other.

"Arthur?" Leo said, his mouth falling open. "Arthur, what on earth are you doing in the Golden Valley in a cloudburst?"

"I have come about your marriage, Leo," Arthur said quietly, stepping forward into the shelter of the barn eaves. "I have come about July of twenty-sixteen."

Clem slipped the billhook into her leather apron loop. She crossed her muscular arms over her chest, looking Arthur up and down with sudden, defensive wariness.

"Our marriage?" she asked, her voice dropping into a hard, suspicious register. "What about our marriage? We've been married ten years, Arthur. We have forty acres of trees, sixty casks in the cellar, and we've never asked a single favour from church or state. Why are you dragging a briefcase through the mud to talk about vows we spoke ten years ago?"

"Because," Arthur said, resting his hand against the massive ash doorpost of the barn, "unless we can find the yellow duplicate certificate from that afternoon before tomorrow at four-thirty, your marriage does not exist. And neither do four hundred others across this county."

Leo looked at Clem. Clem looked at Leo. The rain hammered on the corrugated iron roof with a deafening roar.

"Come inside out of the wet, Arthur," Leo said quietly, opening the heavy timber door. "We'd better make a pot of tea."

Arthur followed them into the warm, dry cavern of the workshop. The space was filled with the magnificent scent of seasoned timber, boiled linseed oil, and bees-wax. Along the walls hung dozens of wooden planes, drawknives, gouges, and hand-saws, each tool polished and maintained with religious devotion. In the center of the floor stood an enormous timber cider press, its massive screws carved from heartwood pear, surrounded by tubs of fresh apple pomace.

Arthur set his muddy briefcase on a clean carpenter's trestle, took off his dripping cap, and felt the deep, restorative warmth of the woodstove wash over his frozen face.

"Right," Clem said, leaning against the apple press with an iron kettle in her hands. "Tell us every word, Arthur. And don't leave out the lawyers."

Arthur sat down on a three-legged stool, unbuttoned his Barbour jacket, and began to recount the story of the purple PDF, the Charity Commission, and Audrey Vance's brass calipers.

Clem walked Arthur along the covered timber walkway connecting the workshop to the apple pressing shed. Outside, the rain beat a relentless tattoo against the weathered slate roof, but inside the air was warm with the dry, fragrant heat of two cast-iron woodstoves burning seasoned oak offcuts.

"Look at these trees, Arthur," Clem said, pointing through the slatted timber vents toward the mist-shrouded orchard. "That row along the stream is Foxwhelp, planted in eighteen-eighty-four. Over on the terrace are Dabinett and Kingston Black. In a commercial orchard in Kent, they spray six times a summer, harvest with mechanical shakers, and sell the pulp to industrial beverage conglomerates in London. Here, Leo and I harvest by hand with ash poles and wooden canvas baskets. We press with our own timber screws, ferment in seasoned oak casks without added sulphites, and let the wild yeasts do their work."

Arthur nodded, his clerical instincts giving way to quiet reverence. "A sacred vocation, my dear. Tending the fruits of the earth."

"It isn't sacred, Arthur. It's just honest," Clem said, stopping beside an enormous wooden vat bound with hand-forged iron hoops. "When my father died in nineteen-sixteen—twenty-sixteen, I mean—the family wanted me to sell the land to a housing syndicate from Cheltenham that wanted to build sixty executive commuter chalets. They told me an uninsulated valley orchard was economically unviable. Leo and I lived in a twelve-foot caravan with a leaking roof for eighteen months. We ate wild garlic and damson jam. We cleared four tons of brambles with billhooks and a pair of Shire horses. And when you came down that lane with your purple stole, Arthur, you told us that what we were doing was holy."

Leo joined them, carrying a clean cotton cloth and a tin of saddle soap. He knelt beside Arthur's muddy wellington boots, gently wiping the red clay from the rubber uppers with steady, practiced movements.

"You gave us our beginning, Arthur," Leo said softly, looking up from the floor. "In London, every document I signed was a lie designed to sell Scandinavian laminate flooring to people who didn't need it. Here, every tenon I cut fits an honest mortise. If Audrey Vance wants to inspect our lives, she can bring her calipers and measure every timber in this barn. There isn't a crooked joint in the entire house."

Arthur looked at the two young people. The weariness of his seventy-two-hour ordeal seemed suddenly insignificant beside the fierce, unbending integrity of their lives.

"We shall find that book, Leo," Arthur said with absolute conviction. "And we shall show Mrs. Vance that some structures are built to outlast all the councils in Christendom."

Clem took Arthur's wet coat and hung it on an iron peg near the woodstove, where the steam began to rise from the heavy wool with a comforting, domestic hiss.

"You've got red marl in your eyebrows, Arthur," Clem said, handing him a clean cotton tea towel. "My grandfather used to say that if a man doesn't have red clay on his boots by noon, he hasn't done an honest day's work in Herefordshire."

"The clay is very tenacious on the ridge, Clem," Arthur remarked, drying his forehead. "It clung to my wellingtons with the tenacity of an inland revenue inspector."

Leo chuckled from the bench where he was sharpening a gouge. "The clay holds the moisture for the apple roots, Arthur. Without that heavy marl, the Kingston Black trees wouldn't survive the summer droughts. It makes life difficult for motor cars, but it makes the cider deep and strong."

Arthur sat on his three-legged stool, feeling the warmth of the fire soak into his bones. The journey across the ridge had tested his physical endurance, but standing here among friends, the trial seemed entirely worthy of the cause.

Arthur walked over to the wooden drying racks where Leo kept his seasoned ash planks. The wood was clean, white, and fragrant, stacked with dry timber stickers between each layer to allow the autumn air to circulate.

"Look at that spacing, Arthur," Leo remarked, running a thumb along an edge. "Two inches of air between every board. If you pack timber tight in a dark shed, the sap ferments and the grain rots from the core. You have to let the wind blow through it. A marriage needs air just like ash timber. You can't crowd two people into a rigid box and expect the joint to hold."

Audrey Vance smoothed out the county map on the vestry table, pinning down the corners with four lead paperweights shaped like miniature sleeping lions. The red boundaries of the civil registration district cut straight across the parish boundary, dividing the churchyard into two jurisdictions and running directly through the middle of the Marsh farm's hay storage barn. She touched the hatched area with the brass tip of her pencil. To the Registrar General, geography was a geometric grid of administrative units, each containing an audited quota of births, marriages, and burials. That a cow could graze in one district and be milked in another was, to her mind, a minor agricultural scandal that Parliament had failed to rectify in nineteen seventy-two.

Arthur smiled, nodding with deep appreciation. "True craftsmanship, Leo. Both in wood and in the spirit."

# Chapter 38 — Dissolution of Grace

At two o'clock that afternoon, a blue police saloon car arrived outside the Shirehall.

Two plainclothes detectives from the West Mercia Constabulary, accompanied by an auditor from the Charity Commission carrying a clipboard, entered the building to take formal custody of the documents surrendered by Alistair Finch.

Arthur met them in the side corridor.

He carried a manila envelope containing Dr. Finch's original 2012 charter, his laminated ordination card with the peeling gold seal, and a certified statement confirming that the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace held zero operational assets, zero bank balances, and zero active ecclesiastical congregations within the County of Herefordshire.

The Charity Commission auditor, a grey-haired man named Mr. Garside who looked thoroughly exhausted, reviewed the statement with a sharp nod of satisfaction.

"This confirms our findings in Swindon, Mr. Pendelton," Garside said, stamping the envelope with a red official seal. "The entity was an uncertified paper shell operated by Finch for private commercial benefit. The High Court winding-up order became absolute at noon today. Under Section forty-eight of the Charities Act, the denomination is formally dissolved."

"And the marriages, Mr. Garside?" Arthur asked quietly.

"Under the doctrine of putative marriage and the customary exemptions certified by Mrs. Vance," Garside explained, pocketing his pen, "all pastoral acts performed by provincial celebrants prior to the dissolution are deemed civilly valid under the common law. The state will not disturb a single home."

Arthur watched as the detectives escorted Alistair Finch—who had been apprehended in a tea shop near the cathedral while attempting to sell his broken laminator to a curate—down the stone steps to the police car.

Finch looked small, pathetic, and utterly defeated inside his oversized blazer. As he reached the car door, he looked back at Arthur.

Arthur did not look away. He offered a gentle, sorrowful bow of his head.

"Grace be multiplied unto you, Dr. Finch," Arthur whispered to the grey street. "Even from Swindon."

The police car drove away, turning toward the bypass.

The Universal Church of Perpetual Grace was gone. The thirty-five-pound diploma had dissolved into history.

Arthur turned and walked back inside, where Audrey Vance was waiting with the four hundred files.

Outside the Shirehall, Arthur watched the post office van depart with the four-thirty dispatch pouch, heading for the railway station and the national archives in Southport.

Beside him on the stone steps stood Canon Barling, who had arrived on the bus from Madley to witness the conclusion of the audit.

"A clean victory, Arthur," the old priest said, resting his hands upon his hazel walking stick. "The dragon of municipal bureaucracy has not slain the shepherd; she has invited him to tea."

"Audrey Vance is not a dragon, Christopher," Arthur said gently, watching the red van disappear around the corner of Broad Street. "She is a guardian who needed to know that our work was true. When she saw the honest timber of Blackthorn Hollow, she recognized the same duty that she has served for twenty-eight years."

"Indeed," Barling murmured with a chuckle. "And what of your retirement, Arthur? Will you return to your garden in Bargates and tend your elderberries?"

Arthur reached into his pocket, his fingers finding his new brass deputy registrar's badge.

"I have three weddings booked for this weekend, Christopher," Arthur said with a bright smile. "Megan and Toby by the duck pond in Kington tomorrow morning; Callum and Sarah on the Black Hill on Sunday afternoon; and a riverside ceremony in Hay-on-Wye with three rescue donkeys."

Canon Barling laughed aloud. "Then God help the donkeys, Arthur. And God bless the deputy registrar of the Western Marches."

Arthur stood on the pavement outside the Shirehall as the afternoon sun began to dip behind the roofs of Hereford.

The breeze was cold, carrying the clean scent of winter from the Malvern Hills. In his pocket, his fingers clicked against his three rolls of peppermint humbugs and his brass calipers.

The shadow of the audit had passed. The threat of defrocking had dissolved.

He turned toward his parked Astra, ready for the journey home to Leominster, feeling the quiet, enduring satisfaction of a duty faithfully performed.

Arthur watched the clock on the Shirehall tower strike four-forty-five.

The sun had sunk behind the red roofs of Hereford, leaving a trail of rose-coloured clouds across the evening sky.

He reached into his pocket, took out his brass calipers, and felt the cool, clean metal against his fingers.

The Universal Church of Perpetual Grace was gone, but the marriages were alive. The people had their homes, their land, and their peace.

The autumn twilight arrived early, draining the color from the hills and turning the orchard trees into dark, jagged silhouettes against the pale western horizon. Arthur switched on the green-shaded reading lamp on the vestry table. The small circle of warm yellow light illuminated the open pages of the certificate file, casting long, dramatic shadows across the stone floor. Outside, the headlights of an occasional tractor swept along the valley road, their beams cutting through the gathering ground mist before vanishing behind the churchyard hedge.

Arthur turned his steps toward Gaol Street, ready for the journey back to Leominster, his heart singing with quiet, untroubled gratitude.

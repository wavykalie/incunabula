# Chapter 23 — The Sawdust Confession

Before they climbed the ladder to the hayloft, Leo Marsh caught Arthur gently by the elbow and guided him into the small timber alcove behind the tool racks.

The alcove smelled of fragrant cedar shavings, beeswax, and sweet pipe tobacco. On a low shelf sat a row of miniature architectural models: timber trusses, cruck frames, and apple press joints carved from scrap pine with microscopic precision.

Leo looked down at his sawdust-covered boots. His broad shoulders were hunched in an agony of embarrassment.

"Arthur," Leo whispered, his voice cracking with shame. "Before we go up there... I have to make a confession."

Arthur turned to him with mild, patient eyes. "A confession, Leo?"

"The certificate," the young man stammered, pulling a piece of folded sandpaper from his pocket and rolling it between his fingers. "The commemorative top copy. The one with the gold seal and the Latin lettering that you handed to Clem."

"Yes?" Arthur asked, his heart giving a small, nervous leap. "What happened to it?"

Leo closed his eyes. "In twenty-eighteen, during the second winter, I was designing the new hydraulic ram for the apple press. We had no paper in the workshop. The snow was six feet deep outside the barn, the lane was blocked for three weeks, and my drawing pad had run out. I needed a sheet of heavy vellum card that wouldn't tear when I laid out the gear ratios with steel dividers."

Arthur stared at him. "You didn't."

"I did, Arthur," Leo groaned, burying his face in his hands. "I took the commemorative certificate off the dresser in the caravan. The back was completely blank and smooth. I pinned it to my drawing board and drew the entire engineering schematic for the two-ton hydraulic ram across the reverse. I used indelible drafting ink and red waterproof felt-tip. And then... when we were assembling the press in the spring, I spilled a pint of unrefined linseed oil across the face."

Arthur took a deep, steadying breath. "The commemorative copy is ruined?"

"It looks like an old chip wrapper, Arthur," Leo whispered miserably. "The gold seal dissolved into greasy yellow paste, and Clem's signature is covered in oil stains. If Audrey Vance saw it, she'd have me arrested for desecration of public records."

Arthur placed a comforting hand on the carpenter's shoulder. A faint, wry smile touched the corners of his mouth.

"Do not torment yourself, Leo," Arthur said gently. "The commemorative top copy was merely a decorative keepsake for your mantelpiece. It possesses no legal standing in any court of the realm. The document that matters—the only document that Audrey Vance's calipers will acknowledge—is the yellow duplicate counter-foil that remained in the hardbound book. That is the statutory record."

Leo looked up, a glimmer of desperate hope in his dark eyes. "You're sure?"

"I spent twenty-two years with the water board, my boy," Arthur said with mild clerical authority. "I know the difference between a decorative receipt and an enforceable counter-foil. Now let us climb that ladder before the daylight fails."

Leo unfolded the commemorative parchment and laid it out across his workbench under the glow of the kerosene lantern.

Arthur leaned close to inspect the damage.

The upper portion of the certificate—the grand ecclesiastical heading that Dr. Finch had designed in Swindon—was indeed translucent with dried linseed oil, giving the printed clip-art crosses a greasy, amber sheen. Across the Latin text, Leo had drawn a series of exquisitely detailed engineering diagrams in red and black technical drafting ink: cross-sections of hydraulic valves, stress diagrams for two-inch ash planks, and mathematical calculations for fluid displacement under a thirty-ton load.

Across the bottom, where Clem's bold signature was inscribed, sat a large circular brown ring where a mug of hot tea had been placed during the cold winter of 2018.

"It is an extraordinary historical document, Leo," Arthur said, tilting his head with genuine artistic appreciation. "A marriage certificate combined with the operational schematics for a two-ton apple press. Leonardo da Vinci would have admired it immensely."

Leo gave a weak, shamefaced laugh. "I felt terrible about it, Arthur. Truly, I did. But every time I turn the valve on that press and watch forty gallons of juice pour into the cistern, I look at that drawing and I remember that Clem and I built this together. Our marriage isn't just words spoken in the straw, Arthur. It's the press. It's the cider. It's the roof over our heads."

Arthur gently touched the corner of the oily vellum.

"And that, my dear boy, is precisely what the law forgot two hundred years ago," Arthur murmured. "The law thought a marriage was ink on vellum. It forgot that ink is merely the shadow cast by human labour and love."

He patted Leo's arm. "Now, let us climb into the rafters and find the shadow that satisfies Mrs. Vance."

Arthur smiled gently at the carpenter.

"In twenty-two years with the water board, Leo, I saw thousands of official plans," Arthur said, his hand resting on the ash bench. "Half of them were drawn on council vellum and ended up shredded in municipal basements. But your plan built a press that feeds a family. That makes it holy work in my eyes."

A robin perched on the handle of the parish wheelbarrow, observing Arthur with black, unblinking eyes. The bird gave a brief, liquid trill that echoed against the north wall of the nave, clear and piercing in the damp autumn air. Country churchyards were populated by creatures that had reached an amicable accommodation with the clergy: rooks in the beech tops, field mice under the floorboards, and robins that regarded every upturned spade of earth as their private entitlement.

Leo gave a quiet sigh of relief and picked up his lantern.

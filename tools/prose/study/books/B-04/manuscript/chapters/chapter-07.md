# Chapter 7 — The £35 Parchment

To understand how Arthur Pendelton came to be the sole apostolic representative of the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace for the Western Marches, it is necessary to examine the peculiar circumstances of his ordination in September of 2012.

At that time, Arthur had just taken early retirement from the municipal water board, following a reorganization that replaced twenty-four regional billing clerks with an automated software package operated from a business park in Warrington. Arthur had been awarded a modest lump-sum gratuity, a commemorative carriage clock made of imitation brass, and a chronic sense of purposelessness that caused him to walk five miles every morning along the disused railway track to Kimbolton.

His sister Clara's sudden decision to marry Gethin Price in a Pembridge hayfield had presented an immediate administrative obstacle. The parish vicar, a rigid man named the Reverend Mr. Hallowell who grew prize marrow in his rectory garden, had informed Clara that the Church of England could not countenance the solemnization of holy matrimony in an agricultural enclosure containing unharvested clover, citing an episcopal decree of eighteen-eighty-four regarding the dignity of the sacraments. The municipal registrar in Hereford had been equally obstructive, demanding structural safety certificates for Gethin's timber gazebo and proof that the pasture was accessible to emergency fire appliances.

Arthur, sitting in his armchair with his dial-up modem shrieking its electronic mating call, had typed into a search engine: *independent wedding officiant legal certification UK*.

The search engine had returned three results. The first was an accredited interfaith seminary in Oxford that required three years of residential theological study and four thousand pounds in tuition fees. The second was an environmental humanist collective in Bristol that required all officiants to demonstrate proficiency in acoustic guitar and Morris dancing.

The third result was a webpage with a dark purple background, decorated with animated twinkling stars and an image of an illuminated parchment scroll. Across the top ran the banner: *THE UNIVERSAL CHURCH OF PERPETUAL GRACE (INCORPORATED 1999) — RAPID MINISTERIAL ACCREDITATION FOR SINCERE HEARTS*.

The text on the website was authored by the Reverend Doctor Alistair Finch, D.D., whose biography stated that he was a former senior lecturer in industrial sociology at a polytechnic in Gloucestershire who had received an undeniable spiritual calling during an international academic symposium on supply-chain logistics in Reno, Nevada.

While in Nevada, Dr. Finch had discovered that the state's liberal marriage statutes permitted ordained ministers of any recognized corporate denomination to perform legally binding ceremonies with minimal municipal oversight. Dr. Finch had promptly incorporated the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace under Nevada law, primarily to provide spiritual invocations and ceremonial blessings for the opening meetings of greyhound racing syndicates and antique car rallies in Washoe County.

Upon his return to the United Kingdom, Dr. Finch had realized that the British Charities Act and the European Convention on Human Rights provided a surprisingly porous framework for non-traditional religious ministries. By registering the church as a private company limited by guarantee and filing annual informational returns claiming an active pastoral mission among itinerant rural populations, Dr. Finch had succeeded in obtaining provisional recognition from the General Register Office as an extra-parochial religious denomination.

The ordination process was simplicity itself. The applicant was required to tick three boxes:

1. *I affirm that I possess a sincere desire to minister unto human affection without regard to conventional ecclesiastical dogma.*
2. *I agree to conduct all solemnizations in a reverent and sober manner, avoiding blasphemous, political, or overtly theatrical elements.*
3. *I enclose the administrative processing and lamination fee of £35.00 via secure electronic debit transfer.*

Arthur had ticked all three boxes. He had entered his debit card details.

Five days later, a stiff cardboard envelope had arrived in the morning post. Inside lay the certificate. It was printed on heavy cream paper that smelled strongly of industrial varnish. The text was in Latin, rendered in an elaborate fraktur typeface: *Universitas Gratiae Perpetuae... Praesbyterum Diocesis Marchensis... Arthurus Reginaldus Pendelton...*

At the bottom of the page was a large circular seal made of embossed gold foil, depicting a dove holding what appeared to be an open ledger in its claws, surrounded by the Latin motto: *VITA BREVIS, LEX DURA, CARITAS SEMPER*. Life is short, the law is hard, love is forever.

Attached to the certificate was a typed letter on blue headed paper signed by Dr. Finch:

"Dear Brother Pendelton: Welcome to the harvest field. You are now authorized to minister grace unto the Western Marches. Conduct your ceremonies with dignity, collect no mandatory tariffs, and retain your receipts. In the event of statutory inquiry by civil registrars, cite Section 26(1) of the Marriages Act 1949 and remain calm. The Lord and the Companies Act are your shields."

Arthur had framed the parchment and hung it on the wall of his spare bedroom above the filing cabinet.

For twelve years, the certificate had hung there, silently gathering dust. Arthur had looked at it every time he retrieved a fresh ceremonial stole from his drawer. It had given him comfort. It had made him feel that when he stood in a muddy field telling two young people that love was the only thing that survived the rust of the world, he was not merely an unemployed water board clerk playing with words.

He was an ordained presbyter.

The damp in the chancel wall smelled of cold moss and dead lime. Arthur ran his fingertips along the mortar joints beneath the east lancet window. The stone was cool and slick, holding the memory of three weeks of unremitting autumn drizzle. A parish church was not a closed building in the modern sense; it was an enormous stone sponge that absorbed rain through its roof slates, breathed moisture through its porous ashlar, and exhaled it gently across the pews in a faint, perpetual mist. When the sun broke through the storm clouds, the wet slate outside shone like wet river stones.

Now, looking through the kitchen window at the grey drizzle falling over Leominster, Arthur realized that his entire twelve-year ministry had been built upon an internet certificate purchased for thirty-five pounds, originally drafted to bless greyhound tracks in the Nevada desert.

# Chapter 30 — The Morning Deadline

At six o'clock on Friday morning, the first pale grey light of dawn broke over the sandstone ridge of the Black Mountains.

Arthur was awake before the birds. He had slept four hours on a clean wool mattress in the spare bedroom of the farmhouse, wrapped in three heavy Welsh woollen blankets.

He washed his face in freezing water from the enamel pitcher, dressed in his trousers—which Clem had miraculously washed, dried by the woodstove, and mended at both knees with neat canvas patches—and put on his clean collar and his tweed jacket.

He carried his briefcase into the farmhouse kitchen. On the scrubbed pine table sat a breakfast of fried eggs, thick back bacon, and a pot of strong black tea that Clem had brewed on the wood range.

Leo was already seated at the table, studying the canvas Ordnance Survey map under the oil lamp.

"The river has dropped eighteen inches since midnight, Arthur," Leo reported, pointing with his pencil. "The Dore has retreated within its banks at Turnastone. The ford is still running fast, but the water is down to fifteen inches over the gravel. My four-wheel-drive Ferguson tractor can tow the Astra across without wetting the spark plugs."

Arthur looked at his watch. Six-thirty.

"Dr. Finch's deadline in Swindon is twelve noon," Arthur said. "Audrey Vance's dispatch pouch closes at four-thirty."

"We'll have you across the river by eight," Clem said, setting a plate of hot toast before him. "And Leo and I are coming with you."

Arthur looked up in surprise. "To Hereford? To the Shirehall?"

"Do you think we're going to let you face that woman with the calipers alone?" Clem asked, her grey eyes flashing with fierce loyalty. "That yellow certificate has our names on it, Arthur. It has our signatures, our thumbprints, and our barn. If Audrey Vance wants to cross-examine someone about what happened under that ash beam ten years ago, she can look me in the eye and ask her questions."

"And what about the other couples?" Leo added quietly. "I called young Robin Vance on the radio last night. The blacksmith in Peterchurch knows forty families in the valley who were married by Arthur. They've heard about the audit, Arthur. Word has spread down the river like wildfire."

Arthur felt a lump rise in his throat. "What have they done, Leo?"

"You'll see when we reach St. Peter's Square," Leo said with a quiet smile.

At half-past seven, the great Ferguson tractor rumbled out of the yard, towing Arthur's Vauxhall Astra through the red mud of the orchard. Leo sat at the tractor wheel in his oilskins, while Clem sat on the mudguard, holding Arthur's briefcase wrapped in a waterproof rubber sheet.

They reached the ford at Turnastone. The river Dore was still a rushing brown torrent, but the iron gate was visible above the water. The Ferguson plunged into the current, its massive lugged tyres churning the gravel, hauling the light saloon car through the spray with effortless agricultural power.

The Astra emerged on the tarmac of the B4348 without a cough from its exhaust.

Leo unhitched the tow chain, wiped his hands on an oily rag, and climbed into the passenger seat beside Arthur. Clem climbed into the back, sitting amidst the boxes of stoles and dried lavender.

Arthur turned the ignition key. The Astra's starter motor gave two sharp clicks, caught, and settled into its familiar, vibrating hum.

Arthur looked at his dashboard clock: eight-fifteen.

He had three hours and forty-five minutes before Finch's deadline, and eight hours and fifteen minutes before Audrey's dispatch pouch was sealed.

He engaged first gear and drove east toward Hereford.

Arthur sat at the farmhouse kitchen table, finishing the last of his black tea while the grandfather clock in the corner struck seven with a heavy brass chime.

Before him lay his leather briefcase, repacked with meticulous, clerical precision.

Inside the case rested the red buckram folio from the sycamore tub, wrapped in clean linen; the certified parchment transcript bearing the red wax seal and the spouses' signatures; his brass magnifying glass; his water board reference manual; and three fresh rolls of peppermint humbugs that Clem had unearthed from an emergency pantry tin behind the pickling jars.

Clem walked in from the scullery, carrying a thermos flask filled with hot sweet coffee and a greaseproof paper package containing four thick bacon sandwiches.

"The road through the Golden Valley is clear as far as Peterchurch, Arthur," Clem reported, adjusting the straps of her heavy oilskin coat. "Young Robin called on the radio. The council road gritter has been through to clear the fallen branches, and the river Dore has dropped another foot. We can reach the A438 in forty minutes."

Leo entered through the back door, carrying the ignition key of the Ferguson tractor.

"Tractor is running, Arthur," Leo said, his face bright with purpose. "The Astra is hitched with a double nylon tow strop. If we encounter standing water on the flats near Madley, I'll pull you through with the differential lock engaged."

Arthur stood up from the table. He smoothed his tweed jacket, adjusted his woollen tie, and picked up his briefcase.

For seventy hours, he had been running from an invisible administrative terror. He had fled from emails, from registered letters, from statutory threats, and from the cold shadow of Audrey Vance's calipers. He had felt like an old sheep chased down an unfamiliar lane by barking dogs.

Now, standing in the farmhouse kitchen with the certified transcript under his arm and two brave young people beside him, the terror had vanished entirely.

He was no longer running. He was advancing.

He was marching upon the Shirehall in Hereford not as a panicked culprit begging for mercy, but as an accredited presbyter of the Welsh Marches bearing the authentic statutory records of his flock. He was going to defend four hundred marriages, four hundred homes, and twelve years of honest pastoral devotion before the full majesty of the British constitution.

He turned to Leo and Clem.

"Right, my dears," Arthur said, his voice ringing with calm, quiet authority. "Let us drive to Hereford and settle the account."

They stepped out into the crisp morning sunlight. The sky above the Black Mountains was pale blue and clear; the autumn mist was burning away from the perry orchards, revealing thousands of yellow leaves glittering with morning frost.

The Ferguson tractor rumbled to life with a deep, confident roar, and Arthur's sea-grey Astra rolled out of Blackthorn Hollow toward the high road to Hereford.

Arthur stood by the Astra's open door in the farmyard, checking his tyre pressures with his silver pencil gauge.

The cold morning air was sharp with the scent of pine needles and damp earth. Across the valley, the high sandstone crest of the Black Mountains was catching the first pink rays of the rising sun, standing clear and sharp against the pale turquoise sky.

"Thirty-two pounds on the front, thirty on the rear," Arthur muttered with satisfaction, screwing the brass valve cap back on. "The suspension will handle the ruts as far as the turnpike."

Leo walked over from the tool shed, carrying an iron tow bar and a box of spare fan belts.

"We have two hours to reach Hereford, Arthur," Leo said, checking his watch. "The road through Vowchurch is clear of debris, but there are patches of standing water near the old railway bridge. Keep the Astra in second gear through the dips, and follow the tractor's wheel tracks."

Clem climbed into the rear seat, placing her boots on an old grain sack to keep the floor clean.

"Don't worry about the dips, Leo," Clem said with a cheerful grin. "Arthur drove water board vans through the great floods of nineteen-ninety-eight. He knows every pothole in the Golden Valley by its Christian name."

Arthur smiled, climbed into the driver's seat, and turned the key. The Astra hummed to life with clean, steady purpose.

Arthur checked his briefcase one final time, verifying that the certified transcript, the red folio, and his water board seal were safely nested in their compartments.

"Remember, Arthur," Clem said as she checked the tractor's hitch pin, "if that woman tries to browbeat you with modern council standing orders, you tell her that Blackthorn Hollow was making perry when her great-grandfather was wearing pinafores. We don't take instruction on cider from Hereford."

"Audrey Vance knows her cider history, Clem," Arthur reminded her with a smile. "She is a Herefordshire woman to her boots. We must appeal to her honour, not her temper."

"Just don't let her take your stole," Clem grinned, climbing onto the mudguard. "You look naked without your gold thread."

Rain hammered against the leaded panes of the south aisle with the fury of a burst water main. A small brown rivulet was already creeping across the encaustic tiles near the pulpit, fed by a blocked hopper on the north clerestory. Arthur fetched a galvanized bucket from the boiler house, placing it directly beneath the leak with a practiced thump. The water struck the zinc bottom with a sharp, resonant ding that repeated every three seconds. Audrey Vance stood beside the lectern, her arms folded tightly across her wool cardigan, watching the rising waterline in the bucket with grim, professional disapproval.

Leo engaged the low-range gearbox, and the tractor rumbled forward into the lane, its diesel exhaust rising into the cold morning sky.

# Chapter 19 — The Registry Waiting Room

Before embarking on his journey to the Golden Valley, Arthur made one final, calculated detour to the Shirehall in Hereford. He needed to verify the precise statutory format required for a duplicate certificate submission under Section 44.

The registry office waiting room was crowded when Arthur entered at ten-fifteen. The damp weather had driven dozens of applicants indoors, filling the high-backed oak benches with a cross-section of rural Herefordshire society.

In the corner sat an anxious young couple from Bromyard, their fingers entwined as they whispered over a folder of utility bills and baptismal certificates. Beside them sat a middle-aged hill farmer in clean tweed, staring straight ahead with the grim stoicism of a man waiting for a veterinarian's verdict on a diseased heifer. At the enquiry hatch, an agitated woman with a pushchair was attempting to explain to Miss Cleary that her child's birth certificate had been lost during a house move to Ross-on-Wye.

Arthur took a seat on the oak bench nearest the corridor. He sat quietly, his hands resting on his leather briefcase, observing the rhythmic machinery of civil administration.

Behind the frosted glass partition, the date stamps continued their steady percussion: *Thump. Slide. Thump. Slide.* Every sound represented a human life being formally pinned to the statutory record of the realm. A birth recorded; a death certified; a union gazetted under the crown crest.

At ten-thirty, the double doors of the inner office opened, and Audrey Vance stepped out into the waiting room.

She carried a leather-bound folio beneath her arm and a pair of brass calipers in her right hand. Her grey hair was immaculate, and her charcoal suit was without a single crease. As she crossed the parquet floor, the room fell silent; the young couple stopped whispering, the farmer straightened his back, and the woman with the pushchair lowered her voice to an apologetic murmur.

Audrey approached the young couple from Bromyard.

"Mr. Davies? Miss Prosser?" she said, her voice clear and measured.

The young people rose hastily to their feet. The boy dropped his folder, scattering photocopies of water rates across the floorboards. He scrambled to retrieve them, his face turning the colour of a ripe beet.

Audrey stood motionless until he had gathered the papers. Then she reached down, took the folder from his hands, and inspected the top document.

"Your notice of marriage has completed the statutory twenty-eight days of public gazetting, Mr. Davies," she said. Her voice was not unkind, but it was completely devoid of personal warmth. "There have been no caveats lodged, and your declarations of freedom to marry are in order under Section sixteen."

The boy let out an audible gasp of relief. The girl began to cry quietly, dabbing her eyes with a paper tissue.

"However," Audrey continued, adjusting her reading spectacles with that terrifying rhythmic snap, "I note from your application that you have requested a civil ceremony in an unroofed timber pavilion on the grounds of an organic vineyard near Ledbury."

The boy swallowed. "Yes, Mrs. Vance. We... we thought it would be lovely among the vines."

Audrey opened her folio. She pulled out a printed copy of the County Approved Premises Regulations 1995.

"Under Regulation four, Mr. Davies, approved premises must consist of a permanent, immovable structure with a solid roof and identifiable perimeter walls. A timber pavilion with canvas drop-curtains does not qualify as an approved structure. If it rains on your wedding morning and the canvas flaps are lowered, the solemnization becomes legally void under Section forty-four."

The girl's shoulders sagged. "Does that mean... we can't marry there?"

Audrey looked at the girl. For three seconds, Arthur watched the Superintendent Registrar's face. The steel lines around her mouth softened almost imperceptibly; her eyes held a flicker of genuine, unvoiced sorrow.

"It means, Miss Prosser, that you must conduct the statutory ceremony inside the vineyard's stone pressing shed, which is fully approved under the Act. You may sign the registers there beneath the crown crest. Afterward, you may walk out into the pavilion, drink champagne among your vines, and have whatever poetry read that pleases your heart. But the law requires the stone roof first."

She handed the folder back to the boy.

"My clerks will schedule your notice for the stone shed on December third. Good morning."

Audrey turned to return to her office. As she reached the corridor, her eyes met Arthur's.

She paused. She looked at Arthur's muddy wellington boots, his wax-cotton Barbour jacket, and the heavy brass torch protruding from his pocket.

"Mr. Pendelton," she said quietly, stepping close to his bench.

"Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied, rising politely.

"The barometer is falling rapidly, Mr. Pendelton," she said, her voice barely above a murmur. "The Environment Agency has issued a flood alert for the Monnow catchment. The river is rising six inches an hour."

"I am aware of the weather, Mrs. Vance."

"If you are driving into the Golden Valley, take the high road through Peterchurch," she said, looking down at her brass calipers. "The low ford at Turnastone was washed out in nineteen-ninety-eight. A municipal water board van was carried fifty yards downstream and had to be winched out by the fire brigade."

Arthur stared at her. "You remember that incident?"

"I signed the certificate of loss for the county surveyor," she said evenly. "I remember every vehicle that has ever drowned in that valley, Mr. Pendelton. Do not let your Astra become another entry in our archives."

She turned on her heel and walked through the mahogany doors into her office.

Arthur stood on the parquet floor, clutching his briefcase.

Audrey Vance had just given him operational advice. The high statutory prosecutor of Herefordshire had warned him off the ford.

Arthur hurried out into St. Peter's Square, climbed into his Astra, and started the engine.

Arthur watched Audrey Vance as she returned to her desk behind the glass partition.

Through the frosted panel, he could see her sitting beneath the high Victorian window, her back straight as an iron rail. She dipped her fountain pen into the black inkwell, lifted a heavy ledger sheet, and began inscribing an entry with deliberate, rhythmic strokes. She did not hurry; she did not hesitate. Every character was formed with the absolute geometric precision of a monk illuminating a psalter.

Arthur realized then why Audrey Vance loved her records. In a world where weather ruined crops, where young men died on roads, and where human affection was constantly battered by poverty, grief, and change, the county register was the only place where order was permanent. An entry made in nineteen-ninety-six was still clean and unbroken in twenty-twenty-six. It was an anchor in the storm.

Arthur walked to the enquiry hatch. Miss Cleary looked up from her computer terminal.

"Yes, Mr. Pendelton?"

"Miss Cleary," Arthur said politely, "could you confirm the statutory fee for lodging a retrospective duplicate counter-foil under Section forty-four?"

Miss Cleary tapped her keyboard. "Retrospective lodgement under the customary exemptions carries an administrative search fee of twenty-one pounds, sir, plus three pounds fifty for the certified crown seal."

Arthur reached into his pocket and placed twenty-four pounds and fifty pence in exact coin upon the wooden counter.

"Please hold that against my account, Miss Cleary," Arthur said with a gentle smile. "I shall return with the document before four-thirty tomorrow afternoon."

Miss Cleary looked at the neat stack of silver coins, then looked up into Arthur's calm, tired eyes.

"Very good, Mr. Pendelton," she said softly, sliding the coins into a metal cash tin. "The Superintendent Registrar will be waiting."

Arthur turned and walked out through the heavy entrance doors into the rain.

While Arthur waited in the registry office foyer, he watched a young couple approach the enquiry counter.

The groom was a shy young forester from the Golden Valley, his face weathered by wind and rain, wearing a brand-new tweed jacket with the price tag still pinned inside the collar. Beside him stood his bride, a dark-haired girl in a simple blue dress, holding a bunch of wild snowdrops wrapped in damp newspaper.

Miss Cleary took their notice forms through the hatch.

"The fee for the civil ceremony in the committee room is sixty-two pounds fifty, Mr. Powell," Miss Cleary said, her fingers hovering over her calculator. "Plus fourteen pounds for two certified copies of the register entry."

The young forester's face fell. He reached into his trousers pocket, pulling out a handful of silver coins and two crumpled ten-pound notes. He counted the money on the wooden shelf, his broad thumbs trembling. He was six pounds short.

Arthur rose from his oak bench, stepped softly across the parquet floor, and laid a ten-pound note beside the boy's coins.

The forester looked up, startled. "Sir... I couldn't."

"Take it, my boy," Arthur whispered with a gentle smile. "A wedding gift from a fellow labourer of the valley. Marriages must not halt for six pounds."

The boy looked at Arthur's kind eyes, swallowed hard, and nodded with profound gratitude. "Thank you, sir. God bless you."

From behind the glass partition, Audrey Vance had emerged from her private office to fetch a reference ledger. She paused, observing the transaction across the counter. Her eyes met Arthur's through the glass hatch.

She did not reprimand him. She did not quote council financial regulations. She simply inclined her head a fraction of an inch—a silent, sovereign recognition between two public servants who understood that the dignity of the people was worth more than the rules of the Treasury.

Arthur gave a polite bow, turned on his heel, and walked out into the square.

Arthur stepped into the side corridor of the Shirehall, watching the junior clerks file past with their green folders.

A young registrar's assistant named Miss Morris, who had worked in the basement since twenty-twenty, paused beside him to adjust her cardigan.

"You look determined today, Mr. Pendelton," she observed with a shy smile.

"Determined and punctual, Miss Morris," Arthur replied, dipping his head politely. "A public servant must never allow his boots to lag behind his duty."

"Mrs. Vance has been in the vault since seven this morning," the girl whispered. "She had us pull the nineteenth-century boundary registers for the Golden Valley. She hasn't spoken three words all morning, but she has three pencils sharpened down to the wood."

Arthur smiled gently. "Mrs. Vance is preparing the ground, Miss Morris. As any good surveyor must."

The public bar of the Red Lion had a coal fire that smoked whenever the wind blew from the south-east over the Malvern ridge. Arthur took a corner table beneath the framed photograph of the nineteen fifty-three coronation cricket eleven. The table was sticky with spilled bitter and linseed oil. Clem Marsh sat with his broad elbows planted firmly on the scrubbed pine, turning a half-pint mug of dry cider between his calloused thumbs. When a man had spent thirty years setting oak dowels and bending ash hoops, his hands took on the color and hardness of seasoned heartwood, impervious to splinters, vinegar, and diocesan summonses alike.

He thanked her and walked out into St. Peter's Square.

# Chapter 10 — The Barn Anomaly

Arthur Pendelton's pocket diary for the year 2016 was a slim, leatherette volume in dark green, issued compliments of the West Midlands Farmers' Cooperative. The front cover bore a small gold emblem of a wheat sheaf and a sheep's head, both slightly tarnished around the edges.

Arthur turned the thin, gilt-edged pages until he reached the month of July.

The entries in the diary were written in his neat water board script:

*July 2 — Haymaking commenced at Lower Hope. Warm.*
*July 9 — Funeral of old Mr. Gwillam at Kimbolton. Very damp.*
*July 16 — 2:30 PM: Leo and Clem Marsh. The Cider Barn, Blackthorn Hollow, Vowchurch. Note: Road very rough after the ford. Bring rubber boots and spare stole.*

Arthur laid his thumb across the entry for July 16th.

Of all the four hundred ceremonies he had conducted over twelve years, the Marsh wedding at Blackthorn Hollow stood out in his memory with peculiar, diamond-hard clarity.

It had not been a conventional outdoor wedding. Clementine Marsh—Clem—was the daughter of a wealthy landowning family near Ledbury who had disowned her when she eloped with Leo Marsh, a brilliant but chronically anxious furniture maker from London who had suffered a nervous collapse after designing retail display units for luxury department stores. Together, they had purchased twenty acres of derelict perry orchard in the most isolated corner of the Golden Valley, living in an uninsulated caravan while they restored an eighteenth-century timber threshing barn and planted traditional cider apple varieties.

They had refused to marry in a church because Clem regarded the diocesan land agents as feudal parasites, and they had refused the registry office because Leo suffered from acute agoraphobia whenever he entered a municipal building with fluorescent strip lighting.

Arthur had driven down to Blackthorn Hollow in his Astra on a scorching Saturday afternoon in mid-July. The lane had been little more than a dry gully between towering hazel hedges, with wild roses brushing against his car doors.

The ceremony had taken place inside the great timber barn. The air had been thick with the sweet, fermented scent of old perry pomace and freshly sawn English oak. Leo had laid clean yellow straw across the earthen threshing floor, and Clem had walked down the central aisle in green gumboots and an unironed linen dress, carrying an armful of flowering elder branches.

Arthur had stood beneath the massive ash tie-beam, wearing his gold stole over his short-sleeved cotton shirt. When Leo had tried to speak his vows, his voice had failed him entirely; he had stood shaking like an aspen leaf in a gale, his carpenter's hands trembling so violently that he could not hold the ring. Arthur had laid a calm, warm hand on the boy's shoulder, looked him straight in the eyes, and whispered: "Take your time, Leo. The wood isn't going anywhere, and neither is Clem."

Leo had smiled through his tears, nodded, and repeated the words with profound, absolute devotion.

And then came the administrative detail that separated the Marsh wedding from every other ceremony Arthur had ever performed.

In the summer of 2016, Dr. Finch had briefly attempted to introduce a standardized ceremonial certificate system. He had sent Arthur a single, hardbound book of official certificates printed on heavy vellum paper, with pre-numbered counter-foils and formal registration stamps. The Marsh wedding was the first—and only—ceremony entered into that official book.

Arthur had filled in the certificate on an upturned perry barrel, using Leo's draughtsman's ink. Clem had signed it, Leo had signed it, and two cider press workers had affixed their thumbprints in apple juice because they had forgotten their reading spectacles.

Arthur had torn the commemorative top copy from the perforations and handed it to Clem. The yellow duplicate copy—the legal counter-foil that was supposed to be returned to the registry—had remained in the book.

And where was the book?

Arthur stood up from the kitchen table and walked into the small boxroom beneath the stairs that served as his household archive. The room was illuminated by a single unshaded forty-watt bulb. It smelled of old cardboard, paraffin, and mothballs.

Stacked against the brick wall were thirty-two cardboard wine boxes, each labelled in black marker pen with a year.

Arthur knelt on the rag rug and located the box marked *2016 — MISCELLANEOUS AND WATER BOARD PENSIONS*.

He pulled the flaps open. Inside lay four years of bank statements, three water rate demand notices, a broken plastic electric kettle, a box of unused wedding sparklers, and an envelope containing forty-five receipts for unleaded petrol purchased at the Texaco garage in Leominster.

There was no hardbound certificate book.

Arthur pulled the contents out onto the floor, sorting through the papers one by one with frantic, cold fingers. He checked behind the pension folders. He checked inside a biscuit tin containing old Christmas cards. He checked under an ancient telephone directory for the year nineteen-ninety-nine.

The book was not there.

Arthur sat back on his heels amidst the scattered receipts. His heart was beating with a slow, heavy thud that reverberated in his ears.

The yellow duplicate certificate from the Blackthorn Hollow barn was the only physical document in existence that bore Dr. Finch's official corporate church stamp, Arthur's licentiate registration number, and the verified signatures of two contracting spouses. It was the sole evidentiary bridge between Arthur's twelve years of informal field blessings and the statutory requirements of the Marriages Act 1949.

If that certificate existed, Arthur could prove that the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace maintained an active, structured registration system in Herefordshire. He could use it as the foundational benchmark to reconstruct the remaining logs.

If that certificate was lost, the entire twelve-year ministry was legally invisible.

Arthur looked down at his green pocket diary. Beside Leo and Clem Marsh's names was a telephone number written in faded purple felt-tip: *Peterchurch 441*.

Arthur rose to his feet, climbed out from beneath the stairs, and walked back to the telephone in the hallway.

He lifted the receiver and dialled the operator.

Arthur closed the diary and sat back in his chair. He remembered Leo Marsh's workshop at Blackthorn Hollow with acute sharpness.

Leo had not merely been a carpenter; he had been an artist of wood grain and joinery. In the corner of the barn had stood a massive apple press constructed entirely of seasoned English ash and heartwood oak, without a single iron bolt. Leo had cut the mortise and tenon joints by hand, securing them with seasoned oak trunnels driven home with a wooden beetle. During the wedding reception, while the guests drank cloudy cider from stoneware mugs, Leo had taken Arthur's hand and placed his fingers upon the main upright beam.

"Feel that, Arthur," the boy had said, his voice quiet with reverent wonder. "That wood has been drying for four summers. When you fit an honest tenon into a clean mortise, the joint holds for two hundred years without glue. That's what marriage is, isn't it? An honest joint that doesn't split when the timber shrinks."

Arthur had never forgotten those words. Of all the ceremonies he had blessed, Leo and Clem's marriage was the one where human intention, craft, and love had reached perfect, unforced harmony.

If that wedding had no legal status, then nothing Arthur had done in twelve years possessed meaning. It was the keystone of his entire life.

Arthur took down the third volume of the county gazetteer, an imposing Victorian folio bound in green cloth with beveled boards. The spine cracked softly as he laid it flat upon the kitchen table. Inside were detailed maps of every rural deanery in the diocese, surveyed in eighteen eighty-five by retired Royal Engineers with an obsessive interest in bench marks, parish boundaries, and the precise acreage of glebe land. He traced the course of the Lea brook with his forefinger, noting how the stream looped through the water meadows before disappearing beneath the stone bridge at Marsh Farm.

He lifted the heavy brass handset of the telephone and listened to the dial tone. With steady fingers, he dialled the operator and asked for Peterchurch 441.

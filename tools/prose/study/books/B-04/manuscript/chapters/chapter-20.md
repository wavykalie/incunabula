# Chapter 20 — Setting Out for Blackthorn

The windscreen wipers of the Vauxhall Astra beat a frantic, rhythmic duet against the glass as Arthur drove west along the A438 out of Hereford.

The rain was no longer a gentle drizzle. It was a torrential November deluge, sweeping across the red clay fields in blinding grey curtains. The drainage ditches along the roadside were already overflowing, sending brown streams of muddy water gushing across the tarmac. Every time a cattle lorry passed in the opposite direction, a massive wave of spray crashed over the Astra's bonnet, momentarily blinding Arthur and sending the car aquaplaning across the ruts.

Arthur kept his hands firmly at ten-and-two on the steering wheel. On the passenger seat beside him sat his emergency field kit: his brass torch, the canvas Ordnance Survey map, a clean towel, a thermos of tea, an unopened box of peppermint humbugs, and a cardboard portfolio containing four blank sheets of high-grade acid-free parchment, two bottles of waterproof black draughtsman's ink, and four new steel nibs.

If he found Leo and Clem, and if the yellow duplicate was undamaged, he would require those materials to prepare a certified evidentiary copy that would satisfy Audrey Vance's calipers.

He reached the turnoff for Peterchurch and turned south into the Golden Valley.

The valley closed in around him like a green gorge. The road narrowed to a single lane between towering ancient hazel hedges whose branches brushed against the car's wing mirrors. The road surface had vanished beneath three inches of fast-moving red clay slurry that hissed against the floorboards.

Arthur slowed to twenty miles an hour.

At half-past eleven, he reached the village of Turnastone. The stone houses were shuttered against the storm; water was pouring from their lead gutters in thick, frothing spouts.

Ahead lay the wooden fingerpost pointing down the rough track toward Blackthorn Hollow: *Private Road — Unsuitable for Motors*.

Arthur hesitated. He remembered Audrey Vance's warning: *The low ford was washed out in nineteen-ninety-eight.*

He drove fifty yards down the track. The lane dipped sharply between steep clay banks overgrown with briars and wild bracken.

At the bottom of the dip, where the track crossed the River Dore, the river was no longer a quiet rural stream. It had burst its banks, transforming the entire hollow into a roaring red lake sixty yards wide. The water was surging over the stone footbridge, carrying broken willow branches, plastic chemical drums, and chunks of river turf in a violent, swirling eddy.

In the middle of the flood, submerged to the wheel arches, sat a rusted iron farm gate that marked the boundary of the Marsh orchard. The water was four feet deep.

Arthur slammed on the brakes. The Astra slid two yards on the wet clay before coming to a halt six inches from the water's edge.

Arthur sat in the driver's seat, his heart pounding in his ears.

The ford was completely impassable. No two-wheel-drive saloon car could cross that torrent without being swept into the Wye.

He looked at the dashboard clock: eleven-forty.

He had twenty-four hours and twenty minutes before Dr. Finch's noon deadline on Friday, and twenty-eight hours and fifty minutes before Audrey Vance sealed her dispatch pouch.

Arthur switched off the engine. The silence inside the car was broken only by the roar of the river and the hammering of the rain on the roof.

He reached into the back seat, pulled on his heavy green wellington boots, buttoned his Barbour jacket up to his throat, and took his black umbrella from the passenger footwell.

If the car could not cross the ford, Arthur would walk the three miles over the mountain track on foot.

Arthur stood by the edge of the swollen river Dore, looking across at the submerged orchard gate.

The roar of the floodwater was deafening, a deep bass rumble that vibrated through the soles of his wellington boots. The rain beat against his wax-cotton jacket, running in cold rivulets down his sleeves.

He looked at the high ridge to the north. A narrow sheep track climbed through the hazel coppice, skirting the edge of the flooded valley before descending into Blackthorn Hollow from behind the timber yard. It was three miles of steep red mud, gorse, and wet bracken, rising four hundred feet above the valley floor.

Arthur took his black umbrella from the passenger seat, locked the Astra's doors, and tested the weight of his briefcase.

Inside the case lay his parchment, his ink bottles, his fountain pens, and his emergency supply of peppermint humbugs.

"Right, Arthur," he said aloud to the stormy sky. "A brisk constitutional over the ridge."

He opened the umbrella. A vicious gust of mountain wind immediately inverted the black fabric, snapping two steel ribs and turning the canopy into a useless black cone.

Arthur looked at the broken umbrella. He gave a quiet, rueful chuckle, folded the ruined frame, and wedged it behind the Astra's front wheel.

He pulled his woollen cap down over his ears, gripped his leather briefcase in his right hand, and began climbing the steep clay bank into the hazel wood.

With every step, the red Herefordshire mud sucked at his boots with a loud, squelching thud. The briars caught at his trousers, and the rain washed across his glasses until the world dissolved into a blur of green and brown.

He did not look back at the car. He looked up at the ridge, where the dark crown of the Black Mountains stood waiting against the storm.

Arthur climbed fifty feet up the muddy sheep track through the hazel coppice, pausing beside an ancient moss-covered boundary stone to catch his breath.

Below him in the valley, the river Dore roared like a freight train, its red waters churning against the submerged willow trees. The Astra looked tiny from the ridge, a solitary grey beetle parked on the wet tarmac beside the flooded fingerpost.

The wind from the Black Mountains hit him with freezing force, driving the rain horizontally against his Barbour jacket. But inside the heavy waxed cotton, Arthur felt warm. His blood was circulating with vigorous, rhythmic energy; his breath came steady and deep, and with every squelching step in the red clay, his confidence grew.

He remembered the words of the Psalm he had read in eighty sheepfolds: *The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil.*

He was not walking through the shadow of death. He was walking through three miles of wet Herefordshire mud to save the marriages of his people.

He reached into his pocket, found an Everton mint, and popped it into his mouth. The sweet peppermint flavour was clean, sharp, and invigorating.

He gripped his briefcase tightly, leaned into the gale, and strode forward along the crest of the hill toward Blackthorn Hollow.

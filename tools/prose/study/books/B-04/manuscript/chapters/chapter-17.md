# Chapter 17 — Finch's Ultimatum

At eight-thirty on Thursday morning, the Leominster postman knocked three times on Arthur's front door with the heavy, authoritative rap reserved for recorded delivery correspondence.

Arthur opened the door. The postman, a cheerful man named Barry with whom Arthur had discussed lawnmower carburettors for twenty years, held out an electronic signature terminal with a black plastic stylus.

"Recorded item from Wiltshire, Arthur," Barry said, shifting his red delivery pouch. "Requires a signature and the time of receipt. Official blue envelope with a gold seal on the reverse."

Arthur took the stylus and scrawled his name across the grey liquid-crystal screen. He took the envelope from Barry, thanked him with an automatic murmur, and closed the front door.

The envelope was made of heavy azure paper, bearing an embossed corporate seal on the back flap depicting a dove clutching an open account book. Pinned to the front was a bright red label: *BY SPECIAL REGISTERED DELIVERY — IMMEDIATE PERSONAL SERVICE*.

Arthur carried the envelope into the kitchen, slit the flap with his butter knife, and pulled out four sheets of crisp cream paper.

The letter was printed in Dr. Finch's signature mixture of gothic script and legalistic jargon, bearing the heading: *FINAL CANONICAL REQUISITION AND STATUTORY NOTICE OF INTENDED PROCEEDINGS*.

Arthur leaned against the kitchen sink to read the text:

"To Officiant Arthur Pendelton, Presbyter Licentiate: TAKE FORMAL NOTICE that under Section 14 of the Constitution of the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace, and pursuant to the emergency powers vested in the High Council of Trustees by the Companies Act 2006, you are hereby ordered to surrender all provincial ceremonial rolls, counter-foil books, and solemnization records into the hands of our authorized inspection delegation no later than Friday, November 13th, at 12:00 noon."

Arthur blinked at the date. Friday at twelve noon? That was five hours earlier than Audrey Vance's deadline.

He read on:

"Notice is further given that the Charity Commission compliance team hath issued a provisional finding of operational irregularity against our Swindon headquarters. Unless verified Western March solemnization schedules are tendered before the Commissioners' plenary session on Friday afternoon, the Board of Trustees shall, by unanimous resolution, enact the following statutory remedies:

"First: Your apostolic warrant shall be declared revoked *ab initio*, terminating your authority under Section 26 of the Marriages Act 1949.

"Second: A formal certificate of default shall be transmitted to Her Majesty's Superintendent Registrar for the County of Herefordshire, notifying said officer that all ceremonies conducted under your purported authority between 2012 and 2026 were celebrated without canonical warrant.

"Third: The Trustees have appointed an external asset recovery agent, Mr. Keith Finch of Swindon Vehicle Liquidations, to attend your residence in Bargates with an automotive transporter, for the purpose of taking possession of the sea-grey Vauxhall Astra registered in your name, which vehicle was acquired and operated under our provincial tax-exempt travel covenant."

Arthur dropped the letter onto the draining board.

"They want the Astra," he said aloud. His voice held a note of pure, bewildered indignation. "The car has a rusted wheel arch and a failing exhaust bracket. How dare Keith Finch bring a transporter to Bargates?"

The threat to the car was absurd, almost laughable. But the threat to the four hundred couples was lethal. If Finch sent that certificate of default to Audrey Vance before Arthur could deliver the verified Marsh document, Audrey would have no legal choice. Her statutory duty under Section 75 would compel her to gazette the invalidation notice immediately.

Arthur looked at the clock above the gas stove. Eight-forty-five.

He had twenty-seven hours and fifteen minutes before Finch's noon deadline, and thirty-one hours and forty-five minutes before Audrey's dispatch pouch was sealed.

He went to the telephone in the hall and dialled directory enquiries.

"Peterchurch," Arthur said to the operator. "A farm listed under the name of Marsh. Clementine or Leo Marsh, Blackthorn Hollow."

"I have no residential listing for Blackthorn Hollow, sir," the operator replied after a pause. "There is an unlisted agricultural workshop under Golden Valley Artisan Timber, but the line has been marked as out of order due to water ingress since Monday's storms."

Arthur set down the receiver.

There was no telephone. There was no internet. There was only thirty miles of flooded rural roads between Leominster and the Black Mountains.

Arthur walked to the hall cupboard, pulled out his heavy wax-cotton Barbour jacket, and grabbed his keys from the sideboard.

Arthur folded Dr. Finch's registered ultimatum into three neat panels and laid it on the telephone table beside his council tax booklet.

The threat from Wiltshire was not merely pompous; it was a desperate death rattle from an internet enterprise collapsing under its own fraudulent weight. Finch was not an apostle; he was an unemployed sociology lecturer who had spent fifteen years selling laminated certificates from an attic room in Swindon to pay off his credit card balances with the Halifax. The moment the Charity Commission inspectors had stepped through his door with their scanners, his entire ecclesiastical facade had crumbled into frantic suburban panic.

Arthur walked into his scullery, washed his breakfast mug under the cold tap, and set it upside down on the draining board to dry.

He thought of Keith Finch and Swindon Vehicle Liquidations. He pictured an oily man in an unbuttoned polyester jacket arriving in Bargates with an automotive recovery truck, attempting to winch the sea-grey Astra onto a steel ramp while Mrs. Gwillam watched through her lace curtains with a pair of opera glasses.

"Let him come," Arthur said aloud to the empty scullery. "Let him bring six transporters. The Astra belongs to the road, not to a trading estate in Wiltshire."

His municipal billing training had given him an instinctive understanding of bureaucratic bullies. When a debtor threatened legal action, the proper response was never panic; it was documentation, precision, and an unshakeable grasp of the governing statute.

Finch had given him until twelve noon on Friday.

"Very well, Dr. Finch," Arthur murmured, checking his watch. "We shall see which authority survives the harvest: your lamination machine in Swindon, or the Marriages Act of nineteen-forty-nine."

He picked up his briefcase, walked out the front door, and locked both bolts behind him.

Arthur stood in his kitchen, examining Dr. Finch's purple letterhead under the electric light.

At the bottom of the fourth page, Finch had affixed a gold-foil adhesive seal stamped with an image of an eagle clutching a pair of accounting scales. Beside the seal was a handwritten postscript in purple felt-tip:

*P.S. Arthur: If Keith Finch attends with the transporter, he will require fifteen pounds for diesel and a signed transfer slip for the logbook. Please do not offer him confectionery. He has an aggressive gastric ulcer and becomes violent when offered mints.*

Arthur gave a quiet, dry chuckle. The idea of Keith Finch—an automotive liquidator from Wiltshire with an ulcer—attempting to seize an eight-year-old Vauxhall Astra in Bargates was so farcical that it dissolved the last remnants of Arthur's anxiety.

Finch was finished. His cyber-diocese was collapsing into bankruptcy; the Charity Commission was seizing his computers, and the police were knocking on his door. He was a drowning man clutching at a purple letterhead.

Arthur folded the letter, placed it inside an old electricity bill envelope, and slipped it into his briefcase.

He would not be intimidated by Keith Finch or Swindon vehicle liquidators. He had an orchard to reach and four hundred marriages to save.

In the pantry, the rectory bread crock held half a loaf of crusty sourdough and a pat of salted butter wrapped in greaseproof paper. Arthur sliced two thick rounds of bread, using a bone-handled knife that had lost its serrations before the second World War. A country vicar survived largely on toast, strong tea, and the occasional lemon sponge provided by sympathetic parishoners after PCC meetings. He chewed slowly, listening to the tick of the grandfather clock in the hall, whose brass pendulum swung through its wooden case with solemn, measured unhurriedness.

He stepped out into the hallway, took his Barbour coat from the hook, and walked out to the car.

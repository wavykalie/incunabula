# Chapter 28 — The Apple Crate Discovery

Arthur laid the red folio down upon the clean carpenter's bench beside the woodstove.

The red buckram cover was warped at the edges, dusted with fine ash sawdust, but the binding was completely intact. The book had survived ten years inside the sycamore butter tub beneath the owl's nest, perfectly preserved against damp and rot.

Leo brought over a clean draughtsman's dusting brush made of soft horsehair. With gentle, patient strokes, Arthur swept the remaining hay dust from the gold lettering on the front.

"Right," Arthur said, his voice quiet with solemn anticipation. "Let us examine the entry."

He turned the heavy front cover.

The pages inside were printed on archival vellum paper, ruled in red and black ink. Each page was divided into two sections: an upper commemorative certificate with perforations, and a lower counter-foil printed on pale yellow paper with the pre-printed heading: *SUPERINTENDENT REGISTRAR'S DUPLICATE RETURN — SECTION 44 (MARRIAGES ACT 1949)*.

Arthur turned to page forty-two.

The upper commemorative section had been torn away along the perforations ten years ago; only the ragged white stub remained.

Below the stub, perfectly preserved on the yellow counter-foil, was the complete statutory record of the ceremony of July 16th, 2016.

Arthur leaned his reading glasses close to the page.

Every box had been filled in with his neat, unflinching water board handwriting, using Higgins black waterproof ink:

*Entry No: PG-WM-2016-042*
*Date of Solemnization: 16th July 2016*
*Place of Solemnization: The Great Threshing Barn & Cider Press, Blackthorn Hollow, Parish of Vowchurch, County of Herefordshire*
*Architectural Designation: Traditional Timber Cruck Barn & Perry Press, Est. 1782 (Exempted Chapel-of-Ease under 6 & 7 Will. IV c. 85)*
*Groom: Leo Thomas Marsh, Age 26, Master Joiner & Timber Craftsman*
*Bride: Clementine Mary Marsh (formerly Vaughan), Age 24, Horticulturalist & Orchardist*
*Condition: Bachelor and Spinster*
*Officiant: Arthur Reginald Pendelton, Presbyter Licentiate (Warrant PG-WM-2012-0041)*

Beneath the particulars were the signatures.

Leo's signature was written in bold draughtsman's ink, clean and elegant. Clem's signature was bold and decisive, the cross on her *t* sweeping like a scythe across the line.

And beneath the spouses' signatures were the witness attestations:

*Witness A: Thomas Gwillam, Cooper, Madley*
*Witness B: David Price, Cider Pressman, Peterchurch*

Beside each witness name was a clear, circular purple thumbprint, made ten years ago with crushed damson juice when the men had forgotten their reading spectacles.

Arthur ran his dry fingers over the yellow paper. The ink was dark, crisp, and completely permanent. There was no smudge. There was no water stain. There was no ambiguity.

It was an authentic, flawless statutory record.

"Look at the architectural designation, Leo," Arthur whispered, pointing his finger at the Latin citation he had inscribed in 2016. "Do you see what I wrote ten years ago?"

Leo leaned over the bench. "*Exempted Chapel-of-Ease under 6 & 7 Will. IV c. 85.* What does that mean, Arthur?"

"It means," Arthur said, looking up with tears glistening in his eyes, "that ten years ago, when I sat on that cider barrel, I remembered the eighteen-thirty-six Act. I cited the exact statutory schedule that Mr. Cloutier showed me in the Shirehall basement. I designated this barn an exempted cider chapel before the vows were even spoken."

Clem stepped up to the bench, looking down at her own twenty-four-year-old signature. Her tough, defiant expression softened into a look of quiet, reverent wonder.

"We're real, Arthur," she whispered.

"You have always been real, my dear," Arthur said gently. "Now the law will know it too."

He opened his briefcase, retrieved his clean bone folder and a sheet of acid-free glassine paper, and carefully wrapped the yellow counter-foil to protect it for the journey to Hereford.

Arthur brought out his emergency portfolio, unfastened the linen ties, and laid out his four sheets of pristine acid-free parchment upon the workbench beside the red book.

"Now, Leo," Arthur said, taking his bone folder and smoothing the parchment flat. "We must prepare the certified transcript. Under Section sixty-four of the Act, when an original register cannot be removed from its custodial sanctuary without risk of physical decay, the celebrant may tender a verified copy, certified under his hand and sealed by two competent householders who witnessed the original solemnization."

He dipped his new Gillott drawing pen into the fresh Higgins waterproof ink.

With absolute, unwavering precision, Arthur began transcribing the text of the yellow counter-foil onto the clean parchment. He did not rush a single stroke. Every gothic capital, every date, every parcel number, and every Latin statutory citation was reproduced with the flawless geometric beauty of an ancient monastic scribe.

Beside him, Leo and Clem watched in breathless silence. The only sound in the barn was the gentle scratching of the steel nib against the heavy paper and the crackle of the oak logs in the stove.

When the transcript was complete, Arthur blew gently upon the black ink to dry it.

He pushed the parchment toward Leo. "Your hand, Leo. Sign in the right-hand margin as master joiner and lawful spouse."

Leo took the pen with steady fingers and signed his name: *Leo Thomas Marsh*.

Arthur turned to Clem. "Your hand, my dear."

Clem took the pen, dipped the nib with surprising delicacy, and wrote her name in bold, sweeping strokes: *Clementine Mary Marsh*.

Arthur took a piece of red sealing wax from his kit, heated it over the flame of the kerosene lamp, and let three drops of scarlet wax fall upon the parchment beside their signatures. He took his brass key ring, pressed the antique seal of the Hereford Water Board into the hot wax, and lifted the brass with a sharp, clean click.

The impression was sharp and permanent: a miniature crest depicting the flowing waters of the River Wye flanked by two wheat sheaves.

"There," Arthur whispered, his heart singing with triumph. "That is a certified statutory transcript under the eighteen-thirty-six provisions. It is waterproof, permanent, and completely unassailable in law."

Leo carefully dusted the yellow counter-foil with a dry horsehair brush, ensuring that every character was clean and legible.

"Look at the witness lines, Arthur," Leo said, pointing with a cedar pencil. "Old Thomas Gwillam passed away in twenty-twenty, but his thumbprint is right there in the damson juice. It's like his hand is reaching across the years to stand up for us."

Arthur looked at the purple circle on the parchment. "A true neighbour's mark, Leo. An honest witness never dies in the memory of the valley."

Arthur checked the signature of Thomas Gwillam once more, smoothing the parchment with his clean bone folder.

"Every stroke of this ink is a testimony to honest rural intent, Leo," Arthur said. "When Audrey Vance lays her calipers across these lines, she will find that the margins are true, the text is unblemished, and the statutory requirements of eighteen-thirty-six have been fulfilled to the letter. This single page will save four hundred families from administrative ruin."

Arthur adjusted his spectacles, examining the fine copperplate handwriting on the nineteen ninety-nine baptismal certificate. The ink had faded to a warm sepia, but every flourish was executed with the confident elegance of a Victorian schoolmaster who believed that poor penmanship was the first step on the road to moral ruin. He turned the page carefully, holding the leaf by its outer margins to avoid transferring oil from his fingers to the dry rag paper. In sixty years of parish administration, not a single entry had been altered without a marginal memorandum signed by two churchwardens and countersigned by the archdeacon.

Leo smiled quietly, resting his hand on his wife's shoulder. "Then let us take it to Hereford, Arthur, and finish the work."

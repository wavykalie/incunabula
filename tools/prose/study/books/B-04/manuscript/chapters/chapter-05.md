# Chapter 5 — The Registry Steps

The Shirehall in Hereford stood in heavy grey neoclassical stonework, gazing down upon St. Peter's Square with an air of permanent municipal disapproval. Behind its fluted columns, tucked into a Victorian red-brick annex that had once accommodated the county inspectorate of weights and measures, lay the County Registry Office.

Arthur Pendelton mounted the four shallow stone steps at eleven o'clock that morning. He was dressed in his second-best tweed overcoat and a dark woollen scarf, with both hands driven deep into his pockets. In his right pocket, his fingers clicked nervously against a roll of barley sugars; in his left lay a folded copy of the Hereford Times he had purchased from the railway newsagent. The drizzle had subsided to a greasy mist that clung to the stonework and turned the square into a mirror of wet asphalt.

Outside the heavy oak entrance, two young solicitors' clerks from a practice in Broad Street were standing beneath the portico, sharing a cigarette in the damp air.

"She rejected three of them before ten o'clock," the taller clerk said, flicking ash into the damp laurel bushes beside the iron handrail. "A deed poll from Kington, a certified translation of a Polish birth record, and a notice of marriage for a couple from Peterchurch."

"What was wrong with the Peterchurch notice?" the second clerk asked, shivering in a thin navy suit.

"The groom had described his occupation as artisanal charcoal burner. Vance told him that charcoal burning was an irregular seasonal occupation rather than a recognized statutory calling, and demanded six months of certified tax schedules before she would enter the banns. When the boy argued, she pulled down the nineteen-twenty-six occupational register and showed him that charcoal burning was categorized under forestry labour, subsection D, requiring an employer's stamp."

The taller clerk shook his head with grim respect. "Audrey Vance doesn't overlook a comma. She has been in that records basement since nineteen-ninety-four. They say she keeps a pair of brass calipers in her desk drawer to measure the margins on death registrations. If the registrar's clerk leaves less than three-quarters of an inch on the vellum, she returns the entire volume for re-binding at the clerk's expense."

"A formidable woman," the other murmured.

"She isn't a woman. She is the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine in a tweed skirt and tortoiseshell spectacles."

Arthur pulled his scarf up over his mouth and slipped past them through the heavy doors.

The registry office foyer was dead quiet, save for the rhythmic, heavy tick of an oak-cased pendulum clock mounted above the enquiry counter. The air smelled of floor wax, damp overcoats, and old vellum paper. Behind the frosted glass partition, two clerks in grey cardigans were stamping stacks of green forms with mechanical regularity. Thump. Slide. Thump. Slide.

On the public wooden benches against the oak panelling sat a nervous young couple. The girl was holding a plastic folder of gas bills, while the boy was staring down at his polished work boots as if terrified they might offend the municipal authorities.

Arthur walked across the parquet floor to the wooden notice board mounted beside the corridor. Pinned to the green baize were the statutory public notices: declarations of intent to marry, cemetery regulations, and public inspection timetables for county records.

Pinned directly in the centre was a printed circular on heavy white paper, bearing the county coat of arms and the bold heading: PUBLIC NOTICE: IRREGULAR RURAL CEREMONIES IN THE COUNTY OF HEREFORDSHIRE.

Arthur's stomach tightened into a hard knot. He leaned close to the cork board to read the text.

"Notice is hereby given under Section 44 of the Marriages Act 1949 that solemnizations of marriage within this registration district may take place ONLY in churches of the established church, registered religious buildings certified under the Places of Worship Registration Act 1855, or premises approved by the County Council for the solemnization of civil marriages."

The notice continued in crisp, uncompromising prose:

"Information hath been received that certain itinerant and online-ordained individuals have purported to solemnize marriages in non-approved rural locations, including agricultural barns, meadows, and private orchards within the Golden Valley and the Black Mountains district. The public are hereby cautioned that such ceremonies do NOT confer lawful civil status. An administrative audit of all non-conformist and independent solemnization records within the county is currently in progress under the direction of the Superintendent Registrar."

At the bottom of the notice, written in sharp, upright black fountain pen ink, was the signature:

Audrey Vance, Superintendent Registrar for the County of Herefordshire.

Arthur stared at the signature. The cross on the t in Superintendent was drawn like a miniature razor blade.

Through the glass panel of the double doors leading to the private offices, Arthur caught a brief glimpse of Audrey Vance herself. She was standing beside a mahogany filing cabinet, holding a heavy leather-bound volume open across her forearm. Her spectacles were perched on the bridge of her sharp nose, and she was examining a page with unblinking scrutiny. She raised her left hand, produced a clean linen cloth, and wiped the corner of the vellum with deliberate, rhythmic pressure before making a sharp notation in the margin with an ebony fountain pen.

"Can I assist you, sir?"

Arthur jumped. A clerk with grey hair pulled back in a neat bun had appeared behind the glass hatch. She was looking at him with mild, professional boredom.

"I... no," Arthur said, pulling a barley sugar from his pocket and instantly wishing he had not. "That is to say, I was merely checking the... the parking regulations for the cattle market."

"Cattle market parking is administered by the council offices in Plough Lane, sir," the clerk said evenly. "This counter handles Marriages, Births, and Deaths."

"Quite so," Arthur said, nodding with frantic politeness. "A most essential department. The great milestones of our earthly journey, if you see what I mean. Bless you."

He turned on his heel and walked out of the foyer as quickly as his overcoat would permit. Behind him, the oak entrance doors swung shut with a heavy sigh, cutting off the rhythmic thump of the date stamps.

Before turning toward the exit, Arthur paused beside the glass display case that stood between the public benches and the records counter. Inside the polished mahogany cabinet lay the historical registers of Herefordshire: heavy calfskin folios with brass corner-pieces, their vellum leaves inscribed in eighteenth-century copperplate ink. A brass label beside the central volume read: *County Marriages Register, Volume 14 (1837–1842), Restored by Order of the County Council*.

Arthur stared through the glass at the clean, unwavering penmanship of the Victorian registrar. Each entry was a miniature fortress of statutory certainty: date, parish, bride, groom, rank, profession, residence, fathers' names, condition. There was no ambiguity. There was no poetry. No bride's veil had blown into an oak tree on those pages; no three-legged lurcher had collided with an elderflower cake. The ink was iron gall, permanent as churchyard granite, drying beneath the crown crest while the statutory fees were ticked off in the left-hand margin.

Beside the cabinet, the public search room door stood slightly ajar. Arthur peered through the crack.

Sitting at a long baize-covered table was an elderly solicitor from Kington, his reading glasses propped on his bald head while he thumbed through an index volume with yellowed fingertips. Across the table, a nervous young farmhand in clean corduroy trousers was clutching a greasy five-pound note between thumb and forefinger, waiting for a clerk to verify a certified copy of his grandfather's wedding lines.

"Section thirty-seven," the solicitor murmured to himself, his fountain pen scratching softly against a notepad. "Notice duly entered in the marriage notice book. Thirty-five shillings paid in coin of the realm."

Arthur looked at the young farmhand. The boy's face held the same mixture of reverence and terror that Arthur had seen in four hundred apple orchards. People feared the registry not because they hated the law, but because they knew the law did not care about their cows, their harvest, or their private griefs. It cared only for the alignment of the date stamp.

Arthur pulled his overcoat tightly around his chest. He felt an intense, irrational urge to walk into that search room, lay his hands on the boy's broad shoulders, and tell him that a promise made in the mud was every bit as holy as an entry in a calfskin folio.

The kettle in the vestry pantry took nine minutes to boil because the copper element was coated in two decades of lime scale from the High Lea chalk springs. Arthur watched the water tremble in the glass gauge. A parish priest spent half his working life waiting for water to boil in drafty sculleries, listening to the drip of condensation off stone mullions and wondering whether the diocesan board of finance had ever spent an entire Tuesday afternoon in wet socks. When the whistle sounded, it was not a shrill shriek but a wheezy, apologetic sigh, perfectly attuned to the deanery's general approach to theological dispute and heating allowances.

Instead, he slipped quietly toward the heavy entrance doors, his wet shoes squeaking on the polished parquet.

# Chapter 2 — The Vauxhall Boot

Arthur stepped out onto the damp gravel in his slippers. A thin drizzle fell from the grey sky, depositing cold beads on the bald crown of his head. He pulled his cardigan tight across his stomach, clutching his heavy brass key ring in his right fist. The air in Bargates carried the smell of coal smoke from a chimney three doors down, mixed with the damp vegetable odour of rotting elderberry leaves along the verge.

Across the low garden wall, his neighbour Mrs. Gwillam was guiding her elderly cocker spaniel down the pavement. The dog paused by Arthur's front wing, snuffling with mournful dedication at the damp rubber of the tyre.

"Terrible damp for November, Mr. Pendelton," Mrs. Gwillam called out, her voice carrying clear through the wet hedge. "My hip has been predicting rain since Sunday evensong. You're out early without your boots."

"Just checking the tyre pressures, Mrs. Gwillam," Arthur replied, producing a polite smile that felt like stretched elastic across his gums. "The change in temperature affects the valves, you know. Cold air causes the rubber to contract."

"My late brother had a Morris Minor that rotted from the inside out," she remarked cheerfully, pausing to let the spaniel investigate an empty crisp packet in the gutter. "He left a cardboard box of hymn sheets in the boot over the winter of nineteen-eighty-one, and by February the spare wheel well had sprouted wild mushrooms. Real field mushrooms, mind. He fried two of them with bacon before my sister-in-law stopped him."

"A very resourceful household," Arthur said, nodding with frantic deference. "One must watch the toadstools. Moisture is a terrible enemy."

"He was an unusual man, my brother," Mrs. Gwillam added, adjusting the knitted hood of her plastic mackintosh. "He kept two tins of sardines and an empty margarine tub under the driver's seat in case the Severn flooded. If you need a plastic margarine tub for nuts or washers, Mr. Pendelton, I have three clean ones in the scullery."

"You are very kind, Mrs. Gwillam. I shall bear that in mind."

He waited in agony until Mrs. Gwillam and her snuffling spaniel had turned the corner into New Street. Then he stepped behind the Astra, inserted the key into the boot lock, and gave it a sharp twist. The metal latch yielded with a reluctant thud.

Arthur lifted the heavy tailgate. Both hydraulic support struts had expired during a hard frost three years earlier, so he reached into the side pocket and wedged a notched wooden broom handle beneath the metal lip to prevent the door from descending upon his neck.

A thick draft of trapped air drifted out into the street. It was a familiar, suffocating atmosphere: dried lavender confetti, damp dry-cleaned wool, eucalyptus pastilles, and the sharp petroleum tang of a spare tyre with a failing rubber stem.

Arthur stood on the gravel and looked down into the hollow.

"Right," he murmured. "Systematic assessment."

The boot was filled to the level of the parcel shelf. On the left sat a heavy cardboard crate that had originally held twelve bottles of Spanish cooking sherry. It was now packed with twenty ceremonial stoles Arthur had stitched himself from discount ecclesiastical satin purchased at a warehouse in Gloucester. Each stole had been sealed inside an airtight plastic freezer bag to protect the embroidery from autumn condensation. Beside the crate lay three brass cufflinks shaped like nautical anchors, four plastic champagne flutes left over from a 2018 reception in a hop yard near Dilwyn, a roll of heavy silver duct tape, and a metal sweet tin containing sixty-two emergency peppermint humbugs.

Next to the sweet tin sat an ancient portable cassette player with a cracked plastic handle, which Arthur had used between 2013 and 2019 to provide recorded processional music in fields where there was no electricity. Tucked into the battery compartment was a cassette tape labelled Pachelbel Canon and Jesu Joy — Do Not Rewind Beyond Pencil Mark. Beside the player lay a hand-drawn map of the parish of Weobley, sketched on the back of a grain merchant's calendar, showing the private farm tracks that avoided council potholes.

Underneath the ceremonial stoles lay the paper.

Arthur reached into the depths of the boot and brought up a thick handful of warped cardstock. They were commemorative orders of service. None of them were green statutory certificates. One was printed on cream vellum card with deckle edges: Leo and Clementine Marsh, 16th July 2016, The Cider Barn, Blackthorn Hollow. Across the front cover, someone had scribbled a telephone number in purple felt-tip pen. Beneath it lay a damp paper napkin bearing a pencil sketch of a wedding seating plan, three receipts for sacks of dried rose petals, and a handwritten sheet of alternative vows wherein a bridegroom had pledged to respect his wife's perry orchard and never prune an apple tree without prior consultation.

There were no official counter-foils. There were no green triplicates. There were no certificates bearing the official embossed stamp of Her Majesty's Superintendent Registrar.

Arthur dropped down onto his knees on the wet gravel. The sharp stones bit through his flannel trousers, chilling his kneecaps. He reached deeper into the corners, moving aside a spare fan belt, an unbranded golf umbrella with a bent rib, and a plastic tub of gold ceremonial glitter that had fractured during a hard corner on the Bromyard bypass, dusting the entire luggage carpet in sparkling dust.

He reached the metal floor of the wheel well. He lifted the circular wooden cover that protected the spare tyre.

There was an inch and a half of murky brown water sitting in the bottom of the steel recess. Floating in the water, swollen like an old sponge, was an accordion file of buff cardboard labelled in faded black marker: SOLEMNIZATIONS 2013-2015.

Arthur lifted the file by its brass clasp. Cold water poured out of the bottom seams in a heavy stream, splashing across the toes of his tartan slippers. When he pulled back the cardboard flap, the sheets inside had fused into a single block of grey pulp. A faint yellow trace of Latin calligraphy clung to the disintegrating margins: Ecclesia Gratiae... In Partibus...

Arthur lowered the file back onto the wet tyre.

A fresh raindrop fell from the edge of the boot lid, striking the centre of his forehead and running down between his eyebrows. He took off his reading glasses and wiped the lenses against the wool of his cardigan sleeve, succeeding only in spreading a smear of damp grease across the glass.

Down the road behind him, a diesel engine clattered. A white Ford Transit van belonging to the county council came crawling along the kerb, its white doors bearing the familiar green shield of Herefordshire Council: Department of Regulatory Services and Public Protection.

Arthur froze on his knees behind the bumper, gripping the broom handle with both hands. The van slowed as it drew level with his driveway. The driver, a young man in a fluorescent yellow vest, looked across at the middle-aged man kneeling in his slippers amidst piles of purple stoles and damp cardboard.

Arthur gave a stiff, polite nod, as if kneeling in wet gravel behind a hatchback was his customary morning routine.

The driver shook his head, tapped his steering wheel, and accelerated away up the hill toward the Leominster bypass.

The parish register safe was built into the three-foot-thick north wall of the chancel, behind a heavy panel of seasoned oak that blended seamlessly into the wainscoting. Arthur unlocked the oak door with a small iron key worn thin by sixty years of parish sextons. Inside, the air was chill and faintly scented with tallow and dry vellum. The registers were arranged in chronological order, their leather covers rubbed at the corners where generations of incumbents had pulled them from the shelf to record a christening, a marriage, or a burial in the damp earth beneath the yews.

Arthur waited until the sound of the diesel engine had faded into the hum of the morning traffic. Then he pulled the broom handle free, caught the heavy tailgate with his left forearm, and let the latch fall home with a flat, hollow bang.

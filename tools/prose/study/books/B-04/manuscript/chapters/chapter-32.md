# Chapter 32 — The Registry Corridor

The long, vaulted stone corridor leading to the Superintendent Registrar's private office was cool and silent as a monastery cloister.

Arthur walked in the center, his wellington boots replaced by his polished black ceremonial shoes, his dark overcoat buttoned neatly across his clean woollen tie. To his right walked Clementine Marsh, tall, upright, and completely unabashed in her mended canvas trousers and clean boots; to his left walked Leo Marsh, carrying the briefcase with both hands as if it contained the crown jewels of England.

Their footsteps echoed rhythmically against the stone flags: *Clack. Thud. Clack. Thud.*

As they passed the open door of the public search room, the assistant archivist Mr. Cloutier looked up from his reading desk. The old man took off his horn-rimmed spectacles, caught Arthur's eye through the doorway, and offered a discreet, triumphant nod of his bald head, tapping the calfskin binding of the 1836 Assizes folio resting beside his blotter.

Arthur returned the nod with a faint inclination of his chin.

They reached the heavy, double-fronted mahogany doors at the end of the corridor. A polished brass plate mounted at eye level read: *HER MAJESTY'S SUPERINTENDENT REGISTRAR — PRIVATE — ADMISSION BY APPOINTMENT ONLY*.

Arthur raised his right hand, curled his knuckles, and knocked three firm, deliberate times upon the wood.

"Enter," Audrey Vance's voice rang out from within.

Arthur turned the heavy brass door handle, pushed the door open, and ushered Leo and Clem into the room.

The office was charged with an atmosphere of intense, statutory concentration.

Audrey Vance was seated behind her double-fronted mahogany desk. The desktop had been cleared of all routine paperwork; only three massive leather-bound registers lay open before her, their green vellum pages held flat by heavy brass paperweights. Beside the registers sat her ebony ruler, her black inkwell, her ivory bone folder, and her gleaming brass calipers, calibrated down to the sixty-fourth of an inch.

On the coat stand behind her hung her charcoal tailored jacket; she was wearing her crisp cream blouse with the sleeves rolled back two inches, revealing her pale, muscular forearms. Her tortoiseshell reading glasses were perched upon her nose, and her grey hair was held in place by two tortoiseshell combs.

To her right, seated on a small wooden chair with a shorthand notebook and three sharpened pencils, was Miss Cleary, looking tense and alert.

Audrey looked up as the three visitors entered. Her sharp grey eyes took in Arthur's polished shoes, Leo's craftsman's posture, and Clem's fierce, unblinking gaze. Her expression did not change by a millimetre, but her fingers paused over the brass calipers.

"Good morning, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey said evenly. Her voice had the cold, resonant clarity of church bells across frosty fields. "You are twelve minutes ahead of your declared appointment."

"A punctual public servant avoids administrative congestion, Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied politely, stepping forward to the edge of the mahogany desk. "May I present Mr. Leo Marsh and Mrs. Clementine Marsh, freeholders of Blackthorn Hollow in the parish of Vowchurch."

Audrey looked at Clem. Clem looked at Audrey.

For five seconds, neither woman spoke. The two female titans of Herefordshire—the unbending guardian of the municipal crown rolls and the fierce protector of the off-grid perry orchard—measured each other with mutual, instinctive recognition.

"Mrs. Marsh," Audrey said, dipping her chin an eighth of an inch.

"Mrs. Vance," Clem replied, matching the inclination with absolute equality.

"You have come in response to the public notice gazetted under Section forty-four," Audrey stated, picking up her ebony pen. "The statutory audit of independent solemnizations within this district closes at four-thirty this afternoon. Mr. Pendelton has claimed that on July sixteenth, twenty-sixteen, a lawful religious marriage was celebrated within an approved agricultural sanctuary at Blackthorn Hollow under the authority of an extra-parochial episcopal warrant."

She laid the pen down and placed her hands flat upon the blotter.

"The burden of proof rests upon the celebrant, Mr. Pendelton. What evidence do you tender to Her Majesty's register?"

Arthur turned to Leo. "The portfolio, Leo."

Leo stepped forward, unclasped the brass buckles of the briefcase, and lifted out the clean parchment transcript and the red Swindon folio. He placed them upon the mahogany desk directly before Audrey Vance's calipers.

As Arthur, Leo, and Clem entered the private office, the contrast between the three rural visitors and the austere municipal sanctum was stark.

The mahogany desk was polished to the reflection of dark water; the green leather blotter was immaculate, and behind Audrey Vance's shoulder hung an engraved portrait of Queen Victoria signing the Marriage and Registration Act of eighteen-thirty-six. The air smelled of archival floor polish, cold tea, and old vellum paper.

Audrey Vance did not rise from her chair. She adjusted her reading spectacles by the bridge with that terrifying, rhythmic snap that Arthur had learned to dread.

"Take your seats," Audrey instructed, pointing her ebony ruler toward two high-backed oak chairs arranged before the desk.

Clem remained standing, her boots planted firmly on the Persian rug, her arms crossed over her flannel shirt. Leo sat down on the edge of the left-hand chair, placing the leather briefcase across his knees like a shield. Arthur sat on the right, his hands resting calmly on his thighs.

Audrey looked at Clem. "You prefer to stand, Mrs. Marsh?"

"I've spent ten years standing on my own feet in that orchard, Mrs. Vance," Clem said evenly, her grey eyes locking with the registrar's. "I don't see any reason to sit down now."

Audrey's mouth tightened by a fraction of a millimetre, but her eyes held a glimmer of professional admiration.

"Very well," Audrey said. She turned to Miss Cleary. "Miss Cleary: take down the appearances. In the matter of the statutory audit of independent and non-conformist solemnizations for the Western District of Herefordshire. Present: Audrey Vance, Superintendent Registrar; Arthur Reginald Pendelton, respondent celebrant; Leo Thomas Marsh and Clementine Mary Marsh, parties in interest."

Miss Cleary's pencil flew across the ruled lines of her shorthand notebook with a rapid, scratching rhythm.

Audrey picked up her ivory bone folder.

"Mr. Pendelton: you have tendered two physical exhibits. Exhibit One is an unverified buckram folio purporting to be a provincial register. Exhibit Two is a certified transcript bearing an impression of the seal of the Hereford District Water Board."

She paused, looking at Arthur over her spectacles.

"Explain to this tribunal, Mr. Pendelton, why Her Majesty's Superintendent Registrar should not immediately reject these exhibits under Section seventy-five of the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine as informal, uncertified, and legally defective memoranda."

Arthur took a deep, steady breath. The moment had arrived. The seventy-two hours of terror had converged upon this single desk.

"Mrs. Vance," Arthur began, his voice calm, resonant, and entirely free of hesitation. "I tender these exhibits not under the modern statutory schedules of nineteen-forty-nine, but under the ancient, unrepealed customary exemptions preserved by Section seventy-nine of that very Act. I tender them under the authority of Six and Seven William the Fourth, chapter eighty-five, Section twenty-two, which recognized the agricultural cruck barn at Blackthorn Hollow as an approved chapel-of-ease for the rural people of the Golden Valley."

Audrey Vance's pencil hovered over her blotter. The room fell into absolute, breathless silence.

Audrey Vance picked up her ivory bone folder and tapped it twice against her palm.

"Before we examine the documents, Mr. Pendelton," she said, her voice dropping into a quiet, measured cadence, "I must remind you that this interview is being recorded in the official minute book of the registration district. Any statement made by you regarding ceremonial procedures or statutory compliance is subject to the penalties of the Perjury Act nineteen-eleven."

"I am fully aware of the statutory penalties, Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied politely. "My testimony shall be simple, truthful, and complete."

"Very good," Audrey said. She turned to Leo and Clem. "Mr. and Mrs. Marsh: you appear here as interested parties whose civil status is directly subject to this determination. You are entitled to be heard, and your statements shall form part of the evidentiary record."

Clem nodded firmly. "We are ready, Mrs. Vance."

Audrey opened her middle drawer, extracted her white cotton gloves, and drew them slowly over her fingers.

Arthur looked up at the high Victorian ceiling of Audrey Vance's office, where decorative plaster cornices depicted oak leaves and Tudor roses.

The room had seen eighty years of marital declarations. It had seen nervous young farmhands during the First World War plighting their troth before embarking for France; it had seen wartime brides in utility suits during nineteen-forty-four; and it had seen generations of rural families recording their names beneath the royal cipher.

Arthur felt the weight of that long procession standing quietly behind his chair. He was not an intruder in this room; he was the latest servant in a three-century-old tradition of provincial stewardship.

Audrey Vance picked up her ebony ruler, laying it parallel to the edge of her blotter with the same precision Arthur had practiced at the water board.

"We have sixteen minutes before the official record begins, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey said, her voice dropping into a neutral, administrative murmur. "Is there any preliminary point of jurisdiction you wish to enter upon the minute?"

"Only this, Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied softly. "That whatever occurs this morning, the intent of the parties was pure, honest, and unblemished by fraud."

Audrey looked at him, and her eyes held a quiet, solemn understanding.

Outside in the lane, the wind was rising, sending swirls of wet beech leaves slapping against the windshield of Audrey's Vauxhall Astra. Finch stood under the dripping lychgate, holding his mobile telephone aloft like a pagan priest offering a sacrifice to an unseen deity. The signal bar flickered from one bar to zero and back again. The rural telephone exchange at High Lea had been installed in nineteen fifty-eight and still utilized copper overhead lines that reacted to autumn gales by producing a low, rhythmic hum that disrupted modem connections across three parishes.

"The law will judge the intent by the record, Arthur," she said quietly.

# Chapter 6 — Form 8B

Arthur Pendelton sat at his kitchen table at two o'clock that afternoon, surrounded by eleven sheets of blank administrative templates he had printed from the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace's secure web portal. The form was designated as FORM 8B (REVISED 2026): QUADRENNIAL RETURN OF SOLEMNIZATIONS PERFORMED UNDER EXTRA-PAROCHIAL APOSTOLIC WARRANT.

Across the top of each page ran an official warning in bold red block capitals: FAILURE TO COMPLETE ALL FIELDS IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE STATUTORY GUIDELINES OF THE GENERAL REGISTER OFFICE SHALL RENDER THE CELEBRANT LIABLE TO SUMMARY DEFROCKING AND FISCAL AUDIT UNDER SCHEDULE 4.

Arthur dipped his Parker pen into a bottle of royal blue washable ink. He shook the nib twice over the blotting paper to prevent blotting.

"Box One," Arthur read aloud. "Name of Solemnizing Presbyter. That is straightforward enough."

He inscribed: *Arthur Reginald Pendelton, Presbyter Licentiate, Western March Province.*

"Box Two: Canonical License Reference Number."

Arthur opened a tin tobacco box where he kept his important documents. Inside lay his original 2012 laminated card. The gold foil seal had peeled away at the top right corner, revealing the sticky grey adhesive beneath. The reference number was printed in tiny black dot-matrix digits: *PG-WM-2012-0041-REV*.

He entered the digits with meticulous care.

"Box Three," he murmured. "Solemnization Date and Temporal Index."

He selected his sister Clara's wedding as the test case. If he could not successfully document his own sister's marriage, the remaining three hundred and ninety-nine unions would be utterly hopeless.

He wrote: *18th August 2012, 2:30 PM.*

"Box Four: Ecclesiastical Nave Designation and Permanent Roof Status."

Arthur stopped. His pen hovered three inches above the cream paper, a tiny droplet of blue ink forming at the split of the nib.

He read the explanatory guidance printed at the bottom of the sheet:

*Guidance Note 4(a): Under Section 26 of the Marriages Act 1949, all solemnizations conducted under non-conformist or irregular episcopal warrants must take place within a permanently roofed architectural structure possessing identifiable structural boundaries, continuous vertical load-bearing walls, and an established compass orientation indicating the ecclesiastical east-west axis. Temporary structures, including marquees, yurts, gazebos, open agricultural sheds, and canopies erected on temporary scaffolding, shall NOT qualify as approved places of religious worship unless certified under Section 18 of the Places of Worship Registration Act 1855.*

Arthur looked at the small rectangular box. The space measured two inches by half an inch.

How was he supposed to fit a clover meadow into two inches?

Clara and Gethin had not stood within load-bearing walls. They had stood twelve yards to the west of a weathered hawthorn hedge, forty yards south of a drainage ditch filled with flowering meadowsweet, and approximately fifteen feet from a rusted iron cattle feeder that Gethin had draped with a crocheted cotton tablecloth to hold the ceremonial cider jug.

Arthur sighed. He wrote in small, defensive handwriting: *Open clover pasture, OS parcel 4412, estate of Mr. T. Prosser, Pembridge. Natural canopy formed by mature ash boughs. East-west alignment approximated by position of midday sun.*

He moved down to Box Five.

"Box Five: Ordnance Survey Twelve-Figure Grid Coordinate of Altar or Ceremonial Focus."

Arthur blinked. Twelve figures? He possessed an ancient canvas-backed Ordnance Survey map of North Herefordshire that he had purchased in 1989 from an antique shop in Hay-on-Wye. The paper had split along four folds and had been repaired with yellowed cellulose tape. A twelve-figure grid reference required precision down to the nearest square metre.

He took his brass ruler from his pencil case, unfolded the map across the tea-stained table, and located Pembridge. He found the green patch marking Prosser's meadow. He laid the ruler along the easting line, squinted through his magnifying glass, and attempted to remember exactly which patch of clover Gethin had stood upon.

"SO 38921 58104," he guessed, writing the numbers into the box with a trembling hand. "Or possibly 58105, if we were standing near the thistle patch."

He moved to Box Six: *Contracting Party A (Groom) — Full Statutory Name, Occupation, National Insurance Number, and Parish of Settlement.*

Arthur wrote Gethin's details easily enough. Gethin was a carpenter; his National Insurance number was recorded in Arthur's address book because Arthur had once helped him complete a council housing repair grant application in 2014.

Then came Box Seven: *Contracting Party B (Bride) — Maiden Surname, Maternal Lineage, and Statutory Declaration of Non-Consanguinity.*

Arthur wrote Clara's name.

Then he reached Box Eight. This was the box that made Arthur's breath catch in his throat.

"Box Eight: Civil Preliminary Certificate and Registrar's Superintendent Certificate Index Number."

The guidance note for Box Eight was printed in bold italic type with an asterisk:

*MANDATORY STATUTORY REQUIREMENT: Every religious marriage solemnized in England and Wales without the publication of Church of England banns MUST be preceded by formal notice given to the Superintendent Registrar of the district. The certificate of notice issued by said registrar under Section 31 of the Marriages Act 1949 MUST be delivered to the officiant PRIOR to the ceremony and entered into the counter-foil book concurrently with the solemnization. Failure to enter the Superintendent Registrar's certificate number renders the return legally void and subjects the celebrant to formal investigation under the Perjury Act 1911.*

Arthur stared at the blank white box.

Clara and Gethin had never given notice to Audrey Vance. They had never set foot inside the red-brick Shirehall annex in Hereford. When Gethin had learned that notice of marriage required displaying their private names and occupations on a public notice board outside the registry office for twenty-eight days, he had declared that the municipal council had no business publishing his domestic intentions to every busybody in the Wye Valley.

"We gave notice to the swallows, Arthur," Clara had said on that August afternoon, laughing while she pinned dried lavender into her hair. "We gave notice to the grass and the cows. God heard us, and you heard us. Why on earth would we pay forty pounds to a woman with a brass date stamp?"

Arthur remembered nodding gently. At the time, standing in the summer breeze with the scent of wild thyme rising from the turf, Clara's argument had seemed completely reasonable, almost holy. Why should two decent young people who simply wanted to build a timber workshop and grow damson trees be subjected to statutory notice periods and bureaucratic cross-examination?

Now, fourteen years later, sitting in a cold kitchen in Bargates with an empty Form 8B, the swallows were nowhere to be seen, the cows were entirely silent, and Audrey Vance was sitting behind her mahogany desk in Hereford with twenty-eight years of municipal statutory authority and a pair of brass calipers.

Arthur looked down at Box Nine: *Official Counter-Foil Index Number from Provincial Register Volume.*

Arthur did not have a provincial register volume. Dr. Finch had never issued one. In 2012, when Arthur had telephoned Swindon to ask where he should record Clara's vows, Dr. Finch had told him in an airy, dismissive tone: "Keep a neat exercise book, dear brother. A good hardback account book from W.H. Smith with red binding will satisfy all ecclesiastical inquiries. We are an apostolic movement of the spirit, not a department of inland revenue."

Arthur had purchased a red hardback notebook. But in the winter of 2017, during a sudden thaw, a pipe had burst in his garage ceiling, soaking the cardboard box where the notebook was stored. When Arthur had excavated the box three months later, the red dye from the cloth binding had run through the pages like cheap port, turning the entire record of ninety-four ceremonies into a soggy, scarlet brick of pulp.

Arthur picked up his pen. His hand was shaking so badly that the blue ink formed a jagged, trembling line along the margin of Box Nine.

He looked at the bottom of the page. There were sixty lines per sheet. Ten sheets per year. Twelve years of quarterly returns.

That meant four hundred and eighty separate sheets of Form 8B. It meant four hundred and eighty twelve-figure Ordnance Survey coordinates. It meant nine hundred and sixty adult witness National Insurance numbers. It meant four hundred non-existent Superintendent Registrar certificate codes.

Arthur laid his pen down in the groove of his pencil case.

The kitchen was completely quiet, save for the hum of the elderly refrigerator in the scullery.

Outside, the November afternoon was beginning to darken into a dull, charcoal twilight. A single crow flew across the grey sky, heading toward the woods above the River Lugg.

Arthur picked up the printed sheet of Form 8B. He turned it over. The back was completely blank and white.

With slow, careful movements, Arthur folded the page into four neat quarters, placed it beneath his Willow-pattern saucer, and set his cold tea mug squarely upon the fold.

Arthur pulled a second sheet of Form 8B toward him. He had resolved to attempt a second test entry: the July 2013 ceremony of young Robin and Holly Vance—no relation to Audrey, so far as Arthur knew—conducted inside a decommissioned Great Western Railway brake van parked on a disused siding near Presteigne.

He filled in Box One and Box Two. Then he arrived at Box Eleven: *Permanent Mechanical and Fire-Safety Classification of Non-Standard Pastoral Sanctuaries*.

The explanatory footnote on the reverse of the sheet stated:

*Guidance Note 11(c): Where a solemnization hath occurred within a mobile, vehicular, or maritime enclosure, the celebrant must furnish a certified mechanical certificate demonstrating that said conveyance hath been rendered permanently immobile by means of welded chassis brackets, concrete foundation piers, or the complete removal of the wheel bogies. An inspection report signed by a chartered structural engineer under the Building Regulations 2010 MUST accompany this schedule.*

Arthur laughed aloud. The sound was dry and hollow in the quiet kitchen.

Welded chassis brackets? Robin Vance was an apprentice boilermaker who had purchased the rotting wooden brake van for seventy-five pounds from a timber yard in Titley. The wheels were still firmly attached to the rusty iron axles, resting on ten yards of overgrown trackbed surrounded by wild blackberry bushes. During the ceremony, whenever the best man shifted his weight to retrieve the gold rings from his waistcoat pocket, the entire carriage had rocked gently on its leaf springs, causing the kerosene storm lamps suspended from the ceiling to swing like ecclesiastical censers.

Arthur wrote in Box Eleven: *Wooden brake van, ex-GWR 1928. Wheel bogies resting on authentic trackbed. Inherent immobility secured by heavy growth of brambles and lack of a locomotive.*

He looked down at Box Twelve: *Statutory Tithe Declaration and Charitable Exemption Counter-Signature.*

Every box was a trap. Every line was a demand for evidence that no rural gathering in an unploughed field could ever produce. Finch had designed the form to look like an authentic government schedule, but beneath the purple ink and the gothic banners lay nothing more than a desperate corporate shield to ward off the Charity Commission.

Arthur set down his Parker pen. He rubbed his aching thumbs together until the skin was warm.

The lane to Marsh Farm dipped between two steep banks of red marl where the oak roots hung exposed like fossilized rigging. In wet weather, the runoff from the high pasture turned the ruts into brick-colored soup that splashed as high as the Morris Minor's door handles. Arthur steered by muscle memory, keeping the nearside wheels on the narrow grass verge to avoid grounding the sump on the center ridge. A flock of black-faced sheep looked up from the gorse, chewing with methodical indifference as the small gray car puttered past. Rural solitude was rarely quiet; it was simply populated by creatures that had no interest in quarterly returns or ecclesiastical courts.

Outside, the first evening star was beginning to glint through the damp mist above the roofs of Bargates.

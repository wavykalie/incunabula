# Chapter 16 — The Missing Barn File

At six o'clock on Thursday morning, Arthur Pendelton was standing in his concrete-floored garage in Leominster, surrounded by the archaeological strata of twelve years of irregular pastoral service.

The garage was unheated, illuminated by a swinging bare lightbulb attached to an extension cord draped over the roof rafters. The air was frigid, carrying the scent of dry timber rot, lawnmower petrol, and damp newsprint. Outside the corrugated iron door, the dawn mist was rising from the gardens of Bargates, thick and white as sheep's fleece.

Arthur was wearing his thickest fisherman's jersey, two pairs of woollen socks inside his gardening gumboots, and a knitted wool cap pulled down over his ears. On the concrete floor before him sat five heavy cardboard boxes, retrieved from the timber shelves above the Astra's parking bay.

Each box was labelled in Arthur's water board script. He knelt before the crate marked *2016 — GOLDEN VALLEY & BLACK MOUNTAINS*.

"Right," Arthur whispered through chattering teeth. "Methodical inventory."

He slit the brown packaging tape with an old bone-handled pocketknife. When he pulled back the cardboard flaps, a dry cloud of dried rose petals and silver foil confetti puffed into the cold air.

Inside lay the detritus of thirty-eight ceremonies conducted across the summer of 2016. Arthur began lifting the items out onto a plastic tarpaulin.

First came a stack of decorative orders of service, printed on laid paper with lavender borders. One recorded the wedding of a sheep shearer from Longtown, complete with an ink sketch of two border collies holding rings in their teeth. Beneath it lay a handwritten recipe for elderberry wine, three receipts for hiring folding wooden chairs from the parish hall in Ewyas Harold, and an unopened box of twenty-four white sparklers labelled *Ideal for Marquee Exits*.

Arthur set them aside.

Beneath the sparklers lay an accordion file of green cardstock labelled *CERTIFICATE CORRESPONDENCE 2016*.

Arthur opened the brass catch with trembling fingers. His thumb tracked through the dividers: *April, May, June, July*.

Under *July*, he found the original enquiry letter from Clementine Marsh, written on rough handmade paper made from apple pulp. The handwriting was bold, upright, and fiercely determined:

*Dear Mr. Pendelton: Leo and I are restoring the old cider press at Blackthorn Hollow. We wish to marry in the threshing barn on Saturday, July 16th, at two in the afternoon. We do not require an organ, hymns, or sermonising about submission. We require a quiet, reverent solemnization that acknowledges the earth, the timber, and our mutual promise. We have been informed that you possess an episcopal warrant that permits rural ceremonies without municipal interference. Please confirm your attendance. We can provide petrol money and forty gallons of sweet cider.*

Tucked behind the letter was Arthur's own handwritten note, scribbled on the back of an envelope from the Hereford District Water Board:

*Marsh wedding confirmed. Issued Certificate No. PG-WM-2016-042 from official Swindon vellum book. Commemorative top copy delivered to bride. Duplicate yellow counter-foil retained in register folio for lodgement with registrar. NOTE: Couple very anxious regarding council interference; do not mention municipal banns.*

Arthur stared at his own note.

"Duplicate yellow counter-foil retained in register folio," he read aloud.

He dropped the envelope onto the tarpaulin and dove into the bottom of the cardboard crate. He pulled out two spare hymnbooks, a box of tapers, an electric extension lead with a cracked plug, and an unbranded brass bell used for outdoor processions.

The register folio was not in the box.

Arthur rose to his feet, grabbed the bare lightbulb by its plastic socket, and inspected the timber shelving along the garage wall. He checked behind the tins of creosote. He moved aside a stack of terracotta flowerpots, disturbing a large family of woodlice who scurried into the mortar cracks. He checked inside a disused galvanized wash tub filled with spare garden hoses.

There was no hardbound certificate folio.

Arthur stood in the center of the cold garage, his breath forming white clouds in the dim bulb light. He closed his eyes and forced his memory back ten years, to that scorching July afternoon in Blackthorn Hollow.

He remembered sitting on an upturned wooden cider barrel in the corner of the great timber barn, with the sweet, heavy smell of fermenting perry pomace in his nostrils. Leo had brought him a bottle of dark draughtsman's ink and a clean steel dip pen. Arthur had filled out the elaborate vellum certificate, filling in the names of Clementine Mary Marsh and Leo Thomas Marsh.

He had torn the top copy along the perforations and handed it to Clem. And then—what had happened to the yellow duplicate?

Arthur gasped as the memory flashed into his consciousness with sudden, terrifying vividness.

The hydraulic jack on Leo's antique apple press had slipped. A massive timber beam weighing two hundredweight had shifted on its pivots, threatening to crush thirty bushels of freshly crushed Kingston Black apples. Leo had shouted for help, and Arthur had dropped the folio onto the workbench, grabbed an iron crowbar, and thrown his whole weight against the lever while Clem jammed a block of seasoned ash beneath the ram.

In the ensuing chaos, while forty gallons of sweet apple juice had poured across the flagstones, the hardbound certificate folio had remained on Leo's carpentry bench. Arthur had driven away at dusk with a flagon of perry on his passenger seat, completely forgetting to retrieve the register book.

The yellow duplicate had never left Blackthorn Hollow. It was still in the barn.

Arthur looked at his plastic watch. Seven-fifteen.

He had thirty-three hours remaining before Audrey Vance closed her dispatch pouch.

He switched off the garage bulb, pulled down the corrugated iron door, and hurried inside to put on his driving coat.

Arthur remembered the exact layout of Leo's workbench at Blackthorn Hollow.

It had been a massive trestle table constructed from a single slab of seasoned English oak, five inches thick and fourteen feet long. At the eastern end sat Leo's sharpening stones—three grades of Arkansas oilstones soaked in neat's-foot oil—and a wooden rack holding twenty-four hand-forged chisels arranged by sixteenths of an inch. At the western end sat the draughtsman's board where Leo sketched his joinery details.

When the apple press beam had slipped that afternoon, Arthur had placed the hardbound certificate folio directly beneath the sharpening rack, behind a wooden box containing spare brass screws and beeswax blocks.

"Behind the screws," Arthur whispered, standing in his Leominster garage. "Beside the mortise chisels."

He walked over to his Astra, opened the driver's door, and retrieved his emergency kit. He checked the contents of his cardboard portfolio: four sheets of heavy archival parchment, two bottles of Higgins waterproof black ink, three new Gillott drawing nibs, and a clean bone folder he had borrowed from his sister Clara's bookbinding drawer.

If the yellow duplicate was still resting on that oak bench, it would be covered in ten years of fine ash sawdust, perry pomace dust, and dried spider webs. Arthur would need to clean the surface with absolute delicacy, ensuring that no iron gall ink was smudged before Audrey Vance inspected it with her brass calipers.

He looked out through the open garage door. The morning light was breaking through the fog, painting the wet roofs of Bargates in shades of pale rose and slate grey.

In twenty-eight hours, the audit would close. The Universal Church of Perpetual Grace would either dissolve into legal non-existence, dragging four hundred marriages into ruin, or it would stand validated upon the cornerstone of Blackthorn Hollow.

Arthur pulled his cap down over his forehead, locked the garage door, and stepped out into the road.

Arthur stood by the workbench in his cold garage, examining his old water board inspection log from nineteen-ninety-eight.

The log was a black imitation-leather notebook filled with his neat calculations of surface water runoff along the River Arrow. Every entry was a demonstration of how the natural world refused to obey municipal geometry. When heavy rain fell on the Hergest Ridge, the water did not consult the council drainage maps; it poured down ancient gullies, flooded sheep pens, and carved new channels through the red marl. The farmers had survived for centuries not by building concrete flumes, but by understanding where the water wanted to run.

Marriage was the same. The lawyers in London drew neat boxes on white paper, decreeing that love could occur only between nine in the morning and five in the afternoon beneath an approved lead roof. But human affection, like mountain rain, poured through hazel coppices and hop yards, defying every municipal schedule.

Arthur closed the water board log and placed it gently in the cardboard box.

He remembered Leo Marsh's face when the young carpenter had signed the certificate ten years ago. Leo had looked down at his own sawdust-stained fingers and said: "Arthur, I've spent six months thinking I was an outcast because I couldn't bear the tube train to Canary Wharf. But standing here with Clem, I feel like my feet are touching bedrock."

That bedrock was what Arthur was defending.

He walked over to the garage window, wiped the cobwebs from the dirty glass, and looked out at the morning sky. The clouds were gathering in the west, dark and heavy with mountain rain.

He had twenty-eight hours.

Arthur picked up his leather briefcase, turned off the garage light, and stepped out into Bargates.

Arthur carried his cardboard boxes back to the wooden shelf, arranging them by date. In twenty-two years with the municipal water authority, he had learned that an archive was not a dead cemetery of past actions; it was an active defense against future encroachment. When a ratepayer challenged a drainage assessment, the dispute was settled not by eloquence, but by producing the yellow carbon copy with the surveyor's signature from nineteen-eighty-four.

Now, as he wiped the frost from his knuckles, Arthur knew that Blackthorn Hollow was the carbon copy of his life.

Audrey Vance stepped across the flagstones of the porch with measured, clinical strides, her heels clicking against the cold Pennant stone with the regularity of an electric metronome. She carried a retractable steel surveyor's tape in her left palm. In her view, the primary defect of medieval parish churches was their total disregard for right angles and standard filing units. The walls leaned outward by three degrees to counter the thrust of the timber roof, the vestry door was two inches narrower at the lintel than at the threshold, and the floor dropped six inches between the font and the chancel steps, ensuring that any rolling coin or dropped fountain pen rolled inevitably toward the crypt grating.

He returned to the kitchen, checked the fuel gauge on the Astra with a glance through the window, and poured his thermos of tea. The day was advancing. He had thirty miles of flooded border country ahead of him, and every minute counted.

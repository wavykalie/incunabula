# Chapter 26 — The Cider Press Emergency

Before Arthur could open the red cover of the register book, a violent, metallic crash echoed from the workshop below, followed by a terrifying hiss of pressurized fluid and a shout of alarm from the yard.

"The ram!" Leo yelled, dropping his torch and lunging toward the loft hatchdoor. "The hydraulic seal on the apple press has blown!"

Clem was already through the opening, dropping down the ladder rungs with the agility of a mountain cat. Arthur tucked the red folio securely inside his Barbour jacket, zipped the heavy brass zipper up to his chin, and descended the rungs as quickly as his muddy wellington boots would permit.

When Arthur reached the workshop floor, the scene was one of total agricultural catastrophe.

The great timber apple press was shuddering violently on its sandstone footings. The two-ton central ash pressing beam had jammed at an angle of thirty degrees; forty bushels of crushed Kingston Black apples in the pressing box were under immense mechanical tension, threatening to burst the two-inch oak side staves like an exploding barrel. From the hydraulic cylinder beneath the limestone bed, a high-pressure jet of amber hydraulic oil was spraying across the workshop, mixing with a torrent of unpasteurized sweet apple juice pouring from the cracked drain spout.

"The release valve is seized!" Leo shouted, his face white with panic as he wrestled with an iron lever at the base of the cylinder. "The threads have crossed! If the pressure doesn't drop in thirty seconds, the ash beam will fracture and take the whole roof truss down!"

"Grab the timber block!" Clem screamed, throwing her weight against a massive six-by-six oak balk, trying to wedge it beneath the descending ram to take the strain off the beam. "I can't lift it alone, Leo! It weighs two hundred pounds!"

Arthur did not hesitate.

He did not think about his second-best tweed overcoat. He did not think about his municipal water board pension. He did not think about Audrey Vance's brass calipers or Dr. Finch's registered threats.

Arthur ripped off his Barbour jacket, tossed it onto a dry workbench where the red book remained safe, and threw himself into the torrent of cider and oil.

The red clay floor was slick as grease. Arthur lost his footing, skated on his knees through two inches of cold, acidic apple pomace, and grabbed the end of the oak balk with both hands.

The wood was rough, splintered, and heavy as iron. The sharp oak grain tore through the wool of his trousers, gouging his shins, but Arthur locked his elbows, set his heels against a flagstone joint, and heaved with every ounce of strength in his fifty-eight-year-old body.

"On three, Clem!" Arthur roared, his voice cutting through the hiss of the hydraulic oil like a trumpet. "One! Two! Three! HEAVE!"

Together, the middle-aged officiant and the orchard bride threw their combined weight upward.

The heavy oak balk rose six inches, cleared the iron lip of the press bed, and slammed home beneath the descending ash beam with a thunderous *CRACK*.

The press shuddered. The descending ram struck the solid oak block with a deafening metallic clang. The pressure gauge needle spun backward from red to zero; the iron side staves groaned, settled, and held firm.

Leo collapsed against the wooden lever, sobbing with relief, his hands trembling violently.

"It held," Leo gasped, looking at the oak block. "Dear God in heaven, it held."

Arthur remained on his knees in the cider trough. He was drenched from head to foot in unpasteurized Kingston Black juice, hydraulic oil, and crushed apple pomace. His flannel trousers were shredded at both knees; his woollen cardigan was stained the colour of dark port, and a large chunk of fermented apple skin was clinging to the left lens of his reading glasses.

He took off his glasses, wiped them on his soaked shirt-tail, and looked up at Clem.

Clem was staring down at him. Her face was streaked with black grease and apple juice, her flannel shirt torn at the shoulder.

For three seconds, the workshop was completely silent, save for the steady *drip-drip-drip* of sweet cider falling into the lead cistern.

Then Clementine Marsh began to laugh.

It was not a gentle chuckle; it was a deep, wild, roaring country laugh that echoed from the oak crucks to the slate roof. She threw her head back, slapped her muddy thighs, and laughed until tears cut clean white tracks through the grease on her cheeks.

Leo joined in, his nervous panic dissolving into exhausted, joyous laughter.

Arthur sat in the apple juice, looked down at his ruined trousers, and began to chuckle. The chuckle grew into a warm, helpless laugh that shook his shoulders until his ribs ached.

"Well," Arthur gasped, wiping a tear of mirth from his cheek with a muddy knuckle. "I believe... I believe that concludes the ceremonial blessing of the press, if you see what I mean."

Clem stepped forward, reached down with both strong hands, and hauled Arthur to his feet as if he were a sack of oats. She pulled him into a fierce, rib-cracking embrace, pressing her wet cheek against his soaked cardigan.

"You're a madman, Arthur Pendelton," she whispered into his ear, her voice shaking with fierce affection. "A complete, certified, magnificent madman. Nobody from the council has ever ruined a pair of trousers for us in their lives."

"The trousers were from Marks and Spencer, nineteen-ninety-nine," Arthur said mildly, patting her back. "They had reached the statutory limit of their service."

Leo walked over, picked up Arthur's dry Barbour jacket from the bench, and reverently handed it to him.

"The book is safe, Arthur," Leo said, his eyes shining. "Not a drop of cider touched it."

Arthur took the jacket, unzipped the front, and pulled out the red folio. His hands were stained with apple juice, but his fingers were steady as stone.

While Clem stoked the woodstoves and set a kettle on to boil for washing, Arthur stood in the center of the pressing shed, examining the great oak balk that had saved the harvest.

The two-ton central ash beam was resting securely upon the six-by-six oak block. The enormous pressure on the side staves had dropped to zero, leaving forty bushels of dark red Kingston Black apples safe in the hopper. A thin film of amber juice was still dripping from the lead lip into a wooden tub, smelling sweet, sharp, and intensely alive.

"That was a near run thing, Arthur," Leo said, stepping up beside him with an oily spanner in his hand. "If that beam had fractured, the flying oak splinters would have taken out the western wall. We would have lost the press, the harvest, and half the workshop."

Arthur looked at his soaked woollen cardigan. The apple juice had dyed the grey wool an uneven shade of maroon; a sticky mixture of hydraulic oil and fruit pomace clung to his cuffs, smelling like a cross between a railway locomotive and a cider cellar.

"In the municipal water board, Leo," Arthur remarked with a dry chuckle, "the worst catastrophe we ever faced was an unmetered car wash in Leominster that drew seventy gallons an hour from an unlicensed hydrant. When the inspector turned the main valve, the water pressure blew the head off a plastic garden gnome sixty yards into an elderberry bush. We thought that was high drama. But holding an oak beam under thirty tons of cider pressure... that is a very different order of public service."

Clem returned carrying a basin of warm water, a bar of carbolic soap, and two thick linen towels.

"Sit down by the fire, Arthur, and let's get that grease off your neck before it sets like varnish," she ordered with maternal authority. "Leo, fetch your clean flannel shirt from the caravan—the green check one with the pearl buttons. Arthur can't go before Audrey Vance smelling like an exploded tractor."

Arthur sat obediently on a three-legged stool beside the woodstove while Clem scrubbed the red clay from his wrists with the carbolic soap. The warm water was heaven against his chilled skin.

"Audrey Vance has a very keen nose for statutory propriety, Clem," Arthur murmured, closing his eyes as the warm towel wiped his forehead. "She will detect the smell of Kingston Black at twenty paces."

"Then let her smell it," Clem said fiercely, wringing out the towel. "Let her smell the earth and the apples. It's time the Shirehall remembered what real work smells like."

Clem brought over a large stoneware flagon of vintage perry, pouring three generous measures into earthenware mugs.

"Drink that down, Arthur," Clem commanded. "It's twenty-twenty-two Blakeney Red. It has the sharpness of wild fruit and the warmth of summer sunshine. It'll put the heat back into your shins."

Arthur took a deep sip. The cider was crisp, golden, and sparkling on the tongue, carrying the clean taste of autumn orchards and oak barrels. A gentle, spreading warmth filled his chest, driving away the chill of the floodwater.

"Splendid," Arthur murmured, smacking his lips with genuine appreciation. "A noble vintage, Clem. The municipal council in Hereford has nothing half so restorative in their committee rooms."

Leo leaned against the wooden press upright, his hands clean of oil. "We press forty barrels every autumn, Arthur. Half goes to the local pubs, and half stays in the cellar for the winter. It's an honest living. We don't make fortunes, but our bread is our own, and our roof doesn't leak. If the law will let us keep this valley, we ask for nothing else in this world."

Arthur looked at the two young people. "The law will let you keep it, Leo. I give you my solemn word."

Clem brought Arthur a clean dry pair of heavy woollen work socks, knitted from the fleece of her own Black Welsh Mountain sheep.

"Put those on, Arthur," she said, kneeling to hand them to him. "Your slippers are floating in the scullery trough. You can't walk into the Shirehall with wet heels."

Arthur slipped the thick wool over his bruised ankles. The socks were soft, scratchy, and wonderfully warm.

"Magnificent wool, Clem," Arthur said. "Warm as an ecclesiastical benediction."

The ladder to the hayloft had fourteen rungs, each worn into a smooth, dished curve by fifty years of hobnailed boots. Arthur ascended slowly, holding the greasy side rails with both hands, his cassock tucked securely into his trouser belt. At the top, the loft opened into a vast shadowy cavern smelling of old straw, dried clover, and roosting pigeons. Cobwebs hung from the purlins like dusty lace curtains. In the far corner, half-concealed behind a broken horse rake and three empty oil drums, stood the green tin chest that had belonged to the village harvest committee since the end of the war.

"Better than a benediction," Clem laughed. "It keeps the frost off your toes."

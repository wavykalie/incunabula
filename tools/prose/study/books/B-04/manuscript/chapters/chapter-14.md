# Chapter 14 — The Vicar's Pint

At half-past one that afternoon, Arthur pulled his Astra into the gravel car park of the Red Lion at Madley.

The Red Lion was an ancient timber-framed coaching inn whose ceiling beams sagged under the weight of four centuries of cider smoke. The pub was empty save for an elderly sheep farmer dozing beside the hearth and a retired Anglican clergyman sitting in the bay window with a pint of bitter and a crossword puzzle from the Daily Telegraph.

The clergyman was the Reverend Canon Christopher Barling, aged seventy-eight, who had served as rector of three Golden Valley parishes until his retirement in 2014. Canon Barling was dressed in a battered tweed jacket with leather elbow patches, an open-collared check shirt, and a pair of muddy corduroy trousers tucked into green wellington boots.

"Arthur, my dear boy!" Canon Barling called out, his weathered face creasing into a network of benevolent wrinkles. "Come and sit down before the draught from the passage gives you lumbago. You look as pale as an unvarnished choir stall."

Arthur ordered a half-pint of mild and a ham roll at the counter, carried them to the bay window, and sat down opposite the old priest.

"I am facing an ecclesiastical crisis, Christopher," Arthur said quietly, laying his hands flat upon the oak table.

Canon Barling took a slow sip of his bitter and looked at Arthur over the rim of his glasses. "The Swindon enterprise? Has Finch finally been visited by the bailiffs?"

"Worse," Arthur confessed. "The Charity Commission is auditing his accounts, and Audrey Vance has published a warning notice in the gazette. She has given me until Friday afternoon to produce certified statutory returns for twelve years of marriages."

Canon Barling gave a soft, wheezing chuckle that sounded like dry leaves rustling in an empty gutter.

"Audrey Vance," the canon murmured affectionately. "A splendid woman, Arthur. A magnificent monument to nineteenth-century civil administrative orthodoxy. When I was rural dean in nineteen-ninety-two, she tried to issue an injunction against my harvest festival because we had placed three bushels of Conference pears within the sanctuary boundaries without an ecclesiastical faculty. She informed me that uncertified fruit in the chancel was an unauthorized alteration to an approved place of worship under the Act of eighteen-fifty-five."

"She told me that my four hundred marriages are merely theatrical loitering," Arthur said, the bitterness breaking through his gentle voice. "She said that without register entries, they are void under Section forty-nine."

Canon Barling set his glass down on a cardboard beer mat. His smile faded, replaced by an expression of deep, sober wisdom.

"Listen to me, Arthur," the old priest said, leaning across the table. "Do you know when the Church of England officially ceased inspecting rural field marriages in this diocese?"

"I have no idea," Arthur admitted.

"Nineteen-eighty-nine," Canon Barling said. "The bishop sent out a questionnaire to every rural dean between Shrewsbury and Newport, asking us to report all unauthorized outdoor ceremonies conducted by eccentric vicars, Methodist local preachers, and independent evangelists. Do you know what we found?"

"What did you find?"

"We found that half the county had been married in orchards, hop kilns, sheep folds, and cider cellars since the days of Oliver Cromwell. In rural England, Arthur, the people have always married where their work was. They married by their apple trees and their cattle pens because the land was their life. The bishop took one look at our report, realized that declaring those marriages irregular would invalidate half the farm mortgages in the West Midlands, and locked the document in the cathedral crypt."

Canon Barling picked up his crossword pen.

"The state needs its registers, Arthur. Governments cannot function without neat rows of figures and certified counter-foils. But do not ever let Audrey Vance or Alistair Finch persuade you that the validity of human love lives in an iron safe in Hereford. God does not keep an office beneath the Shirehall. He was standing in the mud with you and the sheep."

Arthur looked at the old priest. A knot of tension in his chest eased slightly.

"I have to find the Marsh certificate, Christopher," Arthur said. "At Blackthorn Hollow."

"Then go and find it, boy," Canon Barling said, tapping the newspaper. "And if Leo Marsh is running his apple press, bring me back a flagon of the Kingston Black. The pub cider tastes like battery acid this autumn."

Arthur finished his mild, stood up, and buttoned his overcoat.

Canon Barling took another contemplative sip of his bitter, looking out through the rain-streaked leaded glass toward the stone tower of Madley church.

"Do you know the real secret of Audrey Vance, Arthur?" the canon asked, lowering his voice to a conspiratorial whisper.

"I know she has twenty-eight years of service and keeps brass calipers in her drawer," Arthur replied, finishing the crust of his ham roll.

"She was engaged once," Barling said quietly. "In nineteen-ninety-four, when she was a young clerk in the records office. A splendid young man named Martin, an apprentice surveyor with the county road department. They had planned to marry in an open field near the Malverns, beneath the British Camp. Martin had spent six months clearing an old hazel bower and planting wild snowdrops for the aisle."

Arthur looked up in astonishment. "Audrey Vance planned an outdoor wedding?"

"She did indeed," Barling said, nodding his white head. "And do you know what happened? Old Mr. Gwatkin, who was Superintendent Registrar at the time, discovered the plan. He called her into his office, pulled down the Marriages Act, and told her that if she married in an uncertified field, he would terminate her clerkship for professional misconduct and refuse to enter the marriage in the county gazette. He told her she was a servant of the crown and must set an example of rigid statutory obedience."

Arthur stared at the old priest. "And what did she do?"

"She gave in, Arthur. She cancelled the meadow bower. They booked the municipal office in Hereford. But two weeks before the ceremony, Martin was killed in a road accident while surveying the Leominster bypass. She never married. She stayed in that basement, memorizing every clause, every subsection, and every statutory schedule, turning herself into an iron instrument of the law because the law was the only thing that didn't break or die."

Arthur felt a sudden, profound ache in his chest. The terrifying image of Audrey Vance—the woman with the razor-sharp spectacles and the snapping calipers—dissolved into a portrait of human grief and lifelong duty.

"She isn't trying to destroy you, Arthur," Canon Barling said gently. "She is testing you. She wants to see if you have the courage to fight for your couples in a way she was never allowed to fight for herself. If you produce that Marsh certificate, she will not use it as a weapon. She will use it as a bridge."

Arthur set down his glass. The path ahead seemed suddenly clear and inevitable.

Canon Barling pushed his empty beer glass toward the center of the table and leaned back in the horsehair booth.

"One final piece of advice, Arthur," the old priest said, tapping the oak table with a spotted finger. "When you stand before Audrey Vance, do not apologize for the mud. Do not shuffle your feet or mumble about your online certificate. The Church of England lost the rural valleys because its clergy became embarrassed by mud and cows. You did not leave your people. You stood where they lived. If the law has forgotten how to bless an orchard, that is the law's misfortune, not yours."

Arthur looked at the old rector, feeling a sudden surge of gratitude in his throat.

"Thank you, Christopher," Arthur said.

"Now go," Barling said, waving his crossword pen toward the door. "The river is rising, and you have thirty miles of Welsh border ahead of you."

The box in the loft was labeled 'Deanery Choral Festival 1998' in faded purple stencil ink, but when Arthur lifted the split cardboard flap, he found three brass curtain rings, a broken boxwood ruler, and twenty-two unaddressed parish newsletters from the month of the Queen's Golden Jubilee. Dust motes drifted through the beam of his torch. Beneath the newsletters lay a bundle of receipts tied with hairy brown garden twine. He cut the twine with his penknife. The top receipt was for four hundredweight of anthracite delivered to the church hall by horse-drawn dray in the harsh winter of nineteen sixty-two, stamped and signed by a churchwarden three decades in the churchyard.

Arthur rose, stepped out into the misty courtyard, and unhitched his Astra.

# Chapter 31 — The Drive to Hereford

The sea-grey Vauxhall Astra sped east along the A438 bypass toward Hereford, its four cylinders humming with a determined, throaty rattle.

Beside Arthur in the passenger seat sat Leo Marsh, clutching the leather briefcase containing the certified parchment transcript and the red Swindon folio. In the rear seat, Clem sat with her arms resting on the front seats, her keen country eyes scanning the road ahead like an artillery spotter.

The morning sun had burned away the mountain mists, bathing the Herefordshire landscape in crisp, golden light. The orchards along the highway were carpeted with fallen yellow leaves; the red sandstone farmhouses glowed like warm hearths against the green pastures, and in the distance the square tower of Hereford Cathedral rose above the River Wye like a grey rock in an ocean of meadows.

"How is the battery voltage, Arthur?" Leo asked, eyeing the dashboard instruments with craftsman's concern.

Arthur tapped the plastic lens of the dashboard with his knuckle.

"The alternator warning light has been flickering since Kingstone, Leo," Arthur replied calmly. "A loose fan belt, I suspect. It has been threatening to fail since the jubilee of twenty-twenty-two. But the Astra knows we are on statutory business. She will not abandon us on the bypass."

As they crossed the bridge over the Wye into Hereford, the traffic slowed to a crawl. A convoy of three livestock lorries and a council road-sweeper were navigating the narrow archway of the old city walls.

Arthur looked at the dashboard clock: ten-fifteen.

They were two hours ahead of Finch's noon deadline, and six hours ahead of Audrey Vance's dispatch pouch.

"Where are we parking, Arthur?" Clem asked, leaning forward between their shoulders. "The Shirehall courtyard has an iron barrier and a municipal ticket machine that rejects Scottish banknotes."

"We are not parking in the public courtyard, my dear," Arthur said, a subtle smile playing across his lips. "We are parking in the designated municipal space reserved for visiting ministers of religion and authorized statutory officiants, situated immediately adjacent to the side entrance on Gaol Street."

Leo blinked at him. "Do you have an authorized sticker?"

"I have an unlaminated notice issued by the Hereford District Water Board in nineteen-ninety-four, certifying that the bearer is on essential public utility business," Arthur said, pulling a faded card from the sun visor. "In thirty years of municipal service, I have never known a council traffic warden who could distinguish between an emergency water inspector and an itinerant presbyter."

Arthur turned the Astra into Gaol Street, navigated between two delivery vans, and slotted the car smoothly into the reserved bay directly opposite the red-brick Victorian annex of the registry office.

He switched off the ignition. The engine gave one final, satisfied shudder and subsided into silence.

Leo picked up the leather briefcase. Clem checked the straps of her clean flannel shirt, smoothed her dark hair with both palms, and took a deep breath of the Hereford air, which smelled of diesel exhaust, roasted coffee from the high street, and damp sandstone.

Arthur reached into his pocket, found three barley sugars, and handed one to Leo and one to Clem.

"Take your sugar, my dears," Arthur said gently, popping the third sweet into his own cheek. "A steady mouth produces a steady argument."

Together, the three of them stepped out onto the pavement and turned toward the stone steps of the Shirehall.

As the Astra navigated the roundabout outside the Whitecross road, Clem leaned forward, pointing through the windscreen at a white commercial van ahead of them in the traffic.

"Look at that, Arthur," Clem said with a wide grin. "That's Tom Pugh's livestock lorry from Michaelchurch Escley."

On the rear tailboard of the cattle truck, someone had chalked in bold white capitals: *WE STAND BY ARTHUR PENDELTON — KEEP YOUR CALIPERS OFF OUR MEADOWS*.

Behind them, a red tractor driven by a young farmer from Kingstone gave three loud, triumphant blasts of its electric horn, waving a bunch of wild Michaelmas daisies through the open cab window.

Arthur felt a sudden tightness in his throat. He looked at Leo in the passenger seat.

"Leo," Arthur murmured, his voice thick with emotion. "Did the entire valley mobilize?"

"I told you, Arthur," Leo said softly, his carpenter's fingers steady upon the leather briefcase. "When you gave forty people their homes, you didn't just sign a certificate. You built a community. In London, people live in concrete boxes and don't know the names of the people on the other side of the plasterboard. Here, when one man stands before the magistrate, the whole valley walks behind him."

Arthur guided the Astra along the ancient ring road, past the medieval walls of the city. The cathedral bells were beginning to chime eleven o'clock, their deep bronze tones echoing across the roofs of Hereford with timeless majesty.

As Arthur pulled into the reserved municipal bay in Gaol Street, two traffic wardens in yellow-banded caps were standing by the kerb with their electronic ticket terminals. The older warden took one look at the sea-grey Astra, recognized Arthur's water board badge on the sun visor, and tipped his peaked cap with solemn respect.

"Morning, Mr. Pendelton," the warden said. "The Superintendent Registrar has instructed us to keep this bay clear until four-thirty. Good luck with the audit, sir."

"Thank you, officer," Arthur said, stepping out into the fresh November air.

Leo lifted the briefcase from the passenger seat. Clem took Arthur's left arm, holding it with fierce, daughterly strength.

"Right, Arthur," Clem said, looking up at the red-brick Shirehall annex. "Let's go and teach the crown how to bless an orchard."

As the Astra crossed the ancient bridge over the Wye, Arthur pointed out the medieval stone arches reflected in the clear river below.

"Those piers were laid in the fourteenth century, my dears," Arthur remarked. "They were built of red sandstone from the Golden Valley, set in lime mortar. The river has flooded a hundred times since then, but the bridge stands because the foundations were honest. That is what we are taking into the Shirehall today: an honest foundation that can bear the weight of four hundred lives."

Clem smiled and patted his shoulder. "And you're the master mason, Arthur."

As Arthur parked the Astra in Gaol Street, he looked through the windscreen at the red-brick Victorian facade of the registry office.

For twelve years, that building had been a source of latent, unvoiced dread—a forbidding fortress of municipal authority that he had avoided whenever possible. Now, sitting in his car with the certified portfolio on Leo's knees, the building seemed merely an old public workshop where the records of human affection were sorted and filed.

"Right, my dears," Arthur said, unclicking his seatbelt. "Let us step inside and show Her Majesty's registrar how the Golden Valley keeps its promises."

Clem checked her boots, and Leo picked up the briefcase. Together, they walked toward the stone steps.

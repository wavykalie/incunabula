# Chapter 22 — The Off-Grid Republic

The community that had gathered around Blackthorn Hollow over ten years was not merely an organic farm; it was an informal, fiercely independent rural republic.

As Arthur drank his scalding black tea from a chipped enamel mug, Clem explained the precarious reality of their existence on the edge of the Black Mountains.

"We don't have mains electricity, Arthur," Clem said, gesturing with her mug toward the timber rafters where bundles of dried herbs and curing hams hung from iron hooks. "We have a Pelton water wheel on the mill leat that generates twelve volts for the workshop lights, and two solar panels on the pig shed. We don't have a broadband line because the council wanted four thousand pounds to dig a trench through the hazel wood. And we don't have a commercial bank account because the bank manager in Hereford demanded three years of certified audited accounts before he would let us cash a cheque for fifty gallons of cider."

"How do you trade?" Arthur asked, fascinated despite his administrative exhaustion.

"We trade in timber, perry, and labour," Leo said, leaning his hip against a massive ash workbench. "The blacksmith in Peterchurch shoes our horses and repairs our iron hoop bands, and we give him forty gallons of Kingston Black and two cords of seasoned oak offcuts. The stonemason in Longtown rebuilt our barn footings, and I built him a set of dining chairs out of local walnut. We live by honest exchange, Arthur. We don't need Swindon's permission, and we don't need council certificates."

"That is a noble ideal, Leo," Arthur said gently, setting down his mug. "It is an ancient, beautiful way to live. But the modern world does not stop at the cattle grid. When the building society looks at your property deeds, they do not ask how many flagons of perry you gave the blacksmith. They ask if you and Clem are lawfully married spouses under the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine."

Clem's face hardened. She picked up a wooden apple scoop and tapped it rhythmically against the rim of a barrel.

"The building society has been writing to us since June," she admitted in a low voice. "Our agricultural mortgage comes up for renewal in December. The solicitors in Hereford sent a three-page requisition demanding a certified extract of our marriage registration from the Superintendent Registrar's office. When I wrote back telling them that our certificate was signed in this barn by an ordained minister of the church, they informed me that our name did not appear in the county index."

Arthur looked at her with profound sympathy. "And what did they threaten?"

"They threatened to repossess the lower orchard," Clem whispered. Her voice trembled with rare, barely controlled fury. "They said that without proof of marriage, the property title reverts to a tenancy-in-common, and because of an old family dispute with my father's executors, the lower twenty acres can be sold to satisfy estate debts. Our perry trees, Arthur! Trees that were planted when Queen Victoria was on the throne! They want to cut them down for timber to pay London solicitors!"

Leo put his arm around his wife's shoulders. His carpenter's fingers were steady, protective, and firm.

"The certificate matters to us, Arthur," Leo said softly. "We pretend it's just a scrap of paper, but it isn't. When you stood in that straw ten years ago, you made this valley our home. If that document is lost, they will take our land, and they will take our peace."

Arthur rose from his stool. His fatigue had vanished, replaced by an iron sense of purpose that coursed through his veins like strong tea.

"Then we shall find it," Arthur said. "Where is the book, Leo?"

Leo looked at his workbench, then looked up toward the dark opening of the hayloft above the press.

"In twenty-sixteen, when the press slipped," Leo murmured, "I packed all the paperwork from that afternoon into a wooden butter tub and carried it up to the loft to keep it dry while we repaired the hydraulic ram. It has been up there ever since."

Arthur picked up his brass torch. "Show me the ladder."

While Leo fetched a lantern from the tool shed, Arthur stood beside the workshop door, looking out at the cluster of stone and timber outbuildings that made up the farmyard.

Despite the torrential rain, the hollow was far from deserted. At the far end of the yard, beneath an open timber canopy, two local smallholders in oilskin coats were unloading three dozen wooden apple crates from the bed of a Ferguson tractor. One of them was Thomas Gwillam—no relation to Arthur's Leominster neighbour, but a second cousin to the cooper who had witnessed the 2016 ceremony.

"Afternoon, Arthur!" Gwillam shouted across the mud, lifting a wooden crate of red-cheeked cider apples with casual strength. "Heard you were over the ridge! The blacksmith told us you were looking for old lines!"

"I am indeed, Thomas!" Arthur called back, waving his tea mug. "Looking for the July twenty-sixteen folio!"

"It's in the loft, boy!" Gwillam bellowed with a cheerful grin. "Leo put it in the sycamore tub behind the owl! Don't let the bird peck your glasses off; she's fiercely protective of council planning paper!"

Clem laughed softly beside Arthur. "Everyone knows everything in the Golden Valley, Arthur. A tractor doesn't cross a cattle grid between Peterchurch and Craswall without twenty people noting the colour of the mud on the tyres. When you crossed the ford on foot this morning, young Robin Vance spotted your black umbrella from his sheep shed. By two o'clock, half the valley knew that Arthur Pendelton was marching on Blackthorn Hollow to defend their marriages."

"Young Robin Vance?" Arthur asked, pausing with his mug halfway to his lips. "The young man from the railway brake van?"

"The very same," Clem said. "He's Audrey Vance's nephew, Arthur. His father is her younger brother, the one who runs the sawmill in Titley. When Robin married Holly in that railway van, Audrey refused to attend because of the ungrounded wheel bogies. But Robin loves his aunt, and he knows how her mind works. He called me on the radio half an hour ago to tell us that Audrey had secretly marked the Marsh entry in her historical gazette with a question mark. She wants this document, Arthur. She needs an excuse to rule in our favour without breaking her oath to the county."

Arthur looked out at the rain falling across the orchard. The pieces of the puzzle were falling into place with astonishing, geometric elegance.

"Then we must give her an excuse," Arthur said, "that no court in England can overturn."

Arthur finished his tea and set the enamel mug on the carpenter's bench.

"There is an old saying in municipal water billing, Clem," Arthur remarked with a smile. "Water always finds the sea, no matter how many concrete dams you build. Human life is the same. People will find a way to work, to trade, and to love, regardless of how many forms the government prints."

"They certainly will in this hollow, Arthur," Clem said, picking up her pruning hook. "Now let's find that red book and make sure the sea doesn't wash away our orchard."

Leo led Arthur into the small timber drying shed behind the press.

"Look at these pear-wood screws, Arthur," Leo said, pointing to two massive wooden threaded shafts, eighteen inches in diameter. "My great-grandfather cut these threads with a wooden router box in eighteen-eighty. They have pressed forty thousand tons of fruit without stripping a thread. That's what real work is. It stands when the people who made it are asleep in the earth."

The church heating system was an ancient coke-fired gravity system known to the rural deanery as the Leviathan. Its cast-iron pipes, four inches in diameter, ran beneath floor gratings around the entire perimeter of the nave, clanking and groaning whenever the water reached eighty degrees. Arthur descended the stone steps to the boiler pit, carrying a shovel of washed anthracite to bank the fire for the night. The furnace door glowed dull cherry red, casting flickering shadows against the whitewashed rubble walls of the undercroft.

Arthur touched the smooth wood with reverence. "An immortal thread, Leo. Exactly like the vows we spoke in this barn."

# Chapter 34 — The Law of Custom

Audrey Vance reached behind her desk, unlocked the third oak filing press, and pulled down a massive, calfskin-bound volume whose spine was stamped in gold: *STATUTES OF THE REALM — VOL. VI: ECCLESIASTICAL AND MARITIME EXEMPTIONS (1835–1850)*.

She laid the book open between them, turning the yellowed vellum leaves until she reached the Act of 6 & 7 William IV, chapter 85.

"Section twenty-two," Audrey said, reading the antique print aloud in her clear, judicial voice. "*AND BE IT ENACTED, That in the counties of Hereford, Monmouth, and Gloucester, where ancient agricultural tenures do persist, any permanent structure accustomed to be used for the seasonal pressing of apples or pears shall be deemed an approved chapel-of-ease for the purposes of this Act, provided that a register be concurrently kept and submitted for triennial inspection.*"

She looked up at Arthur.

"You cited this statute on page forty-two of your red book, Mr. Pendelton."

"I did, Mrs. Vance," Arthur said. "In Higgins waterproof ink, on the sixteenth of July, twenty-sixteen."

"It is an obscure provision," Audrey remarked, her tone academic and detached. "It was enacted to resolve a dispute between the Bishop of Hereford and the cider makers of the Golden Valley during the tithe wars of eighteen-thirty-four. The farmers had refused to pay church rates on their perry mills unless the magistrates recognized their barns as places of lawful assembly."

"And the provision was never repealed, Mrs. Vance," Arthur noted gently.

"It was preserved by Section seventy-nine of the nineteen-forty-nine consolidation," Audrey agreed, nodding slowly. "Under the doctrine of implied statutory survival. The Law Commission overlooked it in nineteen-seventy-seven because they believed that no one in Herefordshire still pressed perry with timber screws."

She looked at Leo Marsh.

"Mr. Marsh: is your apple press a permanent fixture within the meaning of the law?"

"The bed is six tons of carboniferous limestone set three feet into the gravel, Mrs. Vance," Leo stated with technical pride. "The uprights are mortised into the barn's sleeper beams with four-inch seasoned oak trunnels. It cannot be removed without demolishing the eastern cruck truss."

Audrey picked up her ebony pen.

"Then the press is an immovable architectural fixture annexed to the soil of Herefordshire. And the barn is an approved chapel-of-ease under the customary schedule of eighteen-thirty-six."

She paused, looking at Arthur with an expression of profound, formidable respect.

"You have provided the cornerstone, Mr. Pendelton. The Marsh marriage is valid in law. It was celebrated in an approved sanctuary under an existing statutory exemption, and it was recorded in a concurrent register with verified witness attestations."

Arthur felt a great wave of warmth flood through his chest, so intense that he had to grip the edge of the mahogany desk to keep his knees from buckling.

"Thank heaven," Arthur whispered.

"Do not thank heaven yet, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey said, raising a warning finger. "The Marsh marriage is one union. What of the remaining three hundred and ninety-nine?"

Before Arthur could reply, the heavy mahogany corridor doors burst open with a violent, shuddering crash, and a man came stumbling into the private office, clutching a portable plastic lamination machine under his arm like an errant rugby football.

Audrey Vance turned to the assistant archivist's transcript of the 1836 Assizes records, comparing it with Arthur's yellow counter-foil.

"The doctrine of common repute," Audrey explained, her tone shifting from prosecutor to high administrative jurist, "is one of the most venerable principles of English jurisprudence. In *Campbell v. Campbell (1867)*, the House of Lords ruled that where a man and woman cohabit together as husband and wife, and are treated as such by their neighbours, the law of England will presume that a lawful marriage took place, unless the contrary be proved by clear, decisive, and incontrovertible evidence."

She looked at Leo and Clem.

"Over the past forty-eight hours, my office has received seventy-four telephone calls from residents of the Golden Valley. The parish council of Vowchurch has transmitted a formal resolution certifying that Leo and Clementine Marsh have been recognized as lawful husband and wife by the entire civil parish for ten consecutive years. The vicar of Peterchurch has written a letter confirming that they have contributed thirty flagons of cider annually to the harvest supper and are regarded as pillars of the rural community."

Audrey looked back at Arthur.

"When common repute is supported by an authentic contemporaneous counter-foil executed in an approved historical sanctuary, Mr. Pendelton, the presumption of lawful marriage becomes absolute. It cannot be overturned by an administrative audit, nor by an order from Swindon."

She took up her ebony pen and made a large, decisive black tick in the margin of the Master Register.

"The Marsh marriage stands, Mr. Pendelton. Valid in law, valid in equity, and valid in the records of the County of Herefordshire."

Audrey Vance closed the 1836 Assizes volume and rested her fingers upon the calfskin cover.

"The English common law has many defects, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey reflected in a low, thoughtful voice. "It is slow, it is cumbersome, and it is cluttered with ancient Norman vocabulary. But it possesses one magnificent virtue: it respects long-established human custom. When a community has lived in peace under an ancient tradition, the common law will stretch its shield over them rather than tear their domestic security to pieces."

"A very noble virtue, Audrey," Arthur murmured with profound respect.

Audrey Vance turned to Miss Cleary.

"Miss Cleary: prepare a formal minute of this determination," Audrey directed. "Record that pursuant to Section seventy-nine of the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine, the customary border exemption recognized by the Act of William the Fourth hath been verified in respect of the cruck barn at Blackthorn Hollow. Said structure is hereby declared an approved customary chapel-of-ease for the purposes of rural registration."

Miss Cleary's pencil flew across the notebook with joyous, rapid strokes.

"Minute prepared, Mrs. Vance," Miss Cleary said, her eyes shining with professional pride.

Audrey looked at Arthur, Leo, and Clem.

"The determination is entered upon the county record," Audrey said with solemn finality. "The Marsh marriage is valid, gazetted, and permanent."

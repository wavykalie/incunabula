# Chapter 35 — The Swindon Ambush

The intruder was dishevelled, breathless, and drenched to the skin.

He was sixty-one years old, dressed in a cheap navy blazer whose brass buttons were tarnished, grey flannel trousers with mud splatters up to the pockets, and a clerical collar made of white plastic that had detached at the left stud and was poking out past his ear like a rogue feather. Under his left arm he clutched a beige Fellowes desktop laminator, trailing a six-foot black electrical cord that scraped along the parquet floor. In his right hand he carried an expandable cardboard briefcase bursting with gold-foil adhesive stickers and laminated certificate templates.

It was the Reverend Doctor Alistair Finch.

"Pendelton!" Finch shrieked, skidding on the polished floorboards and catching himself on the edge of Audrey Vance's desk. "I caught the nine-fifteen National Express coach from Swindon! The Charity Commission has sealed my bungalow! They have seized my computer! They have impounded my stationary stock! Hand over the registers, Arthur! Hand them over immediately!"

Arthur stepped back in astonishment. "Dr. Finch! What on earth are you doing in Hereford?"

"I am exercising my canonical authority as apostolic overseer!" Finch gasped, spraying droplets of rain across the mahogany desk. He slammed his cardboard briefcase down on top of the 1836 statute book. "The Charity Commission compliance officer, Mr. Garside, is arriving by car at twelve o'clock! He has agreed to stay the corporate liquidation if I can tender five thousand pounds in delinquent administrative registration fees! I am charging every celebrant in the Western Marches a mandatory retrospective tithe of twelve pounds fifty per ceremony! Four hundred ceremonies, Pendelton! That is five thousand pounds exactly! Sign the debenture, Arthur, or I will defrock you on this very carpet!"

Audrey Vance did not rise from her chair.

She sat motionless, her spectacles perched on her nose, looking at Finch with an expression of freezing, absolute municipal contempt that would have withered an oak tree.

"Who," Audrey asked in a voice that dropped the temperature of the room by ten degrees, "is this damp individual?"

Finch puffed out his chest, attempting to adjust his detached clerical collar.

"I am the Most Reverend Overseer Dr. Alistair Finch, Doctor of Divinity, Chief Executive Trustee of the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace, Incorporated in England and Wales under Company Registration Number 08412991!"

Audrey Vance picked up a single sheet of paper from her middle drawer. It was an official police telegraph received forty minutes earlier from Wiltshire.

"You are Alistair Finch, formerly lecturer in sociology at Gloucester Polytechnic," Audrey stated, her voice like cracking ice. "At nine-forty-five this morning, the High Court of Justice in London, upon the petition of Her Majesty's Attorney General, issued an order of summary winding-up against your corporate entity under Section one-hundred-and-twenty-four of the Insolvency Act. Your company is dissolved. Your trusteeship is terminated. And a warrant for your arrest under Section forty-seven of the Charities Act has been transmitted to the West Mercia Constabulary."

Finch's face turned the colour of cold oatmeal. He dropped the lamination machine; it hit the parquet floor with a hollow plastic crunch, spitting two rubber rollers across the rug.

"Dissolved?" Finch whispered. "My church... dissolved?"

"You have no church, Mr. Finch," Audrey said, rising slowly to her full height and towering over him behind her desk. "You never had a church. You had a fraudulent web domain and a box of gold stickers. You have thirty seconds to remove your plastic appliances from my office before I summon the municipal bailiffs to take you into custody."

Finch looked at Arthur with wild, terrified eyes. He looked at Clem, who took half a step forward with her fists clenched.

Without uttering another syllable, the former Overseer of Swindon scooped up his broken laminator, grabbed his leaking briefcase, and fled from the room, his wet shoes squeaking frantically down the long stone corridor.

After Finch had scrambled down the corridor, his broken Fellowes laminator trailing behind him like a dead anchor, Clem walked to the doorway, looked out into the hallway, and gave a sharp, satisfied whistle.

"The little toad dropped his spare gold stickers on the doormat," Clem said, returning with three sheets of foil seals. "Look at this, Arthur: *Universal Church of Perpetual Grace — Certified by Electronic Apostolic Mandate*. What do we do with them?"

Arthur took the shiny gold stickers from her hand.

He walked over to the wastepaper basket beside Audrey Vance's desk—a heavy municipal wire basket filled with discarded blotting paper and pencil shavings.

Arthur dropped the gold stickers into the bin.

"We leave them where they belong, my dear," Arthur said gently. "In the municipal waste of Herefordshire."

Audrey Vance looked at the wastepaper basket, then looked at Arthur. A faint, approving nod passed between them.

"An appropriate administrative disposition, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey murmured. "Now let us attend to the people in the hall."

Audrey Vance watched the door swing shut after Finch's departure.

"A thoroughly unimpressive rogue, Mr. Pendelton," she observed coldly.

"Thoroughly unimpressive, Mrs. Vance," Arthur agreed. "A sad product of the dial-up era."

The bell ropes in the ringing chamber hung through their ceiling grommets like long, furry bell-ropes tipped with red, white, and blue sally wool. Arthur gave the treble rope a gentle tug, feeling the distant weight of eight hundredweight of cast bronze rocking on its gudgeons seventy feet above. The bell did not strike, but the rope rose and fell in his palm with a slow, hypnotic rhythm, vibrant with potential energy. A parish church was never entirely silent; the timbers contracted in the cold, the wind hummed through the belfry louvers, and the bells swayed gently in their oak frames.

Audrey smoothed her blotter. "Let us return to the real business of the day."

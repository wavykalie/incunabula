# Chapter 8 — The First Audit Call

At ten minutes past four that afternoon, Arthur Pendelton picked up his landline telephone and dialled the Swindon telephone number printed on the purple PDF document.

The dial tone buzzed in his ear with the flat, mechanical indifference of a municipal switchboard. Arthur gripped the cream plastic handset until his thumb turned white. In his cardigan pocket, his left hand fingered a peppermint humbug, turning it over and over like a set of worry beads.

The phone rang four times. Then came a sharp click, followed by the sound of heavy breathing and the rustle of loose paper.

"Sanctuary of Perpetual Grace, administrative secretariat," a voice whispered. It was a breathless, synthetic whisper, pitched somewhere between an Anglican archdeacon and a frightened bank manager during a robbery. "Overseer Finch speaking. Is this an official inspection call from the Charity Commission?"

"No," Arthur said hastily. "No, Dr. Finch. It is Arthur Pendelton. In Leominster. The Western March province."

There was an abrupt silence on the line. Then a long, wheezing exhalation that caused the telephone earpiece to crackle with static.

"Pendelton," Finch hissed. His liturgical whisper evaporated instantly, replaced by a sharp, panicked suburban rasp. "Arthur! Good God, man, why didn't you identify yourself? I thought you were the regional compliance team from Taunton. Have you seen the transmission? Have you printed the schedule?"

"I have printed the schedule, Dr. Finch," Arthur said mildly, sitting down on the telephone stool beside the umbrella stand. "I have fourteen pages on my kitchen table. That is rather why I am ringing. The requirements... the Ordnance Survey twelve-figure coordinates and the Superintendent Registrar counter-foils..."

"Never mind the coordinates!" Finch interrupted, his voice rising to an agitated squeak. "Arthur, you don't understand the catastrophe we are facing! The Charity Commission is literally sitting in my front parlour at this very moment!"

Arthur blinked at the wallpaper. "In your parlour, Dr. Finch?"

"Two investigators!" Finch whispered frantically. "A Mr. Garside and a Miss Thrip. They arrived at two o'clock in a blue Vauxhall Corsa. They have four brown cardboard archive boxes, an electronic barcode scanner, and an official warrant under Section 46 of the Charities Act. They are auditing every single donation, every online ordination fee, and every gift aid covenant dating back to twenty-fourteen!"

"Good heavens," Arthur said, shocked by the sudden vision of municipal terror in Wiltshire. "Are they... are they aggressive?"

"Mr. Garside has eaten three of my ginger biscuits and hasn't smiled once," Finch whispered. "Miss Thrip is photocopying our PayPal statements on my personal Brother all-in-one printer. The toner cartridge is nearly exhausted, Arthur! Every copy is coming out with black streaks across the trustee signatures! If they find a single material discrepancy in our extra-parochial returns, they will issue an immediate freezing order on our bank accounts and refer our incorporation file to the Crown Prosecution Service!"

"I see," Arthur said slowly. "And our marriage records?"

"That is the whole point, Arthur! That is the cornerstone of our corporate defence!" Finch was speaking so rapidly that his words collided like loose marbles in a bucket. "Under the Charities Act, an exempted religious body must prove that it maintains an active, public, non-commercial pastoral ministry. The Charity Commission suspects that Perpetual Grace is merely an online convenience company designed to generate tax-exempt processing fees! I told them that we have an extensive network of active rural presbyters conducting solemn ceremonies across the Welsh border. I told Mr. Garside that our Western March province alone conducts dozens of authentic pastoral unions every single quarter!"

Arthur swallowed. His mouth felt like dry flannel.

"Well," Arthur murmured, "that is true, Dr. Finch. In a manner of speaking. I have conducted ceremonies. Approximately four hundred of them."

"Thank heaven!" Finch gasped. "Where are the duplicate books, Arthur? Where are the green statutory counter-foils? Garside has demanded to inspect the Western March register by Friday at five o'clock. I told him our provincial archives were currently undergoing annual conservation in Leominster. I told him you were preparing the verified summary."

"Dr. Finch," Arthur said gently, his voice trembling slightly. "I do not have duplicate books."

Silence descended upon the telephone line. It was a profound, suffocating silence that lasted for six seconds. Through the earpiece, Arthur could hear the distant, rhythmic clack of a Brother photocopier running in another room.

"What do you mean," Finch said, his voice dropping into a terrifyingly calm register, "you do not have duplicate books?"

"You never sent me any, Dr. Finch," Arthur explained apologetically. "In twenty-twelve, when I telephoned after my sister's wedding, you told me to keep an exercise book from W.H. Smith. And then the garage roof leaked in twenty-seventeen, and the red dye ran through the pages. Most of the couples merely took their commemorative certificates home. I have orders of service in the car boot, and some notes on envelopes, but as for certified green statutory forms..."

"Arthur," Finch whispered, and for the first time his voice sounded entirely broken, like an old gramophone record running down. "Do you realize what you are saying? If we cannot produce a verified statutory schedule for the Western Marches, the Charity Commission will declare our denomination a sham corporation. They will revoke our Section 26 exemption. They will notify Her Majesty's Superintendent Registrar in Hereford. And Audrey Vance will descend upon your county like the Angel of the Lord in the Book of Exodus."

"I have already seen Mrs. Vance's public notice," Arthur admitted quietly. "It is pinned to the board outside the Shirehall."

"Then you know!" Finch hissed, his panic surging back with renewed fury. "You have seventy-two hours, Pendelton! Find the couples! Reconstruct the logs! Get their signatures! If you have to drive down every lane in Herefordshire with a bottle of ink and a clipboard, do it! If the Western March return is not on my desk by Friday afternoon, I will personally revoke your apostolic warrant and surrender your name to the Crown prosecutor!"

From the other end of the line came a sharp knock on a wooden door, followed by a polite, dry voice: "Dr. Finch? Miss Thrip has a question regarding a recurring software subscription to a greyhound genealogy database in Reno."

"Coming, Mr. Garside!" Finch called out in a synthetic, cheerful warble. Then he hissed into the mouthpiece: "Friday, Arthur! Five o'clock sharp!"

The line went dead with a loud click.

Arthur held the buzzing receiver against his ear for several seconds. Then he gently replaced the handset into its plastic cradle.

The hall clock struck the half-hour with a single brass chime.

Arthur reached into his pocket, took out his peppermint humbug, and discovered that in his terror he had crushed the sweet into four sticky fragments inside the cellophane.

Before Finch had abruptly severed the connection, Arthur had attempted to offer a mild word of comfort.

"Dr. Finch," Arthur had ventured, his voice carefully measured. "Would it assist if I posted you a packet of peppermint humbugs? For your throat. Stress often tightens the vocal cords, I find."

"Humbugs?" Finch had gasped, his voice cracking into a shrill shriek that made the earpiece vibrate. "Humbugs, Pendelton? I have two state investigators auditing nineteen-thousand pounds of online ecclesiastical credential receipts, and you offer me confectionery? Mr. Garside has just requested our bank reconciliations for the washroom expansion covenant in Swindon! We don't have a washroom covenant, Arthur! We have a portable chemical toilet in the yard behind the dry cleaner's!"

"Still," Arthur had said gently, "a sweet in the mouth can steady the spirit."

"Silence!" Finch had hissed. "Listen to me, Arthur. If Garside concludes this audit without verified Western March records, he will refer our charter to the High Court under Section 50 of the Charities Act. That means personal liability for every trustee. My semi-detached bungalow in Swindon will be seized by the official receiver! My lamination machines will be sold at public auction! Do you hear me, Arthur? Every single sheet of gold foil seal in my inventory!"

Inside the barn, the smell of fermenting perry pears was thick enough to coat the tongue with a sweet, sour film that tasted of autumn mud and crushed wood. Overhead, the tie beams were rough-hewn chestnut, seasoned by two hundred years of swallow nests and woodsmoke from the boiling vat. Leo Marsh wiped a pair of steel calipers against his denim thigh. He worked with the patient, rhythmic slowness of a man who measured his life by grain direction and timber shrinkage rather than quarterly tax quarters. When he tapped a white oak stave with his mallet, the sound rang deep and true throughout the dark eaves of the roof.

Arthur had nodded into the telephone receiver, picturing the terrified little man in Swindon surrounded by cardboard archive boxes and buzzing photocopiers.

# Chapter 9 — The Ticking Wall Clock

Arthur Pendelton stood in his hallway, staring at the square battery-operated wall clock mounted above the coat hooks. It was four-thirty-five on Tuesday afternoon.

Seventy-two hours.

That meant Friday at four-thirty-five. In practical terms, it meant Friday at five o'clock, when the council offices closed and the last recorded delivery postal van departed from the Leominster sorting office.

Arthur took a pencil and a scrap of paper from the telephone table and began to calculate the mathematics of impossibility.

Four hundred weddings. Twelve years. Seventy-two hours.

If he worked twenty-four hours a day without sleeping, eating, or making a single cup of tea, he would have approximately ten minutes and forty-eight seconds to allocate to each marriage. In that ten minutes, he would need to identify the couple, locate their current address, drive to their farm or cottage, explain that their twelve-year-old wedding was legally non-existent, persuade them not to assault him with garden tools, obtain four separate signatures on Form 8B, measure their living room with a tape measure to establish load-bearing wall boundaries, and locate an eight-figure Ordnance Survey coordinate for the exact spot where they had exchanged rings.

Arthur leaned his shoulder against the doorframe.

"Ten minutes," he whispered. "Ten minutes per marriage."

He walked back into the kitchen. On the wall beside the refrigerator hung his 2026 pictorial calendar, published by the Herefordshire Wildlife Trust and featuring a photograph of a dormouse eating an acorn for the month of November.

Arthur had written his personal engagements on the calendar in neat blue ink.

He looked at the square for Saturday, the fourteenth of November.

Written in the square was: *11:00 AM — Wedding: Toby and Megan. The Old Cider Mill, Kington. Outdoor bower by duck pond. Wear warm stole.*

He looked at the square for Sunday, the fifteenth of November.

*2:00 PM — Wedding: Callum and Sarah. Upper House Farm, Michaelchurch Escley. Sheep meadow beneath the Black Hill. Check wellington boots.*

*4:30 PM — Wedding: Oliver and Beth. Hay-on-Wye, orchard marquee beside river. Bride requests blessing for three rescue donkeys.*

Arthur stared at the dormouse. The dormouse stared back at him with shiny, unblinking black eyes, clutching its acorn as if completely indifferent to the Marriages Act 1949.

Three new weddings. This very weekend.

Three couples who had paid fifty-pound deposits, printed invitations on deckle-edged recycled paper, hired acoustic harpists, ordered hog roasts, and invited eighty relatives from as far afield as Swansea and Wolverhampton to stand in Herefordshire mud and watch Arthur Pendelton, in his gold-braided purple stole, pronounce them husband and wife before God and the Commonwealth.

If Arthur was defrocked by Dr. Finch on Friday afternoon, he would have no more authority to marry Toby and Megan by their duck pond than the duck pond itself. If he officiated without a licence, he would be committing an indictable offence under Section 75 of the Marriages Act, punishable by up to fourteen years' imprisonment in Her Majesty's prison at Gloucester.

And if he cancelled the weddings?

Arthur pictured Megan, a sweet-tempered primary school teacher from Presteigne who had spent seven months hand-pressing damson jam for her wedding favours. He pictured her standing in the rain outside the cider mill, holding an armful of white chrysanthemums, while eighty guests in damp suits looked on in bewilderment.

"No," Arthur said aloud. The word came out of him with surprising firmness. "I cannot cancel Megan."

He looked at his plastic watch. Four-forty.

The clock on the wall gave its dry, relentless click. Sixty seconds had elapsed while he had been looking at the calendar.

He had seventy-one hours and fifty-nine minutes remaining.

Arthur walked to the kitchen table, pulled out his chair, and sat down. He did not touch Form 8B. He reached for his pocket diary for the year 2016.

Arthur stood beside the kitchen window, looking down at the street lamp flickering to life in Bargates.

He thought of Callum and Sarah, the young couple waiting for him on Sunday afternoon beneath the Black Hill. Sarah had lost her mother to illness the previous winter; she had specifically asked Arthur if he would include a short prayer for mothers in heaven before the exchange of rings, because the local parish priest had informed her that the liturgy could not accommodate personal insertions into the authorized text.

Arthur had promised her that he would. He had spent three evenings writing the prayer on an index card with his fountain pen, choosing words that were simple, honest, and quiet.

Was that an indictable offence under Section 75 of the Marriages Act 1949? Was it a statutory crime to stand in a hillside sheep pasture and tell a grieving girl that her mother was watching from the clouds while the wind blew through the bracken?

Arthur pressed his knuckles against the window glass. The glass was ice-cold.

"No," he said softly. "The statute was made for man, not man for the statute."

The telephone on the hall table gave a short, hesitant rattle, the kind produced by old mechanical bells when line maintenance was underway in the valley. Arthur lifted the heavy black handset, listening to the crackle of distant conversations bleeding across the unshielded wires. Somewhere near Ledbury, a farmer was discussing the price of hay heifers with an auctioneer. The operator in Hereford cleared her throat with mechanical boredom. Rural telecommunications were built upon the principle that privacy was an unnecessary luxury for parishes situated more than six miles from a railway station.

He turned back toward the table. The wall clock clicked once, twice, three times, each movement measuring the swift, relentless passage of the seventy-two hours. He did not have time for despair. He had an orchard barn to find.

# Chapter 36 — The Confrontation in the Foyer

As Finch's footsteps died away down the corridor, a strange, resonant murmur began to echo through the Shirehall.

It was not the sound of municipal clerks or solicitors' assistants. It was the sound of dozens of heavy boots on stone stairs, the murmur of country voices, the crying of infants, and the distinct, cheerful jingle of horse brasses.

Audrey Vance frowned, adjusting her spectacles. "Miss Cleary: what is that disturbance in the foyer?"

Miss Cleary went to the private doors, opened them two inches, and peered down the corridor. She gasped, her shorthand notebook falling to the floor.

"Mrs. Vance," Miss Cleary whispered, looking back over her shoulder with wide, astonished eyes. "The foyer is full of people. There are dozens of them. They have filled the hall, they have spilled down the steps, and they are standing three deep in St. Peter's Square."

Audrey walked around her desk, followed by Arthur, Leo, and Clem. Together, they walked down the stone corridor to the main registry foyer.

When they stepped through the inner oak doors, Arthur stopped in his tracks, his breath catching in his throat.

The great neoclassical foyer of the Shirehall was packed from wall to wall with people.

There were elderly hill farmers in tweed jackets from Craswall; young blacksmiths in leather aprons from Peterchurch; dairy farmers with red cheeks from Dilwyn; schoolteachers from Weobley; and carpenters from Presteigne. Standing near the enquiry hatch was Timothy and Fiona Pugh, holding their six-year-old son by the hand; beside them stood the young couple from the Craswall cloudburst, holding a framed photograph of their oak tree ceremony; and near the public notice board stood young Robin and Holly Vance, holding a miniature replica of their Great Western railway brake van.

Every single one of them was holding a commemorative certificate printed on cream parchment with a gold foil seal.

Outside in the square, three vintage Ferguson tractors were parked by the stone portico, their exhaust pipes puffing white steam into the crisp November sunshine, while eighty people in country coats stood quietly beneath the fluted columns, holding bunches of winter chrysanthemums and flagons of sweet cider.

As Arthur appeared in the doorway, the murmuring ceased. A profound, reverent silence fell over the vast chamber.

A young farmer stepped forward from the crowd. It was Gethin Price—Arthur's brother-in-law, who had married Clara in the Pembridge clover meadow fourteen years ago. Beside him stood Clara, wearing the same bright green woollen cardigan she had worn in the hayfield, carrying a bundle of dried lavender in her hands.

"Gethin?" Arthur whispered, his voice trembling. "Clara?"

Gethin took off his flat cap, stepping up to the stone threshold of the private corridor. He looked past Arthur toward Audrey Vance, who stood motionless in her charcoal suit, her calipers held loosely in her right hand.

"Mrs. Vance," Gethin said, his broad Herefordshire voice ringing clear through the vaulted foyer. "We heard from the blacksmith that the county was auditing Arthur. We heard that the government was thinking of declaring our marriages null and void because we stood in fields and barns instead of paying forty pounds to sit beneath your council crest."

He held up his 2012 parchment certificate.

"Fourteen years ago, Arthur Pendelton stood in Prosser's meadow and married Clara and me. We didn't have an approved north wall, Mrs. Vance. We had a hawthorn hedge and a rusted cattle feeder. But Arthur made us swear before God and our family that we would stand together through sickness and poverty. We have built a home. We have raised three children. We have paid our county rates, we have mended our fences, and we have lived in honest peace."

Gethin turned to the crowd. Behind him, thirty men and women lifted their cream certificates into the air like a sea of white banners.

"If Arthur's ceremonies were illegal, Mrs. Vance," Gethin said, his voice echoing from the stone cornice, "then every farm between here and the Black Mountains is illegal. If you strike Arthur from your books, you'll have to strike four hundred families with him. And you'll have to explain to the people of this county why the law cares more about your ink than it does about our lives."

The crowd murmured in deep, unanimous approval. A chorus of voices rose from the foyer:

"He married my parents under the russet tree!"
"He stood in the snow during the foot-and-mouth blizzard!"
"He saved the perry press at Blackthorn!"

Audrey Vance stood on the stone step.

For sixty seconds, the Superintendent Registrar of Herefordshire did not move. She looked at Gethin Price; she looked at Clara's dried lavender; she looked at the young couple holding the picture of the oak tree; she looked at her own nephew Robin, who stood with his arm around his wife, looking back at his aunt with quiet, pleading affection.

Then Audrey Vance looked down at the brass calipers in her hand.

Slowly, deliberately, she slipped the calipers into the pocket of her tailored skirt.

She turned to Arthur. Her grey eyes were bright with an extraordinary, unmasked emotion that was very close to tears.

"Mr. Pendelton," Audrey said, her voice quiet but ringing through the silent hall. "Bring your red book back into my office. Miss Cleary: fetch the certified county seal and four bottles of archival black ink."

Audrey Vance stepped out onto the marble landing of the Shirehall foyer, looking down upon the gathered crowd of four hundred farmers, blacksmiths, carpenters, and wives.

The vast chamber was silent. Every face was turned toward the Superintendent Registrar.

Audrey cleared her throat. She did not raise her voice, but in the echoing acoustics of the neoclassical stone foyer, her words carried to the furthest corners of St. Peter's Square.

"Citizens of Herefordshire," Audrey announced. Her tone was formal, regal, and unimpeachable. "An administrative audit of independent rural solemnizations has been conducted under my statutory authority. Evidence hath been tendered by Mr. Arthur Pendelton demonstrating that the solemnizations conducted in this district over the past twelve years were celebrated in good faith, before competent witnesses, under the customary exemptions preserved by the Marriages Act nineteen-forty-nine."

A collective intake of breath swept through the crowd.

"Notice is hereby given," Audrey continued, her grey eyes scanning the sea of faces, "that every marriage certificate issued under Mr. Pendelton's hand shall be formally validated and entered into the supplemental county registers of this district before four-thirty this afternoon."

For two seconds, the silence held.

Then Gethin Price threw his flat cap twenty feet into the air.

A roar of jubilation erupted from the foyer, so loud that the crystal chandeliers suspended from the stone ceiling vibrated. Men embraced their wives; farmers slapped each other on the back; Clara Price wept tears of joy into her bundle of dried lavender, and outside in the square, three Ferguson tractors began sounding their horns in a rhythmic, deafening salute that rattled the windows of the cathedral library.

Timothy Pugh lifted his six-year-old son onto his shoulders, pointing toward Arthur. "Look at him, boy! That's the man who made us lawful!"

Arthur stood beside Audrey Vance on the stone landing. He looked down at the sea of joyful faces, feeling a warmth in his heart so vast and deep that he could not speak.

Audrey turned to him, her face radiant with a rare, magnificent smile.

"Come, Mr. Pendelton," she said softly. "The ink is waiting."

In the Shirehall foyer, the crowd began to organize themselves into an orderly, double-banked column along the stone walls, making way for Arthur, Audrey, Leo, and Clem to pass back toward the private offices.

As Arthur walked past, old friends and neighbours reached out to touch his sleeve or whisper words of gratitude:

"God bless you, Arthur!"
"We never doubted you, boy!"
"Tell Mrs. Vance she's welcome to a flagon of cider at the Red Lion anytime she visits the valley!"

Audrey Vance walked with her head held high, her expression calm, dignified, and regal. As they passed young Robin Vance, she paused for a brief fraction of a second, laid her gloved hand on her nephew's shoulder, and whispered: "Your railway brake van has been gazetted under Section seventy-nine, Robin. Mind you grease the wheel bogies."

Robin grinned broadly, kissing his aunt's cheek. "Thank you, Aunt Audrey."

Audrey cleared her throat, smoothed her lapels, and marched into her private office with the step of an empress returning to her throne room.

In the Shirehall foyer, old Timothy Pugh stepped forward from the front row of the crowd, holding a half-gallon stoneware flagon of vintage cider in both hands.

"Mrs. Vance," Pugh called out, his weathered farmer's face creased with emotion. "I brought this flagon of twenty-fifteen Foxwhelp perry. It was pressed in the autumn after Arthur married Fiona and me in the sheepfold. We kept it in the dark cellar for ten years, waiting for a day when the harvest was truly safe. I want you to take it, ma'am. Not as a bribe, but as a token from the people of the Golden Valley that we honour your duty and we honour Arthur's work."

Audrey Vance looked at the stone flagon.

She stepped down from the marble landing, walked across the parquet floor, and received the heavy stoneware jar into her own hands.

"I accept the gift on behalf of the county archives, Mr. Pugh," Audrey said, her voice rich with genuine emotion. "It shall be placed in the cellar beneath the Shirehall beside the royal jubilee flagons of nineteen-seventy-seven."

The cider press stood in the center of the outhouse, its massive oak screw black with age and soaked in ninety seasons of apple juice. Leo Marsh laid his heavy hand upon the timber beam, feeling the cold grain under his palms. Every mortise had been chopped by hand with a two-inch framing chisel, and every joint was secured with seasoned oak trunnels that had swollen tight under the damp orchard air. Hidden within the frame, protected from light, mice, and moisture by two inches of heartwood, the original counterfoil slip had rested undisturbed, preserved like an ancient seed in dried peat.

The crowd erupted into another round of deafening applause.

# Chapter 27 — Finch on Speakerphone

While Arthur was washing his face under the cold yard pump and wrapping his torn shins in clean linen strips, the emergency landline telephone mounted beside the workshop door began to ring.

It was an ancient bakelite telephone in dark green, connected to a wire that ran across the orchard to a junction box on the turnpike road. Its mechanical bell rang with a frantic, metallic urgency: *BRRR-ING. BRRR-ING.*

Clem picked up the heavy receiver, switched on the external speakerphone amplifier so Leo could hear from the press, and shouted into the mouthpiece: "Blackthorn Hollow! Who is it?"

A voice burst from the horn speaker. It was high-pitched, breathless, and trembling with hysterical rage.

"Arthur Pendelton! Put Pendelton on the line immediately! This is Overseer Dr. Alistair Finch speaking from the national administrative secretariat in Swindon!"

Clem raised an eyebrow at Arthur, who stood by the woodstove with a steaming mug of tea, shivering in an oversized woollen sweater Leo had lent him.

"Arthur is currently having his shins bandaged after saving forty bushels of vintage cider apples, Dr. Finch," Clem said into the receiver. "You are speaking to Clementine Marsh."

"I don't care if he is saving the Archbishop of Canterbury!" Finch shrieked through the speaker, the audio clipping with static. "Listen to me, Mrs. Marsh! Two inspectors from the Charity Commission are standing on my front doorstep with a police constable from the Wiltshire Constabulary! They have served an emergency closure order under Section forty-four of the Charities Act! Our bank accounts are frozen, our web server has been seized, and our ecclesiastical assets are being sequestered! If Pendelton does not fax or email the verified Western March logs within three hours, the prosecutor will issue an arrest warrant for corporate fraud!"

Clem leaned close to the microphone. Her expression was cold, dangerous, and completely unimpressed.

"Listen to me, you little Swindon pen-pusher," Clem said, her voice dropping into a low, menacing rasp that made the speaker cone hum. "You are calling an organic perry orchard in the Golden Valley. We don't have a fax machine, we don't have email, and we don't take orders from men who run churches out of an attic bedroom above a dry cleaner's."

"How dare you!" Finch gasped. "I am an ordained overseer—"

"You are a swindler who charged Arthur thirty-five pounds for a piece of laminated cardboard," Clem interrupted ruthlessly. "Arthur came out here ten years ago in the rain and married my husband and me with honesty and love. He didn't ask for money, and he didn't ask for certificates. He stood in the straw and gave us his blessing. And today he nearly broke his back saving our winter pressing while you were hiding behind your lamination machine."

From the Swindon end of the line came the muffled sound of a heavy fist pounding on a front door, followed by a voice shouting: "Dr. Finch! Open this door under Section forty-six of the Charities Act!"

"They're breaking the glass!" Finch wailed. "Arthur! For the love of God, Arthur, send the return!"

Clem reached out her calloused thumb, pressed the disconnect button down with deliberate force, and let the bakelite receiver drop back into its cradle.

The workshop fell dead quiet.

Clem looked at Arthur with a broad, ferocious grin.

"That," she said, "is how we handle Wiltshire."

After Clem had hung up the telephone, Arthur sat on his stool by the woodstove, sipping his hot tea and listening to the wind howling through the orchard.

"Do you think they will arrest him, Arthur?" Leo asked quietly, cleaning his spanner with a rag. "Dr. Finch, I mean."

Arthur gazed into the glowing coals of the woodstove.

"The Charity Commission is a very thorough institution, Leo," Arthur replied in his measured municipal tone. "When two inspectors arrive with brown archive boxes and a police constable, it usually indicates that the Crown Prosecution Service has already taken an interest in the company's annual returns. Dr. Finch's internet diocese was built on sand and lamination plastic. It was only a matter of time before the tide came in."

"And what happens to your licence, Arthur?" Clem asked, leaning against the workbench with her arms crossed. "If Finch goes to prison, does the Universal Church of Perpetual Grace dissolve?"

Arthur set down his tea mug. A calm, quiet light shone in his blue eyes.

"If the denomination dissolves, Clem, then the four hundred couples are no longer tied to an internet company in Swindon. They will belong entirely to the civil law of England. And if we can establish the Blackthorn Hollow precedent before Audrey Vance tomorrow afternoon, the county will have to gazette every single one of them as valid rural unions under the customary exemptions. Finch's destruction will be their liberation."

Clem looked at him for several seconds in silence. Then she smiled, a slow, radiant country smile.

"You really are a cunning old water board fox, aren't you, Arthur?"

"Not cunning, my dear," Arthur said with a modest smile. "Merely attentive to statutory schedules."

Clem hung the receiver on the hook and turned to Arthur with a satisfied grin.

"The man was a coward, Arthur," she said. "The moment someone stood up to him without an internet form, he folded like wet cardboard."

The rain had filled the stone horse trough beside the church gate to the very brim, spilling over the mossy rim in a thin, continuous sheet. Arthur watched the water trickle into the road drain. The trough had been placed there in nineteen eleven to commemorate the coronation of King George the Fifth, its inscription half-obliterated by seventy years of sulfurous coal smoke from the parish boiler. Everything in High Lea bore the quiet mark of gradual erosion, yielding to the elements with dignified, uncomplaining resignation.

"Most bureaucratic bullies do, my dear," Arthur said, taking a sip of his tea.

# Chapter 39 — The Registry Gazetting

Between half-past two and four o'clock that afternoon, the private office of the Superintendent Registrar resembled a scriptorium in a medieval abbey during the translation of the Vulgate.

Arthur sat on the left side of the mahogany desk; Audrey Vance sat on the right. Between them sat Miss Cleary, dipping blotting paper and numbering counter-foils with a mechanical rotary hand stamp.

Arthur would call out the entry from his pocket diaries and the orders of service:

"Entry sixty-one: Timothy Pugh and Fiona Powell. Sheepfold, Michaelchurch Escley, June twenty-fifteen. Permanent stone enclosure under Section twenty-two."

Audrey's ebony pen would scratch across the supplemental schedule: *Verified as customary upland agricultural sanctuary.*

"Entry one hundred and two: Robin Vance and Holly Evans. Railway brake van, Titley siding, July twenty-thirteen. Wheel bogies verified stationary under customary boundary tenure."

Audrey's pen did not hesitate for a second. She inscribed her own nephew's wedding lines with firm, beautiful strokes, adding a marginal notation: *Approved under Section seventy-nine.*

By four-fifteen, all four hundred ceremonies had been transcribed, categorized under the customary rural exemptions, and cross-referenced against the Master Register.

At four-twenty, the great oak dispatch pouch was brought into the room by the senior messenger. It was a heavy leather sack reinforced with steel bands and secured by a brass padlock bearing the royal cipher.

Audrey gathered the four hundred verified schedules, tied them with a length of red silk ribbon, and placed the bundle into the pouch.

She locked the padlock with a brass key.

"Miss Cleary: take this pouch to the secure postal van at the rear entrance," Audrey instructed. "It must be logged on the four-thirty dispatch to the Registrar General in Southport."

Miss Cleary took the heavy pouch in both arms and carried it out of the room.

The clock on the wall gave its slow, heavy tick: four-twenty-five.

Five minutes to spare.

Arthur sat back in his mahogany chair, his fingers aching, his eyes burning with exhaustion, but his chest light as an unladen balloon.

Audrey Vance laid her ebony pen down in the groove of her brass inkstand. She took off her reading spectacles, placed them in their leather case, and closed the snap.

She looked across the desk at Arthur.

"You are an extraordinary nuisance, Mr. Pendelton," she said in her crisp, measured cadence.

"I have been told that before, Mrs. Vance," Arthur replied with an apologetic smile. "Usually by the borough treasurer."

Audrey opened her top drawer and extracted a clean sheet of heavy council parchment bearing the county coat of arms.

"Under Section thirty-three of the Local Government Act nineteen-seventy-two," Audrey said, sliding the document before him, "the Superintendent Registrar is empowered to appoint deputy registrars to conduct civil solemnizations in approved rural and maritime locations within the district."

Arthur stared at the document.

The text was printed in crisp black type: *CERTIFICATE OF APPOINTMENT: ARTHUR REGINALD PENDELTON IS HEREBY DESIGNATED DEPUTY SUPERINTENDENT REGISTRAR FOR RURAL AND EXTRA-PAROCHIAL SOLEMNIZATIONS IN THE COUNTY OF HEREFORDSHIRE.*

"Mrs. Vance," Arthur stammered, his voice failing him. "I... I do not know what to say."

"You will say that you accept the appointment, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey stated firmly. "The county of Herefordshire will not have four hundred couples worrying about their titles every time a new registrar takes office. You understand the people of this valley. You understand their dogs, their tractors, and their weather. But from this day forward, you will carry an official council register bound in blue buckram, you will use iron gall ink, and you will submit your counter-foils on the first Monday of every quarter. Do you understand?"

Arthur looked at the appointment certificate. He looked at the royal crown stamped at the top.

"I understand, Mrs. Vance," Arthur said softly, his eyes shining with tears. "And I shall keep the margins strictly to three-quarters of an inch."

Audrey Vance's stern face broke into a broad, genuine, triumphant smile.

"See that you do, Arthur," she said. "Now let us go out and speak to your flock."

Arthur held the certificate of appointment in both hands, feeling the heavy, raised surface of the county seal beneath his thumbs.

*Deputy Superintendent Registrar for Rural and Extra-Parochial Solemnizations.*

It was a brand-new statutory office, created by Audrey Vance under the powers of the Local Government Act to bridge the centuries-old gap between municipal civil registration and the living traditions of the Welsh border countryside.

"Your duties begin tomorrow morning at nine o'clock, Mr. Pendelton," Audrey said, closing her desk ledger with a firm click. "You will be issued with an official county register folio, bound in blue buckram with pre-numbered counter-foils. You will be provided with an official travelling kit containing archival iron gall ink, certified blotting paper, and a portable brass date stamp calibrated to the standards of the General Register Office."

Arthur looked at her with affectionate wonder. "A portable date stamp, Mrs. Vance?"

"Calibrated down to the sixteenth of an inch," Audrey replied with pride. "You may conduct ceremonies in meadows, apple orchards, cider barns, and railway carriages, Mr. Pendelton. You may read Shakespeare, you may bless agricultural machinery, and you may allow three-legged dogs to carry the rings. But when you sign the counter-foil, you will use the county date stamp, and you will ensure that the margins remain strictly within the statutory lines."

"I shall guard those margins with my life, Mrs. Vance," Arthur promised solemnly.

"I know you will, Arthur," she said, her voice dropping into warm, genuine friendship. "And one more thing."

She reached into her drawer and pulled out a small brass object. It was a pair of brass calipers, identical to her own, engraved with the words: *A.R.P. — 2026*.

She placed them into Arthur's palm.

"For your pocket, Arthur," Audrey said with a gentle smile. "In case you encounter an irregular margin in a hayfield."

Arthur closed his fingers around the cool brass calipers. He felt tears prickle behind his spectacles.

"Thank you, Audrey," Arthur whispered.

At that moment, the five o'clock bell began to toll from the tower of Hereford Cathedral, its deep, golden chimes rolling across St. Peter's Square, proclaiming the end of the audit and the triumph of the four hundred.

Arthur stood by the window of Audrey Vance's office, looking out at the cathedral green as the five o'clock bells faded into the autumn twilight.

The postal van had departed; the four hundred schedules were speeding north toward the central registry, and the Shirehall was quiet once more. On the mahogany desk sat his new blue buckram register, fresh and immaculate, waiting for tomorrow's vows.

Audrey walked over to the coat stand, put on her tailored charcoal jacket, and picked up her leather handbag.

"A satisfactory conclusion to the quarter, Arthur," she remarked, fastening her gloves.

"A most satisfactory conclusion, Audrey," Arthur agreed, picking up his briefcase. "The people have their peace, and the crown has its records."

"And the county has a deputy registrar who knows how to walk in the mud," she added with a warm smile. "Good evening, Arthur."

"Good evening, Audrey," Arthur said, opening the door for her.

Arthur walked down the stone corridor of the Shirehall, carrying his new blue buckram register under his arm.

In the foyer, Miss Cleary was packing her shorthand notebooks into a desk drawer. She looked up, her face bright with a warm smile.

"Congratulations, Mr. Deputy Registrar," she said, offering her hand.

"Thank you, Miss Cleary," Arthur replied, shaking her hand with gentle warmth. "I shall endeavour to ensure that my quarterly returns arrive with exemplary punctuation."

"Mrs. Vance will accept nothing less, sir," she laughed.

Arthur walked out through the heavy entrance doors into the twilight of St. Peter's Square. The rain had ceased entirely; the sky was a deep indigo velvet, and the lamps of the old city were reflecting off the wet flagstones.

The Astra was waiting in Gaol Street, its sea-grey paint glowing softly under the street lamp.

Arthur unlocked the door, placed his blue register on the passenger seat, and settled behind the steering wheel. He felt a deep, abiding contentment that spread from his chest to the tips of his fingers.

The Diocesan Chancellor's court convened in the medieval chapterhouse, beneath a vaulted ceiling that soared sixty feet above the stone floor like the ribs of an overturned galleon. The Chancellor, a retired circuit judge with thick white eyebrows and an intimidating habit of polishing his horn-rimmed spectacles with silk handkerchiefs, sat behind an oak bench carved with Tudor roses. Two clerks in black gowns sat below him, their pens scratching against legal parchment with dry, insect-like persistence. Audrey Vance stood at the lectern, her statutory notices stacked in three pristine, color-coded piles before her.

He turned the key, and the engine purred like a contented cat.

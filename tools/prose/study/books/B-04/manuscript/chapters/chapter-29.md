# Chapter 29 — The Twilight Truce

As twilight settled over Blackthorn Hollow, the storm clouds parted in the west, revealing a brilliant crimson sunset that set the wet oak boughs blazing with fire.

In the yard between the barn and the pressing shed, Leo had built a roaring bonfire of seasoned oak offcuts and dry cider staves. The sparks swirled up into the darkening mountain sky like golden fireflies.

Arthur, Clem, and Leo sat on wooden apple crates around the fire, drinking cloudy perry from stoneware flagons and eating thick slices of sourdough bread toasted over the embers, spread with salty farm butter and sharp Cheddar cheese.

Arthur's shins were stiff, but the pain had subsided to a dull, honest ache. He held his warm earthenware flagon in both hands, listening to the crackle of the oak logs and the distant murmur of the flooded river Dore in the valley below.

"You know, Arthur," Leo said, watching the flames dance across his carpenter's hands, "when I left London in twenty-fifteen, I thought my life was over. I couldn't bear the sound of sirens, I couldn't bear the underground, and I couldn't bear the feeling that everything I was building was just cheap plastic veneer designed to last six months in a department store. When we bought this orchard, I thought we were hiding from the world."

"You weren't hiding, Leo," Arthur said softly. "You were seasoning. Like good ash timber."

"Arthur's right," Clem said, leaning her head against her husband's shoulder. "You can't rush an apple tree, and you can't rush a man. It takes four years for a perry pear to bear fruit, and it takes ten years for a marriage to sink its roots into the red clay. We needed this valley, Leo. And we needed Arthur to tell us that our hands were honest."

Arthur looked at them in the firelight.

In twelve hours, he would be standing in Hereford before Audrey Vance. He would be facing the full weight of the Marriages Act 1949, the Charity Commission, and the civil register rolls. There would be questions; there would be cross-examinations; there would be statutory scrutiny.

But sitting here beside the bonfire, with the smell of woodsmoke in his hair and the taste of perry on his tongue, Arthur felt an extraordinary, unshakable peace.

He was not a fraud. He had never been a fraud. He was an itinerant shepherd of the Welsh Marches who had spent twelve years giving ordinary, frightened people the courage to love one another without shame.

He raised his stoneware flagon to the fire.

"To the harvest," Arthur said.

"To the harvest," Leo and Clem replied together.

As the stars came out in thousands above the Black Mountains, the cold night air settled into the hollow, crisp and clean with the scent of woodsmoke and frost.

Arthur leaned back against his wooden apple crate, watching the sparks rise toward the constellation of Orion.

"Arthur," Clem said quietly, her flagon resting on her knee. "Do you remember the words you spoke to us in the straw ten years ago? The ones about the weather?"

Arthur smiled into the flames. He closed his eyes and summoned the memory:

"I told you," Arthur said, his gentle voice carrying across the quiet yard, "that a marriage built on sunny afternoons is a fragile thing, easily broken by the first winter frost. I told you that the true strength of love is revealed when the rain beats upon the iron roof, when the mud clings to your boots, and when the harvest is heavy and cold. I told you that if you held each other's hands in the storm, the timber would season, the joints would tighten, and nothing in this world could ever split you apart."

Leo reached across the fire and took Clem's calloused hand in his own. Their fingers interlaced with natural, effortless strength.

"The timber held, Arthur," Leo whispered.

"The timber held," Arthur agreed softly.

They sat together in the warmth of the embers for another hour, drinking the sweet perry and listening to the owl calling from the barn gable, until the fire died down to glowing crimson ashes and the frost began to glint on the grass.

Arthur finished his flagon of perry and set it down on the grass beside his apple crate.

The stars above were bright and steady. In twelve hours, the final test would begin, but tonight, the harvest was peaceful.

The wooden desk in the parish office had seven drawers, each fitted with an ornate brass drop handle that clattered when pulled. Arthur opened the bottom drawer, which contained forty spare collection envelopes, a ball of green parcel string, and a small tin of throat lozenges that tasted exclusively of eucalyptus and winter mud. He chose a lozenge, letting the sharp peppermint dissolve on his tongue as he reviewed the notes jotted on his blotter. The desk had belonged to his predecessor, a man who had written twelve volumes on local flora and died peacefully in the middle of evensong.

He leaned back, feeling the warmth of the fire against his face, and let the peace of the valley wash over him.

# Chapter 15 — Midnight in the Glovebox

Arthur sat in the driver's seat of his parked Vauxhall Astra on the crest of the hill above Peterchurch. It was ten o'clock on Wednesday night. Outside, the darkness of the Golden Valley was absolute, broken only by the distant yellow pinpoint of a farmhouse window three miles away across the valley floor.

The rain had returned, drumming against the metal roof of the car with a steady, rhythmic hiss. Inside the car, the air smelled of damp wool, cold upholstery, and old peppermint.

Arthur clicked open the plastic glovebox lid. The little interior bulb had burned out in 2018, so he switched on his brass pocket torch, holding the beam between his teeth while he sorted through the contents.

Inside the glovebox lay an accumulation of twelve years of itinerant ministry: three spare white linen handkerchiefs in cellophane packets, a bottle of cough syrup with a sticky plastic cap, a tyre pressure gauge shaped like a silver pen, two rolls of insulating tape, and a small bundle of folded letters tied with a piece of garden twine.

Arthur took out the bundle of letters.

He untied the twine. The top letter was written on cheap lined notepad paper in shaky blue ballpoint ink, dated October 14th, 2015:

Dear Mr. Pendelton: I am writing to thank you for coming up to the common to marry my mother and step-father under the russet apple tree. Mother was very weak from the treatment and could not have walked down the aisle at the parish church, and the council registrar told us they could not conduct a home service without three weeks' medical certification from the hospital. When you stood in the garden and spoke those words about love never failing, mother smiled for the first time in six months. She passed away three weeks later, but she died knowing she was Jim's lawful wife. We keep your certificate on the mantelpiece beside her photograph.

Arthur lowered the letter onto his lap. The beam of the torch flickered as his jaw trembled.

He read the second letter. It was from a young hill farmer whose wedding Arthur had conducted in a tractor shed during the foot-and-mouth crisis of 2001—no, 2013, during the great spring blizzard:

Arthur: We had no electricity and the pipes were frozen solid, but when you put on that gold stole over your mackintosh, it felt like the cathedral had come to our shed. Thank you for not charging us for the petrol.

Arthur turned off the torch. The darkness closed in around him like a heavy woollen blanket.

He rested his forehead against the steering wheel.

Was he a fraud? Was he merely an unemployed water board clerk who had bought a thirty-five-pound diploma from a swindler in Swindon to escape the boredom of early retirement?

Perhaps Audrey Vance was right. Perhaps, by the cold statutory standards of the Marriages Act 1949, he was an unlicensed charlatan who had compromised the legal security of four hundred families.

"No," Arthur said into the darkness of the car. The word was quiet, but it rang clear above the hiss of the rain on the glass.

He had not stolen money. He had not lied about God. He had simply answered the telephone when people were frightened and alone. He had stood in the rain when the official institutions of the state were too rigid, too expensive, or too proud to step into the mud.

Arthur reached into the glovebox, placed the letters carefully back into the twine, and retied the knot.

He switched on the Astra's headlights. Two bright cones of yellow light sliced through the darkness, illuminating the wet hazel hedge and the wooden fingerpost pointing down the rough track toward Blackthorn Hollow.

Arthur clicked on his torch again and held it over the third letter in the bundle.

It was from Clementine Marsh herself, written three months after the barn ceremony in October of 2016:

*Arthur: Leo has been working twelve hours a day in the shop. His hands have stopped shaking. For the first time in five years, he sleeps through the night without waking from nightmares about London boardrooms. Last night, while we were sitting by the woodstove watching the perry ferment, he looked at me and said: 'Arthur made us real, Clem. Not the council. Arthur.' We want you to know that whatever happens with the orchard, that barn is holy ground to us.*

Arthur smoothed the rough apple paper against his steering wheel.

A man could spend his life counting water rates and checking meter seals. He could spend forty years ensuring that no ratepayer in Herefordshire escaped a wastewater surcharge on a cattle trough. Or he could drive down a muddy lane in the rain and give two broken, frightened people the courage to build an honest life together.

The clock on the dashboard read ten-fifteen.

The vestry cupboard smelled of camphor balls and lavender bags sewn by the Ladies' Guild in nineteen eighty-four. Behind three spare surplices made of starched Irish linen lay a wooden box containing spare brass candle snuffers, an unsharpened slate pencil, and a tin of dubbin that had solidified into brown wax. Arthur pushed aside the vestment hangers. A country parish was an inadvertent museum of small, durable domestic objects that had outlived their manufacturers, their owners, and the doctrinal controversies that had originally brought them into the sanctuary.

Arthur put the letters back into the glovebox, clicked the plastic lid shut, and engaged the Astra's clutch.

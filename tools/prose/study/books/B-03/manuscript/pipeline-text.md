# Chapter 01: The Wrong Banner

The email from Apex Talent Management had arrived on Thursday afternoon with a high-priority red exclamation mark and the subject line CONFIRMED: MR TREMAYNE / PREMIERE KEYNOTE & RED CARPET / LINCOLNSHIRE GALA. 

Zoe Barnes had printed three copies on forty-pound heavy bond paper, slipped two into transparent plastic wallets, and folded the third into the zipped inner compartment of her leather tote bag alongside a blister pack of cherry-flavoured antacids, four black gel pens, a spare pair of black tights, and Julian's emergency silver pocket comb. She had worked for Julian Tremayne for five years and two months, which meant she had learned to measure reality not by what talent agents promised over speakerphone from Soho bistros, but by the physical heft of the receipts accumulating in her coat pockets.

The taxi dropped them beside a puddle the colour of cold gravy.

"Zoe, darling," Julian said, lingering inside the heated cabin of the silver Skoda Octavia while the windscreen wipers groaned through their final arc. "Tell me you see the step-and-repeat board. My neck is already catching the damp."

"I see a sign, Julian," Zoe said. She hauled his hard-shell Samsonite suitcase from the boot, her heels sinking an inch into rain-slicked limestone gravel. "Step out carefully. The surface appears to have been dressed with unwashed aggregate."

Julian emerged in three deliberate movements, like an archbishop stepping out of a state coach into an unruly province. He wore his bespoke charcoal wool three-piece suit, cut in nineteen ninety-eight by a tailor in Jermyn Street who had since passed into receivership, paired with a crimson silk pocket square folded into four crisp points and a pair of black patent leather Oxfords polished to a mirror finish. At fifty-four, his hair possessed the silver-streaked density of an expensive rug, brushed back over the ears to flatter what his theatrical publicity bio described as a noble, flinty jawline carved for television drama.

He stood on the gravel, adjusted his lapels, and looked toward the hotel entrance.

The George and Dragon Hotel was a two-storey coaching inn built from damp yellow brick, flanked on the left by an empty beer garden full of sodden picnic benches and on the right by a low red-brick extension roofed with corrugated metal. Suspended across the frontage, strung between two rusted cast-iron lamp posts by lengths of blue plastic baling twine, hung an eight-metre vinyl banner. The letters had been applied in reflective adhesive vinyl that gleamed stubbornly through the October drizzle:

ANNUAL DISTRICT PRODUCE & HEAVY ROOT VEGETABLE AWARDS, SPONSORED BY FENLAND TRACTORS & HYDRAULICS LTD.

Below the lettering, affixed with silver duct tape to the brickwork of the porch, a laminated sheet of card provided further detail: Trade Exhibits from 12:30. Official Opening by Celebrity Guest at 14:00 Prompt. Giant Marrow Weigh-In to Follow at 17:30. Please Wipe Boots Before Entering The Rutland Suite.

Julian did not look at the laminated card. He was staring at the vinyl banner with the mild, generous curiosity of an explorer encountering an interesting native tribal totem.

"A clever angle," he murmured, his deep theatrical baritone rolling across the wet gravel and startling two wood pigeons on the guttering. "Grounded. Gritty. The independent British film industry returning to the agricultural soil. One assumes this is a location-specific promotional tie-in arranged by the distributor. A rural thriller, perhaps? Something with quad bikes and dark secrets in the drainage ditches?"

"Julian," Zoe said gently, pressing her fingertips against the bridge of her nose.

"You recall my work on Whispers on the Marsh," he continued, taking her elbow with stately condescension. "Nineteen ninety-eight. Seven million viewers on ITV every Tuesday evening. Dalrymple standing by the sluice gate in a wax jacket, unravelling the mystery of the drowned solicitor. The country remembers. The common man responds to authenticity, Zoe. They sense when an actor has breathed the Fenland fog."

"Julian, look at the bottom line of the vinyl," Zoe said.

"Sponsorship," Julian said smoothly, waving a manicured hand. "Commercial pragmatism. We cannot all have Criterion backing. If Fenland Tractors has provided the catering budget, we must honour their civic enterprise. Now, where is the liaison officer? I should like a small room with an upright mirror, twenty minutes of vocal silence, and a carafe of ambient mineral water. Tuscan, if available."

Zoe retrieved her phone from her trench coat. The battery sat at forty-one percent, dropping by one percent every seven minutes under the damp chill of the Lincolnshire sky. She opened the calendar event, swiped past the train itinerary, and tapped into the original booking email forwarded from Marcus at Apex Talent. 

She zoomed in on the attached contract.

The top of the document bore the header DEEPING AND DISTRICT HORTICULTURAL SOCIETY, AUTUMN GALA. Marcus, who had been twenty-four years old and permanently distracted by personal fitness trackers when he drafted the summary, had glanced at the words Opening Act, Keynote Address, and Lincolnshire Gala, and had filed the booking into the agency system under Curzon Mayfair, Regional Premiere Outreach.

Zoe read the third paragraph twice. Her throat tightened with the familiar, metallic taste of an impending administrative disaster.

The Society agrees to pay the sum of two thousand five hundred pounds sterling to Julian Tremayne, hereafter The Celebrity, for the formal opening of the Autumn Root Exhibition, the cutting of the ceremonial raffia ribbon at the main doors of the Rutland Suite, and the presentation of the Market Deeping Produce Society Silver Challenge Cup to the winner of Class 4, Marrows and Heavy Squashes.

The contract had been signed three weeks ago by Marcus on Julian's behalf. Below Julian's signature sat the counter-signature of the Society Honorary Secretary: Mrs F. Sterling, JP.

Zoe froze.

"Julian," she said, her voice dropping into the flat, dangerously even register she reserved for cancelled flights and lost passports. "What was Fiona's maiden name?"

"Fiona?" Julian frowned, brushing an invisible speck of soot from his sleeve. "My former wife Fiona? Fiona Briggs, until the decree absolute in 2011. Before that, Sterling. Her family grew sugar beet near Bourne. A frightful, domineering clan with muddy thumbs and no ear for verse. Why on earth do you ask?"

"Because," Zoe said, tilting the phone screen beneath the shelter of her umbrella, "she appears to be running the heavy root vegetables."

Julian blinked. A bead of rain struck the bridge of his nose and trickled toward his upper lip. For four seconds, the silence in the car park was broken only by the thrum of a diesel generator running behind the tractor shed.

"Nonsense," Julian said, though the projection in his voice dropped half an octave into defensive bluster. "Fiona lives in Edinburgh. Or Cheltenham. Some civilized enclave where women arrange dried flowers and attend chamber recitals. She would not be in a car park in Market Deeping."

"Her name is on the contract, Julian. And that green estate car parked beside the fire escape has an Ipswich registration and a bumper sticker that says Keep Calm and Hoe On."

Julian turned his head toward the green Volvo estate. His jaw slackened. The patrician tilt of his chin wavered, just enough for his collar to dig into his neck.

"My dear," he whispered, clutching the handle of his suitcase with sudden, white-knuckled intensity. "Turn the taxi around."

"The taxi left thirty seconds ago," Zoe said. "He had a fare in Peterborough at two o'clock."

"Call another."

"The signal here is one bar of edge network. The app is searching for drivers in Nottingham."

"Then we shall walk," Julian declared, stepping backward toward the road. His left shoe landed squarely in a six-inch rut of liquid Fenland mud with a wet, squelching gasp. The pristine black leather vanished beneath a thick cuff of brown slime.

"Julian," Zoe said, stepping in front of him and catching his lapel before he could overbalance into the nettles. "Listen to me very carefully. If you leave this property before five o'clock, the agency agreement triggers an automatic clawback clause. Marcus took a five-hundred-pound commission advance, and the remaining two thousand pounds was transferred directly to your landlord on Wednesday morning to stop the repossession proceedings on the flat in Maida Vale."

Julian stared at his submerged foot. His chest rose and fell in shallow, theatrical heaves.

"The landlord?" he whispered.

"Mr Gidley," Zoe said, keeping her grip steady on his wool coat. "He has the bailiff on speed dial. If the society revokes the fee, the cheque bounces, and your furniture is on the pavement in Elgin Avenue by Monday noon. Do you understand what that means?"

Julian's lips twitched. He pulled his shoe from the mud with a sound like a wet towel dropping onto bathroom tile. A dark, viscous ring of Lincolnshire clay coated the welt, the laces, and the silk cuff of his trousers.

"The public," he murmured, his voice trembling on the edge of tragedy. "The public must never see Dalrymple in the mire."

"The public is inside eating sausage rolls," Zoe said, handing him a packet of pocket tissues from her bag. "Wipe the toe. Hold your shoulders square. We are going inside, we are cutting the raffia ribbon, and we are going to survive the marrows."

The hotel entrance porch offered little shelter from the October wind that blew unchecked across forty miles of flat black fen. 

Zoe adjusted the strap of Julian's Samsonite suitcase, her knuckles aching in the cold. She took three slow breaths, feeling the damp mist clinging to the wool of her collar. In five years of assisting Julian, she had managed a broken air conditioning unit at an open-air theatre in Chichester, an impromptu press conference in an Aberdeen car park following an alleged altercation with a bagpiper, and an eight-hour delay in a fog-bound departure lounge at Frankfurt airport while Julian recited Macbeth to a charter flight of German dental technicians. 

None of those crises, however, had involved sixty-pound vegetables or an ex-wife wielding a statutory clipboard.

Julian was still inspecting his mud-caked left Oxford, turning his foot from side to side with mournful fascination. "It has penetrated the eyelets, Zoe. Look at the stitching. John Lobb specifically warned me against peaty subsoils. When I played Lord Goring in ninety-four, the wardrobe mistress kept two apprentices on standby with tins of Meltonian cream solely to preserve the lustre of the patent leather. Do you suppose the landlord keeps a shoe brush behind the reception desk?"

"The reception desk is currently occupied by a jar of pickled eggs and a display of car air fresheners shaped like pine trees," Zoe said, steering him firmly toward the glass porch doors. "Keep moving, Julian. If you stand on this gravel any longer, the silt will set like Roman mortar."

She pushed open the inner door, holding it wide with her hip so that Julian's overcoat would not brush against the dripping timber frame. Behind them, the vinyl banner flapped in the wind, its blue baling twine straining against the cast-iron lamp posts.

"Julian," Zoe said, stopping on the third gravel step.

"Yes, my dear?"

"Your overcoat has four spare horn buttons inside the interior lining."

Julian patted his chest through the heavy charcoal cashmere. "Naturally. Anderson and Sheppard insist upon complete sartorial contingency. An actor without spare horn buttons is at the mercy of the prevailing wind."

"If you attempt to flee back to Peterborough," Zoe continued, her breath misting into the sharp fenland air, "I will cut every single one of those buttons off and feed them to the ducks on the River Welland."

Julian blinked down at her with dignified injury. "A monstrous imputation, Miss Barnes. Dalrymple of the Fens never fled. He advanced with measured deliberation toward the truth."

"Dalrymple was paid twelve thousand pounds an episode by Central Television," said Zoe. "You are being paid by a committee of retired greengrocers. Adjust your expectations accordingly."

Julian drew his silk scarf more securely about his throat. "Greengrocers or dukes, Zoe, an audience is an audience. When the curtain rises, the artist owes them nothing less than absolute devotion."

He looked toward the church spire that dominated the flat horizon like a stone needle against the leaden sky. For nearly four decades, Market Deeping had remained a distant provincial dot on the weather maps of the regional news broadcasts, a place associated exclusively with watercress beds, flooded washlands, and heavy tractor collisions on the A15. Now, for forty-eight hours, it was the focal point of the European vegetable world.

"Zoe."

"Yes, Julian?"

"The taxi driver from Grantham."

"What about him?"

"He had a small wooden dog on his dashboard whose head vibrated in direct sympathy with the gearbox. Did you observe the expression of mournful resignation in the creature's painted eyes? It reminded me forcefully of poor Ralph Richardson during the dress rehearsal of The Father in seventy-five. Whenever the revolve stuck, Ralph would stand in the wings with precisely that look of mild, bovine surprise, waiting for the stage manager to kick the hydraulic pump."

Zoe hauled the Samsonite case onto the dry concrete lip of the porch.

"The taxi driver charged thirty-two pounds fifty for eleven miles of unlit fen road," she said, her voice flat with fatigue. "And he refused to take a cheque from Marcus's company account."

"A prudent provincial tradesman," Julian remarked, adjusting his woollen scarf. "One must never quarrel with local financial customs. In eighty-two, when we played the Theatre Royal in Norwich, the landlady of my boarding house demanded payment in postal orders every Thursday evening before she would light the gas ring in the scullery."

"Julian."

"My dear?"

"Go inside."

The hotel's inner entrance hall was paneled in dark, varnished deal that had absorbed eighty years of pipe smoke, wet mackintoshes, and floor wax.

On the wall beside the hat stand hung a framed print depicting the drainage of the South Level in eighteen forty-eight, showing a company of Victorian navvies with clay pipes and shovels standing chest-deep in black mud around an enormous steam pump.

Julian paused before the engraving, his fingers resting upon the brim of his fedora.

"Look at the navvies, Zoe," he said, his baritone softening with genuine reverence. "The indomitable resolve of the English working man. Notice the tilt of the gaffer's beaver hat. He knew that the water was rising, he knew that the boiler might burst at any moment, yet he stood his ground with stoic equanimity."

"The gaffer was being paid sixpence a day by the drainage commissioners, Julian," Zoe pointed out, setting down her briefcase. "And he probably died of marsh ague before he reached forty."

Julian turned to her with a look of pained dignity.

"An ague perhaps, my dear, but an honourable ague. In nineteen eighty-four, when we filmed the second season finale in the washlands near Crowland, the director made me stand in four feet of tidal water for three hours while we waited for the sun to emerge from behind a cloud. Central Television provided a flask of hot oxo and a dry pair of trousers. One does not complain when history is being recorded."

"Marcus complained when the dry-cleaning bill arrived," Zoe noted.

Julian gave a faint, elegant shrug. "Marcus lacks historical perspective. The business mind is inherently temporal; the artist belongs to the ages."

"Julian," Zoe said, peering into the hotel lounge through the frosted glass.

"Yes, my dear?"

"There is a stuffed pike in a glass case above the dartboard."

Julian stepped up beside her, his face illuminating with antiquarian pleasure. "A magnificent specimen of Esox lucius. Caught in the Welland in nineteen thirty-two, if the brass plaque is to be credited. In seventy-six, when we were touring with Priestley's Dangerous Corner, the digs in Grimsby featured a preserved otter in the upstairs bathroom. It sat upon the cistern with glass eyes of remarkable ferocity. Whenever Sir Donald flushed the chain, the vibration caused the otter's left paw to drop three inches, creating the distinct impression that the animal was reaching for one's loofah."

"Did you complain to the landlady?"

"Complain? Donald was enchanted. He insisted upon reciting the prologue to Henry the Fifth to the beast every morning while shaving. He maintained that the otter was the only member of the household who truly grasped his phrasing."

Zoe shook her head, a reluctant smile breaking through her exhaustion. "You are incorrigible, Julian."

"The theatre is an infectious vocation, Miss Barnes," he replied with a courtly inclination. "One finds drama in every corner of the realm, even between a stuffed fish and a dartboard."
# Chapter 02: Patent Leather in the Slush

The porch of the George and Dragon smelled of wet wool, damp carpet underlay, and stale malt vinegar from the adjacent public bar.

Julian Tremayne stood upon the coir matting just inside the double glass doors, scraping the edge of his left Oxford against an iron boot scraper shaped like a dachshund. Each scrape produced a wet, hollow rasp that caused the reception bell on the counter to tremble.

"Look at this welt, Zoe," he said, holding his ankle aloft like a flamingo testing the temperature of a swamp. "Hand-stitched calfskin. Two hundred and eighty guineas at John Lobb. And now it has soaked up half an acre of peaty loam. Do you suppose the establishment keeps a valet? Even a competent scullery boy with a tin of neutral polish?"

"The establishment has a laminated sign behind the bar offering toasties between twelve and two," Zoe said. She set the Samsonite down beside an umbrella stand filled with broken walking sticks. "The scullery boy was abolished in nineteen forty-seven, Julian."

She unzipped the side pocket of her coat, extracted a foil packet of wet wipes, and crouched beside his foot. The mud had set into the grain of the leather, creating a dull gray crust along the welt. She worked briskly with three wipes, folding the soiled paper into a fourth and slipping the bundle into a plastic sandwich bag from her handbag. Her spine gave a small, familiar click. She was thirty years old, held an upper second-class degree in English literature from Manchester, and had spent the previous forty minutes wiping fen silt from an unemployed actor's footwear.

"You are an angel of mercy," Julian sighed, resting a heavy hand upon her shoulder. "A faithful Cordelia in this bleak, unscripted wilderness. When we return to town, I shall speak to Marcus about your quarterly bonus."

"Marcus has not paid your August expense claims, Julian. My quarterly bonus is currently an abstract philosophical concept."

"Administrative friction," he murmured, his gaze drifting toward the glass case mounted beside the dining room doors. "The agency operates on a grander horizon. They are negotiating the Scandinavian rights for the back catalogue. Did I mention the Finns have developed an extraordinary appetite for Whispers on the Marsh? A cult following in Tampere. They appreciate the stoicism of Dalrymple's silences."

Zoe stood, wiped her fingers on a dry paper napkin, and pulled up the booking confirmation on her phone. She pinched the screen, expanding the image until the tiny footnote at the bottom of the fourth page occupied the display.

"Here it is," she said.

Julian leaned down, squinting with theatrical suspicion. "What am I looking at?"

"The fee schedule. Clause 12b. Set in eight-point Univers Light." She held the glass two inches from his nose. "Read the booking title."

Julian cleared his throat, adjusting the imaginary pince-nez he always assumed when forced to confront financial documentation. "'Fenland Produce Galas and Allied Rural Assemblages.' Good heavens. What a preposterous collection of nouns. Why would Marcus agree to such phrasing?"

"Because Marcus did not read past the words Lincolnshire Gala, Julian. He assumed it was the regional launch for the British Film Institute's rural heritage season. The promoter offered two thousand five hundred pounds cash on the nail, fifty percent in advance, payable to client account. And Marcus had your overdue service charge notice sitting on his blotter."

Julian's baritone softened, losing its brassy public sheen. For a brief moment, the vanity dropped from his eyes, leaving behind the tired, sagging grayness of a middle-aged man who had not auditioned for a BBC drama in three years.

"He paid Gidley?" Julian asked quietly.

"He paid Gidley two thousand pounds directly by bank transfer on Wednesday morning," Zoe replied. "The remaining five hundred was retained by Apex Talent as agency commission. If you do not perform the contracted duties today, the Society files a dispute with the bank, the funds are recalled, and the court bailiff visits Elgin Avenue on Tuesday."

Julian looked down at his clean shoe. The leather was damp, but the black polish had regained a dull, sullen gleam.

"Fiona was always merciless with accounts," he muttered, turning his face away toward the frosted glass of the Rutland Suite doors. "When we were at drama school, she kept a five-column cashbook for milk and digestive biscuits. If I bought an evening paper out of the housekeeping jar, she recorded it under frivolous entertainment. She had the soul of an inland revenue inspector even then."

"Well," Zoe said, picking up the suitcase handle, "she is now the magistrate for South Kesteven, and she is holding the microphone. We have thirty minutes to locate the dressing room, pin your boutonnière, and review the pronunciation of the local parish councils."

Julian took two paces across the coir matting toward the inner lobby, pausing before a cracked brass-framed mirror hung above a radiator that clicked like a cooling engine. He inspected his reflection with clinical solemnity. He checked the symmetry of his silk tie, pinched his earlobes to restore a hint of color to his cheeks, and patted the crown of his hair with both palms.

"Tell me truthfully, Zoe," he said, turning his profile to catch the dim light from the porch lamp. "Do I appear diminished? A man of my standing, arriving without an entourage or an executive saloon?"

"You appear like a distinguished visitor from the capital who travels with an understated literary elegance," Zoe said without missing a beat, reciting the third formulaic response from her mental emergency index. "And if anyone inquires about the Skoda, we shall explain that the town car suffered a punctured tyre outside Grantham and you insisted on taking a local minicab rather than keep the committee waiting."

A flicker of genuine relief softened the tight lines around his mouth. "Magnificent. Quick thinking, my dear. The hallmark of a premier theatrical administrator. Perhaps we might also imply that the script for the new autumn detective series was left on the front seat of the town car, thereby explaining why I have no notes for my address?"

"Let us not overcomplicate the fiction, Julian," she said, steering him gently by the elbow toward the double doors of the function room. "The growers do not require a lecture on television drama. They want someone who sounds authoritative, wears a three-piece suit, and does not drop the heavy squash while the photographer from the local advertiser snaps the flashbulb."

Julian drew himself up to his full six feet and two inches, threw his shoulders back until his shoulder blades met, and rested his fingers lightly on his waistcoat pocket.

"Very well," he proclaimed, his chin rising to the heroic angle of a captain taking the bridge of a torpedoed destroyer. "Lead on, Miss Barnes. Let us inspect the root vegetables."

Inside the inner lobby, the smell of warm beer and carpet shampoo was overlaid by the distinct, peppery odour of hot floor polish.

A glass display cabinet stood against the wall opposite the reception bell, containing an assortment of yellowed provincial trophies: a silver-plated bowl awarded in nineteen seventy-eight for best pen of commercial gimmers, a copper plaque commemorating a darts tournament won by the George and Dragon darts team in nineteen eighty-four, and a framed, water-damaged certificate issued by the South Kesteven Rural District Council declaring the hotel cellar free of statutory vermin.

Julian paused before the cabinet, adjusting his cuffs with grave dignity. "You see, Zoe? The historical lineage of the house. Provincial, yes, but steeped in the authentic communal rituals of the realm. An actor must never look down upon the institutions of the soil. When Sir John spoke to the apprentices at Stratford, he always maintained that the English countryside was the bedrock of our national dramatic tradition."

"Sir John had an estate in Sussex and six gardeners," Zoe noted, setting down the Samsonite beside an electric radiator that rattled like a tin box full of loose washers. "And he did not have to pay two thousand pounds to his landlord by Tuesday morning."

Julian sighed, the theatrical brightness fading from his posture. He looked at his reflection in the glass cabinet door, tucking a stray strand of silver hair behind his ear. "Gidley is a barbarian, Zoe. An absolute Philistine. He threatened to seize my oil portrait of Dame Edith Evans. The one painted by the nephew of the Bishop of Ely in nineteen ninety-one. He claimed it had no commercial value beyond the gilt frame."

"Mr Gidley wants his rent, Julian. And if you cut this ribbon and smile at the farmers, Mr Gidley receives his money and your portrait stays in Maida Vale."

Julian drew himself up to his full height, smoothed his waistcoat over his diaphragm, and nodded once with slow, tragic resignation. "Very well, Miss Barnes. Let us confront the committee. But remember: if there are press photographers present, my left profile remains the approved angle."

Zoe checked the receipt clip in her brown leather folder.

"Julian," she said.

"Still here, my dear. Contemplating the municipal silver."

"The society has reserved two adjoining twin rooms in the annex."

"Twin beds?" Julian turned from the display cabinet with a look of theatrical consternation. "In ninety-six, when we were touring The Master Builder, the Theatre Royal in Bath provided a four-poster Chippendale suite with a separate drawing room and an unpasteurised cheese board."

"This is not Bath, Julian. This is a coaching inn behind a beet depot."

"Is there at least a writing desk?"

"There is a laminate dressing table with an electric kettle and two complimentary sachets of decaffeinated instant coffee," Zoe said, tapping the plastic registration card against the desk bell. "And if you break the plastic handle on the kettle like you did in Harrogate, the replacement cost is fifteen pounds fifty, which will be deducted directly from your travel allowance."

Julian sighed, a sound like autumn wind passing through dry reed beds. "You have become terribly commercial, Zoe. When you first came to my rooms in Elgin Avenue, you carried an anthology of Jacobean revenge tragedies and spoke with genuine passion about Webster's use of meter."

"When I first came to Elgin Avenue," Zoe remarked, looking him squarely in the eye, "I believed that professional actors paid their Council Tax on time."

"A bourgeois obsession," muttered Julian, though he averted his gaze toward the umbrella stand.

"Julian," Zoe said, tapping her finger against the hotel registration register.

"Present, my dear."

"The room key has an oval brass fob weighing half a pound."

"A traditional safeguard against accidental theft," Julian observed, nodding with grave approval. "In the old days at the Midland Hotel in Manchester, the fobs were shaped like miniature railway buffers. If an actor attempted to slip one into his overcoat pocket, the garment sagged visibly to the left, alerting the hall porter before he could reach the revolving doors."

The reception bell gave a sharp, metallic ping as Zoe pressed it for the third time.

From behind the frosted glass door marked PRIVATE, a slow, shuffling step approached across the linoleum.

"Look at this corridor, Zoe," Julian whispered, peering down the passage that led to the dining room. "Smell the cabbage and the floor wax. It has the authentic atmosphere of an Edwardian provincial boarding school on the eve of the Michaelmas term. One expects an elderly mathematics master in a chalk-dusted gown to emerge from the still-room carrying a plate of seed cake."

"If an elderly mathematics master emerges from that room, Julian," Zoe said, "I will ask him to calculate our agency deficit."

"An uncharitable thought," Julian sighed.
# Chapter 03: The Green Baize Tables

The double fire doors of the Rutland Suite yielded with the groan of an ungreased brass hinge, releasing a blast of warm, humid air that smelled powerfully of damp moss, radiator rust, and turnip greens.

Zoe Barnes crossed the threshold first, keeping her shoulder firmly wedged against the door so that Julian would not catch his coat on the brass panic bar. She stepped into a vast, low-ceilinged hall that in the mid-nineteen-seventies had evidently been decorated for regional sales conferences. The wallpaper displayed a repeating motif of mustard-coloured heraldic shields, peeled slightly at the skirting board and mottled with damp near the corner fire exit. Above them, four rows of industrial fluorescent strip lights hummed in a harsh, unsynchronized B-flat, casting a flat institutional glare across six long rows of trestle tables.

Each trestle table was draped in heavy green baize, secured at the corners with rusty metal bull-dog clips. And upon the baize rested the produce.

There were eighty or ninety people in the room, almost all of them wearing heavy woollen knitwear, waxed coats with fraying cuffs, or bib overalls tucked into mud-caked wellingtons. A low, continuous rumble of conversation filled the air, punctuated by the clinking of metal measuring tapes, the scrape of wooden crates being shoved across parquet flooring, and the occasional dry hiss of an aerosol mister. 

Nobody looked up. Nobody glanced toward the door. Nobody registered the arrival of a leading television actor who had once held seven million viewers in suspense over the fate of a Norfolk coroner.

"Remarkable," Julian said, his baritone booming into the immediate vicinity of a display of winter leeks. "A rustic tableau worthy of Thomas Hardy. Note the quiet concentration of the peasantry, Zoe. The deep, ancestral devotion to the fruits of the soil. One feels the weight of four centuries of tillage."

A small, wiry man in a brown flat cap and a padded fleece vest turned around. He held a brass caliper in one hand and a spray bottle of diluted cloudy ammonia in the other. He squinted at Julian's crimson silk pocket square with the sharp, suspicious appraisal of a customs officer examining an uninspected parcel of foreign meat.

"Mind your sleeve, mate," the man said in a flat Lincolnshire drawl. "You are leaning over Mr Gedge's prize celery. Trench celery does not like aftershave. It takes the scent right through the stems, and then the judges mark him down for soapy heart."

Julian took half a step backward, smoothing his lapel with dignified haste. "My apologies, sir. An unintentional intrusion upon the celery. I am Julian Tremayne."

He paused, tilting his head slightly to the left and offering the gentle, benevolent smile that had concluded forty-six episodes of *Whispers on the Marsh*.

The man in the flat cap stared at him for three seconds. "Right you are," he said. He turned his back, sprayed a mist of water over a bundle of pale celery stalks wrapped in brown paper, and shouted across the hall: "Artie! Have you got the three-eighths spanner for the scale counterweight? Frank says the south arm is sticking again!"

From the far corner of the room, a gravelly voice roared back above the radiator clatter: "Frank can wait until I have checked the girth on the Emperor! If anybody touches the cradle before the official steward arrives, I am calling the police!"

Zoe glanced at Julian. His smile had frozen into an expression of polite, glassy bewilderment. He remained poised on the parquet floor, one hand resting on the green baize, waiting for the delayed spark of recognition that had accompanied his arrivals at London theatrical book launches for twenty-five years.

"They are engrossed in the competition, Julian," Zoe said, steering him gently toward the central aisle. "The tension is evidently running very high. Agricultural rivalries are famously intense."

"Of course," Julian said quickly, clearing his throat and recovering his posture. "Professional focus. I respect it immensely. The true craftsman has no time for frivolous celebrity distractions. When I worked with Sir John at the National in eighty-nine, he was precisely the same before the curtain. Utterly monastic. One could not speak to him of anything lighter than *Coriolanus*."

They moved down the central aisle between the tables. As they advanced, Zoe began to realize the sheer scale of the exhibits. 

These were not ordinary garden vegetables. These were swollen monsters of horticulture, bred through secret lineages of seeds and fed with chemical cocktails to achieve terrifying biological mass. In Class 2, Heaviest Root, parsnips the thickness of rugby players' thighs lay arranged on beds of dry wood shavings, their tapered roots extending six feet along the baize. On Table 4, a collection of cabbage heads resembled green cannonballs, each accompanied by a handwritten index card listing its seed origin, sowing date, and final certified weight in pounds and ounces.

And then they reached Table 6, where Class 4 was assembled.

Here lay the giant marrows.

There were twelve of them, resting upon padded cotton cushions, old woollen blankets, and foam cradles to prevent their soft underbellies from bruising against the timber trestles.

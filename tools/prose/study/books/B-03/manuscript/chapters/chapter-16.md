# Chapter 16: The Bribery Attempt

The narrow passage leading from the public bar to the resident lounge was dimly lit by a pair of wall sconces shaped like brass coaching horns, one of which lacked an electric bulb.

Zoe had paused there to consult the battery level on her phone when a man stepped out from behind the payphone kiosk.

He was roughly sixty years old, wearing a mustard-coloured corduroy jacket over a green knitted polo shirt, with thick spectacles held together at the bridge by a strip of clear adhesive tape. In his arms, pressed against his midriff like a sleeping toddler, he carried an elongated, pale orange vegetable that resembled an over-inflated rugby ball with warty skin.

"Miss Barnes?" he whispered, his eyes darting toward the open archway of the saloon bar.

Zoe took a step backward, keeping her handbag pressed against her hip. "Yes?"

"Trevor," the man said, offering a rapid, nervous nod. "Trevor Gedge. Class Three, Heritage Winter Gourds and Atlantic Giants. I grow on the south allotments down by the water tower."

"Good afternoon, Mr Gedge," Zoe said in her best administrative tone. "If this is regarding the judging running order, Mrs Sterling is managing the schedule from the dais."

"That is the trouble," Gedge hissed, stepping closer until Zoe could smell the peppery aroma of salt-and-vinegar crisps on his sweater. "Fiona has put Heritage Gourds down for twenty past five. That is twenty minutes after the sun drops behind the gasometer. My Atlantic Giant needs ambient thermal exposure right up to the scale cradle. If the temperature falls below twelve degrees, the skin tightens up and the specific gravity reading drops by two percent."

Zoe looked at the warty orange gourd. "Mr Gedge, the Rutland Suite has central heating radiators."

"The radiators don't work down by Table 3," Gedge replied with desperate intensity. "The pipes are silted up with lime. It is like a meat safe down there. Now, your actor chap... he is the guest of honour. He has the authority to request an adjustment to the ceremonial running order."

He shifted the pumpkin into the crook of his left arm, reached into the fob pocket of his corduroy trousers, and extracted a folded bank note. It was a crisp, purple fifty-pound note, smelling faintly of tobacco and garden compost. He held it out between his thumb and index finger, pressing it toward Zoe's palm.

"Just ten minutes," Gedge whispered. "Tell your man to deliver an extra anecdote about his television series before the heavy squashes. Ten minutes of Dalrymple by the microphone, and the sun stays on my bench until the weigh-master arrives. Fifty quid for your trouble. A drink in the bar on the way back to London."

Zoe looked at the purple note. In five years of assisting Julian, she had been offered many things: stale canapés, promotional baseball caps from failed cable channels, and promises of script development meetings that never materialized. She had never before been offered cash to alter the thermodynamic conditions of an oversized pumpkin.

She folded her hands cleanly over the clasp of her handbag.

"Mr Gedge," Zoe said, her voice dropping into a register of terrifying, polite frost. "Mr Tremayne's stage appearances are governed strictly by the British Actors' Equity Association guidelines and the terms of his agency engagement. He does not provide delayed theatrical monologues for commercial or agricultural advantage."

Gedge blinked behind his taped spectacles. His face turned a deep, embarrassed crimson. "It wasn't a bribe, miss. It was... an administrative gratuity."

"Put the money back in your pocket, Mr Gedge," Zoe said gently, stepping past him into the corridor. "And if you wish to maintain the temperature of your gourd, I suggest you wrap it in your mustard jacket. The colour appears to suit it admirably."

She walked into the lounge, leaving Gedge clutching his pumpkin in the dim light by the telephone directories. Through the low bay window, she watched the rain drumming against the sodden gravel of the car park. Three ducks were swimming in a rut near the dustbins. The afternoon was sliding toward twilight, and the smell of boiled potatoes from the public kitchen had begun to seep into the furniture.

After Trevor Gedge scurried away down the corridor clutching his warty orange gourd, Zoe walked into the public bar to purchase a bottle of still mineral water.

The bar was packed with allotmenteers drinking pints of heavy dark bitter and arguing about soil nitrates. At the far end of the counter, young Henderson was leaning against the brass beer pumps, surrounded by his three farm workers in navy boiler suits.

Henderson was holding court, waving a pint glass of lager as he addressed a small circle of younger growers.

"It's about scale," Henderson was saying, his voice loud with arrogant confidence. "You old boys spend all summer digging trenches by hand with a spade. My firm has twenty thousand square feet of computer-controlled glass in Wisbech. We monitor the sap flow with infrared sensors and inject liquid nitrogen directly into the root zone every four hours. That is where the future of British horticulture lies."

An elderly grower in a flat cap shook his head with quiet disdain. "That ain't horticulture, young Henderson. That's factory chemistry. A show marrow needs the sun and the peat. It needs a man who knows the smell of the furrow."

Henderson laughed, a harsh, dismissive sound that echoed off the timber ceiling beams. "The scale don't care about the smell of the furrow, mate. The scale only reads the pounds and ounces. And at five o'clock, the Henderson name goes onto that silver cup."

Zoe paid for her water with two pound coins, took her bottle, and walked back toward the Rutland Suite. The contrast between Henderson's cold industrial arrogance and Arthur Povey's fierce, ancient devotion made her blood boil. For the first time all day, she realized that she was no longer an impartial observer managing a minor celebrity's diary; she was an active partisan in a civil war of the soil.

Arthur Povey's marrow shelter was constructed from four scaffolding poles and an army surplus tarpaulin, tied securely with yellow nylon cord against the fenland gusts.

Inside the tent, the air was warm, damp, and smelled strongly of wet loam and paraffin vapour.

"Keep the flap tied down," Arthur ordered without looking up from his marrow. "The south-westerly brings a cold draft off the river. Chills the rind."

Julian ducked his head beneath the low lintel, his coat brushing the canvas. "An intensive care unit for Cucurbita pepo. Remarkable devotion, Arthur."

"Devotion don't come into it," Arthur muttered, checking the mercury in a brass soil thermometer. "It's forty-four days of four-hour waterings and two applications of sheep liquor per week. You slack off for twelve hours, and the skin turns leather-hard and stops expanding."

"Like the second act of a Pinero play," Julian observed. "Momentum is everything."

Inside Arthur's marrow shelter, the silence was broken only by the steady hiss of the paraffin burner.

"Arthur," Zoe said gently.

"Aye?"

"The scales are being calibrated by the weights and measures inspector."

Arthur nodded slowly, his calloused fingers resting on the smooth green curve of the Emperor. "Donald's a hard man, but he's straight. He don't favour Wisbech, and he don't favour Deeping. If she makes the weight, he'll sign the book."

"And if she doesn't?" Julian asked from the tent doorway.

Arthur looked up, his small grey eyes meeting Julian's with unblinking stoicism. "Then she goes on the compost heap behind my potting shed, and I start digging the trench for the ninety-nine season come November. The soil don't stop turning because a marrow falls short."

Julian bowed his head, moved beyond speech by the simple, ancient nobility of the old man's resolve.

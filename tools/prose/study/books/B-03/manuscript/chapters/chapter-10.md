# Chapter 10: The Ten-Minute Call

At thirteen minutes to two, the heating boiler in Room 14b emitted a sharp, metallic screech and shut down completely.

The sudden silence in the basement was more alarming than the noise. The pipes ceased their frantic clattering, leaving behind only the distant, muffled thump of rubber wellingtons pacing across the floorboards above and the slow drip of condensation falling from an overhead copper pipe into an empty paint tin.

Julian sat bolt upright on his metal folding chair, his arms crossed over his chest, his eyes fixed on the ceiling with rigid, immovable resolve.

"Ten minutes, Julian," Zoe said, holding his charcoal jacket open. "Slip your arms in. We need to be standing by the double doors when Fiona takes the stage."

"I am not moving," Julian said.

Zoe paused with the jacket. Her phone showed nine percent battery. A cold drop of sweat trickled down between her shoulder blades.

"Julian," she said evenly. "We discussed this. Seven minutes. Professional pride. The landlord."

"I am perfectly cognizant of our financial liabilities, Miss Barnes," Julian replied in a voice of icy, crystalline dignity. "However, there are fundamental professional boundaries beneath which an artist of my standing cannot descend without compromising the integrity of his craft."

"What boundaries?"

Julian pointed a finger toward the wooden ironing board. "Where is the hospitality provision?"

Zoe stared at the ironing board. Upon it rested a clean linen napkin, a bottle of Highland Spring sparkling water that she had purchased with her own money at Peterborough station, and a small packet of dry roasted peanuts.

"There is water, Julian. Sparkling. Scottish."

"Where is the fruit?" Julian demanded, his voice rising into his upper theatrical register. "Where is the selection of fresh, seasonal orchard produce? In twenty-five years of professional public appearances, I have never once stepped onto a platform without an unblemished green apple and a small cluster of seedless grapes in my dressing quarters. It is an elementary vocal necessity! It cleanses the palate of mucus before projection!"

"Julian, you are in a boiler room beneath a pub in Market Deeping. The kitchen is currently frying forty portions of haddock for the lunchtime trade. They do not have seedless grapes."

"Then they must procure them," he said, settling his spine against the back of the metal chair. "There must be a greengrocer in the high street. Send the boots. Send the scullery lad."

"There is no scullery lad, Julian!" Zoe snapped, the calm finally cracking in her voice like brittle plastic. "There is only me! And I am not running down a rain-swept bypass in three-inch heels to buy you a Granny Smith while eighty farmers wait for you to open their pumpkin contest!"

Julian turned his head toward the wall, his lips pressed into a thin, stubborn seam. "Then the opening will be delayed. Let them inspect their leeks in silence. An actor who cannot cleanse his palate cannot be held responsible for his diction."

Zoe looked at him. She looked at his crisp crimson pocket square, his silver-streaked temples, his polished shoes resting an inch above the puddle of boiler condensation.

She felt the five-thousand-pound clawback hovering over her head like a falling guillotine.

She reached into her coat pocket, pulled out the brown paper seed envelope that Artie Povey had given her, and set it down firmly on the ironing board directly in front of Julian's nose.

Julian frowned, his gaze flicking toward the paper. "What is that?"

"That," Zoe said, her voice dropping into a thrilling, conspiratorial whisper, "is the seed line of the nineteen-eighty-four All-England Champion Marrow. Valued at five hundred pounds on the European horticultural exchange."

Julian leaned forward, his curiosity overcoming his petulance. "Indeed? And why is it on my ironing board?"

"Because," Zoe whispered, leaning down until her face was level with his, "the growers believe you are an undercover adjudicator sent down from London by the Ministry of Agriculture to investigate corruption in the committee. They are terrified of you, Julian. Old Artie Povey just attempted to bribe me with his ancestral seeds to secure your favour on the platform."

Julian's eyes widened. A slow, magnificent flush of vanity spread across his cheekbones. He straightened his spine, his fingers rising instinctively to touch his silk lapel.

"An adjudicator?" he murmured, testing the word with his tongue like a vintage port. "An undercover investigator from the capital? Well... of course, Dalrymple spent three seasons working with the Inland Revenue Special Branch in ninety-nine. The gravitas is unmistakable. The public recognizes authority when it enters the room."

"Precisely," Zoe said, holding out the jacket once more. "And an investigator from the Ministry does not sit in a cellar whining about grapes. He goes upstairs, takes command of the platform, and lets the corrupt committee know that London is watching."

Julian rose from the folding chair in one fluid, magnificent sweep of wool and silk. He slid his arms into the sleeves of his tailored jacket, adjusted his cuffs, and patted his pocket comb into place.

"You are quite right, Miss Barnes," he declared, his baritone filling the silent boiler room like a cathedral organ. "Let us ascend. Justice must be done, and Dalrymple is on the case."

In the basement corridor, the countdown clock on Zoe's phone reached five minutes to two.

The air from the kitchen was now thick with the scent of fried chips and malt vinegar, mingling with the heavy, oily smell of the cooling boiler. 

Julian stood outside the door of Room 14b, executing a series of shoulder rolls and neck stretches that caused the silk lining of his waistcoat to rustle like dry autumn leaves. He held Zoe's silver pocket comb in his palm, checking his reflection in the glass panel of the fire alarm box.

"Five minutes, Julian," Zoe said, handing him his freshly lint-rolled charcoal jacket. "Slip your arms in."

Julian paused, holding the comb aloft like a conductor's baton. "Tell me once more, Zoe. The name of the sponsoring enterprise?"

"Fenland Tractors and Hydraulics Limited," Zoe recited smoothly. "The managing director is Mr Raymond Clough. He is sixty-five years old, wears a rotary club lapel badge, and recently donated five hundred pounds to the restoration of the church roof in Bourne."

"And the chairwoman?"

"Mrs Fiona Sterling, JP," Zoe said, looking him straight in the eyes. "Your former wife. Whom you have not seen since twenty eleven, and whom you will address with absolute, faultless professional courtesy."

Julian gave a magnificent, theatrical sigh that fluttered his crimson pocket square. "Courtesy is my second nature, my dear. Dalrymple was renowned throughout the East Midlands for his unfailing politeness to hostile witnesses. Even when confronting the poisoner of Holbeach Marsh, he never once raised his voice above a dignified baritone."

"This is not a television murder mystery, Julian," Zoe reminded him, stepping in front of him to adjust his collar. "This is an agricultural exhibition. Nobody has been poisoned."

"Not yet," Julian murmured with a wry, conspiratorial wink. "Though if Fiona's trifles are as lethal as her temper, we may yet require an inquest before the afternoon is through."

He drew himself up, threw his shoulders back until his posture radiated patrician authority, and offered Zoe his arm.

"Come, Miss Barnes," Julian declared, his baritone filling the basement passage with resonant command. "The hour has struck. Let us ascend to the arena."

"The press photographer has arrived from the Stamford Mercury," Zoe said, looking toward the main doors.

Julian immediately straightened his spine, sucking in his abdomen and tilting his chin upward at precisely thirty-five degrees. "A local paper, but one must never underestimate the provincial circulation. What is his equipment?"

"A battered Nikon with a flash bracket held together with brown parcel tape," Zoe reported.

"A working journalist," Julian pronounced with approval. "None of your digital trickery. An honest craftsman of the silver halide process. Ensure he catches the gleam of the medallion when Fiona presents the badge."

"Fiona has not mentioned any medallion, Julian."

"There is always a medallion," Julian stated with calm, unshakable conviction. "Even in Market Rasen, they had a brass medallion on a green ribbon."

"In Market Rasen, Julian, the medallion was the lid from an old tobacco tin."

"It was presented with great ceremony," Julian insisted, stepping out into the aisle. "And the ceremony is what matters."

"The Stamford Mercury photographer is adjusting his tripod," Zoe warned.

"Is the crimson pocket square visible?"

"Distinctly," she said. "It looks like an exotic bird nesting in your breast pocket."

"Excellent," Julian said, checking his reflection in the glass lid of a display case containing prize shallots. "One must provide the local readership with an image of metropolitan glamour. A hint of the West End amidst the root vegetables."

"The local readership is mostly interested in whether the price of diesel will drop before the sugar beet harvest, Julian."

"The local readership possesses a soul, Zoe, even if it is currently preoccupied with agricultural fuel oil," Julian insisted, stepping out into the chalked aisle. "Now observe: the benevolent smile, the slight inclination of the head, the paternal hand placed lightly upon the marrow's stem. A study in rustic harmony."

The camera's flash bulb fired with a sharp pop and a faint puff of blue smoke.

"Splendid," Julian beamed at the photographer. "Now let us take another for safety."

# Chapter 25: The Emergency Suture

Zoe knelt on the parquet floor beside Arthur Povey, ignoring the damp dust that coated the knees of her tailored wool trousers.

"Hold the sling steady," Zoe commanded the four lifters, her voice crisp, calm, and utterly devoid of hesitation. "Do not lower it to the floor. Keep the tension even on both sides."

The four men looked at her in astonishment, but there was an authority in her tone that commanded instantaneous obedience. They locked their knees and held the heavy canvas straps rigid.

Zoe extracted three items from her leather bag: a small bottle of medical skin-bonding adhesive from her emergency travel kit, a roll of high-tensile silver duct tape that she carried for repairing Julian's split luggage seams, and a packet of sterile surgical cotton pads.

Fiona Sterling stepped forward, her clipboard held aloft like a shield. "Miss Barnes, what do you think you are doing? Tampering with an exhibit during active adjudication is an immediate forfeiture under Rule 8."

Zoe looked up, her dark eyes meeting Fiona's with freezing administrative clarity.

"I am not tampering with an exhibit, Mrs Sterling," Zoe said smoothly. "I am applying Section 19 of the Royal Horticultural Society Field First Aid Regulations, as adopted for regional agricultural exhibitions in nineteen ninety-nine. A specimen suffering mechanical transport trauma prior to scale contact may be stabilized by an authorized steward or designated guest representative, provided the repair material adds no measurable mass."

Fiona blinked, her pencil hovering in mid-air. "Section 19 applies to cut flowers!"

"Section 19 applies to all certified horticultural produce," Zoe bluffed with magnificent, straight-faced conviction, drawing upon five years of inventing imaginary clauses to pacify foreign hotel managers and angry airline gate agents. "Mr Tremayne, as guest of honour, has designated me as executive field steward. Arthur, give me your handkerchief."

Povey thrust his blue cotton handkerchief into her hand without a word.

Zoe worked with rapid, surgical precision. She wiped the bead of sap from the lip of the fissure, ensuring the skin was bone-dry. She applied a micro-thin bead of the medical cyanoacrylate along the split, holding the two edges of the green rind together with her thumbs until the adhesive polymerized in the warm room air.

Then she tore two three-inch strips of silver duct tape, rounding the corners with Julian's nail scissors to prevent peeling, and smoothed them firmly across the fissure at right angles to the split.

The repair was invisible from three feet away, holding the damaged rind in a secure, flexible grip that contained the internal hydraulic pressure of the marrow.

Zoe stood, wiped her hands on a dry paper napkin, and looked at Fiona.

"The epidermal layer is stabilized, Madam Chairman," Zoe said. "The specimen is ready for the scales."

Arthur Povey rose from his knees, his face working with uncontrollable emotion. He looked at the silver tape, then looked at Zoe. A single tear rolled down through the peat dust on his cheek, disappearing into his white stubble.

"God bless you, my girl," Povey whispered, his voice shaking with reverence. "You have got the hands of an angel and the nerve of a highwayman."

He reached into his pocket, pulled out the brown paper envelope containing his ancestral 1984 champion seed line, and pressed it firmly into Zoe's palm, closing her fingers over it with his massive, calloused hand.

"They're yours," Povey said, loud enough for the whole room to hear. "Win or lose, those seeds belong to you. You saved the Emperor."

The entire hall erupted into deafening cheers. Grown men in muddy wellingtons clapped each other on the back; the barmaid from the snug banged an empty tray against the radiator; even Frank on the scales gave a triumphant toot on his referee's whistle.

Fiona stared at the green marrow, then looked at Zoe. For three seconds, a flicker of profound, reluctant admiration passed across the magistrate's face.

She tapped her clipboard once with her pencil.

"Very well," Fiona announced into the microphone, her voice crisp and dry. "The repair is noted on the official record. Stewards... load the Emperor."

As Zoe finished applying the second strip of silver duct tape across the Emperor's fissure, Arthur Povey seized her hand and pressed it to his flannel shirt.

"Forty years," Povey whispered, his voice choked with tears. "Forty years I've grown on the south allotment, Miss Barnes. My Mary used to say that a man who grows a prize marrow leaves a mark upon the earth that the frost can't wipe out. If that Henderson boy had taken the cup with his chemical monster, it would have broken my heart."

"The Emperor is sound, Mr Povey," Zoe said gently, helping the old man to his feet. "The tape will hold the skin until the balance arm settles. But when you hoist, lift from the canvas webbing, not the timber barrow. Keep the weight off the belly."

Frank and the four lifters nodded in grim, determined unison. They were no longer merely committee stewards; they were a surgical team carrying out a high-stakes emergency extraction.

"Webbing only," Frank commanded his crew, spitting into his hands. "Together on three. Lift smooth, and don't let the cradle swing."

Zoe returned through the side entrance three minutes later, her trench coat pockets bulging, her breath short from running across the wet cobbles.

"Did you secure the preparation?" Julian inquired from behind his exhibition programme.

"Eight pounds fifty," Zoe whispered, sliding a small blue tube into Arthur's smock pocket. "Surgical tissue glue from the veterinary counter. Used for closing lacerations on gun dogs."

Arthur touched the tube through his coarse canvas, his eyes widening. "Will it hold against forty pounds of hydraulic sap pressure?"

"It sets in thirty seconds in the presence of surface moisture," Zoe said crisply. "And when it cures, it is completely transparent and waterproof. Now, Arthur, while Julian demonstrates his diction to the committee, reach under the rear shock pad and seal the seam."

Arthur looked at Zoe with an expression that bordered on religious devotion. "Lass, you ought to have been in the Royal Engineers."

"Arthur," Fiona's voice rang out from the table. "You have not signed the indemnity waiver for the hoist."

Arthur wiped his oily hands on his trousers, stepping toward the table with heavy, deliberate boots.

"Where do I sign, Mrs Sterling?"

"Line four, beneath the declared tare weight," Fiona said, extending her black fountain pen. "It states that the Society accepts no liability for vegetative fracture occurring during the mechanical transit between pallet and cradle."

Arthur took the pen, gripping it like a garden spade, and scratched his signature with slow, painful concentration.

"There," he muttered. "Legal and binding."

"Thank you, Arthur," Fiona said, her gaze lingering on his weathered face for a moment. "Good luck on the hoist."

Arthur blinked in surprise, touching his cap with a stiff forefinger. "Thank you, Fiona. That's very civil of you."

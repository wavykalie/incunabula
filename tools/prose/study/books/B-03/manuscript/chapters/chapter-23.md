# Chapter 23: The Weight of Pride

The silence that followed Julian's announcement lasted for four full seconds, broken only by the hum of the beer chillers and the distant rattle of rain against the corrugated roof.

Then young Henderson let out a triumphant whoop that caused two pigeons in the rafters to flutter their wings. He slapped the pine crate with his palm, grinning broadly at his father in the sheepskin coat.

"Seventy-four eight!" Henderson yelled. "County record! Beat that with your horse muck, Artie!"

The allotmenteers did not cheer. A sullen, muttering darkness descended upon the local growers. Men in flat caps looked at their boots; two stewards near the door folded their arms and turned their backs on the platform; someone near the tea urn spat onto the green baize.

Julian stood at the centre of the stage, still holding the microphone, observing the room with deep, clinical curiosity.

In London, Julian had spent five years lamenting the triviality of his commercial voiceover work. He had complained bitterly to Zoe about the indignity of recording carpet advertisements and regional radio jingles, convincing himself that his great artistic gifts were being squandered on a superficial society that cared only for celebrity gossip and streaming metrics.

Yet looking out over this sea of eighty weathered faces, he recognized something he had not seen in the West End since nineteen ninety-two: total, uncompromised artistic devotion.

These men and women did not cultivate giant squash for money. The first prize was a tarnished silver cup and a fifty-pound feed voucher from a local merchant. They spent eight months of every year hauling manure, setting frost alarms, and boiling seaweed extract in tin pans because they believed, with the absolute purity of medieval stonemasons, in the excellence of the work.

Julian raised the microphone once more. His posture shifted; the theatrical grandiosity dropped away, replaced by an authentic, unvarnished warmth that surprised even Zoe.

"Seventy-four pounds and eight ounces of pure agricultural mass," Julian said, his voice quiet, conversational, and carrying effortlessly to the back of the hall. "A formidable achievement of modern horticulture, Mr Henderson. The committee acknowledges the scientific rigor of the Wissington glasshouses."

He paused, letting his gaze travel across the green baize tables to where Arthur Povey stood alone beside Table 6.

"And yet," Julian continued, his tone deepening with quiet reverence, "the competition is not yet concluded. The charter of the South Kesteven Society provides that the reigning champion retains the privilege of the final scale entry. Stewards, clear the cradle. Bring forward the Emperor."

A sudden, sharp ripple of energy snapped through the room. The gloomy silence dissolved into a chorus of shouts and scraping boots.

"Bring him up!" shouted George Baxter from Table 2. "Let's see what forty years of fen peat can do against the chemical tanks!"

Arthur Povey looked up from his table. For a moment, his watery blue eyes met Julian's across the crowded room. The old grower did not smile, but he slowly removed his flat cap, tucking it beneath his arm in a gesture of solemn, respectful salute.

"Frank!" Povey barked, his voice raw with emotion. "Get the four-man sling! And mind you don't touch the underbelly with your cold spanner!"

The discussion regarding specific gravity had now drawn in three retired schoolmasters from Stamford, who were arguing about the Archimedean displacement of giant squash using diagrams drawn on paper beer mats.

"If the internal cavity is hollow," the eldest schoolmaster declared, tapping his pen against a beer mat, "the vegetable displaces more air than its mass warrants! It floats on the atmosphere like a zeppelin!"

"The Emperor ain't got no hollow cavity!" Arthur Povey barked from across the table. "She's solid meat from stem to blossom! You could carve doorsteps of marrow loaf out of her belly without finding a teaspoon of empty air!"

Julian stepped between the disputants, holding his hands aloft in a gesture of benevolent patriarchal conciliation. "Gentlemen, gentlemen. Let us not divide our energies in sterile scholastic debate. The scale is the final arbiter. The brass needle knows no theory; it acknowledges only the honest weight of the loam. Let the Emperor take the stand, and let gravity deliver its judgment."

The schoolmasters fell silent, looking at Julian with sudden, cowed respect. 

"The actor speaks well," one of them murmured to his colleague. "A classical cadence. Very sound on empirical gravity."

"What is the current record, Arthur?" Zoe asked, leaning over the wooden bench where Arthur was checking his brass plumb bob.

"Seventy-two pounds, four ounces," Arthur recited from memory. "Set in nineteen ninety-one by a stationmaster from Littleport. Fed his marrow on canal water and crushed bone meal."

"A formidable standard," Julian said. "Has any grower approached that benchmark in recent years?"

"Nobody within ten miles of Deeping," Arthur stated with grim pride. "Wisbech had a runner in ninety-five that looked eighty on the vine, but she went spongy in the middle and lost four pounds on the scale. Air rot. The grower took to drink and moved to Peterborough."

"A tragic dénouement," Julian murmured, shaking his head. "The vegetable world has its King Lears, it would seem."

"Zoe."

"Julian?"

"Observe the gentleman in the brown flat cap."

"The one eating a ham roll?"

"He has been staring at my overcoat with unmistakable theatrical nostalgia," Julian said, straightening his crimson pocket square. "He recognizes the Dalrymple silhouette. The charcoal cashmere, the silk handkerchief, the authoritative posture of the senior investigator."

Zoe looked at the gentleman, who was currently brushing breadcrumbs from his waistcoat.

"Julian," she said gently.

"Yes, my dear?"

"He is wearing a coat from the same manufacturer. He is wondering if you also bought yours at the Army and Navy Stores clearance sale in ninety-three."

Julian closed his eyes in brief, silent agony. "You have a positive genius for dismantling my romantic illusions, Miss Barnes."

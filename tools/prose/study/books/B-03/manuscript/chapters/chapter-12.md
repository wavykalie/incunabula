# Chapter 12: The Norfolk Incursion

At twenty minutes past three, while Julian was trapped in the corner of the bar explaining the lighting setup of his nineteen-ninety-nine police special to a retired parish surveyor, Zoe slipped through the service double doors behind the hotel kitchen.

The service corridor smelled of commercial floor cleaner, frying oil, and wet timber. 

She walked past the beer barrel racks, her heels clicking against the rough concrete floor, until she reached the emergency exit that opened onto the delivery lane behind the tractor shed. The panic bar on the heavy steel door had been wedged open with half an engineering brick.

Through the two-inch gap, a cold draft blew into the corridor, carrying the smell of diesel exhaust and damp fen ditch-water.

Zoe pushed the door open and stepped out onto the wet concrete apron.

Beside an industrial green waste container overflowing with cardboard vegetable boxes sat a flatbed timber pallet. Upon the pallet rested a shipping crate constructed from rough unplaned pine boards, measuring five feet in length by three feet in width. The timber was fresh, bearing a stenciled black ink stamp on its upper corner: *EAST ANGLIAN HEAVY CUCURBIT LOGISTICS — KING'S LYNN*.

Zoe approached the crate. 

The lid had been secured with three steel bands, but two of the bands had already been snipped with tin snips, their sharp edges curling upward like pig tails. Through the gap between the upper slats, a bundle of wet gray wool was clearly visible, tucked around a massive, glistening green shape that filled the entire interior cavity.

Zoe glanced toward the delivery lane. The lane was deserted, save for a white Ford Transit van parked twenty yards away under the dripping willows, its engine ticking quietly as the exhaust cooled. 

She reached into the gap between the slats and touched the wet wool. Beneath the fleece, the rind was cool, hard, and terrifyingly smooth. It lacked the natural mottling and peat grain of Artie Povey's Emperor; this skin was an unnatural, uniform dark jade, polished until it reflected the gray sky like a waxed automobile bonnet.

"Find what you are looking for, miss?"

Zoe pulled her hand back smoothly, tucking her fingers into the pockets of her trench coat, and turned around.

A young man of about twenty-eight stood in the doorway of the tractor shed, wiping a pair of greasy hands on an oily rag. He wore an insulated navy bodywarmer embroidered with the logo *G. R. HENDERSON FEEDS*, denim jeans tucked into pristine green neoprene boots, and a baseball cap bearing the slogan *Maximum Yield Through Chemistry*.

"Good afternoon," Zoe said, offering her crisp corporate public relations smile. "I was looking for the society committee room."

"Committee room is in the basement," the young man said, not moving from the shed door. "This is the trade delivery entrance. Private unloading area."

"I see," Zoe said. She gestured casually toward the pine crate. "A magnificent specimen. Is this the Henderson entry?"

The young man's eyes narrowed with cautious rural pride. "Seventy-four point eight on the farm weighbridge this morning. Grown under twin-wall polycarbonate in Wisbech. Fed on specialized kelp concentrate and liquid ammonium nitrate through drip emitters every twenty minutes since the first of June."

"Very impressive," Zoe said. "Mr Povey was telling me earlier that exhibits must originate from within the historic drainage boundary of South Kesteven."

The young man let out a short, contemptuous bark of laughter that echoed off the metal shed roof. "Artie Povey lives in the nineteen-fifties. He thinks you can win a modern heavyweight competition with a spade and a bucket of horse muck. My dad paid four thousand pounds for the seed stock in Holland. We hold the regional distribution rights for the entire Fen basin. The Society rules allow corporate sponsor entries under the Open Agricultural Innovation clause."

"And the brass plaque on the trophy?" Zoe asked mildly. "The one that already bears your name?"

Young Henderson stopped wiping his hands. The rag fell still against his palm. His jaw tightened, and he took half a step forward onto the concrete apron.

"My dad donated that cup in twenty-eight," Henderson said, his voice dropping into a flat, defensive tone. "He paid for the engraving in advance because the engraver in Bourne goes on holiday to Skegness on Monday morning. It is not cheating, miss. It is administrative foresight."

"Of course," Zoe said, stepping past him toward the emergency fire door. "Administrative foresight is so rare in local government. I am sure Mrs Sterling will appreciate your efficiency when she begins the caliper measurements."

She slipped back inside the service corridor, leaving young Henderson standing in the rain beside his timber crate, his eyes fixed on her trench coat with deep, uneasy hostility.

Zoe walked back through the service passage toward the main ballroom, her mind racing over the implications of young Henderson's pine crate.

The East Anglian Heavy Cucurbit Logistics label confirmed that the specimen had been grown under commercial glass in Norfolk, miles outside the statutory boundaries of the South Kesteven drainage district. If Fiona allowed the entry, she would be violating the very charter her grandfather had signed in nineteen fifty-three.

Beside the ice machine, Kelly the receptionist was leaning against a stack of beer crates, texting on her phone.

"Kelly," Zoe said, pausing beside the machine. "Do you know who owns that white Transit van parked behind the tractor shed?"

Kelly looked up, her chewing gum snapping with a soft pop. "That's young Henderson's van. He's been here since eleven. His dad booked the private dining room for seven o'clock tonight. Ordered twenty ribeye steaks and four bottles of vintage port from the cellar."

"A celebration dinner?" Zoe asked.

"A victory dinner," Kelly said with a shrug. "Old Mr Henderson told the manager that they were taking the Silver Challenge Cup back to King's Lynn tonight. Said it was about time modern business took over from the allotment pensioners."

Zoe thanked her and pushed through the swing doors into the ballroom. The lines of battle were clear. This was not merely an agricultural contest; it was a commercial takeover of a century-old provincial tradition. And young Henderson had the money, the logistics, and the engraved brass plaque to prove it.

"Look at this display, Julian," Zoe said, guiding him past the row of commercial trade stands.

"What is the product?"

"Pelleted sheep manure from a supplier in Market Harborough," she said, reading the hand-lettered placard. "Fourteen pounds a sack, or three for thirty-five pounds."

Julian paused, inspecting the hessian sack with aristocratic contemplation. "Organic richness. The natural cycle of ovine digestion returning vigor to the soil. There is something profoundly restorative about a manure merchant who takes pride in his pellets."

The manure merchant, an elderly man in a brown flat cap, touched his peak with two fingers. "Guaranteed weed-free, Mr Tremayne. Dried under glass. You could scatter it on your rose beds like demerara sugar."

"I reside in a third-floor flat in Maida Vale, my good man," Julian confessed gently. "My horticultural domain is restricted to an earthenware trough containing two ungrateful geraniums."

"Then you want the half-size bag," the merchant replied without missing a beat. "Seven pounds fifty, fits under the sink."

"Look at this flyer, Zoe," Julian said, picking up a pink sheet of paper from the trade counter.

"What does it say?"

"The South Lincolnshire Thespian Guild is staging The Importance of Being Earnest in the Stamford Arts Centre in February."

Zoe took the flyer from his hand. "They are advertising for a volunteer director with access to a horse and carriage for the exterior promotional photography."

Julian's eyes took on that dangerous, dreamy gleam that Zoe knew all too well. "Lady Bracknell, Zoe. What a role. What an architectural monument of comic timing. If I directed, I could play Bracknell in drag, after the manner of the late, great Jimmy Logan. A formidable, towering dowager with a Lincolnshire drawl."

"Julian," Zoe said firmly, placing the flyer face down upon the counter.

"Yes, my dear?"

"You are not directing amateur theatricals in Stamford for an honorarium consisting of two tickets to the pantomime."

Julian sighed deeply. "You crush my artistic spirit, Zoe."

"I protect your professional standing, Julian. And your bank account."

# Chapter 39: The Receipt Folder

At midnight, in Room 21 of the hotel twin annex, Zoe sat upon the edge of her single bed beneath an itchy orange woollen blanket, reconciling the financial records of the expedition.

The two-bar electric fire in the corner hummed with a dry, metallic buzz, fed by three fifty-pence coins that Kelly had provided from the bar till. The room smelled of lavender air freshener and cold plaster, but the sheets were clean and the draft from the window was kept at bay by a heavy pair of floral curtains.

Upon the laminate bedside table, Zoe arranged the paper trail of the day into three neat, overlapping stacks:

First, the hotel incidental expenses: two portions of toasted cheese sandwiches from the lounge bar, three bottles of Highland Spring sparkling water, one packet of dry roasted peanuts, and sixty-five pounds for the twin room hire. Total: eighty-one pounds and forty pence.

Second, the travel disbursements: seventy-four pounds for the cancelled King's Cross off-peak returns, twenty-six pounds for the Grantham minicab, and twelve pounds for the emergency skin-bonding adhesive from the chemist in Bourne. Total: one hundred and twelve pounds.

Third, and most important, the brown manila envelope that Fiona Sterling had handed to Zoe in the hallway before the bar closed.

Zoe opened the envelope and tipped the contents onto the orange blanket.

Inside lay twenty-five crisp, uncirculated fifty-pound banknotes, tied with a length of green garden raffia. 

Attached to the bundle was an official receipt from the Deeping and District Horticultural Society, stamped with the society's purple rubber seal and countersigned in blue ink by Fiona Sterling:

*Received from the Deeping & District Horticultural Society the sum of £1,250 (one thousand two hundred and fifty pounds sterling), representing the final balance of the celebrity appearance fee for Mr Julian Briggs, paid in full and without deduction.*

*Note: The Society records its profound gratitude to Miss Zoe Barnes for executive field services rendered during the heavy gourd weigh-in.*

Zoe picked up the receipt. Beside the note, written in Fiona's sharp, elegant hand, was a brief postscript:

*Do not give this cash to Marcus. He will take fifteen percent and lose the rest in a Soho wine bar. Pay Julian's electricity bill directly, buy yourself a proper winter coat, and send me a copy of your first play.*

Zoe smiled. She counted the fifty-pound notes, placed five of them into her personal leather wallet to reimburse her own credit card, and slid the remaining twenty notes back into the manila envelope for Julian's landlord.

The accounts were balanced. The record was closed. The debt was settled to the final penny.

She picked up her Pilot pen and pulled her notebook onto her knees.

Across the top of the lined page, she wrote: Characters of the Fenland Exhibition.

Below it, she noted: Fiona Sterling: administrative clarity as an emotional fortification. Arthur Povey: soil science as personal dignity. Julian Tremayne: the theatre of survival in provincial halls.

She tapped the pen against her teeth. In two days, she had seen an elderly actor risk his dignity for twenty pounds of rent, an ex-wife enforce municipal bylaws to preserve an institution, and an old gardener weep over eighty pounds of vegetative flesh. There was a play here, she knew. Not a television murder mystery with staged suspense and convenient confessions, but a true comedy of English manners, where people fought desperately for small dignities in drafty parish halls.

She closed the notebook, placed it carefully beside the envelope of fifty-pound notes, and switched off the bedside lamp.

Outside, the church clock of Market Deeping chimed midnight across the quiet fen. She pulled the itchy orange woollen blanket up to her chin, closed her eyes, and fell into a peaceful, unbroken sleep.

In Room 21, Zoe checked the clasp on her brown leather folder one final time.

The twenty fifty-pound notes were sealed inside the manila envelope for Mr Gidley.

Her own five notes were tucked into the zip pocket of her wallet.

Arthur's seed packet sat on the bedside table beside her notebook.

She picked up her Pilot pen and added one final sentence to her notes:

*The opening act is not about winning the prize; it is about keeping the curtain up until the second act begins.*

She smiled, switched off the reading lamp, and pulled the warm blankets over her shoulders as the church bells chimed the quarter-hour into the silent fenland night.

In Room 21 of the annex, Zoe completed the final entry in her expense log.

She closed the black notebook, placed her Pilot pen into its leather sleeve, and stacked her receipts neatly inside the brown manila folder.

On the laminate table sat the two envelopes: one for Mr Gidley with twenty crisp fifty-pound notes, and one for herself with the five notes from Fiona.

Beside them lay Arthur's seeds, wrapped in their red-wax envelope.

She walked to the window, pulled back the heavy floral curtains, and looked out into the Lincolnshire night.

Above the church spire of Market Deeping, the clouds had broken, revealing a vast, cold expanse of autumn stars that stretched toward the Wash. The flat fen lay silent in the moonlight, dark, ancient, and patient, waiting for the winter rains to replenish the loam.

She had arrived forty-eight hours ago as an anxious, unpaid assistant escorting a difficult client through a provincial embarrassment. She was leaving as a woman who had saved a landlord's rent, held an institution together, and discovered the subject of her first full-length play.

She dropped the curtain, smiled in the quiet darkness, and climbed into bed.

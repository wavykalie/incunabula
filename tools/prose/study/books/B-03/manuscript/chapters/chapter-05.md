# Chapter 05: The Ex-Wife on the Mic

The woman who emerged from the corridor behind the stage did not walk; she advanced with the terrifying administrative momentum of a motorized lawnmower.

She was fifty-two years old, slim, erect, and dressed in a tweed hacking jacket with leather elbow patches over an immaculate cream silk blouse. Around her neck hung a blue lanyard bearing an official plastic pass marked MRS F. STERLING, JP, CHAIR OF JUDGES. In her left hand, she carried a mahogany clipboard fitted with a heavy chrome spring clamp that held a thick sheaf of carbon-copied judging sheets; in her right, an open pair of eight-inch brass calipers.

She stopped at the microphone stand situated at the downstage edge of the riser, adjusted the gooseneck with a sharp, metallic screech, and tapped the foam windshield twice with her caliper blade.

A violent electronic squeal tore through the four PA speakers mounted on the walls, cutting through the hall's murmur like a circular saw hitting a galvanized nail. Every grower in the room winced in unison and fell silent.

"Testing, one, two," Fiona Sterling said into the microphone. Her voice was crisp, melodic, and clear, possessed of that crystalline county-magistrate timbre that could sentence a shoplifter to community service while making it sound like an invitation to afternoon tea. "Ladies and gentlemen, members of the society, exhibitors. May I remind everyone that under Horticultural Society Rule 14b, all chemical surface sprays, synthetic skin glazes, and unauthorized paraffin rubs must cease immediately. The judging stewards will begin their preliminary rind inspections in twenty minutes."

She released the microphone, made a brisk pencil notation on her clipboard, and turned around.

Her eyes fell directly upon Julian Tremayne.

For six full seconds, the only sound on the stage was the electric hum of the amplifier. 

Fiona did not gasp. She did not drop her clipboard. Her expression altered only by the slightest tightening of the muscles around her cheekbones, shifting from institutional efficiency into arctic, personal amusement.

"Good afternoon, Julian," she said, her voice dropping into conversational volume. "I see you managed to borrow a suit."

Julian stood frozen, his left hand still poised above the fake marigolds. For the first time in five years of continuous service, Zoe saw his professional composure disintegrate completely. His jaw dropped half an inch; his eyes darted toward the exit; his Adam's apple bobbed twice in rapid swallows.

"Fiona," he managed, the famous baritone emerging as a thin, cracked husk. "You... you are looking remarkably well."

"I am looking fifty-two, Julian," Fiona replied, stepping forward and inspecting him with the precision of a woman grading a turnip for root rot. "Which is roughly what you would look like if you stopped tinting your temples with boot polish and paid your tax liabilities on time."

"It is not boot polish," Julian protested feebly, colour rising into his cheeks. "It is an organic vegetable extract applied by my London stylist."

"Splendid. Then it will feel right at home among the parsnips." Fiona turned her gaze smoothly toward Zoe, taking in her trench coat, her leather tote bag, and the folder of printed itineraries tucked beneath her arm. "And you must be the current assistant. What is your name, my dear?"

"Zoe Barnes, Mrs Sterling," Zoe said, stepping forward with her right hand outstretched. "I manage Mr Tremayne's diary and appearances."

Fiona took Zoe's hand, offering a brief, dry squeeze before releasing it. "You have my deepest sympathy, Miss Barnes. How long have you survived?"

"Five years, Mrs Sterling."

Fiona's eyebrows arched in genuine admiration. "Five years? Extraordinary. The girl before you, what was her name, Julian? The one with the nose ring and the sociology degree? She lasted exactly three weeks before she threw his laundry into the canal and joined an ashram in Gloucestershire. You must have nerves of cold steel."

"Miss Barnes is an executive administrator of the highest caliber," Julian broke in, attempting to reassert his theatrical dignity. "She handles the professional logistics of an extremely demanding creative career."

"Does she indeed?" Fiona smiled, turning back to the microphone. She clicked the switch on the stem, leaned down, and addressed the eighty growers waiting on the floor below.

"Ladies and gentlemen! May I have your attention for a moment, please. We have a brief announcement regarding the opening ceremony."

The allotmenteers looked up from their crates. Old Artie Povey paused with his rag, squinting toward the stage with open hostility.

"As many of you are aware," Fiona announced, her voice echoing sweetly through the room, "our scheduled guest of honour, Mr Derek 'Chisel' Thompson from BBC Radio Lincolnshire's *Down the Allotment*, was unfortunately taken ill this morning with acute gastroenteritis following an unpasteurized cheese incident in Louth."

A sympathetic murmur rippled across the green baize tables.

"However," Fiona continued, gesturing with her brass calipers toward Julian, "by an extraordinary stroke of municipal fortune, the agency has sent us a replacement entertainment. Please give a warm Fenland welcome to Mr Julian Briggs, performing under the theatrical pseudonym Julian Tremayne, who some of our older members may vaguely recall as a supporting police constable in an ITV drama broadcast in the late nineteen-nineties!"

A scattered, confused spatter of applause arose from three people near the tea urn. Old Artie did not clap; he simply crossed his arms over his chest and spat once into his handkerchief.

Fiona switched off the microphone with a decisive click and turned back to Julian with a radiant, venomous smile.

"You have seven minutes at two o'clock, Julian," she said softly. "Try not to mention Shakespeare. The last time an actor quoted the Bard in this room, two members of the parish council fell into a crate of cooking apples and sprained their wrists."

Fiona Sterling remained upon the wooden dais, watching Julian with a steady, calculating gaze that made Zoe's pulse jump.

Fiona adjusted the papers on her clipboard with three sharp, snapping motions of her fingers. "You haven't changed in twenty-five years, Julian. Still posing by the curtains, still waiting for an ovation from people who are trying to get on with their work."

"I am not posing, Fiona," Julian retorted, stepping onto the stage with his chin lifted. "I am fulfilling a professional engagement contracted by my London management."

"Your London management," Fiona said, the words dripping with honeyed venom, "called me on Wednesday morning begging for an emergency payment to stave off your eviction. Marcus sounded like a terrified schoolboy. He offered to let you judge the pickled eggs if we could throw in an extra fifty pounds for your train fare."

Julian's cheeks burned crimson. "Marcus is an agent, not my biographer. His commercial negotiations do not reflect my personal dignity."

"Your personal dignity," Fiona remarked, glancing down at his patent leather shoes, "is currently covered in slurry from the drainage ditch. And if you think you can turn my vegetable gala into a revival meeting for a cancelled police drama, you are very much mistaken. The judging starts at five o'clock. You will sit quietly at the committee table, you will sign the certificates presented to you by the stewards, and you will not attempt to rewrite the agricultural bylaws."

Zoe stepped between them, her voice calm, bright, and completely steady. "Mrs Sterling, Mr Tremayne is fully prepared to observe all official protocols. If you will direct us to his designated preparation quarters, we will review the presentation timetable in private."

Fiona looked at Zoe, her gray eyes narrowing in sharp appraisal. "Room 14b, Miss Barnes. In the basement beside the boiler. It is the only room in the building where his vocal exercises won't curdle the cream on the trifles."

"Look at that pumpkin, Julian," Zoe murmured, pointing toward the centre dais. "It resembles a sunken naval mine."

"Atlantic class," Julian agreed, squinting through the ambient haze of parish dust. "Notice the ribbing. Reminiscent of the corbelled arches in Lincoln Cathedral."

"It weighs four hundred and twelve pounds," she noted from her schedule. "Grown by an electrical contractor from Bourne who used waste heat from a poultry brooder."

"Ingenious," said Julian. "The rustic mind adapting modern technology to primordial abundance. Shakespeare would have set a comic subplot around such a man."

"Shakespeare would have made sure the contractor was paid in advance," Zoe muttered.

Across the room, Fiona Sterling was already reprimanding an elderly steward who had placed a box of heirloom shallots too close to the public gangway.

"Three inches to the left, George," Fiona commanded. "The fire officer from Grantham specifically designated thirty-six inches of clear thoroughfare. If an alderman trips over your shallots, the insurance excess is two hundred and fifty pounds."

The steward scowled, but he shifted the wooden crate with sullen obedience.

"Fiona has three clipboards," Zoe observed, watching the opposite corner of the pavilion.

"Three?" Julian shuddered slightly.

"Red for health and safety compliance, blue for exhibition regulations, and green for prize fund disbursements."

"In our second year of marriage," Julian recalled in a hushed whisper, "she reorganised my entire collection of classical play scripts by the number of speaking characters in the first act. If a tragedy required more than seven male principals, it was relegated to the bottom drawer of the sideboard on the grounds of theatrical extravagance."

"A very sensible precaution," Zoe remarked. "Think of the costume budget."

"Zoe, it was Chekhov," Julian protested, his hand clutching his lapel. "One does not economise on Chekhov. The Cherry Orchard without the stationmaster and the post office clerk is an aesthetic tragedy."

"The Cherry Orchard without an audience is a financial tragedy, Julian. Fiona understood the box office."

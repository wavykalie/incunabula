# Chapter 32: The Grand Entrance

The Bourne and District Royal British Legion Pipe Band consisted of seven retired servicemen in Gordon tartan kilts, accompanied by a fourteen-year-old snare drummer in gym plimsolls who played with terrifying mechanical energy.

They assembled in the hotel entrance hall beside the umbrella stand at quarter to seven, their drone pipes filling the narrow passage with a deafening, harmonic skirl that caused the chandelier crystals to ring like bicycle bells.

At the head of the procession stood Julian Tremayne.

Julian had discarded his umbrella. He had re-brushed his hair with Zoe's silver comb, straightened his crimson silk pocket square, and buttoned his charcoal waistcoat to the top button. In his arms, held across his chest upon a rectangular cushion of maroon velvet trimmed with gold tassels, rested Arthur Povey's eighty-four-pound champion marrow.

The vegetable was immense, its pale green striations gleaming under the hotel chandeliers, its emergency silver tape suture concealed beneath a decorative sash of royal blue ribbon provided by the Women's Institute. 

Behind Julian walked Arthur Povey, wearing his late wife's best tweed shooting jacket over a clean flannel shirt, his hands clasped behind his back, his chin held high. And behind Povey walked Zoe Barnes, carrying the Society's certified weighing log and the Presentation Challenge Cup.

"March!" shouted the pipe major, a retired postman with a white moustache, bringing his silver-topped mace down with a crack against the parquet.

The bagpipes struck up *The Green Hills of Tyrol*.

The double doors of the Rutland Suite were flung wide by two committee stewards in green armbands.

The sight that met them was extraordinary. Three hundred and fifty people were packed into the hall, standing on chairs, pressing against the baize tables, spilling out into the corridor. The air was thick with the scent of ale, damp tweed, and boiling onions, hot and vibrant with collective communal excitement.

As Julian stepped through the doorway with the heavy vegetable, a roar went up from the crowd that completely drowned the snare drum.

Julian walked down the central aisle with the slow, stately dignity of an emperor presenting a newborn prince to the citizens of Rome. His patent leather shoes, cleaned of the afternoon mud, clicked in perfect time with the pipes. He did not rush; he paused at Table 3 to incline his head toward Harold Finch; he offered a benevolent nod to George Baxter; he smiled with radiant, unvarnished warmth at three elderly ladies who were waving tea towels from the raffle table.

"Look at him," Povey whispered over his shoulder to Zoe, his voice shaking with pride. "He carries it like it was made of solid gold."

"He carries it like it was his own child, Arthur," Zoe replied, smiling despite herself.

They reached the dais. Julian climbed the plywood steps without a single stumble, turned to face the cheering hall, and lowered the maroon cushion onto the presentation table beside the silver trophy.

The pipes ceased on a sharp, unison cutoff that echoed through the rafters.

The crowd remained on its feet, cheering, clapping, stamping their wellingtons against the floorboards until the light fittings swung in gentle arcs from the ceiling.

Julian turned to Fiona Sterling, who was standing beside the microphone in her tweed jacket. He offered her a low, sweeping theatrical bow, his crimson pocket square brushing the velvet cloth.

Fiona looked at him. She looked at the giant marrow on its cushion, then looked out over the three hundred cheering growers who packed her hall.

She tapped her microphone once.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Fiona said, her voice trembling with an emotion that no committee rulebook could suppress. "The guest of honour will now deliver the eighty-second Annual Gala Address."

The procession down the central aisle of the Rutland Suite was an exercise in civic magnificence that Market Deeping would talk about for a generation.

The fourteen-year-old snare drummer in plimsolls rolled his sticks against the skin with deafening, military precision, leading the pipe band past the six rows of green baize tables. Behind the pipes walked Julian, his back as straight as an iron girder, his crimson pocket square glowing against the charcoal wool of his suit.

He held the Emperor upon its velvet cushion with solemn, ceremonial reverence. 

The vegetable seemed to absorb the light of the room, its dark green rind gleaming with moisture, the blue silk sash of the Women's Institute pinned neatly across its dorsal rib like a knight's garter. 

To his left walked Arthur Povey, his eyes shining, his hands clasped behind his back; to his right walked Zoe, carrying the silver Challenge Cup and the official certificate of weight.

As they passed Table 4, three retired railwaymen in flat caps stood up, raising their pint glasses in a silent, solemn toast. At Table 2, George Baxter slapped the table so hard that a bowl of shallots jumped two inches into the air.

"Make way for the champion!" Baxter shouted, his voice echoing above the pipes. "Make way for the Emperor of the Fen!"

Julian inclined his head with the gentle, aristocratic modesty of an Elizabethan admiral returning from the Americas with a Spanish galleon. He did not grin; he offered the crowd a slow, dignified smile of shared provincial triumph that made every allotmenteer feel as if they had placed their own hands upon the spade.

Fiona Sterling stood at the stage steps, her clipboard lowered, watching the procession approach with an expression of quiet, unreadable pride.

Hector Gedge thrust his way through the cheering farmers, his face purple with indignation.

"I demand an immediate tare deduction!" Gedge shouted, waving his rule book. "The lifting sling has not been weighed independently!"

Donald Skelton looked at Gedge over the rims of his brass pince-nez.

"The sling," Skelton said in a voice that flattened all debate, "was tared at fourteen pounds precisely before the chains were hooked. It is recorded in column three of the official weighing book, signed by myself and countersigned by Mrs Sterling."

"The moisture!" Gedge persisted frantically. "The humidity in this hall has added four ounces of water weight to the canvas!"

"Mr Gedge," Fiona Sterling said, stepping between him and the dial, "if we weighed your grievance, it would exceed two hundredweight. Now take your pumpkin to the loading bay. You are obstructing the commemorative photograph."

Arthur Povey stood beside the Avery scale, receiving the congratulations of his fellow growers with laconic nods.

"Good marrow, Arthur," said Harold Finch, offering a rough hand.

"Fair," Arthur conceded. "She took the water well."

"Never thought you'd beat the Littleport record," Finch admitted, shaking his head. "Seventy-six eight. That'll stand for twenty year."

"Soil was warm this September," Arthur said modestly. "And the seed was right."

He caught Zoe's eye across the crowd, giving her a slow, deliberate wink that spoke volumes of shared secrets and veterinary adhesive.

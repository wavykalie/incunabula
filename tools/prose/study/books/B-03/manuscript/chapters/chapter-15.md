# Chapter 15: Rule 14b

The dispute over Rule 14b began at precisely ten minutes to four at Table 6, initiated by Arthur Povey and an eight-inch steel ruler.

By four o'clock, the crowd around the giant marrows had grown four deep. Thirty allotmenteers, six committee stewards in green armbands, and two hotel barmaids holding empty glasses had gathered in a tight, muttering semi-circle around the display trestles.

Fiona Sterling stood in the centre of the circle, her clipboard pressed against her ribs like a breastplate, confronting Arthur Povey across the dorsal ridge of his eighty-four-pound champion marrow.

"Arthur," Fiona said, her voice dropping into the freezing, measured cadence of a magistrate warning a habitual poacher, "the rule is perfectly explicit. Subsection three, paragraph B. The stem must not exceed one inch from the crown of the fruit. Your stem measures one and three-eighths inches."

Povey slammed his steel ruler down upon the green baize with a crack that made the small brass tankards on the next table jump.

"That's fen peat expansion!" Povey roared, his face turning the dark, purplish colour of a pickled beetroot. "The stem was cut at seven-eighths of an inch when we loaded the Emperor into the van at half past eight this morning! It's the humidity in this bloody ballroom! The hot air from your tea boiler has drawn the sap up into the severed vine!"

"The rule makes no provision for barometric expansion, Arthur," Fiona replied, tapping her pencil against the judging form. "One inch is the statutory limit. Any stem exceeding one inch constitutes an artificial moisture reservoir under the South Kesteven bylaws."

"And what about Henderson's entry?" Povey shouted, waving his horn-handled pruning knife toward the service exit. "What about the foreign monster sitting out by the bins in a King's Lynn crate? Have you measured the stem on that, Fiona? Or does young Henderson get an extra half-inch because his firm bought the advertising space on the back of the tea towel?"

A sharp, angry rumble ran through the allotmenteers. 

"Aye, what about the Norfolk crate?" a grower in a tweed cap called out from the back of the crowd. "We saw the horsebox unloading at eleven! King's Lynn ain't in the drainage district!"

Fiona did not blink. She turned her cold, level gaze toward the back of the room, identifying the speaker with instantaneous accuracy. "George Baxter, if you cannot moderate your language in the presence of the exhibitors, the stewards will remove your shallots from Table 2. The Henderson entry has been submitted under the Open Agricultural Innovation category, as approved by the executive committee in nineteen ninety-four."

"Nineteen ninety-four!" Povey spat onto the floorboards. "Your grandfather wrote that clause with a fountain pen in the snug of the Black Bull to let his brother-in-law show a pumpkin from Peterborough! It was corrupt thirty years ago, and it is corrupt now!"

Julian Tremayne stepped through the crowd, parting the allotmenteers with an effortless, patrician authority that made two retired diesel mechanics instinctively touch their caps.

He had removed his overcoat. His charcoal three-piece suit was immaculate; his silk tie sat centered in his stiff collar; his patent leather shoes, cleaned and polished by Zoe in the corridor, caught the fluorescent glare of the room.

"Madam Chairman," Julian boomed, his baritone slicing cleanly through the rising argument. "If I may be permitted an observation from the perspective of procedural natural justice?"

Fiona's pencil paused on her clipboard. She stared at Julian with an expression of sharp, dangerous amusement. "Julian, this is a technical agricultural adjudication. It does not require a soliloquy."

"Natural justice is not a soliloquy, Fiona," Julian replied, stepping to the side of Table 6 and resting his fingertips lightly upon the timber edge. "It is the bedrock of British civil society. In twenty-five years of judicial and investigative roles upon the screen, I have observed that when a rule is ambiguous, the law invariably favours the defendant. If Mr Povey's stem has expanded due to atmospheric heating provided by the venue, the liability rests not with the cultivator, but with the management of the George and Dragon."

A murmur of vigorous approval ran through the crowd. Old Artie looked at Julian with sudden, astonished respect.

"Hear, hear!" shouted George Baxter from the back. "The actor is right! The boiler has been leaking steam all afternoon!"

Julian turned his gaze toward Fiona, offering a gentle, devastatingly polite bow.

"May I propose an equitable compromise, Madam Chairman?" Julian said smoothly. "Let the official calipers measure the stem at five o'clock, immediately prior to the hydraulic weigh-in. Should the stem still exceed the statutory inch, Mr Povey shall have ninety seconds to trim the excess with his certified blade under the direct supervision of the guest of honour."

Fiona stared at him. Her lips pressed into a thin, white line. For the first time that afternoon, Zoe saw her ex-wife's absolute administrative control waver under the sheer weight of public opinion.

"Very well," Fiona said through her teeth, making a sharp pencil mark on her sheet. "Preliminary measurement deferred to quarter to five. But if that knife touches the rind, Arthur, you are out."

She turned on her heel and strode toward the dais, her clipboard held tight against her ribs.

Julian turned to Old Artie, who was staring at him with his mouth slightly open. Julian offered the old grower a solemn, confidential wink.

"Keep the blade sharp, Mr Povey," Julian murmured. "Dalrymple never lets a corrupt committee tamper with the evidence."

The debate around Table 6 had drawn in the society's honorary treasurer, a retired bank manager named Mr Horace Clough, who wore a knitted cardigan beneath his tweed coat and carried an enormous leather-bound ledger dating back to nineteen seventy-one.

"Arthur," Clough pleaded, adjusting his bifocals as Povey brandished his steel ruler. "We must adhere to the precedent of the nineteen ninety-one cabbage dispute. When George Baxter's drumhead cabbage exceeded the certified diameter by half an inch, the executive committee permitted a five-minute trimming interval prior to scale placement."

"A drumhead cabbage ain't a sixty-pound marrow, Horace!" Povey barked, his face red with peat dust and fury. "A marrow has skin tension! If you trim a marrow stem while the fruit is warm, the sap bleeds out and the internal hydraulic pressure drops! It's like cutting the umbilical cord on a newborn lamb!"

Julian Tremayne nodded in profound agreement, stepping closer to the table and resting his hands upon his waistcoat. "A brilliant physiological analogy, Mr Povey. The vascular integrity of the vine must be respected. In forensic pathology, one never interferes with an open incision until the post-mortem weighing has been completed."

The growers murmured in deep admiration. "Listen to him," whispered an allotmenteer in the second row. "The detective knows his pathology."

Fiona Sterling tapped her clipboard against the timber table with a sharp, warning crack. "Julian, if you refer to an agricultural squash as a post-mortem specimen once more in this room, I will hold you in contempt of the society committee."

Julian bowed with devastating theatrical politeness. "Madam Chairman, truth has no contempt in the court of natural equity."

Inside the VIP marquee, a paraffin heater in the corner emitted a soft roar and the sharp smell of burning kerosene.

"Try the quiche, Julian," Zoe suggested, handing him a paper plate.

"What variety of quiche?"

"Cheese and onion," she said. "Made by the vicar's sister."

Julian inspected the yellow wedge with dramatic suspicion. "It possesses the consistency of chilled linoleum, Zoe. An actor cannot project his voice through twenty minutes of compressed cheddar."

"Then drink the ginger beer," Zoe said, handing him a small stone bottle. "Arthur Povey is waiting for us outside."

Across the marquee, Fiona was engaged in a brisk administrative audit of the raffle ticket stubs.

"Three books missing from the butcher's batch," she told the treasurer, marking her ledger with red pencil. "Tell Mrs Briggs that if she cannot account for the forty-two pounds by three o'clock, the society will withhold her sister's sponge cake from the prize enclosure."

The VIP marquee was steadily filling with local dignitaries, parish councillors, and elderly women in tweed skirts and pearl necklaces.

"Julian," a shrill voice called out from the sandwich buffet.

Julian stiffened, his theatrical radar instantly registering a high-status approach.

A lady in a purple felt hat adorned with a pheasant feather bore down upon them, balancing a glass of sweet sherry in one gloved hand.

"Lady Boston," Julian exclaimed, his face breaking into an exquisite smile of aristocratic deference. "How utterly delightful. You have not aged a day since we met at the Lincoln Cathedral appeal in ninety-four."

"Nonsense, Julian," Lady Boston laughed, a sound like gravel sliding down a corrugated iron roof. "I am seventy-eight and my hips are made of Birmingham titanium. But I wouldn't miss Arthur Povey's weigh-in for the world. My father always said that a giant marrow was the truest test of an Englishman's character."

"Your father was a gentleman of profound agricultural discernment," Julian affirmed, kissing the tips of her suede fingers.

Across the room, Fiona watched the exchange with narrow, calculating eyes, her pencil poised above the luncheon vouchers ledger.

In the corner of the VIP marquee, Zoe found herself cornered by the honorary treasurer of the society, a retired bank manager named Mr Percival Clout.

"Miss Barnes," Clout said, peering at her over his half-moon spectacles while stirring a plastic cup of lukewarm instant coffee with a wooden tongue depressor. "I have examined the voucher submissions from your London agency."

"Is there an irregularity, Mr Clout?" Zoe asked with polite, professional firmness.

"The claim for first-class rail travel," Clout said, tapping a red line on his ledger. "Society bylaws restrict visiting artistes to standard off-peak return fares, unless an explicit medical certificate is provided attesting to an incapacity that prevents carriage in second class."

Zoe extracted Julian's booking rider from her leather folder with the precision of a surgeon opening an instrument roll.

"Section Four, clause B, Mr Clout," she read calmly. "Where a celebrity guest is required to appear in theatrical costume or tailored formalwear immediately upon arrival, the host organisation undertakes to provide first-class accommodation to prevent creasing of the garments."

Clout frowned, leaning forward to inspect the small print.

"Tailored formalwear?" he muttered. "He's wearing an overcoat and a woollen scarf."

"Anderson and Sheppard charcoal cashmere, nineteen ninety-one," Zoe stated without blinking. "Value two thousand four hundred pounds. If Julian sits in a second-class compartment next to a basket of live leeks, the pressing cost alone is forty-five pounds. Shall I bill the society for the London valet service?"

Clout swallowed dryly, his pencil retreating. "That won't be necessary, Miss Barnes. The voucher is approved."

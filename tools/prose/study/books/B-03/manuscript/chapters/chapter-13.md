# Chapter 13: Tea and Sandwiches

The ladies' cloakroom on the first floor of the George and Dragon had not been refurbished since the silver jubilee of nineteen seventy-seven. 

The walls were tiled in avocado green ceramic, the taps on the porcelain basins required three complete turns to produce a trickle of lukewarm water that smelled of iron, and the hand dryer sounded like a jet engine preparing for takeoff while emitting air of ambient room temperature.

Zoe stood before the mirror above the middle basin, applying a fresh layer of neutral lip balm. 

The bathroom door swung open with a violent click of the latch, and Fiona Sterling swept into the room. She carried her mahogany clipboard under her arm, having discarded her brass calipers into her tweed jacket pocket. She approached the basin next to Zoe's, set the clipboard down upon the ceramic rim, and turned on the tap with a single, authoritative snap of her wrist.

"Cucumber or paste, Miss Barnes?" Fiona asked, looking at Zoe's reflection in the glass.

Zoe capped her lip balm. "I beg your pardon, Mrs Sterling?"

"The sandwiches on the sideboard in the lounge," Fiona said, lathering her hands with a cake of pink institutional carbolic soap. "The white bread contains processed sandwich spread with chopped pimento; the brown contains cucumber with margarine. I strongly recommend avoiding the egg mayonnaise. The kitchen staff prepared it at nine this morning, and the tray has been sitting beside the heating radiator since midday."

"Thank you for the warning," Zoe said. "I have antacids in my handbag."

"Sensible girl," Fiona murmured. She rinsed her hands, dried them on a continuous roller towel that gave a reluctant screech of its wooden ratchet, and turned to face Zoe directly. Her pale gray eyes were steady, sharp, and entirely devoid of municipal pleasantry. "Tell me the truth, Miss Barnes. How bad are his finances?"

Zoe maintained her neutral agency expression. "Mr Tremayne's professional affairs are managed by Apex Talent in Soho, Mrs Sterling. I handle his daily diary and travel logistics."

"Do not insult my intelligence, my dear," Fiona said softly. "I was married to Julian Briggs for sixteen years. I know the exact correlation between the volume of his baritone and the state of his bank account. When he has money, he speaks in a pleasant, conversational tenor and buys champagne for strangers in railway buffet cars. When he is facing bankruptcy, he booms classical verse and quotes King Lear at chambermaids."

She took a compact silver powder case from her handbag and inspected her nose in the tiny circular mirror.

"Marcus from Apex rang me on Tuesday," Fiona continued, patting her cheek with a velour puff. "He offered to reduce Julian's appearance fee from three thousand guineas to two thousand five hundred pounds cash on the nail, provided we transferred the funds directly to an escrow account marked *Gidley Residential Holdings*. Gidley is his landlord in Maida Vale, isn't he?"

Zoe looked at the avocado tiles. "I am not authorized to discuss client transactions, Mrs Sterling."

"He hasn't paid his service charge since March," Fiona said, snapping the powder case shut with a sharp click that made Zoe blink. "He owes five thousand pounds on his American Express card. And his agent is currently trying to book him to open a carpet showroom in Scunthorpe next Saturday for four hundred pounds and a discount voucher for vinyl flooring."

Fiona leaned against the porcelain basin, folding her arms over her tweed jacket. The sharp, mocking amusement that usually played around her lips had vanished, replaced by an expression of cold, weary familiarity.

"He never changed, did he?" Fiona said, her voice dropping into a tone that was almost gentle. "He spent ten years believing that playing a detective in a muddy coat on ITV made him Laurence Olivier. He mortgaged my grandfather's cottage in nineteen ninety-eight so he could take an absurd one-man show about Lord Byron to the Edinburgh Fringe. We had twelve people in the audience on opening night, Miss Barnes. Six of them were German tourists who thought they were queuing for a ghost tour of the Royal Mile."

"He takes great pride in his craft, Mrs Sterling," Zoe said quietly.

"His craft is vanity," Fiona replied, picking up her clipboard from the basin. "And vanity is an extraordinarily expensive luxury when you are fifty-four years old and your hair is gray at the roots. If you have any sense, Miss Barnes, you will take the five o'clock train back to King's Cross and let him face the bailiffs alone. You are thirty years old. You have your whole life ahead of you. Why spend your youth carrying water for a ghost?"

She turned toward the door, paused with her hand on the brass handle, and looked back over her shoulder.

"The weigh-in starts at quarter to five," Fiona added, the magistrate's edge returning to her voice. "Tell him that if he attempts to use his detective voice during the official circumference measurement, I will have the stewards escort him to the car park."

Zoe emerged from the ladies' cloakroom and walked down the carpeted gallery overlooking the hotel lounge.

Below her, through the timber balustrade, she could see Fiona Sterling standing at the reception desk, conferring with two elderly stewards in green armbands. Fiona held a small pocket magnifying glass to a sheet of carbon paper, checking the entries with the surgical precision that had made her the most feared magistrate on the South Kesteven bench.

Zoe leaned against the mahogany rail, watching the woman who had shared Julian's life during the glory years of television fame.

Fiona was formidable, sharp-tongued, and armed with forty years of municipal bylaws. Yet behind the arctic efficiency, Zoe had caught that fleeting glimpse of vulnerability in the bathroom: the memory of twelve people in a drafty Edinburgh basement, the unpaid laundry invoices, the bitterness of being left behind while an actor chased the fading phantom of London applause.

Julian had not simply left Fiona; he had left her for an audience that had abandoned him five years later.

She pulled out her phone, checking the signal bar. Still dead. In her handbag, the blister pack of antacids had only three tablets remaining. She popped one into her mouth, chewing the chalky cherry tablet as she watched the rain lash the tall sash windows of the lounge.

"Mr Tremayne?"

A plump gentleman in a grey pinstripe suit and an ornate silver chain of office extended a fleshy hand.

"Julian Tremayne," Julian replied, giving the hand a warm, theatrical clasp.

"Councillor Derek Hoddinott," the gentleman announced, puffing out his chest. "Chairman of the South Kesteven Amenities and Drainage Sub-Committee. We are delighted you could spare the time from your television commitments. My wife watches you every Tuesday evening on the weather forecast."

Julian's smile froze for a fraction of a second before recovering its patrician lustre. "A common solecism, Councillor. You are perhaps thinking of Michael Fish. I am an actor. I portrayed Detective Chief Inspector Dalrymple for Central Television."

"Of course, of course," Councillor Hoddinott laughed heartily, completely unchastened. "The one who always knew when the depression was moving in from the Wash! Wonderful graphics on that show."

Zoe stepped between them with professional agility. "The Councillor is needed at the marrow pavilion for the civic blessing, Mr Tremayne. We must not detain him."

"Councillor Hoddinott is returning," Zoe whispered, spotting the silver chain of office advancing past the potato exhibits.

"Quick," Julian murmured. "Direct me toward a complex botanical specimen."

"Stand before this giant cabbage," Zoe instructed. "Look intensely analytical."

Julian clasped his chin between his thumb and forefinger, bending his tall frame over a cabbage the size of a beer keg.

"Marvellous," Councillor Hoddinott boomed, arriving at Julian's flank with a hearty slap on the shoulder. "Admiring the Savoy, Mr Tremayne? Grown by my brother-in-law in Thorney. Won the cup in ninety-three."

"A magnificent specimen, Councillor," Julian said, turning with slow, scholarly gravity. "Notice the intricate venation of the outer leaves. The geometric precision of the crinkles. Reminiscent of a topographical map of the Pennines."

The Councillor stared at the cabbage, completely bewildered. "Well, yes. Good with a bit of boiled ham, too."

"Indubitably," Julian agreed with solemn courtesy. "The culinary destiny of the brassica."

# Chapter 07: Cucurbita Pepo

To understand why eighty adult men and women were prepared to commit perjury, sabotage, and physical assault in the Rutland Suite on a rainy Saturday in October, one had to consult Section 4 of the British Giant Vegetable Growers Association Handbook.

The common marrow, known botanically as *Cucurbita pepo*, is an unassuming summer squash. Left to its own devices in a suburban back garden, it achieves a modest weight of two or three pounds, suitable for stuffing with minced lamb and baking until watery and regretful. 

In competitive British horticulture, however, the marrow ceases to be a vegetable and becomes an unstable hydraulic pressure vessel.

Through selective cross-pollination of heirloom seeds dating back to the late nineteenth century, growers in the peat fens had bred cultivars whose cellular walls were capable of absorbing immense quantities of water without rupturing. A competitive specimen did not grow; it inflated. Fed with high-potash fertilizers, liquid sheep manure, and secret brews of seaweed extract piped directly to the vine roots via medical drip lines, an exhibition marrow could gain up to three pounds in twenty-four hours during the August heat.

At sixty pounds, a marrow ceased to be classified under domestic agricultural produce. According to the George and Dragon's commercial liability insurance policy, a copy of which hung in a clip frame beside the payphone in the front lobby, any agricultural specimen exceeding fifty pounds in weight was legally categorized under Section 3b as "unanchored masonry, heavy ballast, or falling projectile."

The management of the George and Dragon had therefore insisted upon three strict conditions before allowing the Rutland Suite to host the weigh-in:

First, all exhibits weighing over forty pounds had to be transported into the building on four-wheeled pneumatic trolleys rather than carried by hand, following a 2018 incident in which a retired railway signalman from Spalding dropped an eighty-two-pound butternut squash onto his own metatarsals.

Second, the weighing apparatus had to be certified by the local weights and measures inspector from Lincoln, with the calibration weights stamped by the Board of Trade.

Third, and most crucially, the hotel required the presence of an impartial guest of honour to witness the balance arm and countersign the certificate of girth.

This was Julian's true constitutional role. He had not been hired to deliver a stirring cultural oration on the British theatre. He had been engaged as an ornamental human notary, whose signature on the blue certificate would indemnify the committee against civil litigation if two allotmenteers came to blows over a split rind.

Zoe leaned against the radiator in the basement corridor, reading through the laminated society bylaws that Fiona had thrust into her hand on the stairs. 

Rule 14b occupied three densely typed pages:

*14b (i): Any marrow exhibiting surface discoloration, soft spots exceeding one quarter of an inch in diameter, or signs of post-harvest syringe injection shall be disqualified forthwith by the Chair of Judges.*

*14b (ii): The stem of the exhibit must be severed cleanly at a distance of not more than one inch from the crown. Any stem found to be weighted with lead shot, quicksilver, or paraffin wax shall result in a lifetime suspension from all regional galas.*

*14b (iii): In the event of a tie within four ounces, the winner shall be determined by cross-sectional circumference measured at the widest point of the dorsal axis using the Society's certified brass calipers.*

Zoe closed the booklet. She took another antacid tablet from her pocket, cracked it between her molars, and listened to the distant sound of eighty voices arguing about the density of fenland peat. 

In London, her friends from university were currently sitting in Scandinavian coffee shops in Soho, discussing script development deals, quarterly streaming metrics, and executive production assistants' retreats in Suffolk. 

Here, thirty miles south of the Wash, a seventy-one-year-old retired diesel fitter with mud in his eyebrows was prepared to shed blood over a sixty-pound gourd. And her job was to ensure that a vain fifty-four-year-old actor did not knock it off the table before his landlord cashed the rent cheque.

The history of the Market Deeping Produce Society, as recorded in the centenary booklet Zoe found on the radiator shelf, was a catalogue of bitter civil litigation disguised as vegetable exhibitions.

In nineteen twenty-four, the entire committee had resigned following an accusation that a former churchwarden had hollowed out a prize turnip, filled the cavity with molten lead, and sealed the top with warm beeswax before the weigh-in. The dispute had reached the regional assizes in Lincoln, resulting in a three-day trial that cost the parish council forty-seven pounds in legal fees.

In nineteen fifty-eight, a grower from Crowland had been caught piping water from a tractor radiator directly into the main taproot of a giant pumpkin through a rubber bicycle valve, earning a five-year suspension and the confiscation of his brass challenge medals.

The regulations, therefore, had evolved into a legal fortress designed to repel every conceivable form of horticultural fraud.

Rule 14b alone occupied forty-two sub-clauses:

*14b (iv): The use of mechanical support slings, plaster of Paris cradles, or timber splints during the forty-eight hours immediately preceding the official weigh-in is strictly prohibited, save where certified in writing by the Society Medical Officer.*

*14b (v): Any marrow found to have absorbed moisture via external capillary immersion—including the wrapping of the dorsal surface in wet wool, damp peat moss, or soaked blankets—shall be penalized at the discretion of the Chair of Judges.*

Zoe closed the booklet and slipped it into her pocket. The allotmenteers of South Kesteven were not amateur gardeners enjoying a harmless weekend hobby. They were legal gladiators who had spent eighty years probing every loophole in the bylaws with the tenacity of corporate tax lawyers.

And into this nest of agricultural litigation, Marcus had deposited an unemployed television actor with an unpaid rent bill and a penchant for quoting Shakespeare.

Julian leaned close to Zoe's ear as they approached the committee table.

"Did you observe Fiona's boots, Zoe?"

"Sturdy leather walking shoes," Zoe answered. "Entirely practical for a stone floor."

"Grenson country brogues," Julian corrected in a hushed, tragic whisper. "I bought her that exact pair in nineteen eighty-three for our honeymoon in the Lake District. She wore them when we climbed Helvellyn in a freezing drizzle. She did not complain once during the descent, but she timed every rest stop with a silver stopwatch."

"A woman after my own heart," Zoe said.

"She kept a written record of every cup of tea we purchased in Keswick," Julian added mournfully. "She calculated that we spent seven percent more on hot water than the national tourist average."

"And did you?"

"Undoubtedly," Julian confessed. "I insisted upon the tea rooms with linen napkins."

"Look at the church tower through the skylight, Zoe," Julian said, standing beneath the timber rafters.

"Fourteenth century," Zoe noted from the guidebook. "Perpendicular style, with an octagonal lantern added in sixteen eighty."

"In seventy-eight," Julian said, his baritone echoing softly in the rafters, "we performed Murder in the Cathedral in Peterborough. I was the Second Tempter. During the afternoon performance, a pigeon flew into the lantern above the crossing and dropped a feather directly onto the Archbishop's cope just as I delivered my opening strophe. The house held its breath for three seconds. You could hear the mist coming off the river through the clerestory windows."

"Did you drop your lines?"

"Never," Julian declared with theatrical pride. "I caught the feather on the back of my glove and incorporated it into the text. 'The worldly honours fall like plumage from the sky,' I improvised. The archdeacon commended me in the diocesan gazette."

"An archdeacon with low standards of ecclesiastical drama," Zoe murmured.

"A gentleman of discerning taste, Zoe," Julian corrected with a smile.

# Chapter 30: The Final Call

Zoe was on her feet before Povey had finished speaking, leaving her beer untouched upon the table.

She followed the old grower through the service passage at a rapid march, her heels clicking against the linoleum. "Where is Julian?" she demanded.

"He's by the stage," Povey panted, his breath whistling in his chest. "Signing napkins for the ladies from the WI. He don't know what's happening out back."

They reached the double doors of the kitchen service corridor. 

The cold draft from the delivery lane was blowing fiercely through the open emergency exit, smelling of diesel fumes and wet clay. Standing on the concrete apron beside the timber transport pallet was young Henderson, his baseball cap pulled down low over his brow.

In his right hand, Henderson held an eighteen-inch cast-iron sash weight, looped with a length of greasy sash cord. His face was pale with drunken, humiliated fury.

Standing between Henderson and the timber crate that held the Emperor was Julian Tremayne.

Julian had not waited for Zoe. He stood upon the wet concrete in his patent leather shoes, his charcoal jacket unbuttoned, holding his large black umbrella with the crook handle tucked under his arm like a cavalry sword.

"Put the iron down, young man," Julian said, his baritone quiet, steady, and utterly devoid of theatrical affectation.

"Get out of my way, actor," Henderson snarled, taking a threatening step forward onto the pallet. "That old bastard cheated. His marrow split on the floor. A split marrow ain't legal in Kesteven, and everybody knows it. If the committee won't enforce the bylaws, I'll smash the bloody thing to pulp myself."

He raised the sash weight above his shoulder, the heavy iron cylinder glistening in the rain.

Julian did not flinch. He did not step backward into the shelter of the doorway. He took one step forward, planting his polished Oxford firmly into a puddle of gravel slurry, and pointed the steel ferrule of his umbrella directly at Henderson's sternum.

"I spent five years playing a police officer on television, Mr Henderson," Julian said softly. "I have worked with real detectives from the Lincolnshire constabulary. I know that an assault with an offensive weapon upon commercial premises carries a custodial sentence of eighteen months at Lincoln Castle."

Henderson hesitated, his arm trembling in the air. "I'm not assaulting you. I'm smashing a vegetable."

"That vegetable," Julian replied, his voice rising with formidable moral authority, "is the property of Arthur Povey. It has been certified by the Board of Trade. It has been blessed by the community. And if you swing that iron, you will have to strike me first."

For four agonizing seconds, the only sound was the drumming of the rain on the corrugated roof of the tractor shed.

Zoe stepped out onto the concrete apron, holding her phone aloft with the camera light illuminated.

"I am recording this, Mr Henderson," Zoe said, her voice cutting through the damp air like a whip. "The file is uploading directly to the Lincolnshire Police digital evidence portal. You have five seconds to lower the iron before I transmit the location coordinates."

Young Henderson looked from the steel tip of Julian's umbrella to the glowing lens of Zoe's phone. 

The drunken bravado drained from his eyes, leaving behind only the petulant, sulking shame of a spoiled young man whose money had failed to purchase a victory.

With a muffled curse, he dropped the sash weight onto the gravel, turned on his heel, and stalked away toward the white Transit van parked under the willows.

Julian let out a long, slow breath. He lowered the umbrella, leaned upon the handle, and looked down at his ruined shoes.

"A creditable piece of improvisation, Miss Barnes," Julian murmured, his lips trembling with a faint, nervous smile. "Though I suspect your phone battery expired twenty minutes ago."

Zoe looked at her black screen. "Twenty-five minutes ago, Julian," she said. "Now let us go inside. The pipe band is tuning up in the lobby, and they are waiting for you to lead the procession."

In the delivery lane behind the tractor shed, the rain was turning to sleet, slanting through the beam of the porch lantern in fine, stinging needles.

Julian stood upon the wet concrete, watching young Henderson's white Transit van reverse out of the yard, its red brake lights illuminating the dripping willows before it accelerated down the lane toward the bypass.

Julian turned to Arthur Povey, who was leaning against the timber transport pallet, his face pale with the after-effects of the confrontation.

"The lane is clear, Arthur," Julian said gently. "The young man has departed for Norfolk."

Povey looked at Julian's umbrella, which was dripping water onto his ruined patent leather shoes. "You stood in front of that iron weight, Julian. He could have taken your head off."

Julian gave a modest, patrician shrug, though his hands were trembling slightly as he folded the umbrella fabric. "A minor professional hazard, Arthur. In ninety-nine, during the filming of the shipyard riots in Boston, a stuntman threw a real pewter tankard that missed my left temple by three inches. The director wanted to stop the take, but I delivered my lines to the final syllable. One does not flinch before an amateur."

Povey reached out, his massive, peat-stained hand clamping down upon Julian's shoulder with crushing affection.

"You're a true champion, Julian Briggs," Povey said softly. "The fen will never forget this night."

Donald Skelton stood before the Avery scale, gripping the iron hoist crank with two gnarled hands.

"Class Four!" Skelton bellowed, his voice vibrating through the iron roof girders. "Heavy Marrow Open Championship! Exhibit Number Seven, Arthur Povey of Deeping Fen!"

Two stewards in white butcher's coats stepped forward, carrying the broad canvas lifting sling.

"Easy under the belly, lads," Arthur warned, hovering over the vegetable like an anxious mother. "Keep the sling centered on the third rib. Don't let her tip."

Julian stepped down from the dais to take up a position beside Zoe, his hands trembling slightly inside his cashmere pockets.

"This is the moment of crisis, Zoe," he whispered. "The curtain is rising on the final scene."

"Keep your fingers crossed, Julian," Zoe said quietly. "If that surgical bond gives way under seventy pounds of tension, we are all going to be covered in raw squash."

"Seventy-six pounds, eight ounces!" Donald Skelton's announcement thundered through the rafters.

The cheer that followed shook the glass panes in the clerestory windows.

Men cheered, clapped, and stamped their heavy boots upon the floorboards until the dust rose in clouds beneath the spotlights.

Julian threw both arms into the air, his baritone joining the roar with unrestrained theatrical joy.

"Victory!" he shouted. "Victory for Arthur Povey! Victory for the Fens!"

Beside him, Zoe smiled, a warm, deep feeling of relief washing through her tired limbs.

The fee was won. The vegetable was intact. The opening act had reached its triumphant resolution.

# Chapter 19: Cold Coffee at the Desk

At ten past five, Zoe retreated to the small timber desk in the corner of the reception hall to organize Julian's departure notes and expense receipts.

Upon the desk sat a cardboard cup of cold hotel coffee, a crumpled packet of paper tissues, and a leather-bound diary with brass corner protectors that someone had left beside the room reservation book.

The diary was bound in dark green morocco leather, its spine stamped in gold lettering: F. BRIGGS, ENGAGEMENTS 2004.

Zoe paused with her gel pen. She looked toward the hallway; the reception desk was empty, Kelly having departed to the staff kitchen to fetch fresh tea bags. 

Zoe touched the green leather. The cover fell open easily, its pages dog-eared and softened by twenty years of handling.

Tucked into the pocket of the front endpaper lay a folded newspaper clipping from the Lincolnshire Echo, dated October 1999. The headline read: LOCAL STAR BRINGS TV GLAMOUR TO REGIONAL SHOWS, DI DALRYMPLE VISITS BOURNE.

Beside the text sat a black-and-white photograph of Julian, thirty-seven years old, standing on the steps of the Bourne Corn Exchange in a double-breasted tweed suit, his arm draped affectionately around the shoulders of a radiant thirty-year-old Fiona Sterling, who was laughing into the camera while holding a presentation bouquet of yellow chrysanthemums.

Below the clipping, in Fiona's precise, elegant handwriting, was a single diary entry written on the evening of the nineteen ninety-nine gala:

Julian magnificent on the platform tonight. Two hundred people in the hall, all hanging on his every word. He forgot the name of the parish clerk, but covered it with an anecdote about Olivier that had everyone in stitches. We had fish and chips in the car on the way back through the fen, laughing about the giant parsnips until our sides ached. He says next year he will take me to the television awards in London.

Zoe stared at the faded ink. 

The image of Fiona Sterling, the terrifying magistrate with the brass calipers and the frozen smile, sitting in a damp car eating chips with a younger, laughing Julian was so completely at odds with the afternoon's hostility that Zoe felt an odd, unexpected catch in her throat.

Beneath the newspaper clipping lay a receipt from a jeweller's shop in Stamford, dated August 2008: To engraving silver challenge cup: Julian Briggs, in recognition of fifteen years of patience with the cabbages, twelve pounds and fifty pence.

Zoe turned the paper over. The date on the receipt coincided exactly with the year Julian's agent had announced his transition from television drama to regional theatre.

A sound of footsteps made her look up. 

Fiona Sterling stood in the archway of the hall, her coat collar turned up against the draft, holding a fresh pack of clipboard forms. Her eyes dropped immediately to the green diary on the desk.

For four seconds, neither woman spoke.

Zoe did not close the book. She simply took a sip of her cold coffee and looked at Fiona with quiet, steady professional neutrality.

Fiona walked to the desk, picked up the green diary with two fingers, and slipped it into the deep pocket of her tweed coat. Her face was completely impassive, though a faint, momentary softness lingered around the corners of her eyes.

"The scales are in position, Miss Barnes," Fiona said quietly. "Tell Julian that if he wishes to inspect the balance arm, he should do so now."

Zoe sat at the timber reception desk in the quiet lobby, holding the green morocco diary in her hands for a moment longer before closing the cover.

The handwriting on the yellowed pages told a story that no public relations release could ever convey: sixteen years of a marriage between an ambitious provincial girl and a handsome young actor who had briefly touched the heights of television stardom. 

There were entries recording small domestic triumphs: *Julian booked for the regional radio play in Leeds, twenty-five pounds and travel expenses*; *Dinner at the Italian restaurant in Stamford to celebrate his third audition for Dalrymple*; *Julian bought me a velvet scarf for my birthday with his first ITV payment*.

And then, as the pages advanced toward two thousand and eight, the entries grew sparser, shorter, and darker with unacknowledged sorrow: *Julian spent the afternoon staring at the telephone in the hallway*; *The agency says the series will not be renewed for a sixth season*; *Julian refused to attend the parish dinner because he said people would pity him*.

Zoe felt the heavy silence of the old coaching inn settling around her like a blanket.

She had always viewed Julian's vanity as a professional nuisance: an irritating obstacle of hair dye, suit lapels, and demands for luxury water that she had to manage on a daily basis. But reading Fiona's diary, she understood that his vanity was not an act of arrogance; it was a desperate, fragile wall constructed to keep out the terrifying cold of obscurity.

She heard Fiona's footsteps approaching from the archway and looked up, her expression calm, respectful, and completely neutral.

In the pale beam of Zoe's torch, the defect was unmistakable.

A hairline split, barely three inches long, ran along the lower curve of the belly, where the weight of the marrow pressed against the pallet slats. A tiny droplet of pale amber sap was slowly welling from the crack.

"God almighty," Arthur breathed, his face draining of colour beneath his weathered tan. "She's bursting her girth."

"Can it be stopped?" Zoe asked, keeping her voice low and calm.

"If Fiona sees that split, she'll write disqualified on the card before the chains are round her," Arthur said, his rough hands trembling against his smock. "Rule fourteen: any exhibit showing structural puncture or liquid seepage is excluded from competition."

Julian stood above them, his expression shifting from detached amusement to genuine, urgent distress. "Rule fourteen is a bureaucratic technicality, Arthur. The vegetable is intact in all its essential moral dimensions."

"Rule fourteen don't care about morals," Arthur choked out. "Rule fourteen is Fiona Sterling."

"The inspection team is approaching," Arthur hissed.

Fiona Sterling led the way, flanked by the weights and measures inspector and two burly stewards from the South Holland district.

"Exhibit Number Seven," Fiona announced, consulting her blue clipboard. "Arthur Povey. Certified local entry. Class Four Open."

The inspector, a thin man in a grey dust coat with a steel tape measure hanging from his belt, knelt beside the Emperor.

He tapped the rind with the knuckle of his middle finger. A dull, heavy, resonant thud echoed through the timber pallet.

"Sound," the inspector reported, writing in his notebook. "No hollow resonance. Rind intact. Clean stem cut at the second node."

Arthur held his breath, his knuckles white against his canvas smock.

"Check the base, Mr Briggs," Fiona instructed.

The inspector slid his steel rule beneath the rubber shock pads, checking for concealed metal weights or illegal ballast. The rule slid freely from end to end without obstruction.

"Clear," the inspector said, standing up. "Platform hoist approved."

Zoe exhaled, feeling the tension release in her shoulders like a drawn bowstring relaxing.

# Chapter 29: Stale Bitter and Pretzels

At twenty minutes past six, while the ballroom was engulfed in the deafening celebrations of the post-judging raffle, Zoe slipped away to the empty resident lounge at the front of the hotel.

She sat in a deep, sagging armchair upholstered in brown floral velour beside a cold stone hearth. 

Upon the small glass-topped coffee table before her sat a half-pint glass of flat commercial bitter, a small bowl of stale salted pretzels that had lost their crispness in the damp weather, and her leather notebook, open to a clean white page.

She had survived.

She reviewed the afternoon's ledger in her mind with the cold, methodical precision of an accountant:

Julian's dignity had been preserved. The five-thousand-pound contract clawback had been neutralized. The bank transfer to Mr Gidley was safe. Arthur Povey had won his championship with an authentic seventy-five-pound vegetable, and Fiona Sterling had achieved the most widely publicized agricultural gala in the eighty-two-year history of the society.

And who had achieved this?

Not Marcus, who was currently sitting in a wine bar in Covent Garden sending automated emojis to casting assistants. Not Julian, who had spent the first two hours hiding in a boiler room whining about the absence of seedless grapes.

She had done it.

Zoe Barnes, thirty years old, who had spent five years believing that her role in life was to apologize for difficult men, had managed a room of eighty hostile farmers, negotiated with a formidable county magistrate, performed emergency surgical triage on an eighty-pound marrow, and out-maneuvered a corporate agricultural conglomerate.

She looked at her hands. Her cuticles were stained with a faint trace of silver duct tape adhesive; her fingertips smelled faintly of peat and cherry antacid tablets.

She reached into her bag, pulled out the brown paper envelope containing Arthur Povey's 1984 champion seed line, and laid it upon the glass table beside her beer.

Beside it, she placed her personal iPad. 

She unlocked the screen and opened the file that had sat untouched in her documents folder for eighteen months: *THE ASSISTANT — A COMEDY OF MANNERS IN THREE ACTS — BY ZOE BARNES*.

She read the opening scene. It was sharp, funny, and brimming with the absurd, painful truth of contemporary metropolitan ambition. She had shelved it because Marcus had told her that nobody wanted to read plays about executive assistants; they wanted high-concept thrillers about international hedge funds.

Marcus was an idiot.

Zoe took a sip of the stale bitter. It was lukewarm and slightly sour, but as it slid down her throat, she felt a sudden, magnificent sense of sovereign clarity that she had not experienced since leaving university.

She was not going to spend the next five years carrying luggage for an aging television detective. She was going to see Julian through this weekend, ensure his dignity remained intact, and then she was going to resign.

The door from the corridor opened, and Arthur Povey stuck his gray head into the lounge.

"Miss Barnes?" he whispered. "You'd better come back into the hall. Young Henderson is out by the fire exit with a sash weight, and he's looking at the Emperor."

In the quiet resident lounge, Zoe sat back in her floral armchair, listening to the rain drumming against the bay window.

She looked at her iPad screen. The opening scene of *The Assistant* was staring back at her, its sharp, rhythmic dialogue capturing the exact absurdity of the world she had lived in for five years:

*ACT I, SCENE 1: An executive office in Soho. A desk covered in mineral water bottles, color-coded itineraries, and unpaid dry-cleaning receipts. A young woman with an upper second-class degree sits at the telephone, apologizing to a theatrical producer for the third time before eleven o'clock.*

She picked up her gel pen, crossed out the word *apologizing*, and wrote above it: *dictating terms*.

She smiled. A quiet, fierce satisfaction filled her chest. She had spent five years absorbing other people's panic, smoothing other people's tantrums, and paying other people's debts. 

Tomorrow morning, on the platform at Grantham, she would hand Julian his ticket, ensure his rent money was safe, and then she would walk onto the London train as her own master.

"Well handled, Fiona," Julian murmured from the edge of the platform as she walked past.

"I do not require your approbation, Julian," she replied in an undertone, not looking at him. "I applied the regulations impartially, as I have done since nineteen eighty-nine."

"You always had a magnificent legal mind, my dear. Remember when you contested the parking meter fines in Cheltenham?"

"That was thirty-two pounds of our joint holiday fund that you carelessly abandoned outside a theatrical wigmaker," Fiona said coldly.

"The wig was essential for my Richard the Third," Julian pleaded softly.

"The wig was synthetic nylon and smelled of damp goat," Fiona countered, checking her clipboard. "And you left it in a taxi in Gloucester."

"Arthur," Zoe said softly, touching the old man's canvas sleeve as the scale needle settled.

He was trembling, his eyes fixed upon the dial as if he were watching the verdict of an ancient court.

"Look at her, lass," he whispered, his voice cracking with emotion. "She's hanging true. Not a tremble in the rind."

Julian stood beside them, his hand resting on Arthur's shoulder with quiet, patriarchal dignity.

"A masterpiece, Arthur," Julian said softly, without a trace of theatrical mannerism. "A triumph of human patience over the elements."

The old man nodded once, unable to speak, his rough hand coming up to clasp Julian's fingers in a tight, desperate grip.

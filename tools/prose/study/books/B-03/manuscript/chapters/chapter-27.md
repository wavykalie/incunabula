# Chapter 27: Julian at the Rostrum

Julian Tremayne did not ask for the microphone. He stepped directly in front of Fiona Sterling, placed his hand over hers on the chrome stand, and gently but irresistibly removed the gooseneck from her grip.

Fiona's eyes flashed with warning. "Julian, step down. This adjudication is complete."

"The adjudication is not complete, Fiona," Julian said, his voice carrying clearly to the front four rows of tables even without the amplifier. "Because the court has not yet heard the summation for the defence."

He switched the microphone to his right hand, turned to face the three hundred growers packed into the Rutland Suite, and let out a single, resonant cough that silenced every whisper in the room.

Zoe watched him from the foot of the stage. She recognized that posture. It was the stance Julian had assumed in ninety-six during the celebrated season finale of *Crown and Country*, when he had played Sir Edward Marshall Hall defending a Yorkshire poacher against a rigged jury of local magistrates.

"Members of the jury," Julian began, his baritone swelling with magnificent, rolling theatrical authority.

A chuckle rippled through the front row, but it died instantly as Julian raised his left hand, his face grave with absolute solemnity.

"I say jury," Julian continued, "because in this room today, you are not merely exhibitors of root vegetables. You are the custodians of English natural justice. You are the heirs to a tradition of common fairness that stretches from the Magna Carta to the drainage of these very fens!"

He paused, letting the silence hang in the air for three full beats.

"The learned Chairman of Judges cites Rule 14b, subsection six," Julian boomed, pointing his index finger toward Fiona's clipboard. "A rule imposing a draconian penalty of twelve pounds for an act of emergency preservation. But I ask you to examine the wording of that statute! Does subsection six refer to repairs executed by the competitor? Yes! It speaks of intentional deception practiced by a grower seeking an unfair mechanical advantage!"

He stepped to the very edge of the riser, his patent leather shoes gleaming in the stage lights.

"Did Arthur Povey apply that silver ribbon? No! The repair was executed by an officer of the platform! An independent representative of the British theatrical arts acting in the supreme interest of public safety to prevent eighty pounds of pressurized vegetable mass from collapsing onto the feet of the assembled citizenry!"

The crowd began to murmur, a low, rising rumble of agreement building from Table 1 to Table 6.

"In the eyes of the common law," Julian thundered, his voice reaching the magnificent, operatic heights of his prime, "an act of necessity performed to preserve life, limb, or municipal property cannot be construed as a violation of the sporting code! To penalize the Emperor for an emergency suture is to penalize the patient for the skill of the surgeon! It is contrary to equity! It is contrary to reason! And above all, it is profoundly, unforgettably un-English!"

He turned on his heel, facing Fiona directly, his chin held high, his chest heaving with passionate eloquence.

"Madam Chairman," Julian proclaimed, his voice softening into a thrilling, intimate challenge. "The scales have spoken. The balance arm has touched the agate. The certified weight of that majestic vegetable is seventy-five pounds and four ounces. I demand, in the name of the British viewing public and the sacred honour of South Kesteven, that you declare Arthur Povey the champion!"

The entire ballroom erupted into an absolute frenzy of cheering. Men stood on chairs; flat caps were hurled into the rafters; four members of the parish council began rhythmically chanting: "Artie! Artie! Artie!"

Fiona Sterling stood before the microphone, surrounded by three hundred shouting allotmenteers. She looked at Julian. Her lips twitched once, twice, and then, for a fraction of a second, a small, genuine smile of pure amusement broke through her icy facade.

She raised both hands to quiet the room.

"The committee," Fiona announced, her voice trembling slightly with suppressed laughter, "has reviewed the legal submissions of our distinguished guest of honour. The penalty under subsection six is hereby dismissed. The winner of the nineteen twenty-six Challenge Cup... is Arthur Povey!"

Julian's voice was an acoustic thunderbolt that arrested the rising violence in mid-air.

He did not merely speak; he commanded the room with the complete, terrifying authority of a theatrical titan who had mastered every trick of vocal dynamics across three decades of performance. He modulated from a booming, righteous roar to an intimate, conspiratorial whisper, drawing three hundred people into his rhetorical web until nobody breathed.

When he reached his final declaration of natural justice, even the hotel bar staff had stopped pouring drinks, standing frozen with beer jugs in their hands, staring at the stage with open mouths.

Fiona Sterling stood before him, her clipboard held tight against her chest. She had ruled South Kesteven with an iron will for a decade, but looking at Julian in this moment, she saw the man she had fallen in love with in nineteen ninety-two: fearless, eloquent, and passionately devoted to the drama of human dignity.

When she announced the dismissal of the penalty, the explosion of joy that ripped through the hall was like the lifting of a siege.

Julian bowed to her once more, his eyes meeting hers with deep, unspoken gratitude, before turning to catch Arthur Povey in a massive, triumphant bear hug that lifted the old grower six inches off the parquet floor.

Under the cover of Julian's sonorous declamation on Tennyson, Zoe stood beside the pallet, her eyes fixed upon the small puddle of amber sap on the wood.

The chemical reaction was instantaneous.

As the adhesive contacted the organic moisture, a faint, wispy trace of white vapour curled from beneath the vegetable's rind. The amber droplet solidified into a hard, glass-clear bead, sealing the hairline fissure like a crystal weld.

Arthur pulled his hand back, wiping his sticky thumb against his trousers.

"She's sealed," he whispered to Zoe, his breath hot and ragged. "Tight as a ship's timber."

"Check the surface," Zoe instructed. "Is there any excess glue visible to the naked eye?"

"Smooth as a baby's cheek," Arthur reported, his voice shaking with relief. "The rind's taken it right into the cuticle."

The iron hoist creaked as the steel cable tightened around the central pulley.

"Slowly, Frank!" Donald Skelton shouted, his hand resting on the Avery balance arm. "Half an inch at a time!"

The Emperor lifted from the wooden pallet slats, swinging gently in the broad green canvas sling.

Zoe held her breath, her eyes locked onto the lower seam where the hairline crack had wept its amber droplet.

The surgical adhesive held firm. Under seventy pounds of sheer gravitational load, the bonded fissure remained as solid and impenetrable as cold slate.

"Clear of the timber!" the steward called out.

The full weight of the vegetable transferred to the heavy coil springs within the Avery column.

A collective intake of breath swept through the hall like a sudden gust of wind through dry fenland reeds.

The tension in the assembly bay was so thick that the only audible sound was the slow, rhythmic ticking of the electric clock on the church hall wall.

Zoe knelt beside the rear pallet runner, her breath held tight in her chest.

Through the narrow gap between the green canvas sling and the wooden slats, she could see the seam where she had applied the veterinary adhesive.

The transparent bead of resin had set hard and cold, forming an artificial rind that locked the fractured cells together. Under the upward pull of the crane tackle, the green skin around the crack stretched taut as drum parchment, but the seam did not yield. Not a drop of sap escaped; not a single microscopic fissure opened.

Arthur Povey was leaning against the safety barrier, his eyes closed, his lips moving in silent, desperate prayer.

"Hold her, Frank," Donald Skelton whispered, his hand hovering over the damping valve on the scale column. "Just let the springs take the dead weight."

The cable groaned. The iron hook settled. The dial needle swept upward in a smooth, steady arc past sixty pounds, past sixty-five, past seventy.

Zoe let her breath out in a long, silent sigh of sheer technical relief.

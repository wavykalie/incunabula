# Chapter 26: Fiona's Move

The four lifters stepped onto the Avery weighing platform and lowered Arthur Povey's Emperor into the suspended rope cradle.

The cast-iron machine shuddered once, its green pillars absorbing the immense downward thrust of the vegetable. The knife-edge rocked in its agate seat, and the brass balance arm rose smoothly from its resting bracket, climbing toward the top of the dial with steady, hydraulic momentum.

Frank adjusted the twenty-pound counterweight, slid the auxiliary brass poise along the graduated scale, and held his breath as the pointer swung across the white enamel face.

The pointer passed sixty pounds. It passed seventy pounds. It crept past young Henderson's seventy-four pounds and eight ounces, slowing against the oil damper.

It stopped at seventy-five pounds, four ounces.

A roar went up from the allotmenteers that shook the dust from the plastic chandeliers. Hats flew into the air; beer was spilled over the green baize of Table 3; George Baxter seized Frank by the lapels of his boiler suit and danced him in a circle around the scale pillars.

"Seventy-five four!" Povey bellowed, throwing his arms around Julian Tremayne's shoulders with such force that Julian's crimson pocket square was crushed flat against his lapel. "The Emperor has done it! The fen has taken the cup!"

Julian staggered beneath the old man's embrace, but his famous baritone rose above the din with triumphant delight. "Magnificent, Mr Povey! A victory for classical endurance! A triumph of the soil!"

On the dais, Fiona Sterling stood completely still, her hand resting upon the microphone switch. She waited until the cheering had subsided into a continuous, happy rumble of back-slapping and hand-shaking.

Then she switched on the microphone.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Fiona said. Her voice was ice. It cut through the celebration like a freezing fog drifting across the fen drains.

The room fell quiet, grower by grower, until only the hum of the beer chillers remained.

"Under Horticultural Society Rule 14b, subsection six," Fiona announced, reading directly from her clipboard, "all foreign adhesive repairs, non-organic topical applications, and synthetic tensile reinforcements applied to a specimen during competition must be deducted from the gross weight at a statutory penalty rate of two pounds per square inch of surface coverage."

Arthur Povey's smile vanished. His jaw dropped. "What did you say?"

"The tape," Fiona said, looking down from the stage with calm, unyielding administrative authority. "The silver tape applied by Miss Barnes covers approximately six square inches of epidermal surface. Six square inches at two pounds per inch yields a statutory penalty deduction of twelve pounds. Net certified weight for the Emperor: sixty-three pounds, four ounces."

A stunned, horrified silence gripped the hall.

"No!" Povey roared, his face turning an apoplectic purple. "That's robbery! That's daylight murder! The tape don't weigh two ounces, let alone twelve pounds!"

"The rule does not measure the mass of the tape, Arthur," Fiona replied smoothly. "The rule imposes a punitive penalty to deter artificial structural support. The net weight stands. And therefore, the winner of Class Four and the recipient of the Market Deeping Challenge Cup is Exhibit Number Seven: Mr G. R. Henderson, with seventy-four pounds, eight ounces."

Young Henderson leaped onto his pine crate, punching the air with both fists. "Yes! Get in there!"

His father removed his unlit cigar and began shaking hands with the committee stewards.

On the floor, Arthur Povey looked toward Julian with eyes full of helpless, desperate agony. "Julian," the old man choked out, using the actor's Christian name for the first time. "You're the guest of honour. Don't let her do this to the fen."

Julian Tremayne looked at Povey. Then he looked at Zoe, who was standing beside the scales with her lips pressed tight in cold, furious concentration.

Julian drew a long, slow breath. He adjusted his waistcoat, squared his shoulders, and walked up the steps of the dais with the deliberate, terrifying majesty of an ancient king preparing to depose a corrupt chancellor.

The silence in the Rutland Suite after Fiona's announcement of the twelve-pound tape penalty was thick with dangerous, muttering fury.

Three allotmenteers in the front row took off their cloth caps and threw them onto the floor; George Baxter stepped toward the stage with his fists clenched, his face dark with rage; behind the scales, Frank refused to record the deduction on the official slate, laying his chalk down upon the green iron frame.

"I won't write it, Mrs Sterling," Frank called out, his voice shaking with stubborn rural rebellion. "I've been scale clerk for twenty-two years, and I've never written a twelve-pound lie on the Avery slate. You write it yourself if you want it on the record."

Young Henderson's father stepped forward, his sheepskin coat open, brandishing a leather wallet stuffed with business cards. "The Chair of Judges has made her ruling! The bylaws are binding upon all exhibitors! If the clerk refuses to record the weight, my company will instruct our solicitors in Lincoln to serve a writ of mandamus upon the society before the banks open on Monday morning!"

The allotmenteers surged against the rope barrier, shouting insults at Henderson and his son. 

In another thirty seconds, the dispute would have degenerated into a physical brawl among the parsnips. 

And then Julian Tremayne took the stage.

Julian stepped onto the raised timber dais, smiling with expansive, radiant charm at the assembled crowd of fenland farmers.

"Ladies and gentlemen! Members of the Society! Friends of the British soil!" his magnificent voice rang out across the hall, drowning out the rattle of the tea trolleys and the hum of the paraffin heaters.

The crowd ceased their mutterings and turned as one toward the podium.

"As I look upon this splendid gathering," Julian proclaimed, his right arm sweeping toward the harvest bunting, "I am reminded of the words of the late Lord Tennyson: 'The old order changeth, yielding place to new, and God fulfils himself in many ways.' But in this corner of Lincolnshire, the ancient bond between man and earth remains unbroken!"

Behind him, Arthur Povey quietly uncapped the blue tube and slid his weathered hand beneath the Emperor's belly.

Julian stepped to the edge of the platform, his resonant baritone effortlessly commanding the attention of the crowded hall.

"My friends," Julian said, his hands outspread in a gesture of magnificent hospitality. "We stand upon the threshold of decision! For five months, the soil of Lincolnshire has laboured in darkness and in rain. And now, the fruits of that patient devotion are brought before the judgment seat of the scale!"

The farmers paused in their chatter, flat caps tilted back, listening to the golden cadence of a voice that had once filled the Olivier Theatre.

"A splendid speaker," an elderly woman in the front row whispered to her companion. "Sounds just like the Archbishop of Canterbury on the wireless."

"He was on the television," her companion whispered back. "He caught the poisoner of Holbeach Marsh in eighty-nine."

"Ah," the woman nodded with deep respect. "A man of great discernment."

# Chapter 21: The Scales of Justice

The industrial butcher scales stood at the centre of the ballroom like a green iron gallows, flanked by two brass-bound beer barrels to prevent the crowd from surging into the balance arm.

Avery of Birmingham had built the machine in nineteen fifty-five for the municipal slaughterhouse at Grantham, and its cast-iron pillars bore the scars of half a century of heavy commercial service. The fulcrum consisted of an eight-inch hardened steel knife-edge resting in an agate groove, so delicately balanced that a brass penny laid upon the platform would cause the pointer to dip half an inch.

Frank, the scale steward, knelt beside the base with a brass spirit level and an oily rag, checking the alignment of the leveling screws. 

"Dead level, Mrs Sterling," Frank called out, rising with a grunt and wiping his knees. "Spirit bubble is dead between the brass wires. She will weigh true to two ounces up to one hundredweight."

Fiona Sterling stood at the stage microphone, her clipboard held firmly in both hands. "Thank you, Frank. Exhibitors in Class Four will present their entries in catalogue order. Stewards, please bring forward Exhibit Number One."

The crowd of allotmenteers parted to allow the passage of a four-wheeled wooden sack barrow. 

Upon the barrow rested the first contender: a sixty-two-pound striped marrow grown by a retired bus driver from Donington. Four men in leather aprons lifted the vegetable in a canvas sling, their faces red with strain, and lowered it gently into the Avery rope cradle.

The iron frame groaned. The heavy lead counterweight on the threaded screw slid forward with a harsh, metallic rattle, and the polished brass pointer swung wildly across the white enamel dial before trembling to a halt at sixty-one pounds, twelve ounces.

"Sixty-one pounds, twelve ounces!" Fiona announced into the microphone. "Recorded for Mr Harold Finch."

A polite ripple of applause circulated through the hall. Harold Finch touched his cloth cap, took his certified yellow slip from the clerk, and helped his team wheel the specimen toward the display staging.

Julian Tremayne stood at the side of the weighing platform, his hands clasped behind the tails of his charcoal jacket. He was watching the needle with the keen, clinical concentration of a pathologist observing a toxicological assay.

"Notice the dampening on the indicator, Zoe," Julian whispered, leaning down toward her shoulder. "The hydraulic shock absorber in the base. Precision engineering. In ninety-six, when we filmed the post-mortem sequence in the third episode of *Whispers*, the Home Office technical advisor showed me an identical balance used for weighing anatomical specimens. The drama inherent in a calibrated balance is extraordinary. Every fraction of an ounce carries the weight of a human obsession."

"Julian," Zoe said under her breath, keeping her eyes on the crowd. "Henderson's crate is moving."

Through the double doors of the service exit, young Henderson and three burly farm workers in navy boiler suits had entered the room. They were pushing a heavy hydraulic pallet truck across the parquet floor, the nylon wheels humming with a deep, vibrating drone.

Upon the pallet sat the pine shipping crate from King's Lynn. 

The crowd fell silent as the truck rolled toward the scales. The smell of cold peat and diesel exhaust followed in its wake, mingling with the stale vinegar from the bar.

Old Artie Povey pushed his way to the front of the rope barrier, his pruning knife clutched in his hand, his eyes fixed on the pine slats.

"Here comes the Dutch import," Povey growled, his voice carrying clearly to the platform. "Look at the width of the timber. You could bury a sheep in that box."

Young Henderson did not look at Povey. He operated the hydraulic lever on the truck handle with three rhythmic strokes, lowering the pallet until the timber settled onto the ballroom floor with a heavy, solid thud that rattled the tea cups on the sideboard.

The weigh-master, a retired coal merchant named Mr Donald Skelton, took his seat upon a high wooden stool beside the Avery dial, wearing a brown shopkeeper's coat and a pair of brass pince-nez spectacles tied to his lapel with black silk cord.

Skelton had weighed every exhibition squash in South Kesteven since nineteen seventy-two, and his authority was absolute.

"Order on the floor!" Skelton shouted, tapping the brass beam with an iron rod. "Exhibitors will remain behind the yellow chalk line. No leaning on the platform rails. No breathing on the damping cylinder. Frank, check the zero after each hoist."

Julian Tremayne watched the proceedings with deep, professional admiration, resting his hands upon his waistcoat. "A magnificent figure, Zoe. Look at the stern, incorruptible brow of the weigh-master. He has the gravitas of a Venetian inquisitor. One feels that forty centuries of British municipal rectitude are concentrated in those brass spectacles."

"He also has a hearing aid with a flat battery," Zoe noted quietly, checking her watch. "Arthur told me Skelton has not heard a conversational word spoken below seventy decibels since nineteen eighty-nine. That is why everyone has to shout their submissions."

Julian's eyes lit up with theatrical excitement. "Then he will appreciate my diction, my dear. The baritone was designed by nature for the hard of hearing."

"Arthur," Zoe called out over the din of the hall.

"Aye, lass."

"Skelton has put on his brown cotton coat."

"Then the hour is come," Arthur said, wiping his palms on his canvas apron. "Once Donald puts on the brown coat, he don't listen to appeal nor petition. He weighed a squash for a duke's bailiff in eighty-six and docked him four ounces for three pea pods stuck to the stem. The duke complained to the Lord Lieutenant, and Donald told the Lord Lieutenant to take his hat off in the pavilion."

Julian adjusted his tie, his eyes shining. "A man of unbending civic virtue. An incorruptible tribune of the vegetable garden. If only the Arts Council possessed officials of such uncompromising moral clarity."

"If the Arts Council had Donald Skelton, Julian," Zoe said, "you would have been audited for seven missing hotel towels in Newcastle."

"Donald," Arthur called out, his voice cutting through the hiss of the paraffin heaters.

"Aye?"

"The sling's level."

"I'll judge the level," Skelton barked back, leaning over the Avery dial with his brass pince-nez perched upon the bridge of his bulbous nose. "I've levelled beams in three counties since the coronation. Don't you tell me about level, Arthur Povey."

Julian stepped closer to the barrier, his hands resting lightly upon his waistcoat.

"Look at Skelton's profile, Zoe," Julian whispered. "The stubborn, granitic jaw of the fenland yeoman. He could step onto the stage at Stratford as the Constable of France without a single rehearsal. When an Englishman takes a brass scale into his keeping, he assumes the moral dignity of an entire legal system."

"He also has chalk on his sleeve," Zoe noted.

"A minor detail," Julian insisted. "The spirit is incorruptible."

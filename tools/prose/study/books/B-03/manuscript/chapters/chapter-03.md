# Chapter 03: The Green Baize Tables

The double fire doors of the Rutland Suite yielded with the groan of an ungreased brass hinge, releasing a blast of warm, humid air that smelled powerfully of damp moss, radiator rust, and turnip greens.

Zoe Barnes crossed the threshold first, keeping her shoulder firmly wedged against the door so that Julian would not catch his coat on the brass panic bar. She stepped into a vast, low-ceilinged hall that in the mid-nineteen-seventies had evidently been decorated for regional sales conferences. The wallpaper displayed a repeating motif of mustard-coloured heraldic shields, peeled slightly at the skirting board and mottled with damp near the corner fire exit. Above them, four rows of industrial fluorescent strip lights hummed in a harsh, unsynchronized B-flat, casting a flat institutional glare across six long rows of trestle tables.

Each trestle table was draped in heavy green baize, secured at the corners with rusty metal bull-dog clips. And upon the baize rested the produce.

There were eighty or ninety people in the room, almost all of them wearing heavy woollen knitwear, waxed coats with fraying cuffs, or bib overalls tucked into mud-caked wellingtons. A low, continuous rumble of conversation filled the air, punctuated by the clinking of metal measuring tapes, the scrape of wooden crates being shoved across parquet flooring, and the occasional dry hiss of an aerosol mister. 

Nobody looked up. Nobody glanced toward the door. Nobody registered the arrival of a leading television actor who had once held seven million viewers in suspense over the fate of a Norfolk coroner.

"Remarkable," Julian said, his baritone booming into the immediate vicinity of a display of winter leeks. "A rustic tableau worthy of Thomas Hardy. Note the quiet concentration of the peasantry, Zoe. The deep, ancestral devotion to the fruits of the soil. One feels the weight of four centuries of tillage."

A small, wiry man in a brown flat cap and a padded fleece vest turned around. He held a brass caliper in one hand and a spray bottle of diluted cloudy ammonia in the other. He squinted at Julian's crimson silk pocket square with the sharp, suspicious appraisal of a customs officer examining an uninspected parcel of foreign meat.

"Mind your sleeve, mate," the man said in a flat Lincolnshire drawl. "You are leaning over Mr Gedge's prize celery. Trench celery does not like aftershave. It takes the scent right through the stems, and then the judges mark him down for soapy heart."

Julian took half a step backward, smoothing his lapel with dignified haste. "My apologies, sir. An unintentional intrusion upon the celery. I am Julian Tremayne."

He paused, tilting his head slightly to the left and offering the gentle, benevolent smile that had concluded forty-six episodes of *Whispers on the Marsh*.

The man in the flat cap stared at him for three seconds. "Right you are," he said. He turned his back, sprayed a mist of water over a bundle of pale celery stalks wrapped in brown paper, and shouted across the hall: "Artie! Have you got the three-eighths spanner for the scale counterweight? Frank says the south arm is sticking again!"

From the far corner of the room, a gravelly voice roared back above the radiator clatter: "Frank can wait until I have checked the girth on the Emperor! If anybody touches the cradle before the official steward arrives, I am calling the police!"

Zoe glanced at Julian. His smile had frozen into an expression of polite, glassy bewilderment. He remained poised on the parquet floor, one hand resting on the green baize, waiting for the delayed spark of recognition that had accompanied his arrivals at London theatrical book launches for twenty-five years.

"They are engrossed in the competition, Julian," Zoe said, steering him gently toward the central aisle. "The tension is evidently running very high. Agricultural rivalries are famously intense."

"Of course," Julian said quickly, clearing his throat and recovering his posture. "Professional focus. I respect it immensely. The true craftsman has no time for frivolous celebrity distractions. When I worked with Sir John at the National in eighty-nine, he was precisely the same before the curtain. Utterly monastic. One could not speak to him of anything lighter than *Coriolanus*."

They moved down the central aisle between the tables. As they advanced, Zoe began to realize the sheer scale of the exhibits. 

These were not ordinary garden vegetables. These were swollen monsters of horticulture, bred through secret lineages of seeds and fed with chemical cocktails to achieve terrifying biological mass. In Class 2, Heaviest Root, parsnips the thickness of rugby players' thighs lay arranged on beds of dry wood shavings, their tapered roots extending six feet along the baize. On Table 4, a collection of cabbage heads resembled green cannonballs, each accompanied by a handwritten index card listing its seed origin, sowing date, and final certified weight in pounds and ounces.

And then they reached Table 6, where Class 4 was assembled.

Here lay the giant marrows.

There were twelve of them, resting upon padded cotton cushions, old woollen blankets, and foam cradles to prevent their soft underbellies from bruising against the timber trestles. They did not look like food; they looked like sleeping, mottled green swine, ribbed with pale yellow striations and glistening with moisture. The largest of them, stationed at the end of the table under a hand-lettered cardboard sign that read THE EMPEROR, A. POVEY, DEEPING FEN, was easily four feet in length. Its girth was monstrous, bulging out over the edge of the timber like an overfilled canvas sail.

Beside it stood Arthur Povey himself. He was a broad-shouldered septuagenarian with massive, gnarled knuckles stained dark with peat, wearing a pair of mud-spattered bib overalls over a faded flannel shirt. His hair stood up in tufts of white stubble, and his jaw worked continuously on an invisible piece of chewing tobacco. In his right hand, he held a six-inch curved pruning knife with a polished horn handle, using the flat of the blade to gently stroke the dorsal ridge of the vegetable.

"Look at that rind," Julian murmured, stepping closer with genuine, unfeigned astonishment. "It is monstrous. It is Falstaffian. Zoe, look at the girth. One could carve a canoe from such an object."

Arthur Povey's eyes flicked up. They were small, watery blue, and entirely devoid of warmth.

"Stand back six inches, governor," Povey growled, pointing the curved tip of the pruning knife directly at Julian's silk tie. "You are breathing on the skin."

"My good man," Julian began, his chest expanding beneath his waistcoat, "I assure you I mean no harm to your magnificent gourd. I was merely admiring the sheer vegetative majesty."

"I do not care if you are admiring it or cursing it," Povey said, his voice dropping into a menacing whisper that cut cleanly through the background hum of the hall. "A show marrow breathes through its pores until the final weigh-in. You have hair oil on, and you smell like a chemist's shop in Peterborough. If that rind softens before five o'clock, I will have the skin off your back."

Julian blinked. The majestic baritone vanished, replaced by a sudden, dry squeak. "I beg your pardon?"

"You heard me," Povey said. "We have had spies from the Norfolk border sneaking around since ten this morning. Frank spotted a strange van with King's Lynn plates parked behind the bins. If Mrs Sterling thinks she can let fancy Londoners wander around the tables without an official steward's badge, she has got another think coming."

Zoe stepped forward smoothly, placing herself between Povey's knife and Julian's waistcoat. She reached into her bag, pulled out her laminated schedule, and offered Povey a crisp, professional agency smile.

"Good afternoon, Mr Povey," she said, her voice bright, firm, and completely unflappable. "I am Zoe Barnes, personal assistant to Mr Julian Tremayne. Mr Tremayne is the guest of honour engaged by Mrs Sterling to deliver the keynote address and present the Challenge Cup this evening. We are currently looking for the committee room."

Povey lowered the knife half an inch, squinting at Zoe's clipboard with deep, sullen suspicion. 

"The guest of honour?" he muttered, looking Julian up and down from his silver-streaked hair to his patent leather shoes. "What is he honouring? Has he ever grown a sixty-pounder?"

"Mr Tremayne is a distinguished classical actor," Zoe said smoothly. "He starred for five seasons as Detective Inspector Dalrymple in *Whispers on the Marsh*."

Povey spat into a blue handkerchief. "Never heard of him. We only watch the snooker. If you want Fiona Sterling, she is up on the stage fiddling with the silver. Tell her from me that if she lets young Henderson use the digital scales without a spirit level check, the whole committee is going to the magistrates' court."

Julian opened his mouth to deliver what would undoubtedly have been a magnificent defence of his BAFTA nominations, but Zoe took his forearm with a grip that dug straight through the tweed to the bone.

"Thank you, Mr Povey," Zoe said. "We shall certainly convey your compliments to the chair."

She turned Julian firmly on his heel and marched him up the central aisle toward the raised wooden dais at the end of the hall. Behind them, Povey returned to stroking the flank of his giant marrow with the horn-handled knife, muttering dark obscenities about Norfolk interlopers and hair tonic.

They navigated the perimeter of the hall, walking slowly between the rows of trestle tables.

At Table 3, two men in green oilskin coats were engaged in a fierce, low-voiced argument over a wooden box of heirloom parsnips. One of the men, who had a wooden measuring gauge tucked behind his ear, was holding a magnifying glass to the crown of a four-foot parsnip that looked like an ancient, tap-rooted mammoth tusk.

"Look at that crown, George," the man with the gauge muttered, jabbing a blunt thumb at the root. "There's sand in the shoulder. You've been growing them in a plastic drainage pipe filled with sharp builder's sand to stop the fork."

"It's river silt from the Welland," the other man retorted, his face darkening with righteous rural fury. "Clean Fen silt, washed by the spring tides! My grandfather grew parsnips on that bed before you were out of short trousers, Harold!"

Julian stopped, watching the dispute with deep fascination. "Extraordinary," he whispered to Zoe. "The intensity of the dramatic stakes. Observe the tension in the jaw muscles, my dear. The subtext is brimming with ancestral grievance. It is pure Arthur Miller. The crucible of the allotment."

"Keep moving, Julian," Zoe murmured, keeping her hand on his elbow. "If you get drawn into a parsnip feud, we will be here until Tuesday."

They moved past Table 5, where an elderly woman in a floral apron was polishing an enormous red pumpkin with a yellow duster until the rind gleamed like a newly waxed sports car. She looked up as Julian passed, her eyes scanning his three-piece suit with cautious bewilderment before returning to her polishing with increased vigour.

"They have no idea who I am, do they?" Julian said quietly, his voice dropping into a tone that was half-amused, half-wounded.

"They are focused on their produce, Julian," Zoe said gently. "It is not a reflection upon your career."

"In ninety-eight," he murmured, his eyes fixed on the distant stage, "when Dalrymple visited the agricultural show at Spalding, four hundred women queued in the pouring rain to obtain my signature on their souvenir programmes. The local constabulary had to assign two sergeants to manage the crowd. And now... I am less interesting than a four-foot parsnip."

"The parsnip took nine months of manure and weeding, Julian," Zoe said softly. "Give them time. Once you take the microphone, they will remember."

"Arthur," Fiona Sterling said, her voice cutting cleanly through the murmur of the hall like a steel wire through cold butter.

Arthur Povey turned slowly, holding a brass vernier caliper in his soil-stained fingers. "Mrs Sterling."

"You are leaning against the table leg, Arthur. The table leg is nineteenth-century deal pine. It has a slight structural rot at the joint."

"The table is as sound as an oak beam, Fiona."

"The surveyor from Spalding disagrees," Fiona replied, her pencil poised above her green clipboard. "If your marrow collapses that trestle, the society will withhold forty pounds from your exhibitor prize fund to cover carpenter repairs."

"She won't collapse no trestle," Arthur growled, his jaw tightening beneath his grey stubble. "The Emperor sits on four rubber shock pads rated for naval ordnance. My nephew brought them out of the dockyard at Chatham."

Julian stepped closer, his hands clasped behind his back in his signature Dalrymple pose. "Fascinating. Naval damping systems applied to fenland agriculture. The marriage of military engineering and pastoral fecundity."

Arthur looked at Julian's crimson silk handkerchief, then down at his polished leather Oxfords. "You the actor fellow?"

"Julian Tremayne," Julian replied with a courtly bow of his head.

"Mind you don't drop no ash from that pipe near my vine," Arthur said. "She's sensitive to foreign particulates."

"Arthur," Zoe said, stepping around the wooden wheelbarrow.

"Aye?"

"How long have you grown for the Deeping Show?"

"Forty-eight year," Arthur said, not pausing in his examination of the vine. "First showed a pair of long white marrow in nineteen fifty-four. Queen had just been crowned. My old father took me on the crossbar of his bicycle from Baston Fen with the marrows in a sack across his handlebars. We got third prize behind a blacksmith from Langtoft."

Julian stepped closer, his face softening with genuine, unfeigned respect.

"Forty-eight years," Julian repeated softly. "A half-century of continuous artistic engagement with the soil. That is no mere hobby, Arthur. That is an epic cycle. Wagner in a greenhouse."

"Wagner had nothing to do with it," Arthur said. "It was three cartloads of dry pigeon muck from the church tower and a bucket of soot every third Saturday in August. You keep the roots warm and the frost off the blossom, and the plant does the rest."

"Fascinating," Julian said. "The discipline of the master craftsman."

Behind the trestle table, Arthur Povey kept his seed catalogue inside a battered green tin that had originally contained Scottish shortbread biscuits.

"Look here, young lady," Arthur said, opening the tin with two blackened thumbs. "Nineteen sixty-three. That was the big freeze. Frost was four feet deep in the loam. Most of the growers in Baston lost their stock. I kept my marrow seedlings in the scullery next to the coal range, wrapped in two woollen blankets my mother took out of the workhouse at Bourne."

Zoe looked at the handwritten entries in the small notebook.

"You recorded every frost date," she observed, turning the yellowed leaves.

"Course I did," Arthur said. "Fen frost don't come from the north like people think. It rolls in off the Wash on an easterly drift, cold as ironmongery. If you don't earth up the roots before the dusk turns pink, the frost catches the sap while it's rising and splits the vascular bundles. Once the bundles go, the marrow's finished. Might as well throw her to the pigs."

Julian nodded slowly, his hands tucked behind his back.

"The vascular bundles," Julian murmured with quiet relish. "A magnificent phrase. It sounds like an early drama by John Arden. The Struggle for the Vascular Bundles. I could play the stubborn tenant farmer who defies the river bailiff."

"You'd last twenty minutes in Baston Fen, Mr Tremayne," Arthur said, with a dry, gravelly chuckle. "Your shoes would dissolve in the ditch water before you found the sluice gate."

"A harsh assessment, Arthur," Julian said smiling. "Though not entirely without foundation."

"You recall the seventy-four dispute, Arthur?" Julian asked, leaning against the timber rail.

Arthur's leathery face darkened with ancient, tribal remembrance. "Seventy-four. The Baston business. Vicar's curate had an allotment behind the tithe barn. Claimed he'd grown a forty-pound squash on holy water and parish compost. When the weigh-master cut the rind after the show, he found sixteen four-inch masonry nails driven into the marrow's core to bring her up to weight."

Julian gasped with genuine theatrical horror. "Sacrilege! A clergyman tampering with the balance of the sanctuary!"

"Old curate got transferred to a parish in Nottingham," Arthur recounted, tapping his pipe against the heel of his boot. "Bishop wouldn't have him in the diocese no more. Said a man who'd nail a vegetable would forge a baptismal register without a second thought. Donald Skelton kept the nails in a glass jar on the committee mantlepiece for fifteen year as a warning to the clergy."

"A formidable deterrent," Julian said with deep approval. "Moral accountability enforced through municipal ironmongery."

"Donald don't care about gowns and dog collars," Arthur said simply. "If you're four ounces light, you're four ounces light."

# Chapter 18: The Caliper Incident

The preliminary inspection of Table 6 began at sixteen minutes to five, conducted under the personal supervision of Fiona Sterling.

Fiona carried a pair of antique brass measuring calipers, their curved arms polished to a brilliant gold, fitted with a finely graduated vernier scale calibrated in sixteenths of an inch. She approached Arthur Povey's Emperor with the grim solemnity of an executioner verifying the height of a scaffold.

"Arthur," Fiona said, her pencil poised over her clipboard. "Stand away from the cushion, please."

Povey did not move. He stood with his muddy boots planted four square upon the parquet floor, his arms crossed over his bib overalls, his jaw set like an iron anvil. "I am standing where I have stood for forty-eight years, Fiona. You measure the fruit, not the grower."

Behind Fiona stood young Henderson, accompanied by his father, a prosperous-looking man in a sheepskin coat who held an unlit cigar in his teeth. 

"Measure the stem first, Mrs Sterling," the elder Henderson said, his voice rich with the complacent authority of a regional agricultural feed magnate. "The regulations are quite clear on moisture retention."

Julian Tremayne stepped forward from the committee table. He had buttoned his charcoal waistcoat to the top, his silk pocket square arranged in four sharp points, his chin held high.

"As the independent witness designated under the Society charter," Julian declared, stepping between Povey and the elder Henderson, "I must insist that the calipers be zeroed in the presence of all parties before application to the specimen."

Fiona stopped with her calipers open. She turned her icy gray eyes upon Julian. "The calipers were zeroed in nineteen eighty-four, Julian. They do not require a metropolitan audit."

"Every precision mechanical instrument requires calibration before use, Madam Chairman," Julian replied, his voice rolling across the silent crowd with forensic weight. "In ninety-eight, when Dalrymple investigated the tampering of the grain silos at Boston docks, the case collapsed because the prosecution failed to establish the zero-point of the weighbridge. The Court of Appeal was scathing regarding administrative complacency. We cannot permit a similar cloud to hang over the Emperor."

A murmur of agreement rippled through the eighty growers. 

"Aye!" shouted George Baxter. "Show us the zero, Fiona! Young Henderson's father bought the drinks at the Black Bull last night!"

Fiona's cheekbones flushed with a faint, angry pink. With a sharp snap of her wrists, she closed the caliper tips together and held them up to the fluorescent light. The tips met with a hairline gap barely wider than a postage stamp.

"There," Fiona said coldly. "Zero. Are you satisfied, Julian?"

"Immensely," Julian bowed. "Now proceed, Madam Chairman. Justice has its eyes upon the brass."

Fiona knelt beside the table. She opened the calipers, carefully sliding the curved brass arms around the dorsal circumference of Povey's giant marrow. The metal jaws slid over the mottled green rind with a soft, scraping sound that held the entire room in breathless suspense.

She read the vernier scale with a magnifying lens attached to her clipboard.

"Circumference," Fiona announced into the silence. "Sixty-two and five-eighths inches."

A low whistle escaped from the back of the room. 

"And the stem?" Povey demanded, his voice trembling slightly.

Fiona shifted the tool to the severed crown of the fruit. The brass jaws hovered over the thick, fibrous stem. She squinted through her lens, her pencil hovering over the paper.

"The stem," Fiona said, her voice dropping into a razor-sharp whisper, "measures exactly fifteen-sixteenths of an inch. Within the statutory limit by one-sixteenth."

Old Artie let out a long, ragged exhale of tobacco smoke and peat dust. He slapped his thigh with a crack that sounded like a pistol shot. "Legal! Clean as a whistle!"

The crowd broke into spontaneous, cheering applause. Julian turned to Povey, offering a slow, dignified nod of mutual triumph, while Fiona rose from her knees, her face pale with administrative fury, making a sharp pencil check on her sheet.

"The hydraulic scales arrive in ten minutes," Fiona said into her microphone, her voice cutting through the cheers. "Exhibitors in Class Four will assemble their transport cradles immediately."

The tension around Table 6 had reached fever pitch by the time Fiona completed her stem measurement.

Arthur Povey was leaning over his Emperor, his knuckles white as he gripped the edge of the green baize, his breath coming in short, raspy gasps that rattled his chest. Beside him, George Baxter was writing down the measurement in a small leather notebook with the stub of a carpenter's pencil, checking the figures three times with a pocket calculator.

"Fifteen-sixteenths," Baxter muttered, showing the screen to Harold Finch. "Clean within the limit by a hair's breadth. The Emperor is legal, Frank!"

Across the table, young Henderson's father took the unlit cigar from his mouth and stepped forward, his heavy sheepskin coat brushing against the display staging.

"Mrs Sterling," the elder Henderson said, his voice smooth with commercial menace. "My firm donates twelve hundred pounds annually to the district agricultural fund. We have a right to demand a second reading using a digital laser micrometer. These antique brass calipers are subject to thermal expansion in a warm room."

Julian Tremayne stepped forward instantly, planting his polished Oxford shoes firmly between Henderson and the table.

"Sir," Julian thundered, his baritone filling every corner of the hall with crushing judicial authority. "To challenge the certified calipers of the South Kesteven Society is to impugn the honour of the Chair! The measurement has been taken. The record has been inscribed. In British law, an adjudication delivered by a magistrate cannot be reopened upon the frivolous speculation of an interested commercial party!"

The allotmenteers cheered with deafening enthusiasm. 

"Get him told, Julian!" shouted an old grower from the fourth row. "The London fella is right! The calipers have spoken!"

The elder Henderson glared at Julian, his jaw working on his cigar, but he saw the united hostility of eighty angry farmers facing him across the green baize. With a curt, furious nod, he turned on his heel and retreated to the back of the room, muttering dark promises about next year's sponsorship budget.

"Arthur," Zoe said, her eyes catching a dark patch upon the wooden pallet beneath the Emperor's rear flank.

"What is it?"

"There is moisture on the lower crossbar."

Arthur dropped to his knees with a speed that belied his seventy years, his heavy canvas trousers scraping against the timber pallet.

He touched the dark stain with his forefinger, bringing it to his nose. "Water. Cold water."

"Could it be condensation from the tarpaulin?" Julian asked, leaning down with furrowed brow.

"Condensation don't pool in the shade," Arthur whispered, his voice trembling slightly. "It's weeping. The base is weeping."

Zoe knelt beside him on the damp grass, switching on her small pen torch. "Let me see the underside."

She shone the narrow beam beneath the rounded bulge of the vegetable, tracing the grey rubber shock pads to where the skin met the timber.

"Zoe," Julian said, his voice tense with suppressed anxiety as they stood by the assembly ropes.

"Yes, Julian?"

"What if the veterinary adhesive leaves a residue on the scale plate?"

"The adhesive cures to an inert resin within sixty seconds," she said under her breath. "It is completely non-transferable. The Avery plate will remain immaculate."

Julian took a slow breath, adjusting his waistcoat buttons. "You have thought of everything, Miss Barnes. In ninety-one, when we had a live badger on the set of Dalrymple, the animal wrangler assured the director that the creature was fully domesticated. Within four minutes, it had consumed half the sound technician's lunch and burrowed into the upholstery of the inspector's Wolseley."

"A vegetable cannot burrow, Julian."

"One never knows in the Fens, my dear," Julian murmured. "The soil here has an extraordinary vitality."

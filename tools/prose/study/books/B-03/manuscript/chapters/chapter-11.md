# Chapter 11: The Opening Remarks

The double doors at the rear of the Rutland Suite parted at two minutes past two, admitting Julian Tremayne with the solemn, measured tread of a High Court judge entering the Old Bailey.

Zoe walked two paces behind his left shoulder, carrying his leather document folio, an unspillable travel mug of lukewarm tap water, and three spare dry paper napkins. She watched his shoulder blades beneath the charcoal wool. They were set hard, locked into that rigid, defensive symmetry that had carried him through thirty years of theatrical auditions, commercial voiceovers, and divorce depositions.

Fiona Sterling stood at the stage microphone, tapping her clipboard against the gooseneck.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Fiona announced, her crisp magistrate's voice cutting through the clatter of the tea urn, "the moment has arrived. To declare our eighty-second Annual District Produce Exhibition officially open, the committee presents Mr Julian Briggs, professionally known to television audiences as Julian Tremayne."

A scattering of polite, bewildered clapping arose from the growers near the door. Julian stepped onto the plywood riser, paused to allow the riser's groan to subside, and took the microphone with both hands.

"Thank you, Madam Chairman," Julian boomed, his baritone instantly filling every corner of the room, vibrating the glass panes in the emergency exit. "Members of the Society, distinguished cultivators, custodians of the English soil. Standing before you this afternoon in the ancient heart of Kesteven, I am reminded of an evening in ninety-seven at the Theatre Royal, Haymarket, when Lord Olivier's former dresser said to me in the wings: 'Julian, my boy, the drama of human life is played not upon the red velvet carpets of the West End, but upon the loam and clay from which our fathers sprung.'"

The allotmenteers stared at him in absolute, petrified silence. 

At Table 2, an elderly woman holding an oversized shallot in a plastic sandwich bag slowly lowered her arm. Near the radiator, Frank from the scale committee ceased wiping his three-eighths spanner and looked at Julian as if a large, exotic bird had flown into the ballroom through the ventilation duct.

Julian did not notice the frost. He warmed to his theme, extending his right hand in an expansive rhetorical sweep that narrowly missed knocking over a glass of tap water on the card table.

"For what is an actor, my friends, if not a cultivator of the human heart? We sow the seeds of tragedy; we prune the thorny brambles of deceit; we await the golden harvest of artistic truth. In *Whispers on the Marsh*, when Detective Inspector Dalrymple stood upon the desolate salt flats of the Wash to confront the smugglers of Holbeach, what sustained him through the long, solitary watches of the night? It was not the cold machinery of metropolitan jurisprudence. It was the memory of the furrow! The quiet dignity of the humble English root!"

From the fourth row of tables, Old Artie Povey cleared his throat with a sound like two dry bricks being rubbed together. "What about the calcium nitrate?"

Julian paused, his arm frozen in mid-gesture. "I beg your pardon, sir?"

"The calcium nitrate," Povey repeated, his gravelly voice rising above the background hum of the radiators. "You said you were talking about the root. Did you use the four-sixteen-twenty-four feed during the July dry spell, or did you stick with liquid blood?"

Julian blinked. His eyes darted toward Zoe at the side of the stage. Zoe kept her face completely impassive, though her fingers tightened around the handle of his document folio.

"A pertinent inquiry, my good man," Julian improvised, his baritone deepening by two full tones as he slipped unconsciously into his television detective persona. "The selection of soil nutrients is indeed the very fulcrum upon which horticultural destiny turns. In the late summer, when the fenland sun bakes the peat to dust, Dalrymple always favoured a rigorous application of high-potash nitrogen feed, balanced with an abundance of organic matter."

A collective murmur stirred through the hall. Three men in waxed coats at Table 4 nodded in slow, solemn agreement.

"High potash," a grower with a white beard muttered to his companion. "Hear that, George? The London fella says high potash on the peat. I told you that bone meal from Spalding was too sour for the marrow crowns."

Fiona Sterling stepped forward, her eyes narrowing as she observed the sudden, unexpected thaw across the room. She tapped her watch with her fingernail, smiling thinly at Julian.

"Thank you, Julian," Fiona said sweetly, reaching for the microphone. "A most informative theatrical digression. I am sure our exhibitors will bear your nitrogen observations in mind as we proceed to the preliminary inspections."

Julian did not release the microphone. He gripped the chrome stem with the tenacity of a bulldog on a postman's trouser leg, flashing his four-point crimson pocket square to the crowd.

"And therefore," Julian declared, his voice ringing out with triumphant authority, "by the power vested in me by the British television arts and sciences, I declare the Eighty-Second Annual District Produce Exhibition open! Bring forth the squashes!"

The entire room erupted into genuine, enthusiastic applause. Even Old Artie gave two slow claps with his peat-stained palms before turning back to spray his Emperor with water.

Julian released the microphone, stepped down from the riser, and took his water mug from Zoe with a modest, patrician inclination of his head.

"You see, Zoe?" he murmured under his breath, his eyes gleaming with professional satisfaction. "The common man responds to authenticity. One simply needs to speak their language."

"You told seventy farmers to put nitrogen on an August squash, Julian," Zoe said quietly, steering him toward the exit. "If their marrows rot from the inside out next week, the National Farmers Union will have a warrant out for your arrest."

As Julian stepped down from the platform, George Baxter approached him with a chipped mug of hot tea and a plate containing two custard creams.

"A word with you, Mr Tremayne," Baxter said, leaning close to Julian's ear while looking around to see if Fiona was watching. "You mentioned the four-sixteen-twenty-four feed on the peat. Did you apply that dry around the drip line, or did you dissolve it in warm rain water?"

Julian took the mug with an elegant bow of his head, holding it between his palms as if it were a chalice at a communion rail. "In ninety-eight, Mr Baxter, when Dalrymple walked the dykes of Holbeach, the local growers always dissolved the nutrients in ambient water drawn from the ditch at sunset. The soil shock is minimized, you see. The root system absorbs the solution without defensive contraction."

Baxter nodded with deep, religious conviction. "Ambient water at sunset. Hear that, Harold? The London fella knows his chemistry. I told you that tap water from the mains was chilling the fibers."

Zoe stood beside them, recording Julian's travel receipts in her notebook. She took a sip of lukewarm tap water from her bottle, watching Julian effortlessly hold court between a display of runner beans and a box of giant pickling onions. For five years, she had watched him struggle through television casting interviews where young producers in trainers asked him if he could do an urban accent. Here, surrounded by seventy retired farm workers who smelled of peat and tobacco, his booming theatrical presence was treated not as an obsolete relic, but as an ancient, comforting authority.

"The scissors, Fiona," Julian prompted, holding his hand palm upward with dignified patience.

"The society uses shears, Julian. Tailor's shears. Cast iron, Sheffield steel, nineteen twenty-two."

Fiona produced the shears from her green baize bag and placed them in Julian's hands with the gravity of an armaments officer issuing a sidearm.

"Do not let them slip," she instructed. "The blade is sharp enough to sever a bullock's tendon. If you nick your thumb, we will have to suspend proceedings while St John Ambulance applies a dressing."

Julian tested the hinge with a practised theatrical snip. "Magnificent balance. One senses the industrial heritage of the West Riding. With tools such as these, our ancestors carved an empire out of ironstone and steam."

"They cut tweed for workhouse trousers, Julian," Fiona said, consulting her wristwatch. "Now step forward, face the photographer from the Mercury, and deliver your speech in under four minutes. The tea urns boil at eleven-fifteen, and the ladies of the guild cannot hold the scones indefinitely."

"Julian," Fiona said, stepping onto the low timber dais beside the weighing scale.

"Fiona."

"Your speech was forty-two seconds over the allotted four minutes."

"Artistic license, my dear," Julian murmured with a flourish of his hand. "A speech that touches the heart cannot be constrained by the mechanical dictates of a quartz stopwatch."

"The tea ladies were forced to keep the scones in the warming oven for an additional five minutes," Fiona noted, her green pencil making a brisk check mark on her clipboard. "As a consequence, the butter has melted through the crusts, resulting in a three-percent reduction in anticipated refreshment margins."

Julian looked at her with mournful affection. "You have not changed by a single syllable since nineteen eighty-five, Fiona."

"Someone in this family had to maintain solvency, Julian. If I had listened to your theories on dramatic cadence, we would have lived in a converted railway carriage in Norfolk."

"It had a magnificent cast-iron stove," Julian pleaded softly. "And views across the salt marshes."

"It had dry rot in the floorboards and rats in the water butt," Fiona retorted, turning back to the register.

The official dais for the opening ceremony consisted of three beer crates covered with a strip of green baize, secured around the edges with brass drawing pins.

Fiona Sterling stood at the microphone, her spectacles resting on the bridge of her nose, her ledger open before her.

"Members of the society and visitors," Fiona announced, her crisp vowels cutting through the hubbub of the hall like a sharp knife through parchment. "We welcome Mr Julian Briggs, known to many of you for his dramatic work upon regional television, who has consented to open our annual exhibition."

Julian stepped onto the green baize, adjusting his cuffs with majestic grace.

"Mr Tremayne," Julian corrected in a gentle, carrying whisper that reached the third row of the produce display.

Fiona did not blink.

"Mr Briggs," she repeated into the microphone without missing a beat, "will now perform the ceremonial severance of the harvest ribbon."

A murmur of polite, provincial applause rustled through the hall.

Julian smiled, completely unruffled by the bureaucratic provocation. He took the Sheffield tailor's shears, held them aloft to catch the halogen lights, and inclined his head toward the front row.

"Friends and neighbours," Julian said, his baritone filling the space with the warmth of an old mahogany cello. "To stand among the produce of this ancient fen is to be reminded that while empires rise and fall, the honest labour of the gardener endures through all our fleeting seasons."

Even Fiona Sterling paused with her pencil in mid-air, a look of grudging aesthetic respect flickering across her stern features.

Following the ribbon-cutting, Mrs Derek Hoddinott, the wife of the district amenities chairman, stepped onto the platform carrying an oblong package wrapped in tissue paper.

"Mr Tremayne," Mrs Hoddinott said into the microphone, her pearl necklace trembling with civic excitement. "On behalf of the Ladies' Guild and the Drainage Sub-Committee, we present you with the commemorative linen tea towel of the South Kesteven washlands."

Julian accepted the parcel with both hands, bowing from the waist with the majestic grace of a medieval monarch receiving a treaty of peace.

He shook out the tea towel, displaying it to the audience with practiced theatrical flair.

The linen was screen-printed in bottle green and mustard yellow, depicting the seventeenth-century windmill pumps of Baston Fen surrounded by a border of wild celery and marsh harriers.

"A magnificent cartographic achievement," Julian declared, his voice echoing to the rafters. "I shall frame it and place it in my Maida Vale study, where it will remain an enduring reminder of this triumph of drainage and horticulture."

Mrs Hoddinott flushed with intense delight, while in the front row, Fiona Sterling made a sharp notation in her ledger: *Commemorative tea towel gifted to guest artiste; nominal inventory valuation two pounds fifty pence; written off against miscellaneous civic entertainment.*

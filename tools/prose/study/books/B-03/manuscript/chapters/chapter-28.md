# Chapter 28: The Trophy Inscription

The presentation table on the dais was surrounded by four rows of photographers from the local press, their camera flashes popping with harsh, magnesium bursts that illuminated the dust floating above the velvet cloth.

Zoe stood beside the card table, holding the Market Deeping Produce Society Silver Challenge Cup while the committee clerk prepared the presentation certificate.

Julian was currently trapped between two reporters from the *Stamford Mercury*, demonstrating the exact vocal inflection he had used to arrest the Holbeach grain smugglers in nineteen ninety-eight. He looked radiant; the exhaustion had vanished from his eyes, replaced by the brilliant, intoxicating flush of public adoration.

Zoe turned the silver cup over in her hands, her fingers resting upon the mahogany plinth.

She remembered the receipt she had seen in Fiona's diary an hour earlier: *To engraving silver challenge cup: Julian Briggs, in recognition of fifteen years of patience with the cabbages — £12.50*.

She looked at the underside of the heavy square wooden base.

The base had been lined with green baize felt to prevent the wood from scratching council chamber tables. But in the centre of the felt, an oval patch had been neatly cut away with a razor blade, exposing the polished mahogany beneath.

And there, cut into a circular brass disc recessed flush into the timber, was an inscription that had never appeared in any society programme:

*FOR JULIAN — WHO WAITED IN THE CAR IN THE RAIN WHILE I COUNTED THE ONIONS — WITH ALL MY LOVE, FIONA — OCTOBER 2008*.

Zoe touched the cool brass with her fingertip. 

Two thousand and eight. That had been the year *Whispers on the Marsh* had been cancelled by ITV. Julian had spent that entire autumn sitting in his car in drafty provincial car parks, waiting for Fiona to finish her weekend judging duties, too proud to enter the halls where nobody recognized him anymore, yet unable to bear the silence of an empty flat in town.

And Fiona, despite her sharp tongue, despite her clipboards and her icy municipal rules, had paid twelve pounds and fifty pence to have his name permanently bonded to the foundation of the cup.

"A touching sentiment, is it not?"

Zoe looked up. Fiona Sterling stood beside the table, holding a glass of lukewarm white wine. Her tweed jacket was unbuttoned, and she had removed her official magistrate's lanyard.

"He never looked under the base, did he?" Zoe asked softly.

"Julian never looked at anything that didn't have a mirror attached to it, my dear," Fiona replied, taking a small sip from her glass. "He carried that trophy onto the stage four times between two thousand and eight and two thousand and eleven, and he never once turned it upside down. He assumed the weight came entirely from the lead."

She looked across the dais toward Julian, who was currently laughing with Old Artie Povey while holding an imaginary pair of calipers to the reporter's nose.

"He was magnificent just now," Fiona added, her voice dropping into a quiet, wistful register. "Completely preposterous, of course. Everything he said about natural justice was utter nonsense. But he made those eighty old men feel as if their lives mattered. That was always his real gift. When he forgot about his billing, he could make an entire room believe in the magic of the stage."

"Why did you divorce him, Mrs Sterling?" Zoe asked, the question slipping out before her administrative filters could stop it.

Fiona smiled, a sad, clear-eyed smile that crinkled the skin around her eyes. "Because living with a performer, Miss Barnes, means you are always part of the supporting cast. And after sixteen years, I realized that I wanted to be the chair of my own committee."

She tapped her wine glass against the rim of the Challenge Cup with a soft, musical clink.

"Tell him the presentation begins in five minutes," Fiona said. "And tell him that if he kisses Old Artie on both cheeks, the local paper will put it on the front page."

Beside the presentation table, Zoe examined the underside of the silver Challenge Cup once more before the photographers assembled.

The inscription from October 2008, FOR JULIAN, WHO WAITED IN THE CAR IN THE RAIN WHILE I COUNTED THE ONIONS, WITH ALL MY LOVE, FIONA, gleamed softly in the stage light.

She looked at Fiona, who was currently watching Julian sign an autograph for a twelve-year-old boy in a scout uniform.

"You never told him, did you?" Zoe asked softly.

Fiona took a slow sip of her wine, her eyes distant and thoughtful. "Julian never listened to things that were whispered, my dear. He only heard words that were shouted from a gallery. By two thousand and eight, my voice had become too quiet for him to hear."

She looked at Zoe, her gaze sharp and penetrating.

"Do not make the same mistake, Miss Barnes," Fiona added quietly. "You have an exceptional mind and an extraordinary will. Do not spend your life whispering in the wings of someone else's performance."

Zoe looked down at the silver cup, feeling the truth of the words settle into her chest with cold, undeniable force.

Hector Gedge pushed his way through the front row of the spectators, his yellow waterproof smock rustling loudly.

"Mrs Sterling!" Gedge called out, pointing an accusing finger at Arthur's pallet. "I request a preliminary inspection under Rule Nine!"

Fiona Sterling froze, her fountain pen suspended above the weighing register. "On what grounds, Mr Gedge?"

"Unauthorized support materials!" Gedge declared triumphantly. "Povey has four rubber shock pads beneath that marrow! The schedule specifies bare timber or clean horticultural straw!"

Fiona walked slowly across the chalked floor, her leather brogues clicking with judicial precision. She looked down at the Emperor, then bent double, inspecting the rubber pads with her magnifying glass.

"These pads are stamped Admiralty Stores, nineteen forty-four," Fiona pronounced, straightening up. "Synthetic neoprene, non-adhesive, and permitted under Section Four of the regional bylaws as protective transport cushioning."

Gedge scowled, his sallow face darkening. "It's an unfair mechanical advantage!"

"It is sound logistical hygiene, Mr Gedge," Fiona snapped. "Now return to your station behind the yellow line."

Hector Gedge stepped forward, his eyes burning with frantic suspicion as he watched the Avery balance arm swing.

"Skelton!" Gedge cried out. "Check the counterweight! The brass slide is not seated in the groove!"

Donald Skelton did not even turn his head. He reached out with his left hand, lifted the heavy iron slide, and dropped it with an authoritative clack into the certified notch.

"Seated," Skelton said in a voice of doom. "Inspected by the Board of Trade in nineteen seventy-four, and inspected by Donald Skelton today. Go sit down, Hector, before I have the police constable escort you to the car park."

A ripple of laughter passed through the crowd.

Gedge's mouth opened and closed like a stranded carp, but he retreated behind the yellow chalk line with a dark and bitter scowl.

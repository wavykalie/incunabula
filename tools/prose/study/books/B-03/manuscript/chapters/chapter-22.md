# Chapter 22: Dalrymple's Voice

The hall had grown uncomfortably hot. The heat from three hundred bodies, combined with the humid exhalations of sixty exhibition squashes, had turned the air into a damp, aromatic blanket that smelled of wet compost, warm wool, and tea urn steam.

Fiona Sterling tapped her microphone twice. "Stewards, uncrate Exhibit Number Seven, please."

Young Henderson stepped forward with a crowbar. He hooked the curved steel claw beneath the pine lid of the crate, leaned his full weight onto the shaft, and levered upward. The nails pulled from the unseasoned timber with a sharp, shrieking rasp that made half the room wince.

He cast the lid aside and pulled away four layers of damp gray wool fleece.

A collective intake of breath stirred through the Rutland Suite.

The Norfolk marrow lay upon a bed of shredded wood wool. It was gargantuan. It measured four feet and eight inches from severed stem to flower scar, with a girth so immense that its dark jade flanks bulged out like an over-inflated barrage balloon. The skin was unnaturally dark, completely devoid of the pale yellow stripes that characterized the traditional Fenland cultivars, gleaming under the fluorescent tubes with an oily, chemical sheen.

"Good heavens," Julian whispered, his eyes widening. "It looks like an unexploded torpedo from the Battle of Jutland."

Four stewards stepped forward, fitted a wide canvas lifting sling under the dark hull, and hauled on the rope slings. Their boots slipped on the polished parquet; their veins stood out along their temples; one of the older men let out a sharp grunt of hernia-inducing effort as they hoisted the monster into the air.

They swung it over the Avery platform and lowered it into the rope cradle.

The cast-iron frame of the scales shrieked. The knife-edge rocked in its agate groove, and the brass balance arm slammed upward against the safety stop with a violent, reverberating clang that echoed like a church bell.

Frank scrambled to adjust the counterweights, sliding a twenty-pound iron cylinder along the notched beam. "She has bottomed the spring!" Frank yelled. "Pass me the five-pound doughnut from the tool box!"

The crowd surged forward against the rope barrier. The tension in the room was electric, thick with suspicion and hostile regional pride.

Fiona leaned into the microphone, but before she could speak, Julian Tremayne stepped smoothly onto the dais. He reached down with his right hand, took the microphone from the stand with the effortless grace of a seasoned performer, and held it to his lips.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Julian boomed.

The effect was instantaneous. The sheer, resonant power of his baritone—projected with thirty years of classical training, rich with the melancholic authority of Detective Inspector Dalrymple—cut through the muttering like a knife through soft rind. Every eye in the hall turned from the scales to the actor.

"Observe the balance arm," Julian commanded, his voice dropping into that deliberate, hypnotic cadence that had once kept millions of Britons glued to their television screens on wet Tuesday nights. "Look at the brass pointer as it seeks the true centre of gravity. In every inquest upon the marsh, there comes a single moment when speculation ceases and the physical evidence speaks. We are witnessing that moment now."

He extended his left arm toward the Avery dial, his four-point crimson pocket square catching the glare of the lights.

"Frank," Julian said, his baritone filling the silence with dramatic suspense. "Release the arresting lever."

Frank, looking completely bewildered but entirely hypnotized by Julian's voice, pulled the brass lever on the side of the housing.

The balance arm settled. The lead counterweights swung down. The polished pointer trembled, passed the seventy-pound mark, slowed against the hydraulic damper, and came to rest with absolute, mathematical finality.

Julian stepped forward to the very edge of the riser, peered down at the dial, and raised his head to the crowd.

"Seventy-four pounds," Julian announced, his voice ringing with tragic majesty. "And eight ounces."

Julian stepped down from the dais to inspect the Avery balance arm at close quarters.

He leaned over the safety rope, his crimson pocket square catching the light, his fingers tracing the air above the polished brass notches. "Notice the calibration marks, Mr Finch," he said to the elderly grower standing beside the barrier. "Graduated in ounces. Each notch represents sixteen drams of pure vegetative matter. In ninety-eight, when we recorded the opening episode of the fourth season, we spent three days filming the calibration of the tide gauges at Sutton Bridge. The director wanted a fast cut, but I insisted on lingering on the dial. The audience must feel the tension of the rising tide."

Harold Finch blinked through his thick spectacles, clutching his certified yellow certificate. "Is that so, Mr Tremayne? Well, I don't know much about tides, but I know that seventy-four pounds of marrow puts four tons of pressure per square foot on the timber barrow. If Frank don't grease the axle before the next class, the wheel will seize solid."

Julian nodded with profound solemnity. "The friction of mechanical life, Mr Finch. An eternal struggle against the resistance of the material world."

"Look at the hoist beam, Julian," Zoe said, pointing upward toward the iron girder supporting the tackle.

"A sturdy piece of Victorian ironmongery," Julian observed, head tilted back. "Cast at the Stamford Foundry in eighteen eighty-two, if I read the maker's stamp correctly."

"It has a certified safe working load of ten hundredweight," she noted from the council certificate pinned to the upright stanchion. "Plenty of margin for an eighty-pound marrow."

"In melodrama," Julian mused, his baritone rumbling pleasantly in his chest, "the rope always begins to fray in the third act just as the heroine is suspended above the mill race. I played the miller's nephew at the Lyceum in seventy-nine. We used hemp treated with petroleum jelly to ensure an authentic creak."

"If this tackle creaks, Julian, we will have eighty pounds of wet vegetable matter in your lap," Zoe reminded him. "Step three paces to the left."

"Arthur," Zoe said, leaning across the barrier.

"Lass?"

"The winch cable is steel braid."

"Quarter-inch wire rope," Arthur said, nodding with satisfaction. "Tested to eighteen hundredweight at the engineering works in Spalding. We had an iron chain snap at the Peterborough autumn show in eighty-one. The marrow went clean through a trestle table and squashed two boxes of award-winning Cox's Orange Pippins into cider mash. Took four stewards with shovels to clear the gangway."

Julian shuddered at the memory. "A catastrophe of epic proportions. The tragic spectacle of crushed pomology."

"It was sixteen pounds of prime dessert fruit ruined," Arthur said plainly. "The grower sued the committee in the county court."

"Did he succeed?"

"Settled out of court for eight pounds and a crate of brown ale," Arthur replied.

The weigh-in platform was constructed from four-inch baulks of English elm, planed smooth and banded with hoop iron around the perimeter.

Donald Skelton walked the length of the timber, testing each joint with the heel of his boot.

"No bounce," Skelton muttered to his assistant Frank. "If there's bounce in the elm, the dial needle oscillates and we can't take a legal reading. Remember eighty-eight? That big marrow from Spalding had the beam swinging like a clock pendulum for ten minutes because the timber wasn't pegged."

Julian watched the procedure with rapt, childlike fascination.

"The elm," Julian remarked to Zoe. "The traditional wood for coffins and water wheels. It resists decay under constant moisture. In seventy-two, when I played Kent in King Lear at Nottingham, the stage carpenter built the stocks from seasoned Nottinghamshire elm. My ankles were confined in them for forty minutes every evening. One develops an intimate respect for the grain of English timber when one is strapped to it by the shins."

"Arthur's marrow is not an actor, Julian," Zoe said quietly. "It has no vanity to wound."

"All living matter possesses vanity, Miss Barnes," Julian said, his eyes twinkling. "Especially when suspended before four hundred critical spectators."

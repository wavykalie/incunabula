# Chapter 20: The Stage Dispute

The final countdown to the giant marrow weigh-in began at half past five, signaled by the arrival of the hydraulic butcher scales on a four-wheeled industrial dolly.

The apparatus was a massive, green-painted cast-iron contraption manufactured in nineteen fifty-five by W. and T. Avery of Birmingham. It stood five feet high, equipped with a polished brass balance arm, a heavy lead counterweight on a threaded screw, and a suspended cradle woven from thick manila rope designed to cradle an eighty-pound vegetable without bruising the rind.

The growers had formed a tight circle around the stage, three hundred eyes fixed upon the brass indicator needle.

In the hallway behind the velvet curtain, Julian Tremayne stood with his back to the wall, his arms folded across his chest, refusing to step onto the riser.

"Julian," Zoe whispered, her phone battery at four percent. "The scales are set. Frank is holding the spirit level. They are waiting for you."

"I am not appearing under that billing," Julian said in a low, furious baritone that vibrated against the stage timber. "Fiona has printed the supplementary programmes on green paper. Have you seen the heading?"

Zoe looked at the green slip of paper clutched in his hand.

It read: SPECIAL ATTRACTION: WEIGH-IN ADJUDICATION BY MR JULIAN BRIGGS (RETIRED TELEVISION EXTRA).

"Julian," Zoe said through clenched teeth. "This was a clerical error by a volunteer in the parish office."

"This was no accidental slip of the pen, Miss Barnes! Fiona has deliberately drafted a public humiliation! A retired extra! I had four lines in an episode of Inspector Morse before I ever set foot on the marsh! I carried the entire autumn schedule of ITV for forty-six consecutive weeks! I will not stand before these turnip merchants and be announced as an extra!"

From the other side of the curtain, Fiona's voice boomed through the PA system: "Will our guest of honour please take his position at the weighing cradle? The stewards are ready to load the first exhibit."

Julian did not move. He stood like a statue of Wellington, his jaw clamped shut, his eyes fixed on the ceiling rafters.

Zoe leaned close to his ear. "Julian. If you do not walk through that curtain right now, I will personally telephone Mr Gidley in Maida Vale and inform him that your bank transfer was fraudulent."

Julian's eyes flicked down. "You wouldn't."

"I will," Zoe said, her voice dropping into the terrifying, icy certainty of a woman who has reached the absolute end of her professional endurance. "And then I will take the last fifty pounds in my purse, purchase a bottle of gin from the public bar, and leave you to carry your Samsonite suitcase down the A16 in the rain."

Julian stared at her. For the first time in five years, he saw that the assistant had vanished. In her place stood an executive authority far more formidable than any television director he had ever encountered.

He swallowed. He straightened his tie. He patted his crimson pocket square.

"You have a remarkably ruthless administrative temperament, Miss Barnes," he murmured, his baritone softening into reluctant admiration.

"Thank you, Julian," Zoe said, pushing the velvet curtain aside. "Now go out there and weigh the vegetable."

Backstage behind the velvet curtain, the argument between Julian and Zoe reached its climax as the hydraulic butcher scales settled onto the floorboards with a heavy iron thud.

Julian stood clutching his green paper programme, his eyes wide with tragic theatrical outrage. "A retired extra, Zoe! Look at the typography! Set in bold capitals! It is a public execution!"

"Julian, look at me," Zoe said, catching both of his lapels and pulling him down until his eyes were level with hers. "Do you want to go back to London as a defeated man who ran away from an agricultural show because of a piece of green paper?"

Julian blinked, his mouth slightly open, the wind taken completely out of his sails by her sudden, fierce physical proximity.

"Do you?" she demanded, her voice dropping into a thrilling, urgent whisper. "Or do you want to walk through that curtain, take command of that platform, and show three hundred people what thirty years of theatrical training can do when the stakes are real?"

Julian stared at her. Slowly, the petty wounded vanity drained from his features, replaced by a spark of genuine, authentic artistic pride that had lain dormant in his chest for a decade.

"You believe I can do it?" he whispered, his baritone trembling with quiet vulnerability.

"I know you can do it, Julian," Zoe said softly, releasing his lapels and smoothing the charcoal wool with her palms. "You are not an extra. You never were. Now go out there and be Dalrymple."

Julian drew a long, chest-expanding breath of stage dust and radiator air. He adjusted his cuffs, smoothed his four-point crimson pocket square, and turned toward the velvet curtain with the magnificent, fearless composure of a champion taking the field.

Fiona Sterling's whistle sounded shrill and clear across the lawn from the main doors of the church hall.

"Ten minutes to official weigh-in!" her voice echoed through the damp October mist. "Class Four heavy gourds will report to the assembly bay! Stewards, prepare the lifting sling!"

Zoe switched off her pen torch and stood up, brushing peat flakes from the knees of her beige trousers.

"Arthur," she said firmly.

"It's over, lass," the old man said, staring at the floor of his tent. "Five months in the polytunnel. Three hundred gallons of river water. And she's leaking sap like an old cabbage."

"Listen to me," Zoe said, grabbing his shoulder. "Julian, take Arthur's canvas coat and stand in the doorway of the tent. If anyone approaches, engage them in conversation about the history of television drama."

"I can deliver the entirety of the tavern scene from Henry the Fourth," Julian offered with instant, focused determination.

"Deliver whatever you like," Zoe said, checking her watch. "Just don't let anyone step inside this tent for the next seven minutes."

"Prepare the sling!" Donald Skelton's command cut through the murmuring hall.

Julian stepped forward to Zoe's side, his eyes shining with intense theatrical concentration.

"This is the fifth act, Zoe," he whispered. "The trial scene from The Merchant of Venice. The scales are balanced upon the table, the court is assembled, and the judgment of the realm is at hand."

"Just don't demand a pound of flesh, Julian," Zoe said under her breath. "Arthur's marrow needs every ounce it has."

Fiona Sterling glanced across at the two of them, her sharp blue eyes flicking from Julian's polished shoes to Zoe's calm, determined face.

A tiny, almost imperceptible nod passed between the two women across the chalked floor of the pavilion.

"Hoist away, Frank!" Skelton shouted, his hand dropping like a starter's flag.

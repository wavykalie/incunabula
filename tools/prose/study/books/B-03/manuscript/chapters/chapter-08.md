# Chapter 08: The Contract Clawback

In the basement corridor beside the ice machine, Zoe opened the Apex Talent Management portal on her phone.

The battery icon showed thirty-four percent. The screen brightness had dimmed to preserve power, casting a muddy amber tint across the agency's standardized client agreement. 

She swiped directly to Section 9: *CANCELLATION, DEFAULT AND PENALTIES*.

The contract was a masterpiece of contemporary talent agency cowardice. Drafted by a junior solicitor in Chancery Lane whose primary skill lay in shielding the agency from client insolvency, every single paragraph placed total financial liability upon the artist.

Zoe read the clauses aloud under her breath, her voice competing with the hum of the ice machine:

*Clause 9.1: The Artist covenants to appear at the specified venue at the contracted time, properly attired and in a fit state to perform the duties outlined in Schedule A.*

*Clause 9.2: Should the Artist fail to appear, depart the venue prior to the formal conclusion of the ceremonies, or conduct himself in a manner prejudicial to the reputation of the Promoter or the Society, the Promoter reserves the unconditional right to cancel the agreement and demand the immediate restitution of all sums paid.*

*Clause 9.3: In the event of default under Clause 9.2, the Artist shall become immediately liable to pay liquidated damages in the amount of £5,000 (five thousand pounds sterling) to the Promoter, covering administrative costs, venue hire compensation, and alternative talent procurement.*

Zoe closed her eyes and rested her forehead against the damp emulsion of the wall.

Five thousand pounds.

Julian's current bank balance, which Zoe checked every Tuesday morning from her laptop, stood at forty-two pounds and sixty-eight pence. His overdraft facility had been frozen in July following an unfortunate incident involving an antique silk dressing gown purchased at an auction in Bath. His two credit cards were maxed out to their limits, one of them incurring daily penalty charges for an unpaid dental crown.

If Julian walked out of the George and Dragon, the Horticultural Society would invoke Clause 9.3 before the six o'clock news. The bank transfer to Mr Gidley would be reversed by court order. The eviction proceedings on Elgin Avenue would proceed without right of appeal.

And worse: Zoe's own corporate credit card, issued by Apex Talent with a five-thousand-pound emergency guarantee limit, had been registered as the secondary indemnity on the booking form. Marcus had slipped that clause into the paperwork while Zoe was on the phone to British Airways in August trying to locate Julian's lost stage wig.

If Julian defaulted, the agency would deduct the five thousand pounds directly from her salary in twenty monthly installments of two hundred and fifty pounds.

She would be paying for an unjudged marrow until the summer of 2028.

"Zoe?"

Julian's head appeared around the door of Room 14b. He held a small plastic cup filled with brown tap water from the boiler room basin. His eyes were wide with genuine apprehension.

"Is the carriage arriving?" he asked. "Have you contacted the minicab office?"

Zoe slipped the phone into her coat pocket, smoothed her hair with both hands, and looked him straight in the eyes.

"No carriage is arriving, Julian," she said, her voice dropping into the cool, absolute authority of a surgeon entering an operating theatre. "We are staying here. We are doing the speech. We are smiling at Fiona. And we are going to applaud whichever pensioner grows the heaviest squash."

Julian blinked. "But Zoe, the humiliation. Fiona intends to reduce my career to a parish footnote."

"Fiona can say whatever she likes into that microphone, Julian. But unless you want to spend the winter sleeping on Marcus's sofa in Clapham while your furniture is auctioned off by the bailiffs, you are going up those stairs and you are going to give them seven minutes of pure, unadulterated Dalrymple."

Julian swallowed. He looked at the paper cup in his hand, then looked back at Zoe. Slowly, the theatrics drained from his posture, replaced by a quiet, desperate nod.

"Seven minutes," he said.

"Seven minutes," Zoe repeated. "And not a syllable about Shakespeare."

Zoe leaned against the cold tiles of the basement corridor, reading through the final schedule of Julian's agency contract on her phone.

The document was titled *SCHEDULE C: SPECIAL INDEMNITIES*.

*Paragraph 4: The Artist warrants that he shall conduct himself throughout the engagement with due decorum, maintaining the highest standards of professional presentation, and shall not bring the Promoter, the Society, or the Sponsor into public disrepute through drunkenness, political agitation, or unscripted theatrical controversy.*

*Paragraph 5: In the event of a breach of Paragraph 4, the full performance fee shall be forfeited immediately, and the Artist shall reimburse the Promoter for all legal costs incurred in defending the commercial reputation of the event.*

Zoe rubbed her temples. "Public disrepute." If Julian stood on that stage and accused Henderson of rigging the competition, the sponsor's solicitors in Peterborough would file an injunction before the local evening news went to air.

She opened her mobile banking app. Her personal current account showed an available balance of two hundred and fourteen pounds and twelve pence. Her savings account held eighty-seven pounds, earmarked for her mother's birthday train ticket to Manchester next weekend.

If Marcus deducted the clawback from her salary, she would not be able to pay her own room rent in Finsbury Park. She would be as destitute as Julian, sitting on a park bench with her laptop and a bundle of unread screenplays.

She walked back to Room 14b, pushed the door open, and looked at Julian.

He was sitting on his metal chair, practicing his vocal warm-ups in a low, rumbling hum: "Many mumbling mice making moonlight music..."

"Julian," she said.

He stopped humming. "Yes, my dear?"

"When you go up those stairs, remember one thing," Zoe said, her voice hard and clear as glass. "You are an actor. Your job today is not to fight the committee, nor to expose the sponsor, nor to settle old scores with Fiona. Your job is to perform the role of a gracious, distinguished guest of honour who knows nothing about fertilizer and cares only for the peace of the shires. Can you play that part?"

Julian looked at her for three seconds. A small, dry smile touched his lips.

"My dear Zoe," he said, rising from his chair and adjusting his crimson pocket square. "I have spent twenty-five years pretending to be things I am not. I believe I can manage seven minutes of civic benevolence."

Arthur Povey was kneeling upon a rubber mat beside the Emperor, a small camel-hair artist's brush in his right hand.

"Arthur," Zoe said gently, stepping past the safety stanchion.

"Aye, young lady."

"The schedule says the celebrity opening remarks take place at eleven precisely."

"Schedule says what it likes," Arthur mumbled, gently brushing a grain of peat moss from the pale green flank of the marrow. "Fiona thinks a giant vegetable grows to a timetable like a railway engine. Nature don't run on British Rail time."

"Mr Tremayne has his speech prepared," Zoe said.

Arthur glanced up, his small, dark eyes resting upon Zoe's face. "The actor looks like he hasn't had a proper hot dinner since Tuesday."

"He had half a pork pie at Grantham station," Zoe said defensively.

Arthur grunted, reaching into the deep pocket of his canvas smock. He produced an apple, green and slightly bruised, wrapped in a square of greaseproof paper. "Give him that. Russet from my orchard. Keeps the stomach settled when the wind turns."

Arthur was polishing a small brass plate with a piece of oily rag.

"What is that plate, Arthur?" Zoe asked.

"Challenge cup base," the old man replied. "Won it three times running between eighty-four and eighty-six. If you win it four times, you keep the plinth for good. That's what I'm after today. The plinth."

Julian leaned forward, inspecting the worn lettering. "Povey. Povey. Povey. Three names carved into the mahogany. A permanent memorial to fenland horticultural excellence."

"It's just seasoned oak," Arthur said, though his eyes lingered on the brass. "My grandson wants to make a clock case out of it. Told him he'll have to wait till I'm in the churchyard before he puts a pendulum on my marrow stand."

"A very proper sentiment," Julian agreed. "The artist must defend his copyright to the very end."

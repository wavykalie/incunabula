# Chapter 36: The Agency Call

At nine o'clock, Zoe's phone finally resumed full service as she stood by the porch doors of the hotel, breathing the damp night air.

The screen illuminated with twelve missed calls and four text messages, all bearing the caller identification: *MARCUS — APEX TALENT*.

Before she could slip the phone back into her pocket, the device vibrated violently in her palm. The screen displayed Marcus's professional headshot, showing him smiling in a linen shirt against a backdrop of exposed Soho brickwork.

Zoe answered. "Hello, Marcus."

"Zoe! Thank God!" Marcus's voice crackled through the earpiece, loud with the frantic, caffeinated urgency of a talent agent who had spent his evening in a cocktail bar managing a PR crisis. "Where have you been? I've been ringing you since four o'clock! Why wasn't Julian's phone responding?"

"Julian's phone battery died at two o'clock, Marcus," Zoe said calmly. "And the signal in Market Deeping is intermittent."

"Listen to me," Marcus said, speaking so fast his words tripped over each other. "We have a catastrophic situation. The distributor for the Nordic streaming platform has moved the launch date for the Scandinavian crime retrospective to Monday morning. They need Julian in Stockholm for a press junket on Sunday afternoon."

Zoe looked out across the gravel car park, where two allotmenteers were loading a wooden sack barrow into the back of an estate car under the amber glow of the porch lantern.

"Julian is currently in Lincolnshire, Marcus," Zoe said.

"I know where he is! Did he finish the agricultural thing? Did he cut the ribbon?"

"He presented the awards, Marcus. He delivered an exceptional keynote address. The entire town gave him a standing ovation."

"Splendid, brilliant, whatever," Marcus dismissed with an impatient click of his tongue. "Listen, I've booked him on the six-forty flight from Heathrow to Arlanda tomorrow morning. You need to get him into a minicab right now, drive to London tonight, pack his winter wardrobe, and have him at Terminal Two by five a.m."

Zoe drew a slow, deep breath of the cold Fenland air. She felt the heavy brass room key to the twin annex in her pocket, resting beside Arthur Povey's envelope of champion seeds.

"No, Marcus," Zoe said.

A sudden, stunned silence fell over the London end of the line. 

"What did you say?" Marcus asked, his tone shifting from manic authority to sharp disbelief.

"I said no," Zoe replied, her voice dropping into a register of cool, absolute administrative sovereignty. "All rail services on the East Coast Main Line are suspended until tomorrow morning due to a signaling cable fire at Grantham. There are no minicabs available in South Kesteven on a Saturday night. Mr Tremayne has performed seven hours of exhausting public appearances on behalf of the agency, and he is currently asleep in the hotel annex."

"Zoe, are you out of your mind?" Marcus hissed. "This is a twelve-thousand-euro international promotional contract! If he misses that flight, the agency loses the commission!"

"Then the agency should have checked the train timetable before booking him into an agricultural awards gala seventy miles from the capital, Marcus," Zoe said with icy precision. "Mr Tremayne will take the eight-fifteen train from Grantham tomorrow morning, arriving at King's Cross at nine-thirty. If you wish to send him to Stockholm, you may re-book his flight for Sunday evening."

"You don't dictate terms to me, Zoe!" Marcus shouted. "You are an assistant! I pay your salary!"

"Actually, Marcus," Zoe said, smiling into the autumn darkness, "Apex Talent has not paid my salary expenses for August or September. And unless the outstanding travel vouchers are settled in full into my account by Monday noon, you will be handling Mr Tremayne's luggage yourself."

She tapped the red end-call icon with her thumb.

The screen went black. Zoe looked up at the stars beginning to appear through the breaking clouds above the church spire of Market Deeping. She had never felt so completely, magnificently alive.

After terminating the call with Marcus, Zoe leaned against the damp brickwork of the hotel porch, taking three deep breaths of the cold night air.

Her phone screen showed three percent battery, but her heart was beating with calm, steady confidence. In five years, she had never spoken to Marcus with anything other than cautious, apologetic deference. She had accepted his broken promises about salary increases, his late expense repayments, and his endless demands for emergency weekend travel.

Tonight, she had told him no.

She looked down at her tote bag. Inside lay her notebook, her iPad, and the small brown envelope of Arthur Povey's champion seeds. Those seeds represented fifty years of patient, unhurried craft: the antithesis of the frantic, disposable world that Marcus inhabited.

She walked back through the double doors of the porch. In the resident lounge, the fire was burning low in the grate, throwing warm orange light across the empty armchairs. She felt as if a heavy iron yoke had been lifted from her shoulders. She was no longer Julian's servant; she was his ally, seeing him through the final hours of his campaign before stepping into her own future.

Arthur Povey found Zoe in the hallway outside the cloakroom.

"Miss Barnes."

"Arthur. Congratulations again. A wonderful victory."

He looked around the empty corridor, then reached into his smock pocket and pulled out a small brown manila envelope, sealed with red wax.

"What is this?" she asked.

"Seeds," Arthur said gruffly, pressing the envelope into her hands. "From the Emperor's sister plant. Grown in nineteen eighty-four and back-crossed three times. You keep them dry in a tin. When you get yourself a bit of ground, you put two in the soil come April. They'll give you fifty pounds of marrow before the first frost."

Zoe looked down at the envelope. "Arthur, I live in a fourth-floor flat in Finsbury Park. I only have a window box."

"Then you get a big pot," Arthur said firmly. "A marrow don't care about Finsbury Park, so long as you feed the root."

Arthur Povey walked with Zoe to the entrance porch, carrying a wooden box of heirloom apples.

"Here," he said, setting the box down on the gravel. "Bramleys for baking. Good for pies."

"Arthur, you have already given me the seeds."

"A girl living in London needs proper fruit," the old man insisted stubbornly. "City apples taste like damp cotton wool. These have got fen acid in them. Clear your head in the morning."

Zoe touched his arm, genuinely moved. "Thank you, Arthur. For everything."

"You did the engineering, lass," Arthur said with a twinkle in his eye. "Remember what I said about the pots. Two seeds, plenty of muck, and keep the frost off."

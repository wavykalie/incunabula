# Chapter 09: Artie Povey's Warning

The tea station had been set up in an alcove near the emergency exit of the Rutland Suite, under a faded print of the 1977 Queen's Silver Jubilee.

It consisted of a thirty-litre stainless steel Burco water boiler that groaned continuously on its heating element, two brown plastic washing-up bowls filled with chipped porcelain mugs, three boxes of catering-pack tea bags, and four commercial tins of assorted biscuits from which all the chocolate bourbons had already been extracted.

Zoe stood beside the boiler, waiting for the tap to produce enough boiling water to dissolve a packet of instant decaffeinated coffee. Her head throbbed in time with the radiator pipes.

A heavy hand landed upon the timber table beside her mug with the force of an falling fence post.

Zoe did not jump. She looked down at the hand. The knuckles were scarred and calloused, with dark rings of peat ground indelibly into the cuticle beds. A copper wristband, worn green with age, encircled the hairy wrist.

She looked up. Arthur Povey stood beside her, his breath smelling of strong rolling tobacco and peppermint lozenges.

"You're his keeper, then," Povey said, nodding toward the stage where Julian was currently pacing behind the curtains, testing his vocal projection with low hums.

"I am Mr Tremayne's executive assistant, Mr Povey," Zoe said, picking up a wooden stirrer.

"Same difference," Povey grunted. He reached into his coat pocket, produced a battered silver tin of Golden Virginia tobacco, and rolled a cigarette with one thumb and forefinger with terrifying mechanical precision. He did not light it; he simply tucked it behind his left ear. "Listen to me, young woman. Your fancy actor wants to watch his step this afternoon."

Zoe paused with the stirrer. "Mr Tremayne has no intention of interfering with the judging, Mr Povey. He is here purely to present the awards."

"That's what she says," Povey muttered, his small blue eyes darting toward the dais where Fiona was conferring with two elderly stewards in green armbands. "Fiona Sterling. She thinks because her grandfather had fifty acres of beet in forty-eight, she owns the fen. But I know what's in that wooden crate out by the bins."

Zoe felt a prickle of alertness at the back of her neck. "What wooden crate?"

Povey leaned down until his whiskers brushed the collar of her trench coat. "Young Henderson. The sponsor's boy. He drove in from King's Lynn at eleven o'clock in a covered horsebox. Frank seen him unloading a timber pallet behind the kitchen fire door. It had four sacks of wet sheep fleece over the top to keep the moisture in."

"A marrow?" Zoe asked.

"Not just a marrow," Povey growled, his knuckles whitening on the edge of the trestle. "A Norfolk hybrid. Grown on chemical effluent from the sugar factory at Wissington. They pump them full of liquid urea and heated sludge through black plastic pipes. It ain't natural farming. It's industrial cheating."

"The rules allow hybrid seeds, Mr Povey," Zoe noted, recalling the bylaws she had skimmed five minutes earlier.

"The rules allow seeds grown in the historic drainage district of Kesteven," Povey snapped, jabbing a blunt thumb toward the floorboards. "Rule 14b, subsection four! Any exhibit originating east of the River Nene is foreign produce! It belongs in the East Anglian open class, not the South District Challenge Cup!"

He leaned closer still, his voice dropping into a rasping conspiratorial hiss.

"Henderson already has his name on the brass plinth, Miss Barnes. I saw it through the stage curtains. Fiona had it cut yesterday at the cobbler's in Bourne. She's planning to declare my Emperor disqualified on a technicality—some rot about surface skin fissures—and hand the silver cup to Henderson to secure his firm's sponsorship for next year's seed trial."

Zoe stared at him. "Has she inspected your marrow yet?"

"Not yet," Povey whispered, his jaw setting into a hard, granite ridge. "She sends the stewards round at quarter to four. If she finds so much as a thumbnail scrape on the belly, she rules it cracked skin under Rule 14b. And once she rules, there's no appeal. She's the magistrate, ain't she? She does what she likes."

He reached into his pocket, pulled out a small brown paper seed envelope, and slid it across the green baize toward Zoe's coffee mug.

"What is this?" Zoe asked, looking at the pencil notation on the envelope: *POVEY EMPEROR LINE — 1984 BACK-CROSS*.

"Insurance," Povey said. "That seed came off the sixty-eight-pounder my late wife pruned in eighty-four. If your actor stands up on that stage and reads Henderson's name off that cup without the balance arm touching the deck, I'm releasing the affidavit to the Lincolnshire Echo. But if he insists on a public weigh-in—in front of the whole hall, with the weights called out loud—those seeds are yours. You could sell them to a German grower for five hundred quid."

Zoe looked at the brown envelope. She did not touch it.

"Mr Povey," she said softly, "Mr Tremayne is an actor, not a horticultural tribunal."

"He's the guest of honour," Povey retorted, turning away toward the tables. "And in this county, the guest signs the sheet. You tell him to look at the scales before he touches that cup. Or there'll be glass on the floor before six."

Beside the Burco tea urn in the Rutland Suite, Arthur Povey was still nursing his rolling tobacco, watching the entrance to the hall like a hawk on a fence post.

"You look like a sensible girl, Miss Barnes," Povey said, leaning against the trestle table until the green baize creaked beneath his weight. "You have got a London coat on, but you have got the eyes of someone who knows what an invoice looks like."

"I handle Mr Tremayne's accounts, Mr Povey," Zoe said, stirring her decaffeinated coffee.

"Then you know about mechanical advantage," the old grower grunted, using the words in their physical sense. "Young Henderson thinks he can lever that seventy-four-pounder into the cradle and walk away with the silver. But a marrow grown on chemical feed has no heart, Miss Barnes. It's like an over-inflated inner tube. One little prick on the skin, and the whole thing goes pop."

"Has his marrow got a flaw?" Zoe asked, keeping her voice casual.

Povey tapped the side of his nose with a gnarled peat-stained finger. "Frank seen it when they unloaded the crate. There's a soft spot near the stem, no bigger than a sixpence, where the chemical drip line was attached. If Fiona inspects the rind with her brass glass, she'll see the puncture mark clear as daylight. But she won't look, will she? Because Henderson's dad promised to sponsor the society tea towels for the next three years."

Zoe looked across the hall to Table 6, where the Emperor rested on its padded cotton cushions like a sleeping dragon.

"And your marrow, Mr Povey?" Zoe asked. "Is it sound?"

Povey's face softened, the suspicion draining away to reveal forty years of quiet, solitary passion. "Grown on horse manure from the dray horses at the brewery, Miss Barnes. Sown on the fifteenth of April under a double frame of glass, watered with rainwater from the butt every evening at seven o'clock. Not a chemical drop in her. She's sound from crown to tail. If the scales are honest, she'll pull seventy-five pounds without a creak."

He tucked his blue handkerchief back into his pocket and picked up his pruning knife.

"Just tell your actor to watch the balance arm," Povey said, his voice dropping into a solemn whisper. "If the balance touches seventy-five, he must call it out before Fiona can reach for her pen. That's all I ask. An honest reading for an honest fruit."

"Arthur gave you an apple, Julian," Zoe said, offering the paper-wrapped fruit.

Julian took it with two fingers, examining the mottled skin with delicate appreciation. "A gesture of peasant fealty, Miss Barnes. The soil acknowledges the artist."

"It is a russet, Julian. Eat it before your sugar drops."

Julian took a crisp, audible bite, his eyebrows lifting in genuine surprise. "Superb. Sharp, acidic, with a lingering note of dry cider. Quite magnificent. Reminds me of the orchard behind the rectory in Barchester."

"You never played Barchester, Julian."

"I read the pilot script," he said between bites. "A very evocative reading. I was considered very seriously for the Archdeacon."

"Eat the apple, Julian."

"I am eating the apple, Zoe. You are remarkably persistent today."

"Because if you faint during the ribbon-cutting, Fiona Sterling will bill Marcus for the ambulance call-out."

"Julian," Zoe said, handing him a paper napkin. "You have mustard on your left cuff."

"Mustard? Impossible."

"From the pork pie you ate behind the paraffin heater."

Julian dabbed at his sleeve with dramatic dismay. "A catastrophic blemish. Colman's English mustard is notoriously aggressive upon worsted wool. Quick, Zoe, your bottle of mineral water. A drop of carbonated moisture on the corner of the linen."

Zoe uncapped the bottle and applied three precise drops to the napkin.

"Gently, my dear," Julian cautioned, wincing. "Rub with the grain of the cloth, not against it. A good twill has a delicate constitution."

"The twill will survive, Julian," Zoe said, working the cloth with steady fingers. "The stain has lifted completely."

Julian inspected the cuff in the pale daylight from the window. "A miracle of domestic science, Miss Barnes. You are an indispensable member of the company."

"I am an unpaid member of the company," Zoe reminded him. "Until Fiona signs that cheque."

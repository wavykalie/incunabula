# Chapter 06: The Green Room Boiler

The designated green room was not a green room. It was Room 14b, situated in the basement corridor between the staff changing quarters and the beer cellar, and its primary architectural function was the housing of a nineteen-eighty-two commercial cast-iron heating boiler.

The boiler was cylindrical, painted in flaking silver heat-resistant enamel, and wrapped in thick jackets of deteriorating fiberglass insulation secured with silver duct tape. It shuddered every forty seconds with a violent mechanical thud that rattled the radiator pipes, followed by a sustained, roaring hiss that sounded like a steam locomotive ascending a steep gradient in a gale.

Beside the boiler, crammed into a space roughly seven feet wide by nine feet long, sat two folding metal chairs with peeling gray vinyl seats, a wooden ironing board, and twelve plastic laundry hampers overflowing with soiled hotel tablecloths.

Julian sat upon one of the folding chairs, his knees drawn up toward his chest to avoid touching the hot water pipes that ran along the skirting board. His head rested against his knuckles; his patent leather shoes were tucked neatly under the rung; his silk pocket square drooped from his breast pocket like a wilted tulip.

"A supporting constable," he whispered, his voice vibrating in sympathy with the boiler's combustion cycle. "She introduced me to three hundred vegetable farmers as a supporting constable. Zoe, I was the titular lead! Dalrymple was the detective inspector! The supporting constable was played by a young lad from Hull who forgot his lines in the quarry scene and had to be edited out with reaction shots of a sheepdog!"

Zoe stood beside the ironing board, using her phone torch to inspect the contents of her emergency kit. She found a strip of ibuprofen, two safety pins, a lint roller, and three cherry antacid tablets. She popped one antacid into her mouth, chewing the chalky tablet with grim, rhythmic determination.

"She was attempting to provoke you, Julian," Zoe said. "It is an elementary negotiating tactic. If you lose your temper, she controls the narrative. If you remain impeccably professional, she looks petty."

"She was born petty," Julian groaned, rocking slightly upon the metal seat. "Do you know what she did on the day our decree nisi arrived? She sent me an itemized invoice for twelve years of laundry detergent. She had calculated the exact proportion of Persil powder consumed by my dress shirts between nineteen ninety-nine and two thousand and eleven. She charged me three hundred and forty-two pounds and eighteen pence!"

"And did you pay it?"

"Of course I did not pay it! I countered with an invoice for eighteen months of theatrical vocal coaching she received when she attempted to play Lady Bracknell with the Stamford Amateurs! We ended up in a binding mediation before a retired county court judge in Peterborough!"

He sighed, letting his arms drop limply between his knees. His gaze fell upon a stack of cardboard boxes shoved beneath the lower shelf of the linen rack.

The top box was water-damaged, its flaps held together by yellowed packing tape that had lost its adhesive grip. Through the gap in the cardboard, a glossy black-and-white surface caught the orange glow of the boiler pilot light.

Julian reached down, hooked his fingers under the cardboard flap, and pulled.

The tape parted with a dry tear. Inside lay four hundred glossy ten-by-eight publicity photographs, wrapped in bundles of fifty with perished rubber bands.

Julian pulled the top photograph from the bundle and stared at it in the dim light.

It was a headshot from 1999. In the image, Julian was thirty-seven years old, his jaw sharp and lean, his dark hair falling across his forehead in artful waves, his eyes gazing into the middle distance with brooding, detective intensity. Across the lower white border, printed in silver foil lettering, ran the legend: JULIAN TREMAYNE, DI PETER DALRYMPLE, WHISPERS ON THE MARSH, EXCLUSIVE MANAGEMENT: ICM LONDON.

Julian turned the photograph over. On the reverse, someone had written in black felt-tip marker, in Fiona's unmistakable handwriting: DUMP IN RECYCLING SKIP, DO NOT RETURN TO LONDON FLAT.

Julian held the photograph against his waistcoat. His fingers trembled slightly against the glossy paper.

"She kept them," he whispered. "Twelve years in a hotel cellar beside the laundry baskets. She told me the removal men had lost the entire archive on the A1."

Zoe looked at the photograph, then looked at Julian. The boiler gave another shuddering clank, releasing a cloud of warm, metallic steam from its pressure relief valve. 

"She did not dump them in the skip, Julian," Zoe noted quietly. "She put them in a box with tape."

Julian looked up, his eyes suddenly bright with an absurd, fragile hope. "Do you think so, Zoe? Do you suppose that there remains some lingering vestige of respect? A memory of our shared artistic youth?"

"I think she probably forgot they were down here," Zoe said, refusing to indulge his melodrama. "Now sit still. I need to lint-roll your shoulders. You have boiler soot on your left lapel, and your speech begins in nine minutes."

In the basement boiler room, Julian paced the seven-foot concrete floor between the hot water pipes and the stacks of dirty tablecloths.

Each stride took him three paces forward before he had to pivot on his heels to avoid the boiler casing. The heat in the room was intense, radiating from the unlagged copper elbows and filling the small space with the dry, baked smell of scorching enamel.

"Room 14b," he muttering, gesturing at the walls with his silver comb. "A linen cupboard. A damp, subterranean coal hole. When I opened the civic centre in Grimsby in ninety-six, the mayor presented me with a bottle of vintage port and a bouquet of red roses for my dressing room. There were two armchairs, Zoe! Real leather armchairs and a bowl of fresh fruit!"

"This is not Grimsby in ninety-six, Julian," Zoe said, sitting on the corner of the wooden ironing board and checking her phone. "This is Market Deeping on a wet Saturday. And if we stay down here, at least nobody can see your shoe."

Julian stopped his pacing, staring down at the water-damaged cardboard box of publicity photographs he had uncovered beneath the linen rack.

He picked up a second photograph from the bundle. This one showed him in full costume as DI Dalrymple: standing beside a rusty sluice gate on the fen, wearing his wax jacket and a woollen flat cap, his hands resting on his hips as he gazed grimly toward the horizon.

"Look at the jawline, Zoe," he said, holding the print to the yellow lightbulb. "Flinty. Resolute. The critics called it an iconic representation of English stoicism. The Daily Telegraph said Dalrymple was the spiritual heir to Inspector Morse."

"You were very handsome, Julian," Zoe said, feeling a sudden pang of sympathy for the aging man before her.

"I was relevant, Zoe," he said quietly, his voice dropping into absolute, unvarnished honesty. "That is the terrible thing. For five years, people stopped me in the street to ask about the plot. They cared about what Dalrymple thought. And when the show ended... the phone simply stopped ringing. One week you are in the TV Times, and the next week you are sitting in a boiler room in Lincolnshire while your former wife laughs at your shoes."

He set the photograph down gently on the cardboard box, covering it with a dry paper napkin as if concealing a painful secret.

"Miss Barnes," Fiona said, appearing at Zoe's elbow with the silent efficiency of a hunting owl.

"Mrs Sterling."

"Your principal is wandering toward the floral arrangements," Fiona observed, her pencil pointing like a bayonet toward Julian, who was currently sniffing a vase of late-blooming chrysanthemums. "The flower guild has spent two days constructing the commemorative harvest arch. If he dislodges the oasis foam, Mrs Clout will take it as a personal insult."

"Julian is merely admiring the composition," Zoe said smoothly. "He played Oberon in ninety-one."

"Oberon was in an Athenian wood, Miss Barnes. This is the Deeping St James Church Hall. We do not permit unscripted revels amongst the begonias."

"I will ensure he remains on the central carpet."

"Do so," Fiona said, turning her sharp blue eyes toward Zoe's beige raincoat. "And Miss Barnes?"

"Yes?"

"You have chalk dust on your left shoulder. This is a public exhibition, not a rehearsal room in Soho. Maintain some standard of presentation."

"Miss Barnes," Fiona called out across the central aisle.

"Coming, Mrs Sterling," Zoe answered, quickening her pace.

"The St John Ambulance volunteer requires a signed indemnification form for the urn in the refreshment marquee," Fiona said, thrusting a buff-coloured form into Zoe's hands. "The thermostat on the water boiler has an intermittent fault. If it scalds an exhibitor, the society's liability is capped at fifty pounds, provided the warning placard is displayed at eye level."

"I will ensure the placard is positioned correctly," Zoe said.

Fiona looked at her closely, her sharp eyes taking in the neatness of Zoe's dark hair and the clean collar of her blouse.

"You have a tidy mind, Miss Barnes," Fiona observed, her voice softening by a fraction of a degree. "It is a pity you waste it managing Julian's administrative wreckage. A woman with your organizational capacity could run a district hospital."

"Julian needs looking after, Mrs Sterling."

"Julian is a grown man of sixty-four years who still believes that money arrives in envelopes from the sky," Fiona stated crisply. "However, I concede that his trousers are pressed. That, at least, is an improvement upon our honeymoon."

# Chapter 31: Guarding the Vegetable

The produce storeroom at the back of the tractor shed had formerly served as an oil storage depot for the parish drainage board. 

The walls were built of unpointed engineering brick, sweating with condensation in the October chill, and the single unshaded lightbulb was protected by a wire mesh basket that cast diamond-shaped shadows across six empty diesel drums.

Upon an iron worktable in the centre of the room rested Arthur Povey's Emperor, resting safely upon its padded canvas sling.

Arthur Povey sat upon an overturned plastic fertilizer tub, his curved horn-handled pruning knife resting across his knees, sharpening the edge with an oiled carborundum stone with slow, hypnotic strokes. Each scrape of the stone produced a fine, rhythmic hiss that blended with the drumming of the rain on the corrugated roof.

Julian Tremayne stood beside the locked fire door, his back straight, holding his black umbrella upright against his trouser leg like a sentry's rifle. He had turned up the collar of his tailored jacket, and a smudge of soot from the boiler room still lingered above his left temple, giving his aristocratic profile the weathered aspect of an officer on foreign service.

"They won't come back, Arthur," Julian said, his baritone quiet and steady. "The young man's nerve broke the moment he realized the police were an immediate reality. I have seen the type on countless occasions. The bullies of the rural shires rely entirely upon isolation and municipal silence. Once the light of public scrutiny is cast upon them, they retreat to their vehicles."

Povey paused with the carborundum stone, wiping the blade on his thumb. "Henderson's father has got twenty men on the beet harvesters down at Holbeach, Julian. When the pubs turn out at ten, they'll be full of rough cider and looking for trouble."

"Let them come," Julian replied, resting his hand upon the curved timber crook of the umbrella. "In ninety-seven, during the filming of the shipyard riots in Hull, we were besieged in the production trailer by forty dockers armed with scaffolding poles. I went out onto the steps in my full police regalia, delivered the St Crispin's Day speech from Henry V, and had them singing sea shanties in the rain within twenty minutes. The mob is susceptible to cadence, Arthur. One simply has to master the pitch."

Zoe sat upon a wooden crate of spare tractor gaskets near the door, drinking cold tea from a chipped enamel mug. Her heels were speckled with wet gravel, and her trench coat was damp across the shoulders, but her headache had receded into a dull, manageable throb.

She looked at the two men. 

An unemployed television actor who hadn't booked a speaking part since the late nineties, and a seventy-one-year-old widower who talked to sixty-pound vegetables. Two hours ago, they had been strangers separated by fifty years of metropolitan pretension and rural suspicion. Now, united by a hairline crack in a marrow rind and a shared contempt for an agricultural feed merchant, they stood shoulder to shoulder in an unheated oil shed like brothers in arms.

A sharp thud rattled the timber fire door from the outside lane.

Julian did not startle. He shifted his grip upon the umbrella, stepping smoothly into the centre of the doorway.

"Who goes there?" Julian demanded, his voice ringing out with terrifying military authority that caused a pigeon in the rafters to take flight.

"It's Frank, mate!" a muffled voice called through the keyhole. "Open the latch! The pipe band has arrived from Bourne, and Fiona says the hall is packed to the rafters!"

Arthur Povey rose from his plastic tub, sliding his pruning knife into the leather sheath on his hip. He looked at Julian, offering a slow, gap-toothed grin that transformed his weathered face.

"Time for the coronation, governor," Povey said. "Let us take the Emperor to the stage."

The wind outside the tractor shed had shifted to the north-east, blowing off the salt marshes of the Wash with the bitter bite of approaching winter.

Julian Tremayne paced the narrow concrete floor between the diesel drums, his patent leather shoes clicking with crisp theatrical precision. He paused before an unglazed window opening covered with wire chicken mesh, gazing out into the dark delivery lane where the rain was splashing against the gravel.

"Observe the tactical situation, Arthur," Julian said, his baritone full of conspiratorial gravity. "In ninety-six, when we staged the ambush sequence in the second series, the production designer created a fortified farmstead in the fens. We had three camera units stationed on the perimeter. The key to defensive security, my good man, is the control of the chokepoints. As long as we hold the fire door, the Emperor is impregnable."

Arthur Povey blew through his white moustache, testing the curved edge of his pruning knife against his thumbnail. "I don't know about chokepoints, Julian, but I know young Henderson's father has got two cousins on the parish council and an uncle who owns the quarry at Helpston. When money gets beaten by horse muck in public, money gets spiteful."

"Let money rage," Julian said, his chin lifting with magnificent patrician disdain. "Money cannot buy forty years of soil nitrogen. Money cannot command the poetry of the marrow. We have the moral high ground, Arthur, and we have an eight-pound umbrella."

Zoe looked at her watch. It was twenty past seven. The pipe band had begun warming up in the front lobby, the skirl of the drones drifting through the service corridor like the cry of a distant sea bird. She finished her cold tea, stood up from the gasket crate, and brushed the limestone dust from her trousers.

"The stewards will be here in five minutes, gentlemen," Zoe announced, taking charge of the room. "Julian, button your waistcoat. Arthur, put the pruning knife in its sheath. We are entering that ballroom in triumph."

"Look at the dial, Zoe!" Julian gasped, gripping her elbow with theatrical fervor.

The black iron pointer trembled between seventy-six and seventy-seven pounds. Donald Skelton tapped the casing with his wooden pencil. The needle settled with a dry, mechanical click upon seventy-six pounds, eight ounces.

"Seventy-six pounds, eight ounces!" Skelton roared to the roof timbers. "A new All-England and District Championship record!"

A tremendous cheer went up from the fenland growers. Men in flat caps threw their programmes into the air. Two stewards from Bourne clapped each other on the back with such force that a tray of brass commemorative spoons clattered to the floor.

Arthur Povey did not shout. He stood before the scale, his hands buried deep in the pockets of his smock, his grey head bowed. Two tears ran down the deep furrows of his weathered cheeks, disappearing into his grey stubble.

Julian stepped forward, took Arthur by both hands, and raised them aloft. "Triumph, my dear Arthur! A triumph for the Fen! The earth has spoken!"

"Zoe," Julian said, his eyes still bright as the crowd surged around the weighing platform.

"Yes, Julian?"

"The local committee has invited us to join them for a celebratory glass of sherry in the vestry."

"We have seven minutes before the presentation ceremony," she said, checking her timetable. "Drink the sherry, Julian, but do not promise to perform King Lear in the churchyard next August."

"A passing thought, my dear," Julian admitted with a sheepish grin. "The Norman arch would have provided a magnificent acoustic backdrop for the heath scene."

"The Norman arch is currently supporting the parish noticeboard, Julian," Zoe said. "Keep your focus on the silver rose bowl."

"Always practical, Miss Barnes," he sighed happily. "The anchor of my artistic ship."

The All-England and District record certificate was brought out from the committee office in a gilt frame that had been wiped clean with a duster.

Fiona Sterling presented the document to Arthur Povey with formal, ceremonial precision.

"Arthur Povey," Fiona said, her voice ringing clearly across the quieted hall. "The Deeping and District Horticultural Society hereby records your achievement of seventy-six pounds, eight ounces, certified under official inspection."

Arthur took the certificate in both hands. He did not look at the camera; he did not look at the applauding crowd. He looked at Fiona.

"Thank you, Fiona," he said simply. "My old father would have been glad to see this."

Fiona's stern expression softened into an authentic, affectionate smile that transformed her face, peeling twenty years of administrative severity from her eyes.

"He would have been proud of you, Arthur," she said softly. "Very proud indeed."

Julian stood beside them, his eyes moist with genuine emotion, his hand resting lightly upon the brass rim of the scale.

"An unforgettable afternoon," Julian whispered to Zoe. "An afternoon that restores one's faith in the dignity of our national life."

# Chapter 14: Mirror in the Stalls

In Room 14b, Julian sat before a cracked shaving mirror propped against a stack of clean linen sheets, applying theatrical pancake makeup with an oval latex sponge.

The single bare lightbulb hanging from the ceiling cast harsh, yellow shadows beneath his cheekbones, exaggerating the fine web of lines around his eyes and the gray stubble emerging along his jaw. He worked with practiced, rhythmic precision, dabbing the tinted cream over his temples to blend the silver streaks into his dark hairline, then dusting his forehead with translucent rice powder from a circular cardboard tub.

Zoe entered quietly, closing the boiler room door behind her to shut out the smell of stale beer and frying fish.

"Fiona knows about Gidley," Zoe said, setting her tote bag down upon the ironing board.

Julian's hand paused in mid-air. The latex sponge hovered two inches from his left cheekbone. For three long seconds, the only sound in the room was the steady, rhythmic drip of water into the paint tin.

"She spoke to Marcus?" Julian asked, his voice barely louder than a whisper.

"Marcus told her on Tuesday to secure the advance," Zoe replied. "She knows the rent was overdue. She knows about the service charge. She knows about the carpet warehouse in Scunthorpe."

Julian did not boom. He did not drop into classical verse. He slowly lowered his hand, set the sponge down upon the edge of the ironing board, and looked at his reflection in the cracked mirror.

Without the theatrical projection, his face looked old. The skin beneath his eyes was thin and bruised with fatigue; his shoulders, stripped of his tailored suit jacket, appeared narrow and fragile beneath his cotton shirt.

"Do you know when I last worked, Zoe?" he asked softly.

Zoe leaned against the doorframe. "You recorded the radio commercial for the building society in July, Julian."

"A forty-second commercial for a regional building society," he said, turning his head slightly to inspect the gray hair above his ears. "Recorded in a basement booth in Soho in twenty minutes. Twelve hundred pounds before agency deductions. That was July. Before that, nothing since the murder mystery weekend at a golf hotel in Cheshire in November."

He picked up a clean paper tissue and wiped a smudge of powder from his cuff.

"Six months without a single speaking audition," he murmured. "My casting agent does not return Marcus's calls. The BBC casting department does not acknowledge my headshots. When I walk down the street in Maida Vale, occasionally an elderly woman with a shopping trolley stops me to ask if I am still on television, and I tell her that I am reading scripts for a new autumn drama. But there are no scripts, Zoe. There has not been a script on my kitchen table since two thousand and nineteen."

Zoe looked at him. In five years of managing his diaries, fielding his creditors, and listening to his endless anecdotes about theatrical luncheons at the Garrick Club, this was the first time she had ever heard him speak without an audience in his mind.

"You are a fine actor, Julian," she said, surprised by the sudden, genuine warmth in her own voice.

Julian gave a sad, self-deprecating smile that crinkled the makeup around his mouth. "I was an adequate television detective, my dear. I had a good profile in a wax jacket, and I knew how to pause before delivering the arrest warrant. But the world moves on. They want young lads from drama school with northern accents and six-packs who can play cyber-security consultants. They have no use for a fifty-four-year-old baritone who knows how to speak blank verse."

He picked up his tailored jacket from the back of the metal chair, shook the sleeves out, and slipped his arms into the silk lining.

"Fiona is right, of course," he added, adjusting his cuffs with practiced care. "I am an absurd, vain creature. But I will not let her see me crawl. If this is to be my final public appearance, Miss Barnes, I intend to conduct myself like a gentleman."

He turned to the mirror, checked the four points of his crimson silk pocket square, and drew himself up to his full height.

"Now," he said, his famous baritone returning with quiet, resonant dignity. "Tell me about the Norfolk marrow. Old Artie said there was some question regarding the legality of the stem."

Julian sat back in his metal folding chair, setting the latex makeup sponge down upon the edge of the wooden ironing board.

"Do you remember the audition for the BBC legal series in twenty twenty-one, Zoe?" he asked softly, staring at his reflection in the cracked glass. "The one where the director asked me if I could play a retired judge with a gambling addiction?"

"I remember, Julian," Zoe said, leaning against the boiler casing. "We spent three days rehearsing the monologue from The Merchant of Venice in your kitchen."

"I was prepared, Zoe," he whispered, his fingers tracing the edge of his collar. "I knew every syllable. I walked into the room, and there were three young women sitting behind a trestle table with iced lattes, looking at their iPads. The lead producer did not even look up when I spoke. She simply checked my Instagram follower count on her phone and said: 'Thank you, Julian, we are looking for someone with a more contemporary digital footprint.'"

He gave a short, bitter chuckle that died in the hum of the central heating pipes.

"A digital footprint," he murmured. "Thirty years on the British stage, two BAFTA nominations, forty-six episodes of prime-time television... and my career was judged by the number of teenagers following my Twitter account."

Zoe stepped forward and placed her hand firmly upon his shoulder. "That producer was twenty-three years old, Julian. She had never seen a production of Lear in her life."

"Perhaps," Julian said, looking up at her with sudden, moist clarity in his eyes. "But she had the power, my dear. That is the tragedy of the modern theatre. The gatekeepers have no memory."

In the payphone kiosk beside the emergency exit, Zoe inserted her final fifty-pence piece into the coin slot.

The line clicked four times before Marcus's voice crackled through the earpiece, surrounded by the ambient clatter of cutlery and laughter.

"Marcus?"

"Zoe! Darling girl! How is the rustic paradise? Are the peasants worshipping at the shrine of Dalrymple?"

"Marcus, where are you?"

"Just having a quick working luncheon at the French House," Marcus shouted over the din of Soho. "Networking, Zoe. Essential industry groundwork. I had a very promising conversation with a development producer from Granada about an afternoon panel show."

"Did you pay the BT bill, Marcus?"

"Monday morning, sweetheart! First thing when the bank opens! Word of honour!"

"Marcus, the landlord in Elgin Avenue has threatened bailiffs."

"Landlords always threaten bailiffs, darling! It is part of their theatrical DNA! Tell Julian to sign a few autographs, charm his ex-wife, and collect the cash in a brown envelope! Must dash, the steak frites has just arrived!"

The line went dead. Zoe replaced the receiver with a slow, deliberate click.

In the quiet corner beside the fire hose reel, Zoe sat on an upturned wooden crate, reviewing her expenditure receipts.

Grantham railway tickets: seventy-four pounds.

Minicab: twenty-six pounds.

Emergency adhesive from the Bourne veterinary counter: eight pounds fifty.

Hotel incidentals: eighty-one pounds forty.

Total disbursements incurred: one hundred and eighty-nine pounds ninety pence.

If Fiona paid the twelve hundred and fifty pounds appearance fee in full, and Julian's Maida Vale landlord received one thousand pounds for the rent arrears, there would remain sixty pounds and ten pence in the net balance.

Not a fortune, Zoe calculated, tapping her Pilot pen against her chin. But enough to buy a winter coat from the charity shop in Camden and pay her electric bill through November.

"Zoe?"

She looked up. Julian was standing before her, holding two plastic cups of lukewarm tea.

"Here, my dear. I secured two sugar cubes from the tea ladies. They were under the impression that Dalrymple took brown sugar, but I assured them that a true Fenland detective preferred white granulated."

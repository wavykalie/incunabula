# Chapter 04: The Engraved Cup

The raised dais at the northern end of the Rutland Suite had been constructed from interlocking plywood risers that groaned whenever anyone weighing more than eleven stone took a step. 

Upon the centre of the stage stood a folding card table draped in maroon velvet. And upon the velvet sat the ceremonial silverware of the Deeping and District Horticultural Society.

There were eight minor shields for runner-up onions, four brass tankards for heirloom runner beans, and, dominating the display like a medieval reliquary, the Market Deeping Produce Society Silver Challenge Cup.

It was an Edwardian silver two-handled trophy standing roughly eighteen inches high, crowned with an ornamental lid shaped like an artichoke. The silver was heavily tarnished along the fluting, displaying that distinctive dull purplish bloom that comes from being stored in a damp cardboard box in a parish council cellar for eleven months of the year. 

Zoe stepped onto the riser, her heels clicking smartly against the plywood, and approached the table. 

Julian followed more slowly, pausing at the edge of the stage to inspect an artificial floral arrangement composed of plastic marigolds and dusty pampas grass. "A charming artifact," he said, indicating the cup with a gesture of his left hand. "Dignified. Solid provincial craftsmanship. There is something touching about these modest civic traditions, Zoe. Generations of humble allotmenteers passing the same vessel from hand to hand. I shall refer to it in my opening remarks. Something about the sacred continuum of the furrow."

Zoe picked up the Challenge Cup by its left scroll handle. It was surprisingly heavy, weighted at the base with lead to keep it upright in drafty marquee tents. She turned the bowl toward the fluorescent light to inspect the inscription panels.

Around the upper rim, engraved in elegant Victorian copperplate, ran the founding dedication: DEEPING AND DISTRICT HORTICULTURAL SOCIETY, CHALLENGE CUP FOR HEAVY ROOTS AND GOURDS, FOUNDED 1894.

Below that, arranged in neat double columns across the silver-plated body, were the names of previous champions: 1921, H. V. Povey, Esq. (Marrow, 42 lbs); 1953, T. Sterling (Marrow, 51 lbs 4 oz); 1984, A. Povey (Marrow, 68 lbs 2 oz).

Then Zoe's eyes dropped to the shiny brass plaque affixed to the mahogany plinth.

The plaque had been freshly cut, its bevelled edges gleaming with yellow brilliance against the tarnished silver. The engraving had not been executed by a patient Victorian silversmith with a graver, but by an automated diamond-tip pantograph machine at a high-street key-cutting booth.

The inscription read:

2026 CHAMPION, G. R. HENDERSON (NORFOLK HYBRID MARROW, 74 LBS 8 OZ), PRESENTED BY G. R. HENDERSON AGRICULTURAL FEEDS AND FERTILIZERS LTD.

Zoe stared at the plaque. She read it three times.

"Julian," she said.

Julian was busy testing the acoustic resonance of the room by humming a sustained middle C against the velvet curtains. "Mmm? What is it, my dear? Does the metal require a duster? I believe I have a silk handkerchief in my breast pocket if the photographer insists on a polish."

"The cup has already been engraved, Julian."

Julian stopped humming. He blinked, stepped across the riser, and peered down over Zoe's shoulder.

"What do you mean, engraved? The judging does not commence until half past five."

"Look at the plinth," Zoe said, pointing a fingernail at the brass plate. "G. R. Henderson. Seventy-four pounds, eight ounces."

Julian squinted at the letters. His brow furrowed in genuine theatrical outrage. "Preposterous! That is a gross violation of natural justice! The weigh-in has not even taken place! Old Artie is still out there lubricating his vegetable with ammonia!"

"G. R. Henderson," Zoe murmured, checking her notes. "Where have I seen that name?"

She pulled out the Society's printed programme from her tote bag and turned to the inside back cover. There, occupying a full-page advertisement bordered with clip-art ears of wheat, sat the answer: G. R. HENDERSON FEEDS, PROUD PLATINUM SPONSOR OF THE 2026 DISTRICT PRODUCE GALA, SUPPLIERS OF NITROGEN-RICH FERTILIZERS TO THE EAST MIDLANDS.

"The sponsor," Zoe whispered, feeling a fresh headache blossoming behind her left temple. "The sponsor has already engraved his own name on the trophy."

Julian's baritone swelled with the righteous indignation of King Lear defying the storm. "Corruption! Petty, municipal tyranny! They have brought me here, Julian Tremayne, who played opposite Dame Maggie at Chichester, to act as the ornamental stooge for an agricultural feed merchant's rigged pumpkin contest!"

"Keep your voice down, Julian," Zoe hissed, looking around the room. Fortunately, fifty yards away, the allotmenteers were currently engaged in a shouting match regarding the calibration of a set of Victorian grocer's scales.

"I will not keep my voice down!" Julian proclaimed, his chest heaving. "My integrity is at stake! What will Equity say if it becomes known that Dalrymple awarded a fraudulent cup to an unweighed marrow?"

"Equity does not care about marrows, Julian," Zoe said through clenched teeth. "Equity cares about whether your dues are paid up to date, which they currently are not. Now put the cup down before someone sees us tampering with the plinth."

She replaced the trophy upon the velvet cloth. As she did so, the sound of sharp, rhythmic footsteps echoed across the wooden floorboards from the backstage corridor.

Zoe stepped closer to the presentation table, inspecting the mahogany base of the Challenge Cup.

Beside the newly cut brass plate bearing Henderson's name, four tiny screw holes in the timber showed where a previous inscription plaque had been recently prised away with a screwdriver. A tiny sliver of chipped varnish lay on the velvet cloth beside the trophy.

"Look at this, Julian," Zoe said, pointing to the empty screw holes. "There was another plate here until very recently."

Julian leaned down, his brow furrowing with genuine investigative curiosity. He pulled a silver pen from his pocket, using the point to turn over the sliver of varnish. "Fascinating. An act of historical revisionism. Who held the trophy last year?"

Zoe pulled out the printed society programme, flipping through the lists of past winners on page six. "In twenty twenty-five... the Challenge Cup was awarded to Arthur Povey, with an eighty-one-pound marrow."

"Old Artie," Julian murmured, glancing across the hall to where the old grower was still pacing beside Table 6. "So Henderson didn't just engrave his name in advance. He had Povey's plaque removed before the weigh-in even began."

"It appears so," Zoe said, feeling a sudden surge of professional disgust. In London, talent agencies and television production companies were ruthless, but their ruthlessness was usually wrapped in corporate legal jargon and expensive mineral water. Here, in this provincial hall, the corruption was raw, blatant, and nailed directly onto an Edwardian trophy with brass screws.

"A monstrous indignity," Julian proclaimed, his chest heaving with theatrical indignation. "To strip a veteran cultivator of his recorded glory before the scale arm has even swung! Fiona should be ashamed of herself. Even in the depths of our matrimonial disagreements, I never believed her capable of such administrative tyranny."

"Fiona is running an organization that relies on Henderson's sponsorship money, Julian," Zoe noted pragmatically. "When a feed merchant pays for the hall hire and the printing, the committee tends to listen when he wants his name on the cup."

"Then the committee is corrupt!" Julian declared, his baritone vibrating the plastic marigolds. "And Dalrymple will not stand by and witness the perversion of the furrow!"

"How bad is the agency account, Zoe?" Julian asked, his voice dropping to a confidential whisper as they walked along the side aisle.

"Marcus has not paid the telephone bill for seven weeks," she said plainly. "When I left the office on Friday afternoon, incoming calls were being redirected to an automated answering service in Croydon."

Julian winced. "Croydon. How dreadful."

"The landlord on Greek Street has changed the cylinder on the main entrance door twice since August," Zoe added. "If Marcus does not lodge three thousand pounds into the client deposit account by Monday noon, the bailiffs will inventory the photocopier."

Julian touched his silver hair with a slender hand. "Marcus assured me that the German residuals from the winter re-run of Dalrymple were imminent."

"The German broadcaster went into liquidation three years ago, Julian. The residuals are forty-two deutschmarks, held in an escrow account in Frankfurt that requires a notary from Munich to release."

"I see," Julian said softly. He pulled his coat collar up against the draft from the emergency fire doors. "Then this exhibition is not merely a courtesy, Miss Barnes. It is our entire winter position."

"It is your rent, Julian. And my train fare back to Finsbury Park."

"Marcus called again, did he?" Julian asked as they sat on two wooden folding chairs by the fire exit.

"He left a message with the hotel receptionist," Zoe said.

"And?"

"He said that a production company in Leeds is developing a six-part documentary series about heritage drainage systems."

Julian's eyes widened. "Heritage drainage? A noble subject. The taming of the Great Level by the Dutch engineers in the seventeenth century. Cornelius Vermuyden! A magnificent historical figure. I could narrate from the lock gates at Denver Sluice in a tweed ulster."

"Marcus said they have four hundred pounds for the presenter fee," Zoe said gently. "And the presenter has to provide his own waders."

Julian fell silent for a moment, looking down at his polished leather shoes.

"Waders," he murmured, with a faint, melancholy chuckle. "How the mighty are fallen, Miss Barnes. From the Royal Shakespeare Company to four hundred pounds and personal waders in a drainage ditch."

"It is four hundred pounds, Julian," Zoe said softly. "We will look at the contract on Monday."

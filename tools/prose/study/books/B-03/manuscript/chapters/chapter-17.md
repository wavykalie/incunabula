# Chapter 17: The Canceled Train

At four minutes past four, the National Rail app on Zoe's phone finally refreshed.

The screen displayed a red banner across the top of the interface: SERVICE SUSPENDED, GRANTHAM TO LONDON KING'S CROSS.

Below the banner, a line of italicized red text explained the situation with the cheerful corporate indifference of the British railway network: Due to a major signaling cable fire following an electrical substation lightning strike outside Grantham, all southbound services on the East Coast Main Line are suspended until approximately six tomorrow morning. Rail replacement road transport is not available due to regional driver shortages. Passengers are advised not to travel.

Zoe leaned against the wooden counter of the hotel reception desk. 

The receptionist, a teenage girl with blonde hair in a messy bun and a name badge that read KELLY, TRAINEE SUPERVISOR, was engrossed in applying glitter nail varnish to her left thumbnail.

"Excuse me, Kelly," Zoe said.

Kelly blew on her wet thumb without looking up. "Yeah?"

"The train to London has been cancelled. Are there any local taxis that would consider a journey to Peterborough?"

"Not on a Saturday, love," Kelly said. "The Peterborough drivers do not come out this far once the football starts. There is Dave with the silver minibus, but he is doing a stag party in Skegness until midnight."

"And the bus?"

"The four-fifteen to Bourne broke down by the roundabout," Kelly said, inspecting her thumbnail under the desk lamp. "Radiator hose split. Next one is the morning school run at seven-twenty."

Zoe drew a slow breath, feeling the antacid tablets dissolving in her stomach. "I see. Do you have two single rooms available for tonight?"

Kelly tapped three keys on a computer keyboard that produced a hollow clacking sound. "We have the twin annex out across the beer garden. Room 21. Two single beds, washbasin, shared bathroom down the landing. Sixty-five pounds including continental breakfast."

"Is the heating functional in the annex?"

"It comes on for an hour at six, and then again at half past five in the morning," Kelly said. "If you get chilly, there is a two-bar electric fire in the wardrobe, but you need fifty-pence coins for the meter."

Zoe reached into her tote bag, pulled out her personal debit card, and laid it upon the counter. 

"We will take it," she said.

She took the heavy brass room key, attached to a triangular block of stained pine, and slipped it into her pocket. They were trapped. There was no escape via rail, no road carriage, no refuge in the capital. They were anchored to the peat until the dawn train from Grantham.

She looked toward the double glass doors of the Rutland Suite. From within came the sudden, unmistakable sound of Julian's baritone, booming out across the tables as he engaged in an impassioned argument regarding the legal definition of an onion.

In the hotel annex across the beer garden, Zoe inspected Room 21.

The room smelled of damp distemper and synthetic air freshener, furnished with two narrow single beds covered in thin nylon counterpanes that displayed a pattern of geometric brown diamonds from nineteen seventy-four. A chipped porcelain washbasin stood in the corner beside a tin wastepaper basket, its cold tap dripping with a rhythmic, hollow ping that echoed against the linoleum.

She opened the small wardrobe. Inside, beside three wire coat hangers and a spare blanket that smelled of mothballs, sat the two-bar electric fire with its brass coin meter.

She pulled out her purse, extracted two fifty-pence pieces, and slotted them into the meter. The brass dial clicked, the heating elements hummed with a low electric buzz, and after forty seconds, a faint, glowing orange line appeared across the lower bar, emitting a dry, metallic warmth that smelled of scorching dust.

She sat upon the edge of the bed, feeling the cold springs sag beneath her weight. 

In London, her flat in Finsbury Park had central heating, high-speed fibre broadband, and a kitchen full of fresh ground coffee and oat milk. Here, she was marooned in an unheated annex behind a market town coaching inn, listening to the rain dripping from the guttering while an aging television detective practiced his baritone in a boiler room across the yard.

Yet as she looked around the shabby little room, she felt a strange, quiet sense of peace. For five years, she had lived in a state of perpetual metropolitan panic: checking emails at midnight, fielding hysterical telephone calls from talent agents, managing other people's bruised vanity. Here, stripped of the digital noise and the corporate pretension, life had reduced itself to the simplest and most ancient of realities: shelter, warmth, and the survival of the marrow.

"Look who's skulking by the water bowser," Arthur spat suddenly, gesturing through a slit in the tarpaulin with the stem of his pipe.

Zoe peered through the canvas flap. A tall, gaunt man in a yellow waterproof smock was leaning against the water tank, holding a wooden clipboard and watching Arthur's tent with hawkish intensity.

"Hector Gedge," Arthur whispered with dark satisfaction. "Wisbech grower. Won the title in ninety-seven when my marrow caught blossom-end rot in the wet August."

"A formidable competitor?" Julian asked, peering over Zoe's shoulder.

"A scoundrel," Arthur declared flatly. "Feeds his squash on condensed milk and iron filings. Claims it's an old Norfolk recipe. It's chemical witchcraft, that's what it is."

"Competition brings out the darker passions in all creative endeavours," Julian sighed. "When Gielgud opened in forty-four, Wolfit had his claque hiss from the upper circle."

"Wolfit never had to weigh sixty-eight pounds of marrow against a Wisbech bailiff," Arthur retorted.

"Look at Gedge's marrow," Julian whispered, peering through the tent gap toward the Wisbech enclosure.

"What do you make of it?" Zoe asked.

"Lacks proportion," Julian evaluated, his actor's eye judging the aesthetics. "It has bulk, certainly, but it is an ungainly, asymmetrical bulk. It looks like an unbaked cottage loaf that has collapsed on one side in the proving drawer. Arthur's Emperor, by contrast, possesses classical symmetry. A noble, Roman contour."

Arthur chuckled from the depths of his smock. "Listen to the actor! Roman contour! It's an eighty-pound squash, Mr Tremayne, not a statue of Oliver Cromwell."

"All great forms aspire to sculpture, Arthur," Julian maintained with serene conviction. "Phidias himself would have admired that stem."

"Phidias would have had to help hoist her onto the barrow," Arthur retorted. "And he'd have needed two hernia trusses to do it."

# Chapter 35: Fiona's Truce

At twenty past eight, while the ballroom was clearing and the non-winning cabbages were being loaded into wheelbarrows, Zoe walked into the small hallway behind the stage.

Sitting on a pair of plastic stacking chairs beside the props cupboard were Fiona Sterling and Julian Tremayne.

Between them sat an upturned wooden crate used as a table, upon which rested a chilled bottle of Italian prosecco and two green hotel wine glasses. Julian had removed his tailored jacket, draping it over the back of the chair, his shirt sleeves rolled up to the elbows. Fiona had kicked off her leather brogues, her stockinged feet resting comfortably upon the lower rung of her chair.

They were laughing.

It was not the sharp, defensive laughter of their earlier encounters, but the relaxed, familiar chuckle of two people who had shared sixteen years of domestic comedy and had finally run out of reasons to be angry.

"Do you remember the Edinburgh Fringe?" Julian was saying, topping up Fiona's glass with the pale sparkling wine. "Nineteen ninety-eight. The basement venue in the Cowgate that smelled of damp beer and raw sewage?"

"The one where the stage was three feet wide?" Fiona laughed, taking a sip from her glass. "And you insisted on wearing a six-foot velvet cape that swept the front row's drinks off their tables every time you turned around to address the ghost of Lord Byron?"

"I had to create an illusion of space, Fiona!" Julian protested with a grin. "The reviewer from the Scotsman wrote that my performance possessed an elemental, brooding velocity!"

"He wrote that you possessed an elemental, brooding velocity because you were trying to catch the midnight sleeper back to London before the landlord locked the flat, Julian!"

Julian laughed, a rich, open baritone chuckle that held no trace of vanity. "Perhaps he was right. We were absurdly young, weren't we?"

"We were absurdly stubborn," Fiona corrected gently, looking at the bubbles rising in her glass. "We spent sixteen years trying to turn each other into our own supporting cast. You wanted an adoring audience in the drawing room, and I wanted a dependable magistrate's husband who would mow the lawn on Sunday mornings."

Julian looked down at his glass. "I was an impossible husband, Fiona. I know that."

"You were an exhausting husband, Julian," she replied, touching his wrist with her fingertips. "There is a difference. But you were magnificent tonight. That speech... Olivier himself could not have done better."

"Olivier never had to deal with an eighty-pound marrow, my dear," Julian murmured with a modest inclination of his head. "The technical challenges were unique."

Zoe stepped into the hallway, clearing her throat politely. "Excuse me, Mrs Sterling. Julian."

Fiona looked up, smiling warmly. "Ah, the heroic Miss Barnes. Come in, my dear. Have a glass of prosecco. You have earned half the bottle after saving that ridiculous vegetable."

"Thank you, Mrs Sterling," Zoe said, taking the third glass that Fiona offered from the crate. "I came to check on the morning transport arrangements."

"Fiona has kindly offered us a lift to Grantham station in the Volvo," Julian explained, beaming with delight. "Seven o'clock sharp. We shall catch the eight-fifteen express back to town."

Fiona clinked her glass against Zoe's. "To the opening act, Miss Barnes. And to the women who make sure the curtain actually rises."

In the quiet corridor behind the stage, the bottle of Italian prosecco was half empty.

Julian Tremayne sat on his plastic stacking chair, his waistcoat unbuttoned, his legs stretched out before him on the concrete floor. "Fiona," he said, turning his glass in the dim light. "Tell me the truth. Did you really believe Henderson's marrow was legal?"

Fiona took a sip of her wine, a wry smile touching her lips. "Henderson's father threatened to withdraw the sponsorship for the district flower show, Julian. Twelve hundred pounds. In local government, twelve hundred pounds pays for the senior citizens' Christmas lunch and the heating in the library."

"And yet you let Dalrymple overrule you," Julian noted with a sly, affectionate wink.

"I let natural justice take its course, Julian," Fiona replied, her gray eyes softening as she looked at him. "And besides... it was worth twelve hundred pounds to hear you deliver a summation without fluffing your lines."

Julian laughed, a rich, open baritone chuckle that echoed against the props cupboard. "I never fluffed a line in ninety-eight, my dear. Not once."

"You forgot the name of the corpse in the third episode, Julian! You called him Lord Abernathy for three scenes until the script editor threw a clipboard at your head!"

"A minor textual variation," Julian murmured with a patrician wave of his glass. "The emotion was impeccable."

"You haven't lost your edge, Fiona," Julian said, sipping the cold prosecco from his green hotel glass.

"Someone has to keep order in this county, Julian," she replied, cutting a toasted sandwich with her pocket knife. "If I left the society to the men, they would award the championship to the loudest voice in the bar and lose the receipts in the car park."

"You always were magnificent at logistical control," he said softly. "I remember when we moved into the flat in Maida Vale. You labelled every single tea chest in alphabetical order."

"And you unpacked the collection of theatre programmes first and left the kitchen utensils on the pavement," she retorted, though a faint smile touched the corners of her eyes. "You were completely hopeless, Julian. But you always had good manners."

"Good manners cost nothing, my dear," Julian murmured.

"Good manners don't pay the Council Tax either," Fiona said, but her tone was dry rather than bitter.

"Fiona," Julian said as they sat together in the annex lounge.

"Julian."

"Thank you for the engagement. It was... an exceptional afternoon."

"The society made a modest surplus on the gate," Fiona said, closing her blue accounts folder with a crisp snap. "And the local coverage in the Mercury will assist our grant application to the District Arts Fund. We are both satisfied."

She looked at him across the low table, her sharp gaze softening with memories thirty years old.

"Take care of yourself, Julian," she said softly. "You are not as young as you were when you played Hamlet in Bristol."

"None of us are, my dear," Julian murmured with a gentle smile. "None of us are."

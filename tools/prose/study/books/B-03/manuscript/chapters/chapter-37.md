# Chapter 37: The Marrow Feast

By ten o'clock, the Rutland Suite had been transformed into the venue for the traditional post-gala supper.

The giant marrows that had not qualified for weight certificates had been wheeled into the hotel kitchen on wooden trolleys, where the chef, an enormous man named Cookie who wore an apron stained with tomato purée, had sliced them into thick, translucent disks with a commercial meat cleaver.

The disks had been fried in salted butter on the flat-top griddle, dusted with black pepper and dried thyme, and served on large oval platters alongside mounds of fried Lincolnshire sausages, crusty white rolls, and jars of sweet marrow pickle prepared by the Women's Institute.

Julian Tremayne sat at the central table in the resident bar, surrounded by fifteen allotmenteers.

He had removed his silk tie, unbuttoning his top collar button, and was drinking a pint of dark, bitter local ale from a dimpled glass tankard. Upon the table before him lay a stack of paper beer mats and white cocktail napkins, upon which he was signing his autograph with Zoe's black gel pen.

*To George — Keep the trench deep — Julian Tremayne (DI Dalrymple)*.

"He's a proper gentleman, ain't he?" George Baxter said, showing his signed napkin to Harold Finch with childlike delight. "Not like that stuck-up fella from BBC Radio Lincoln we had in twenty-two. That one wouldn't even touch the parsnips without rubber gloves."

"A true artist respects the soil, George," Julian said, taking a generous draught of his ale with the hearty satisfaction of an Elizabethan monarch holding court in a tavern. "When we filmed the harvest festival episode in ninety-nine, I insisted on digging the ditch myself. The director wanted a stunt double, but I told him: 'Brian, the public knows when an actor is faking the spade-work.' Authenticity cannot be forged."

Arthur Povey sat at Julian's right elbow, eating a fried marrow slice from a paper plate with his pruning knife. 

"You've got a good head on you, Julian," Povey said, chewing thoughtfully. "If you ever get tired of the acting game, I could use a partner on the south allotments. We could put in four beds of the Emperor cross and enter the national trials at Malvern next autumn."

Julian laughed, a warm, resonant sound that mingled with the clinking of glasses and the chatter of the room. "An enticing proposition, Arthur. Perhaps I shall take a sabbatical from the stage. There is much to be said for the monastic life of the marrow bed."

Zoe watched them from the corner of the bar, leaning against the timber counter with a small glass of cider. 

She saw Julian's face in the warm tavern light. The anxiety, the bruised vanity, the desperate metropolitan insecurity that had plagued him for five years had completely melted away. For this one evening, among these eighty farmers who had never heard of BAFTA and cared nothing for streaming numbers, Julian Briggs was precisely what he had always dreamed of being: a respected, beloved, and authentically heroic figure.

She finished her cider, set the glass on the counter, and walked out into the cool night air toward the annex across the beer garden.

The atmosphere in the resident bar had reached that mellow, expansive stage where every man was a brother and every grievance of the agricultural year had been forgiven.

Julian Tremayne was holding court at the corner table, flanked by Arthur Povey and George Baxter, his dimpled glass tankard filled with real Lincolnshire ale.

"Now, Julian," George Baxter said, leaning forward across a plate of cold marrow chutney. "Tell us about the scene with the tractor in the bog. In the ninety-nine Christmas special. Was that a real Massey Ferguson you drove into the dyke?"

Julian smiled, leaning back against the timber settle with the relaxed grace of a country squire. "A genuine ninety-eight model, George. The director wanted a stunt driver from Pinewood, but I told him: 'An English detective must know how to double-declutch in a bog.' I took the wheel myself, put her into low ratio, and drove straight into four feet of fen mud. Ruined an eight-hundred-pound Barbour coat, but the shot was magnificent."

The growers laughed, raising their glasses in collective approval.

Across the room, Cookie the chef emerged from the kitchen carrying a fresh tray of fried marrow fritters dusted with sea salt. "Hot off the griddle, lads!" Cookie shouted. "Eat up! There's thirty pounds of champion marrow still in the pantry!"

Arthur Povey took a hot fritter with his fingers, offering it to Julian on a paper napkin. "Taste that, Julian. That's the Emperor's sister. Sweet as an apple and tender as butter. That's what real soil tastes like."

Julian took a bite, closing his eyes as he chewed. "Superb, Arthur. An absolute triumph of the culinary arts."

At nine o'clock, Councillor Hoddinott cornered Julian near the reception desk.

"A quick pint in the saloon bar, Mr Tremayne? Several members of the parish council would be honoured to buy you a Scotch."

Julian hesitated for half a second, his fingers touching his crimson pocket square. Then he glanced down the corridor toward Zoe, who was checking the train timetables from Grantham.

"You are exceptionally kind, Councillor," Julian said with graceful warmth. "Under normal circumstances, nothing would give me greater pleasure. But an actor must respect the demands of his instrument. I have a script waiting in my room that requires my complete, undivided contemplation before morning."

Councillor Hoddinott blinked, impressed. "The theatre! An exacting master, eh?"

"Exacting indeed," Julian agreed with a solemn nod. "Good night to you, Councillor."

"Julian," Zoe said as they walked down the quiet high street toward the hotel.

"Yes, my dear?"

"Marcus left another message on my mobile."

"What does the scoundrel want now?"

"He says the director of the Leeds documentary wants to meet us on Wednesday afternoon."

Julian stopped beneath a street lamp, the yellow light catching his silver hair.

"Does he indeed?" Julian said, his baritone lifting with renewed theatrical vigour. "The historical drainage series! I knew it! Vermuyden! The battle against the sea! I shall prepare an outline of the opening sequence immediately!"

"After a good night's sleep, Julian," Zoe said firmly. "After a good night's sleep."

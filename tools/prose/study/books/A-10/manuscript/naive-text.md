# The Winter Measure

### Chapter 1

The lantern was made of pierced horn, and in a headwind it smelled of old mutton fat and scorched tin. Martha Garrow carried it low against her petticoats, where the draught from the ditch could not find the air-holes.

Two miles of ditch lay between her cottage on the Cross-Spur and the turnpike gate at Broad-Duntish. In November the ditch was black water and rotting hemlock, but tonight, three days before Twelfth Night, the mud had set like iron under a sky without stars. The ruts left by the timber wains in October stood up in grey ridges sharp enough to cut through the welt of a shoe.

Martha knew every ridge. She had walked the three parishes when the road was limestone powder in July, when it was brown porridge up to the hubs in Michaelmas rains, and when the snow drifted off the downs and filled the sheep-leazes until the churchyard wall at High Duntish looked no higher than a curb. For twenty years she had carried her basket along this track. Her mother had carried it before her, but her mother had died of the quinsy in the year the King lost America, and since that frost Martha had gone to the beds alone.

Eight hundred and forty-two. That was the count in the small sheepskin pocket-book she kept in the flour chest beneath the dry salt. The ones that drew breath were marked with an ink prick; the ones that came blue and slack, or with the cord twice about the neck like a hempen collar, were marked with a cross. There were ninety-two crosses in twenty years, which the commissary from the bishop’s registry at Wells had once told her was a fair reckoning for an upland district where the mothers worked the turnips until their waters broke in the furrows.

Tonight there was no call in her basket. She had no dried bistort root, no pennyroyal, no bladder of hog’s lard, no clean linen rollers. The wicker was empty save for her shoes—her second-best pair, greased with neatsfoot and tied with leather thongs—and the tin cylinder that held her episcopal licence.

Simon Clout walked three paces ahead of her. He was the tithing-man for Broad-Duntish, though he had neither coat nor staff to show for it, only a Constable’s warrant folded into the crown of his hat and an ash stick he had cut out of the parson’s copse. His sheepskin waistcoat had lost its fleece at the elbows, and when he coughed the sound was like sheep coughing in the mist.

"He won’t give you no beer," Simon said without turning his head. His breath came back over his shoulder in grey puffs that smelled of salt herrings. "Mr. Scrope don’t keep the barrel running for witnesses. Only for the rent-audit, and then it’s small beer with the water from the horse-pond."

"I did not ask for his beer," Martha said.

"He had Thomas Vowles in the kitchen four hours on Monday. About the hay-rick at Cruxton that took fire. Thomas come out with his breeches frozen hard as ash-boards and nothing inside him but water from the pump."

"Thomas Vowles was five miles off when the rick took fire," Martha said. "He was at the smithy with his roan colt."

"Aye," Simon said, striking his stick against a frozen clod. "And Mr. Scrope kept him till the colt went lame with the standing. He’s got the new books from London on his table. The ones with the blue backs about the grain supply and the French King's head. He says if the poor rates go up another farthing in the pound, the parish will be eaten out by bastards before the barley's in."

They came to the turnpike gate. The bar was padlocked against the coal-carts from the Somerset pits, but the side-stile was open, its top rail polished yellow by forty years of smock-frocks and milking pails. Beyond the gate, the drive to Scrope Hall turned off through an avenue of young beeches that the old Squire had planted before the war. The young trees had no shelter against the east wind; they rattled their dry brown leaves together with a sound like dry husks in a winnowing fan.

The Hall had seven windows on the ground tier and nine on the first, all flat-faced sash windows that looked down the valley toward the church tower of Broad-Duntish like rows of teeth. Two of the lower windows were yellow with candlelight. The rest were dark, reflecting the cold, grey curd of the clouds that moved off the downs.

Simon went to the back gate by the laundry-yard, but the boy who swilled the dairy flags met them at the pump and pointed a red thumb toward the garden door.

"The Justice is in the library," the boy said. He was twelve, with chilblains on his knuckles that had split open to the raw meat. "He said you was to take off your pattens at the mat, Mrs. Garrow, and leave the man in the lobby."

# The Ledger of Deliverances

### Chapter 1

The lantern was made of pierced horn, casting circles of pale yellow over the frozen ruts of the High Road. Martha Garrow kept her hood low against the east wind, her woollen cloak pinned tightly at the throat by an iron brooch her father had hammered from a horseshoe forty years before. 

In the parish of Nether-Coombe, three miles south of the Somerset chalk ridge, the road was nothing more than an ancient ditch between double hedges of thorn and blackthorn. In summer it was limestone dust that settled white upon the dock leaves; in November it was grey clay up to the axles; but on this January dusk in 1794, the ground was iron. The frost had held fourteen nights. The sheep had been brought down from the combes into the sheep-cotes, their fleece matted with rime, and the mill-pond at Lower Duntish was frozen thick enough to bear a bullock.

Martha knew every yard of the three parishes—Nether-Coombe, High Duntish, and Saint Jude-in-the-Fens. For twenty-one years, ever since her mother’s hands had grown too bent with the rheum to tie a navel-twine, Martha had walked these lanes with her wicker pannier on her arm. She had walked them by starlight and by noontide, through driving sleet and through the dead heat of July hay-harvest. In the oak chest beneath her bed lay the small leather register that Bishop Moss had signed at Wells when she was nineteen years old: her licence to practice the art of midwifery within the diocese of Bath and Wells, endorsed with the parish seal.

Inside the back cover of that register, in a neat, careful clerk’s hand taught her by the old curate, Martha kept her reckoning. Seven hundred and ninety-one times she had been called to a bed. Seven hundred and ninety-one times she had washed her hands in hot well-water, hung a kettle over the embers, and waited out the long, silent hours between evening and dawn.

Tonight, however, the wicker basket on her forearm held no swaddling bands, no dried bistort root for stauncing, no pot of mutton tallow, and no silver spoon for testing a newborn’s palate. It held only two crusts of hard rye bread wrapped in a clean linen towel, her leather register, and the parchment summons delivered that morning by the parish beadle.

Simon Clout, the tithing-man of Nether-Coombe, walked four paces ahead of her, his ash cudgel striking the frozen ground with a dull, hollow knock. He was a small, sour-faced weaver whose loom had stood idle since Michaelmas for lack of yarn, and he wore his constable’s staff tucked under his arm like an old flute.

"Mr. Scrope will not be kept waiting past the candle-lighting," Simon said, without turning his head. His breath flew out in grey woolly rags against the black hedge. "He had the churchwardens in his parlor by ten this morning, and the Overseer of the Poor until the noon bell. He has three new law books from Bristol on his desk, bound in calf. They say the parish rates are double what they were before the war began, and the magistrates have orders from the Assizes to clear the rolls."

"Mr. Scrope may read his calfskin books as long as pleases him," Martha replied. Her voice was quiet, even, and low, the voice she used in quiet bedchambers when the mother’s courage began to fail. "The law does not make women bear in season, Master Clout, nor does it make the winters milder."

"The law makes people answer when they are called," the tithing-man muttered. "And it makes them name what they saw."

They turned off the common track into the long beech avenue that led to the Court House. The trees stood bare and straight, their grey trunks resembling cathedral columns against the cold purple of the winter twilight. At the end of the vista rose Nether-Coombe Court: five bays of dressed Hamstone, three storeys tall, with high chimney-stacks sending thin plumes of woodsmoke into the freezing air. It was a solid, handsome house built in Queen Anne’s time, belonging to a family that had owned two-thirds of the arable land in the vale since the Civil Wars.

At the side door leading into the magistrate’s wing, Simon knocked once with the iron knob of his stick. 

A maidservant in a striped woollen bedgown opened the door, a tallow candle held high above her cap. She glanced at Martha’s grey hood, recognized her, and stepped back into the stone-flagged corridor.

"Take off your pattens, Mrs. Garrow," the girl whispered. "The Justice is in the library with Mr. Finch and Mr. Harford. They have had the fire going since noon, but he is in an ill humour. The carrier from Ilchester brought word that wheat reached seventy shillings a quarter on Monday."

Martha bent down in the outer porch, unbuckling the iron pattens from her thick shoes and setting them neatly beside the scraper. She shook the road-dust from her skirt, removed her woollen mittens, and smoothed her apron. 

The warmth of the house met her like an open oven, smelling of dry oak logs, wax polish, and the damp wool of coats hung up to dry. It was a rich smell, a house that had never known the pinch of a short crop or the dread of an empty grain-bin.

The tithing-man led her down the stone passage to the heavy mahogany door of the library. He knocked with his knuckle, waited for the curt command from within, and opened the door just wide enough to let her pass.

"Martha Garrow, your worship," Simon announced, bowing until his hat touched his knees. "The midwife of Nether-Coombe."

"Bid her come in, Simon, and wait in the hall," a dry, clear voice replied. "Close the door."

The library was lined on three sides with tall glass-fronted bookcases filled with gilt-lettered volumes. A massive fire of ash logs blazed in the marble fireplace, throwing bright tongues of flame across the crimson Turkey rug. 

Three men sat about a long mahogany table strewn with folios, parish rate-books, and folded sheets of stamped paper.

At the head of the table sat Justice Scrope. He was sixty years old, lean and sharp-featured, with grey hair drawn back into a neat queue tied with black ribbon. His dark broadcloth coat was plain but of the finest Yorkshire wool, and his silver-rimmed spectacles rested halfway down a thin, aristocratic nose. On his right sat the Reverend Mr. Harford, the rector of Nether-Coombe—a portly, mild-faced gentleman in a powdered wig, whose plump white hands rested nervously on an open parish baptismal register.

On the magistrate’s left sat Farmer Gervase Finch of High Barn.

Martha looked at Gervase Finch, and for the first time that evening her pulse gave a slow, heavy throb against her ribs.

Gervase Finch was fifty-five, a broad-shouldered, barrel-chested yeoman whose family had farmed High Barn for four generations under the Scrope estate. His blue frieze coat had silver buttons, his leather gaiters were clean and well-oiled, and his thick, weathered face had the ruddy color of a man who spent twelve hours a day in the wind. But tonight his eyes were hooded, fixed stubbornly on the grain of the mahogany table, and his big hands, scarred from fifty years of billhooks and plough-stilts, gripped the arms of his chair with white-knuckled force.

For twenty years, High Barn had been Martha Garrow’s refuge. In the terrible frost of 1783, when three-quarters of the parish sheep died on the downs and Martha had nothing in her larder but dried beans, Gervase Finch’s wain had stopped at her gate, leaving two sacks of sound winter oats and a side of salted bacon. Every Martinmas since her mother died, a tub of butter and a cord of seasoned split oak had arrived at her wood-shed from High Barn, unasked and unpaid for. When her thatched roof had failed under the great August tempest seven years ago, it was Gervase Finch’s sons who had brought the wheat-reed and spent three days re-thatching the ridge while Martha was away across the marsh attending the miller’s wife.

"Mrs. Garrow," Justice Scrope said, setting down his goose-quill and looking at her over his spectacles. "Step to the table, if you please. The rector has the register before him."

Martha walked forward until she stood opposite the fire. She made a quiet, modest curtsy to the gentlemen, keeping her hands folded beneath her apron.

"Good evening, your worship. Good evening, Mr. Harford. Master Finch."

Gervase Finch’s chin lifted a fraction of an inch, but his eyes did not meet hers. He gave a short, jerky nod, like a horse twitching against a tight curb.

"You have been midwife to this district a considerable time, I understand," the magistrate began, drawing a fresh sheet of paper toward him and dipping his pen.

"Twenty-one years at Lady Day, your worship," Martha said. "And my mother thirty years before me."

"A long service," Scrope said. "And one that carries a heavy trust. You were licensed by the Chancellor of the Diocese under the episcopal seal?"

"I was, sir. In the third year of the Bishop’s translation. The licence is here in my basket."

"Keep it where it is," Scrope waved a long, pale hand. "Mr. Harford has verified your entry in the visitation book. You are aware, then, of the oath administered to midwives under the ecclesiastical constitution?"

"I know it by heart, sir," Martha said steadily. "To minister to rich and poor alike with equal care; to use no secret crafts or enchantments; to preserve the life of mother and child to the utmost of my skill; to report every birth truly to the minister for lawful baptism; and never to conceal, switch, or make away with any infant born alive."

The magistrate nodded once, his pen scratching a short note across his paper.

"An admirable summary," he murmured. "And particularly that last clause, Mrs. Garrow: *never to conceal or make away with any infant born alive.* It is upon that very point that this examination turns."

He leaned back in his leather chair, resting the tips of his long fingers together. The firelight glinted on his silver shoe-buckles.

"Two weeks before Michaelmas last—on the seventeenth day of September, to be precise—you were summoned in the night to High Barn."

The silence in the room became absolute. The only sound was the steady crackle of the ash logs and the faint sigh of the east wind through the beech avenue outside.

"I was, your worship," Martha said.

"To attend upon Eleanor Ward, formerly housemaid and dairywoman in the employ of Master Finch."

"Yes, sir."

"You delivered her of a child?"

"I did, sir."

Justice Scrope leaned forward, his dark eyes fixing upon Martha’s face with the sudden, sharp intensity of an owl watching a hedgerow.

"The girl Eleanor Ward was brought before me yesterday at the Petty Sessions in Ilchester," Scrope said, his voice dropping into a colder, measured cadence. "She was found wandering in the lanes near Somerton without settlement, without bread, and without a child. The Overseers of Somerton took her up for vagrancy. Under examination, she admitted she had been delivered of a male child at High Barn in September. But when asked where that child now lies, she fell into a state of hysterical distraction and refused to speak."

The magistrate paused, allowing the words to settle into the warm, scented air of the library.

"The parish of Somerton has served Nether-Coombe with an order of removal," Scrope continued. "They demand that we take her back as a settled pauper, or that we account for the maintenance of her infant. More grave still, there are rumours circulating through the lower village—ugly rumours, Mrs. Garrow—that the child never left High Barn alive, and that its body was put into the horse-pond or buried beneath the cider orchard to avoid the cost of maintenance upon the parish rates."

A sharp, ragged breath escaped Gervase Finch’s chest. The farmer shifted in his chair, his heavy boots scraping against the wainscot.

"It is a lie, your worship," Finch said, his voice thick with gravel. "A wicked, malicious lie set about by the weavers. Eleanor Ward was a flighty, ungrateful wench. She left my house of her own free will three weeks after Michaelmas, with her wages in her pocket and her bundle on her back. If the child is missing, she made away with it on the road herself, after she left my gate."

Justice Scrope raised a single finger, silencing the farmer without turning his head.

"That is what we are here to discover, Master Finch," Scrope said softly. "Under the statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First, if a woman be delivered of issue that would be a charge upon the parish, and she conceals the death thereof, it is accounted murder unless she can prove by one witness at the least that the child was born dead."

The magistrate turned his spectacles back toward Martha Garrow.

"You were the only person in that bedchamber, Mrs. Garrow, aside from the mother herself and Mrs. Finch. Master Finch has sworn upon his oath that when the girl left his house, she had no child with her. He swears further that he never saw the infant alive after the morning of its birth, and that his wife informed him the babe had failed to thrive and had died in the night."

Scrope picked up his pen and held it poised over the blank white sheet.

"Now, Mrs. Garrow. You delivered Eleanor Ward on the seventeenth of September. Tell us the condition of that infant when it entered the world. Did it breathe? Did it cry? And when you left High Barn at dawn on the eighteenth, did you leave a living soul in that cradle, or a corpse?"

Martha felt the gaze of all three men resting upon her face like a weight.

To her right, the rector looked at her with mild, sorrowful concern, his hands clasped over his belly. Across the table, Justice Scrope sat like an instrument of iron, calculating rates, settlements, statutes, and penalties.

And beside him sat Gervase Finch.

For the first time, the farmer raised his head. His eyes met Martha’s across the polished wood. There was no threat in his face; there was something far worse—a desperate, pleading weariness, the look of an honest man who had stood between his household and ruin for thirty years, and who now saw the precipice crumbling under his feet.

Martha knew what Master Finch wanted her to say. She knew what his sworn statement required.

If she said the child was stillborn—if she said it came blue and silent, that its lungs never opened to the autumn air, that she had wrapped it in a piece of old flannel and laid it aside—Eleanor Ward would be safe from the scaffold. A mother could not be hanged for concealing the death of a child that was never born alive. The charge of murder would vanish like smoke. Master Finch’s household would be cleared of every dark whisper; the parish of Nether-Coombe would pay no maintenance; and when next November’s frost set in, the split ash and the tub of salted butter would arrive at Martha’s wood-shed just as they had done every winter since her youth.

It was so simple. A single sentence would do it. A nod of the head, a quiet affirmation to the rector, and the ink on Scrope’s paper would dry upon a harmless verdict of natural infirmity.

Martha tightened her hands beneath her apron. She felt the smooth, cold edges of her episcopal licence inside the basket at her side.

*Never to conceal, switch, or make away with any infant born alive.*

She closed her eyes for the space of a single heartbeat. In the dark behind her eyelids, she did not see Justice Scrope’s library or the flickering ash fire. She saw the small, low-ceilinged dairy-chamber above the cheese-press at High Barn. She smelled the bitter tang of pennyroyal and vinegar, the sharp steam of hot water, and the sour curd of old whey.

She saw the girl Eleanor Ward—nineteen years old, her dark hair plastered to her forehead with sweat, her teeth bitten through her lower lip to keep from crying out while the master of the house paced the flagstones in the kitchen below.

And she remembered the child.

It had not come blue. It had not come silent. It had slipped into Martha’s greased palms with a fierce, wet vigor, its skin red as a winter apple, its dark eyes blinking open in the candlelight. When Martha had cleared its throat with her forefinger and struck the soles of its tiny feet, it had let out a cry so clear, so sharp, so full of greedy, stubborn life that Mrs. Finch had dropped her teacup upon the hearthstone in sheer terror that the men downstairs would hear it.

It was a boy. Eight pounds if it was an ounce, with a thick crest of black hair and the broad, square thumbs that belonged to only one bloodline in the parish of Nether-Coombe.

Martha opened her eyes. The firelight flickered across Justice Scrope’s waiting pen.

"Your worship," Martha said, and her voice did not waver by a hair’s breadth. "The child was born at four o'clock in the morning, two hours before the cock crew for dawn."

"Go on," Scrope said.

"It was a male child, sound in wind and limb, without blemish or defect," she said clearly. "He drew breath the moment he left the womb. He cried strongly for twenty minutes while I washed him and bound his navel. I laid him to Eleanor Ward’s breast, and he took her milk before the sun was above the ridge."

Gervase Finch made a strangled sound in his throat and dropped his head into his hands.

Justice Scrope’s pen scratched steadily across the paper, the sharp nib grating against the dry parchment with a sound like a beetle in the wainscot.

"He was born alive, then," Scrope said, without looking up. "Fully alive, and vigorous?"

"He was as lusty a child as ever I delivered in twenty years," Martha said. "And when I left High Barn at eight o'clock that morning, he was sleeping in the cradle by the hearth, with Mistress Finch watching over him."

The magistrate finished his line, drew a neat sander across the wet ink, and blew the grey dust away into the fire.

Then he looked up, not at Martha, but directly at Gervase Finch.

"Master Finch," Justice Scrope said, and his voice had the cold finality of an iron gate falling shut. "You have sworn upon the Holy Evangelists that this child was born dead, and that no living infant ever drew breath under your roof in September. You had better consider your position very carefully, sir, before the Grand Jury meets at Taunton on Tuesday."

### Chapter 2

The cold outside was sharper than it had been at sunset. As Martha stepped from the porch onto the gravel, the east wind caught the hem of her cloak, whipping the heavy wool against her shins. The sky had cleared; the clouds had drifted away to the south, leaving a high, brittle vault dusted with thousands of cold white stars.

Simon Clout was waiting by the beech trees, stamping his feet in their thin shoes.

"What did you tell him?" Simon asked, peering under her hood with eyes like small black currants. "Gervase Finch come out the side door just now looking like he’d seen the Black Dog on the road. He didn't even unhitch his mare from the ring; he walked off down the avenue leading her by the bridle, with his head down like an ox that’s taken the pole-axe."

"I told the Justice what was in the register," Martha said. "Give me the lantern, Simon. You have half a mile to walk to the village, and your shoes are thin."

"He'll have the constable at High Barn tomorrow," the tithing-man muttered, surrendering the horn lantern with an uneasy shrug. "Mark my words, Mrs. Garrow. You’ve put a torch to the thatch tonight. Old Gervase has been the head of this parish since my father was a boy. If he goes down, there's twenty cottagers on the common that won't have bread before the spring."

Martha took the iron ring of the lantern. "Good night, Master Clout."

She did not take the turnpike road back to her cottage. Instead, she took the field path that skirted the edge of the glebe lands and climbed the shoulder of the combe toward High Barn.

It was a mile out of her way, over rough stiles and sheep-pastures where the frozen tussocks of grass snapped under her boots like dry bones. But she could not go home to her cold hearth while Gervase Finch was walking the road with his head down.

The Garrows had lived on the edge of the Finch lands for three generations. When Martha’s grandfather had his cottage pulled down by the enclosure commissioners in sixty-two, it was old Matthew Finch—Gervase’s father—who had given him the strip of waste on the Cross-Spur and three loads of stone from the quarry to build the chimney. When Martha’s mother lay dying of the throat-canker, it was Mistress Finch who brought the elderberry cordial and the mutton tea twice a day across the fields.

Midwives were not paid in gold in the vales. The parson gave them five shillings for a church-gate certificate when the parents were substantial, and the poor gave them a bag of potatoes, a pat of butter, or sixpence when the harvest was sold. In a bad year, when the murrain took the cattle or the rain rotted the wheat in the ear, a midwife lived on the charity of the great farms. She lived on what was left in the corners of the threshing-floor, the sour cider that the harvesters could not finish, the skimmed milk from the third cheese-vat.

Gervase Finch had never given her sour cider. He had treated her as his mother had treated hers: with the dry, quiet respect that agricultural people gave to those who knew the secrets of the body, the dates of women’s blood, and the hour of their delivery.

Now she had stood in the magistrate’s library and sworn away his name.

When Martha reached the crest of the hill, the lights of High Barn appeared through the bare branches of the ash orchard. It was a vast, sprawling homestead of grey limestone, roofed with heavy stone slates from the Ham Hill quarries. Behind the house rose the great threshing barn, sixty paces long, with its high porch large enough to take a loaded hay-wain without unyoking the team. Around it clustered the cattle-byres, the calf-pens, the granary set on twelve stone staddles, and the cider-house with its massive stone press.

Everything about High Barn spoke of ancient, stubborn abundance. It was the kind of farm that held six hundred sheep on the downs, forty dairy cows in the vale, and twelve fat cart-horses in the stables.

As Martha approached the yard gate, a sheepdog barked once from the barn—a low, warning bark—then stopped as it caught her scent on the wind.

The kitchen door stood ajar. A ribbon of orange light fell across the frozen dung-heap in the yard.

Martha stepped over the threshold without knocking. In forty years she had never knocked at the back door of High Barn.

The kitchen was twenty feet across, paved with broad flags of blue lias that were polished smooth as glass by generations of hobnailed boots. From the blackened oak beams hung two dozen flitches of salt bacon wrapped in grey calico, bundles of dried sage and winter savory, and three strings of smoked onions. A fire of split oak burned in the great open hearth, heating a heavy cast-iron cauldron of hog-wash that simmered with a thick, sour smell of barley-meal and boiled roots.

Mistress Finch sat in the high-backed settle beside the fire. She was fifty, a small, faded woman who had borne six children and buried four of them before they reached their teeth. Her hands, red and swollen from thirty years of cheese-making in cold dairy-slabs, were folded tightly over her linen apron.

Gervase Finch stood with his back to the room, leaning his elbows against the high mantle-tree, his grey head resting against the rough stone of the chimney. His hat and whip lay where he had thrown them upon the long ash dining-table.

Neither of them moved when the latch clicked.

"You told him," Mistress Finch said. It was not a question; it was the quiet, lifeless statement of a woman who had already heard the bell toll.

Martha set her lantern on the bench by the bread-trough and pulled back her woollen hood. Her grey hair, streaked with white at the temples, was pinned in a tight, neat knot at the nape of her neck.

"I told him what the register says, Mary," Martha said softly. "The child breathed. It was born alive."

Gervase Finch did not turn round, but his broad shoulders rose and fell in a heavy, shuddering sigh.

"You could have said it was still," the farmer muttered into the chimney stones. "One word, Martha. Just one word to the parson. You’ve laid out fifty babes in this parish that never drew breath. Who would have questioned it? The girl was a parish apprentice from Taunton. She has no people. No one would have asked a second question if you had said the cord was fouled."

"The girl would have been hanged for it, Gervase," Martha said, stepping closer to the hearth.

Finch swung round then, his face dark with sudden, furious blood.

"Hanged? Why would she be hanged if the babe was stillborn? There is no murder where there is no life!"

"Justice Scrope has the Taunton calendar on his desk," Martha replied, her voice cold and level as water in a well. "He told me himself. If a natural child is born in secret, and its body is not found, the law presumes the mother destroyed it unless she can prove it came dead from the womb. If I had sworn it was stillborn, Scrope would have demanded the carcass. He would have asked where you buried it, Master Finch. He would have sent Simon Clout with three labourers and a spade to dig up the orchard, to dig up the cider-cellar, to search the manure-heaps."

The anger went out of Gervase Finch’s face all at once, leaving his skin the color of dirty tallow. He reached out blindly with one hand, gripping the edge of the mantle-tree to steady himself.

Mistress Finch covered her mouth with both hands, letting out a thin, mewing whimper that was more terrible than a scream.

"You see?" Martha said, looking from the man to the woman. "You did not bury it in the orchard. There is no dead babe in the cellar."

The great kitchen fell into a silence so deep that the bubbling of the barley-meal in the cauldron sounded like the muttering of a third person in the shadows.

"Where is the child, Gervase?" Martha asked.

The farmer looked at her, his eyes wild and bloodshot.

"I don't know," he whispered.

"You do know," Martha said. "You know, or your wife knows. You gave Eleanor Ward ten guineas to leave this parish at Michaelmas. That was half a year’s rent on the Lower Combe meadow. A tenant farmer does not give ten guineas to an apprentice dairymaid because she has broken a milk-crock. And he does not let an infant vanish into the air between midnight and dawn unless he knows whose hand took it."

Mistress Finch rocked herself back and forth in the settle, her fingers twisting the hem of her apron into tight, white knots.

"It was Julian," the mother whispered.

The name dropped into the quiet kitchen like an iron plumb into deep water.

Julian Finch was their only surviving son. At twenty-four, he was the pride and the curse of High Barn: a tall, handsome, reckless lad who could break a colt better than any horse-dealer in the county, but who spent his market-days at the George in Ilchester drinking French brandy with the smugglers from the coast, and his nights riding the downs with the yeomanry cavalry.

"Julian," Martha repeated. She did not sound surprised. "I knew the boy’s hands when he came into the world. He had the Finch thumb. Wide and flat at the joint, like your father’s."

"He was to marry the miller’s daughter at Easter," Mistress Finch sobbed, her voice breaking into ragged gasps. "Old Miller Trask has five hundred pounds in the bank at Yeovil, and the lease of the three water-mills on the Parrett. If Julian married Phoebe Trask, the lease of High Barn was safe for another thirty years. Mr. Scrope promised the renewal on the wedding-day. But if this came out—if the parish knew Julian had gotten the apprentice girl with child—old Trask would have burned the settlements on his kitchen hearth."

"So you sent Eleanor away," Martha said.

"We did not hurt the babe!" Mistress Finch cried, leaping to her feet, her hands outstretched in passionate denial. "As God is my judge, Martha Garrow, not a hair of that child’s head was harmed under this roof! Julian took it himself. On the night you left, after the sun went down. He wrapped it in his cavalry cloak and rode out toward the marsh. He said he had an aunt—a cousin of his father’s sister—who lives near Bridgewater, a woman whose own babe died of the thrush in August. He said she would take it for five pounds and rear it as a foundling."

Martha looked at Gervase Finch. The farmer had not looked up; his eyes were fixed on his boots, his lips trembling.

"Did you see the cousin, Gervase?" Martha asked.

"No," the farmer croaked.

"Did Julian bring back a letter? A receipt? A word from the parson at Bridgewater?"

"No," Finch whispered. "He said... he said the less was written down, the better for all of us. He came back at sunrise with his horse covered in foam and his cloak empty. He said the child was safe with a wet-nurse on the levels, and that we should never hear of it again."

"And Eleanor?"

"She stayed three weeks in the cheese-room until her milk dried," Mistress Finch wept. "I gave her two of my old shifts, a good woollen gown, and Gervase gave her the gold. We told her to take the carrier’s wagon to Bristol and find service in the city. We thought she was gone, Martha! We thought she was fifty miles away in the factories or the great houses! How could we know she would wander back into the lanes like a crazed creature, begging her bread from door to door?"

Martha stood by the table, her heart cold and heavy as a river-stone.

"She came back because a mother does not leave her milk seventy miles behind her," Martha said. "A cow will break three post-and-rail fences to find the calf that was taken from her at dawn. Did you think a nineteen-year-old girl would have less memory than a beast in your stall?"

She picked up her lantern and pulled her hood over her face.

"What will you do?" Gervase Finch asked. The old authority in his voice was gone; he sounded like a child asking about the dark.

"I will go home to my cottage," Martha said. "And tomorrow morning, when Justice Scrope sends Simon Clout to fetch me to the Petty Sessions at Ilchester, I will take my register and go before the magistrates."

"Martha," the farmer pleaded, stepping toward her with his hands open. "If Scrope commits me—if this goes to the Assizes—the lease is forfeited. The landlord will turn us out. My sons... my boys will have no roof over their heads. Everything my father cleared from the common—the orchard, the lime-kiln, the sixty acres on the hill—it will all be seized for the costs. We will be in the workhouse at Somerton before the wheat is in ear."

Martha turned at the door. Her face, framed by the rough grey wool of her hood, was carved into lines of sorrow as deep as the furrows in the winter wheat.

"I have eaten your bread for twenty years, Gervase Finch," she said. "Your wood has warmed my hearth through every frost since my mother was put into the churchyard. If my own life would save your house, I would give it to you freely on the road tonight."

She touched the tin cylinder that hung beneath her cloak.

"But my oath belongs to the Bishop, and the child belongs to God. I cannot sell the breath of a living babe to save your lease, Master Finch. If I do that, who will trust the midwife when the candle is put out in the next bed?"

She opened the door and stepped out into the biting wind, leaving the great kitchen of High Barn behind her in the dark.

### Chapter 3

The cottage on the Cross-Spur stood alone where four cart-tracks crossed upon the open heath. It had been built of rough field-flints laid in clay, with a thatch of rye-straw that was green with moss on the northern slope. A low turf wall enclosed an eighth of an acre of garden ground, where a dozen rows of winter kale stood frozen stiff as tin plate, and three old apple trees leaned away from the prevailing southwest gales.

Martha let herself in at the wicket-gate. Her fingers were so numb with cold that she could scarcely turn the heavy iron key in the lock.

Inside, the cottage was cold and dark, smelling of dry lavender, dried peppermint, and the sour soot of an unlit hearth. She struck a spark from her flint-box into the tinder, blew the red spark into a yellow tongue of flame, and touched it to a small tallow dip.

The single room was as clean and bare as an anchorite’s cell. In the corner stood her low truckle-bed with its chaff mattress and patchwork quilt; beside it, the deal chest where she kept her linen rollers, her scissors, her thread, and her sheepskin register. On the high mantelpiece stood two brass candlesticks, a stoneware jar of salt, and her mother’s Bible—a heavy, calf-bound volume printed in Oxford sixty years before, with the names of three generations of Garrows written on the flyleaf in faded brown gall-nut ink.

Martha did not light the fire. Ash wood was precious, and she had only three small billets left in the corner. She wrapped herself in her second-best woollen shawl, sat down at the small three-legged table, and opened her register.

The tallow candle flickered in the draught from the door-crack. The light fell across the narrow sheepskin pages, ruled into columns by her own hand:

*Date. — Parish. — House. — Name of Mother. — Sex of Child. — Condition at Delivery. — Remarks.*

She turned the leaves slowly. Every line was a night of her life.

*1775. Oct. 4. High Duntish. John Vowles, blacksmith. Mary his wife. Girl. Alive. Strong.* That girl was now thirty, married to a wheelwright at Somerton, with six children of her own.

*1781. Dec. 12. Nether-Coombe. Widow Crane’s cottage. Susan Crane. Boy. Dead. Born before his time; the cord twice twisted.* She remembered Susan Crane’s weeping, and the bitter cold of that winter night when the tea froze in the saucer beside the bed.

*1789. July 14. Saint Jude. The Parsonage. Mrs. Harford. Boy. Alive. Weak in the breast; baptized privately by the rector at sunrise.* That boy was now at school in Sherborne, wearing a little blue coat with silver buttons.

And then, near the foot of the penultimate page, written in ink that had faded from black to a deep, dark chestnut:

*1793. Sept. 17. High Barn. Eleanor Ward, single woman. Boy. Alive. Vigorous and lusty. Delivered at 4 of the clock in the morning. Witnessed by Mary Finch.*

Martha rested her index finger against the name of Eleanor Ward.

She remembered the girl’s face as the grey morning light had begun to filter through the diamond panes of the dairy-chamber. Eleanor had been an orphan from the Taunton workhouse, bound out at nine years old to the dairy-work at High Barn. She had the pale, delicate skin that often went with red hair, and long, slender hands that seemed too fine for scouring milk-pails and turning fifty-pound Cheddar cheeses.

During the delivery, while the contractions had torn at her like wolves, the girl had never spoken Julian Finch’s name. Even when Martha had leaned over her in the final, terrible agony—when the ancient law required the midwife to demand the father’s name under the peril of the mother’s soul before the child left the body—Eleanor had only bitten her lip until the blood ran down her chin, shaking her head against the bolster.

* "Tell me, child,"* Martha had whispered in the dark, her hands steadying the crowning head. *"The churchwardens must have a name, or the parish will cast you into the bridewell. Name the man who brought you to this bed."*

And Eleanor had looked up at her with eyes wild with pain and devotion, whispering through her broken teeth: *"He will take care of us. He swore it on his mother’s ring. He will take us away to the western country, where no one knows our names."*

Poor, foolish, faithful creature. She had believed him. She had believed the handsome young yeoman who rode with the cavalry and carried a silver-mounted whip.

And while Eleanor lay exhausted in the cheese-room, Julian Finch was already bargaining in the parlor below with his father and the miller from the Parrett.

Martha closed the book. The sheepskin cover shut with a soft, dry slap.

There was a tap at the window-pane.

Martha did not start. In twenty years, taps at her window had come at every hour between dusk and dawn. It was the summons of her trade: the hurried knock of a frightened husband, the rattle of gravel thrown against the glass by a barefoot boy sent running through the mud from some outlying farmstead.

She stood up, took the candle in her hand, and went to the casement.

The glass was frosted with delicate fern-leaves of white ice, but through the clear circle her breath melted, she saw a dark shape standing in the garden path between the kale rows.

It was not Simon Clout. It was not a parish officer.

The man outside was tall and broad-shouldered, wearing a heavy, many-caped riding-coat of dark green cloth, with high boots of oiled black leather that reached to the thigh. A low-crowned beaver hat was pulled down over his brow, but the candlelight caught the sharp, handsome line of his jaw and the arrogant curve of his nose.

Julian Finch.

Martha set the candle down upon the table. She walked to the door, slid back the heavy oak bolt, and opened it six inches.

"Julian," she said.

The young man did not wait for an invitation. He pushed the door open with his gloved shoulder, stepped into the cottage, and closed the oak leaf behind him with a sharp click of the latch. He filled the low room with his presence—smelling of cold air, horse-sweat, expensive tobacco, and the faint, sweet perfume of pomatum.

He took off his gloves, slapping them against his thigh, his dark eyes sweeping over the bare room, the cold hearth, and the open register on the table.

"You've done for us, Martha," he said. His voice was lower than his father’s, lighter and more musical, but there was a hard, dangerous edge beneath the charm. "The old man is sitting in the kitchen like a dead man, and my mother has taken to her bed with the hysterics. Scrope has sent a messenger to the Sheriff at Taunton. By noon tomorrow, the whole county will be talking of nothing else."

"Your father should have told the truth three months ago," Martha said, remaining standing by the table. "A lie grows faster than hemlock in a wet ditch, Julian. The longer it stands, the more ground it poisons."

"The truth?" Julian laughed, a short, ugly bark that had no mirth in it. "What truth? That an apprentice wench had a bastard in the cheese-room? Half the dairymaids in Somerset have done the same. Old Scrope has two natural sons living on his home farm at this moment, drawing thirty pounds a year out of the timber-sales. It isn't the child he cares about, Martha. It’s the money. The parish rates are up, and he wants an excuse to break my father’s lease."

"He cares about the child’s life," Martha said. "And so do I."

Julian walked to the table, his spurs clinking softly on the flagstones. He looked down at the open register, then reached out with one finger and touched the dry sheepskin.

"You delivered the boy," he said softly. "You know he was strong. You know he didn't die at High Barn."

"I know it," she said. "I swore it to the Justice two hours ago."

"Then swear the rest," Julian said, leaning across the table until his face was within a foot of hers. His dark eyes were bright, dilated with feverish excitement. "Swear that Eleanor took the child with her when she left the house. Swear that she had it wrapped in a woollen shawl, alive and crying, when she crossed the yard gate on Michaelmas morning."

Martha looked into his handsome, reckless face.

"Did she take it, Julian?"

Julian hesitated. The shadow of a flicker passed across his eyes—a brief, involuntary tremor that was gone almost before Martha could mark it.

"Of course she took it," he said.

"Your mother told me you took it yourself," Martha said quietly. "On the night of the delivery. Wrapped in your military cloak, on the road to Bridgewater."

Julian’s jaw tightened. A muscle twitched beside his mouth.

"My mother is an old woman," he snapped. "Her wits are wandering with the shock. She doesn't know what she remembers."

"Your father remembers it too," Martha said. "He remembers the horse covered in foam at sunrise. And he remembers that you brought back no child."

Julian turned away from the table, taking three long, restless strides across the narrow room. He stopped before the small, unlit hearth, looking up at the two brass candlesticks on the shelf.

"You think I killed it," he said. His back was turned to her, his voice muffled by the chimney-breast. "That's what Scrope thinks. That's what the gossips are saying over their small beer at the George. That Julian Finch took his own flesh and blood and dropped it into a peat-cut on the levels, like a blind puppy in a bucket."

"I don't know what you did, Julian," Martha said. "I only know that a living soul came into my hands in your mother’s house, and now it cannot be found. If you gave it to an honest woman to nurse, tell me her name. Tell me where she lives. I will take my basket and walk to Bridgewater tonight. I will find the house, I will see the child with my own eyes, and tomorrow at the Petty Sessions I will swear before the magistrates that the boy is alive and thriving. Then your father’s lease will be safe, Eleanor will be cleared of vagrancy, and this parish will have peace."

Julian stood motionless for a long time. The candle flickered, throwing his giant shadow up against the whitewashed rafters.

When he turned round, his face was pale, stripped of all its arrogance and easy confidence.

"I cannot tell you," he whispered.

"Why?"

"Because she isn't in Bridgewater," he said.

"Where is she, then?"

Julian looked at her with eyes that were hollow with fear.

"I gave the child to the drover," he said. "To Luke Coombes. The man who brings the black cattle from North Wales down to the Bristol market."

Martha felt the cold air in the room tighten about her heart like an iron band.

"Luke Coombes?" she breathed. "The Welsh drover?"

"He was at the inn at Cross-Hands that night," Julian said, the words tumbling out of him now in a desperate, broken stream. "I had twenty pounds from my uncle’s legacy in my pocket. I gave Luke fifteen guineas in gold—good guineas, Martha, from the bank at Yeovil. He swore by the Holy Cross that his sister in Glamorganshire had lost her husband in the coal-pits and had two cows and no children. He swore she would take the boy, christen him with her own name, and rear him as a Welsh farmer. He took him in a wicker hamper lined with sheepskin, on the pack-horse behind his saddle."

Martha stared at him in utter horror.

"A Welsh drover," she said, her voice scarcely louder than the wind in the chimney. "You gave a newborn infant—twenty-four hours from the womb—to a vagrant cattle-dealer on the turnpike road? A man who sleeps in the ditches and the alehouse stables? A man who changes his name at every county line?"

"He swore to me!" Julian cried, his voice rising in frantic self-defence. "He was an honest man, Martha! He had handled cattle for my father twenty years!"

"He took your fifteen guineas, Julian," Martha said, "and he took the hamper. Did you see him cross the Severn? Did you see him reach the hills? How do you know that child was not left beneath a hay-rick before the horse reached the first toll-bar?"

Julian sank down upon the bench by the door, dropping his face into his hands. His broad shoulders shook with silent, dry sobs.

"I wanted to save the farm," he wept. "My father worked forty years for that lease, Martha. If Trask broke the marriage, the landlord would have taken the stock for the arrears. We would have had nothing. Nothing but the clothes on our backs."

Martha walked across the room and stood over him. She looked down at the young man she had delivered from his mother’s body twenty-four years ago, whose first breath she had received, whose small, red limbs she had washed in the very basin that now stood upon her dresser.

"You have destroyed the farm, Julian," she said, with infinite sadness. "And you have destroyed your father. But worst of all, you have destroyed that girl."

"Eleanor?" Julian looked up, his face smeared with tears and sweat. "She knows nothing of Luke Coombes. She thinks the child is in Bristol. She would never speak against me, Martha. She loved me. She swore to me on her mother’s soul that she would die before she brought trouble upon High Barn."

"And she is like to die," Martha said. "In the jail at Ilchester, on a bed of wet straw, with thirty pounds of irons on her ankles."

She went to the door and pulled it open to the night.

"Go home, Julian," she said. "Go home to your mother. The wind is coming round to the north. We shall have snow before the morning."

### Chapter 4

The snow began at midnight—not the large, soft flakes that fall in quiet woods, but the fine, hard, driving dust that rides upon a gale from the German Ocean. It sifted under the cottage door, piling in a small white drift across the blue flags, and hissed against the horn lantern as Martha set out at six o'clock for the market-town of Ilchester.

It was six miles across the fen-country to the county jail. Under normal skies it was a two-hour walk along the causeway, but today the ditches were indistinguishable from the road, filled with level drifts of white that hid the deep, stagnant water beneath. 

Twice she stumbled into the ditch-bank, sinking to her waist in the freezing powder before she could haul herself out by the roots of the willows. Her skirts were frozen into stiff, rattling boards of ice that chafed her knees until they bled, and her hands inside her woollen mittens were so stiff that she had to carry the handle of her basket looped over the crook of her elbow.

Yet she did not turn back.

In her basket, wrapped in three layers of dry flannel to keep out the driving snow, lay a loaf of sound wheaten bread, half a pound of hard cheese, and a small stone flask of elderberry wine she had heated over her three billets of ash before leaving the cottage.

She reached the outskirts of Ilchester as the church bells were chiming nine for morning prayers. 

The ancient borough looked grey and desolate beneath the storm. The river Ivel ran black and sluggish between its crumbling stone walls, choked with floating cakes of anchor-ice that ground against the piers of the bridge with a heavy, groaning sound. Beside the bridge rose the massive, windowless walls of the County Gaol: a grim fortress of dark lias stone, its iron-studded gates coated with rime, its roof bristling with iron spikes to deter escapes.

Martha crossed the bridge and pulled the heavy iron bell-chain beside the wicket.

A turnkey with a face like a lard-bladder opened the hatch, squinting through the iron grate with red, rheumy eyes. He held a short Dutch pipe between his teeth, the smoke smelling of stale shag and gin.

"What's your business?" he barked. "The Justice doesn't sit till noon, and the debtors aren't allowed visitors till the bell rings."

"I am the midwife of Nether-Coombe," Martha said, her teeth chattering with the cold. "I have come to see the girl Eleanor Ward, who was brought in yesterday from Somerton."

"The girl from the parish?" The turnkey gave a coarse, greasy chuckle. "She's in the common ward with the vagrants and the shop-lifters. You're wasting your shoe-leather, mistress. She won't speak to nobody. The parson’s curate spent an hour with her yesterday trying to get her to sign a confession about the bastard, and she bit his sleeve like a wildcat."

Martha reached into her apron pocket. Her numb fingers found a silver sixpence—one of the three she had received from the blacksmith’s wife at High Duntish in November. She held it up between her finger and thumb.

"I have bread for her," Martha said. "And the sixpence for your trouble, Master Warder."

The man’s red eyes dropped to the silver coin. His dirty thumb shot out through the grate, scooped the sixpence into his palm, and the heavy bolt shot back with a screech of rusted iron.

"Five minutes," he growled, swinging the wicket open. "And don't you go leaving no files or iron hoops in the straw, or you'll find yourself on the other side of the hatch with the irons on your own shins."

He led her through a labyrinth of dark, dripping stone vaults that smelled of unwashed wool, rotting straw, stale beer, and human misery. The air was colder here than in the open street, trapped between ten-foot walls of solid rock that had absorbed the damp of sixty winters. From the debtors’ side came the faint, melancholy scraping of a fiddle and the clink of pewter pots; from the felons’ side came only the dull rattle of chains and the sound of someone coughing—a deep, hollow, rattling cough that sounded like a shovel striking gravel.

The turnkey unlocked a heavy oak door bound with iron straps and kicked it open.

"Visitor for the Somerton wench," he shouted into the dark. "Five minutes, and then the key turns."

He slammed the door behind her, and the bolt fell back into its socket.

The common ward was a long, low vault thirty feet by twelve, with a single grated opening high in the external wall through which the snow was sifting down upon the stone floor. A dozen women lay huddled together on heaps of grey, foul-smelling straw, wrapped in old sacking and horse-cloths to keep the breath in their bodies. Some were old crones with faces blackened by the smoke of gin-shops; some were girls no older than fifteen, their dresses torn, their eyes dull with the sullen stupor of the cage.

In the farthest corner, where the wall was black with running moisture, Eleanor Ward sat with her knees drawn up to her chin.

She had no cloak. She wore only the thin, soiled linsey-woolsey bedgown she had worn when she left High Barn, torn at the shoulder where the parish constable had seized her. Her red hair hung in matted, tangled strings over her face, and her bare feet, tucked beneath her petticoats, were blue and swollen with frostbite.

Martha knelt in the straw beside her. The smell of the place turned her stomach, but she did not shrink away. She set her basket on the stones, took off her own dry woollen cloak, and wrapped it gently about the girl’s shivering shoulders.

"Eleanor," she whispered.

The girl did not move. Her eyes were fixed on the opposite wall with a blank, unseeing stare. Her lips were cracked and crusted with dried blood, and her breathing came in short, dry gasps.

"Eleanor, child," Martha said again, laying her warm hand against the girl’s icy forehead. "It is Martha. Martha Garrow, from the Cross-Spur."

A violent shudder ran through the girl’s body. Slowly, painfully, as if her neck were made of rusted iron, she turned her head. Her sunken eyes flickered with a sudden, agonizing gleam of recognition.

"Mrs. Garrow," she croaked. Her voice was cracked, stripped of all its youthful sweetness. "Did... did you bring him?"

"No, child," Martha said gently. "I could not bring him."

Eleanor’s head dropped back against the damp stone. A low, dry sob shook her chest, but no tears came; her body was too parched for weeping.

"He is dead," the girl whispered. "I know he is dead. They told me... the constable said they found his bones in the ditch."

"They have found no bones," Martha said firmly, taking the girl’s hands in hers and rubbing the stiff, wooden fingers. "Listen to me, Eleanor. Look at me."

The girl looked up.

"The Justice Scrope called me to the Court House last night," Martha said. "He wanted me to swear that your baby was born dead. He wanted me to say it came still from the womb, so that no one would look for it."

A faint, bitter smile touched Eleanor’s lips.

"They want to save Julian," she muttered.

"Yes," Martha said. "They want to save Julian, and they want to save High Barn. But I did not swear it, Eleanor. I told the magistrate that your boy was born alive, sound and lusty, and that he drew his mother’s milk before the sun was up."

Eleanor stared at her in wide-eyed amazement.

"You told him that?" she whispered. "You told the Justice?"

"I did."

"Then... then they will look for him?"

"They are looking for him now," Martha said. "The Sheriff has the matter in hand. But Eleanor... you must tell me what you know. You must tell me the truth, as you hope for God’s mercy when your own hour comes."

She pulled the stone flask of elderberry wine from her basket, drew the cork with her teeth, and held it to the girl’s lips. The warm, spiced liquid brought a faint tinge of color back into Eleanor’s hollow cheeks.

"Where did Julian take him?" Martha asked. "Did he ever name the woman? Did he ever speak of Luke Coombes?"

At the name of Luke Coombes, Eleanor’s eyes widened with sudden, dreadful terror.

"Coombes?" she gasped, her hands clutching Martha’s wrists with frantic strength. "The drover? Did he... did Julian give my boy to that man?"

Martha felt a sick certainty settle in her chest.

"You didn't know," Martha said softly.

"He told me... he told me he had an aunt at Bridgewater!" Eleanor wept, the tears breaking through at last, washing clean tracks through the dirt on her cheeks. "He swore she was a decent churchwoman, with an orchard and two cows! He swore he would pay her four shillings a week until we could be married! He said... he said when the old man died, he would bring the boy home to High Barn, and no one would ever know he was not born in lawful wedlock!"

She collapsed forward into Martha’s lap, her forehead pressed against the midwife’s coarse woollen apron, her slender body shaking with uncontrollable weeping.

"Oh, God," she cried into the cloth. "My baby. My poor, sweet baby. He was so little, Mrs. Garrow. He had that little mark on his hip—the brown strawberry mark, just like the one Julian has on his side. He will be dead in the snow. Luke Coombes has thrown him into a bog, or sold him to the chimney-sweeps in Bristol!"

Martha stroked the girl’s matted red hair with her rough, gentle fingers.

"Hush, child," she murmured. "Hush. We do not know that. Luke Coombes is a hard man, but he is a churchman after his fashion. He has a wife and six children living in the hills above Abergavenny. I know the man; he has bought sheep from Master Finch these fifteen years."

She reached into her basket and pulled out the loaf of wheaten bread and the cheese, pressing them into Eleanor’s lap.

"Eat this," Martha commanded. "Keep the cloak about you. Do not give a crumb to the turnkey, or he will take the rest. I am going back to Nether-Coombe."

"What can you do?" Eleanor sobbed, clinging to Martha’s skirts as the midwife rose to her feet. "They will bring me before the Grand Jury on Tuesday! The lawyer said... the lawyer who came to the gate said if the child cannot be produced, the law will hang me by the neck until I am dead!"

Martha looked down at the girl. Her face was calm, pale, and unyielding as the Hamstone walls of the Court House.

"They will not hang you, Eleanor," Martha said. "Not while I have breath in my body to speak the truth. And if that child is drawing breath between here and the Welsh mountains, I will find him before the Assize judge puts on the black cap."

The turnkey’s key grated in the lock. The heavy oak door swung open with a screech of iron.

"Time's up, mistress," the man called from the vault. "The Justice's carriage has just crossed the bridge. He'll be in the court-room in ten minutes, and the hall has to be cleared."

Martha bent down once more, kissed the girl’s cold forehead, and walked out into the corridor without looking back.

### Chapter 5

When Martha crossed the river bridge on her return to Nether-Coombe, the snow had ceased falling, but the wind had veered into the north-northwest, bringing an Arctic bitterness that seemed to turn the very stones of the houses to glass. 

The clouds hung low and yellow over the Somerset levels, heavy with the promise of more snow before nightfall. The trees along the causeway stood like white skeletons against the leaden sky, their branches bowing under the weight of the rime.

Two miles north of Ilchester, where the track to High Duntish branched off from the Bristol turnpike, she saw a horseman riding toward her through the drifts.

It was not Julian Finch. It was Simon Clout, mounted upon the parson’s old grey pony, his short legs sticking out straight on either side of the pack-saddle, his woollen comforter wrapped four times about his ears.

He pulled up the pony with a jerk, the beast’s breath steaming in the frosty air.

"There you are!" Simon shouted, waving his cudgel. "I’ve been half-way to the borough looking for you! Mr. Scrope has sent the beadle to your cottage, and the rector is at the vestry with the parish books! They’ve called a special meeting of the Select Vestry at the church at two o'clock!"

Martha did not stop. She kept her steady, rhythmic stride along the frozen verge, her empty basket swinging on her arm.

"A meeting about what, Simon?"

Simon turned the pony and walked it beside her, the beast’s hooves crunching through the crusted snow.

"About the licence," the tithing-man said, leaning down from the saddle with an eager, malicious gleam in his watery eyes. "Mr. Scrope says you’re in league with the girl to lay a bastard on the parish. He says you knew the child was alive, and you let it be conveyed away out of the county to cheat the Overseers of their bond! He’s written to the Bishop’s Registrar at Wells, demanding that your licence be suspended under the sixty-seventh canon!"

Martha felt a sudden, sharp coldness in the pit of her stomach—colder than the wind off the fen.

"The Bishop’s Registrar?" she said.

"Aye!" Simon nodded with evident satisfaction. "And that's not the half of it. Gervase Finch has had a seizure. Fell down on his kitchen floor at ten this morning while the bailiffs were serving the writ of distress for the rent arrears. They had to carry him up to his bed like a sack of barley. His mouth is pulled round to his ear, and he can't speak a word that a Christian can understand."

Martha stopped in the middle of the road. She looked up at the little weaver on the parson’s pony.

"Gervase had a seizure?" she asked, her voice dropping to a whisper.

"Struck down like an ox," Simon said. "The doctor from Yeovil was riding past the gate and bled him twelve ounces from the neck, but he says the old man will never hold a plough again. Julian has taken the best mare and ridden off toward Bristol. Some say he’s gone to fetch a lawyer, and some say he’s gone to take ship for the West Indies before the Sheriff’s officers can lay hands on his collar."

The road ahead seemed to waver before Martha’s eyes.

For twenty years, the order of her world had been as fixed as the seasons: the seed-time, the hay-harvest, the shearing, the cider-pressing, and the winter deliveries. Gervase Finch had been the foundation of that order—the man who paid the day-labourers their eighteen-pence a week, who kept the parish bull, who lent his teams to the poor to haul their winter turf from the heath, and who stood between the cottagers and the cold, legal arithmetic of Justice Scrope.

Now, in less than twenty-four hours, the foundation had cracked to the bedrock.

And it was her word—her testimony in the library—that had struck the first blow.

"I must go to High Barn," Martha said, turning toward the combe path.

"You can't go to High Barn," Simon shouted after her, reining in the pony. "Mistress Finch has barred the door against you! She told the doctor that if Martha Garrow set foot in her yard, she’d set the sheepdogs on her! She says you’ve eaten their bread for twenty years only to sell their house to the landlord!"

Martha stood in the intersection of the two roads, the snow swirling about her boots.

The village of Nether-Coombe lay half a mile ahead, its thatched roofs clustered about the square tower of the parish church like a brood of brown partridges in the snow. From the chimney of the alehouse, the sign of the *Sheaf and Sickle* swung back and forth on its rusted hinges, screeching with every gust of the north wind.

She could see groups of men standing in the road outside the churchyard gate: smock-frocked labourers, weavers with their aprons tucked up, and smallholders in frieze coats, all gathered in the bitter cold, their breath rising in a common cloud as they talked.

As Martha approached the village street, the talking ceased.

A strange, unnatural silence fell over the crowd. The men did not touch their hats to her. They did not bid her good day. They parted slowly, sullenly, leaving a wide space in the center of the road, looking at her with cold, suspicious, hostile eyes.

These were the men whose children she had brought into the light.

There was John Vowles, whose wife she had sat with through three days of agony when the twins were born; there was William Clout, whose first-born she had saved from choking with a feather when the phlegm rose; there was old Giles the road-mender, whose daughter she had sheltered in her own cottage when the fever took the parish seven years ago.

Now they looked at her as if she were a stranger from another country—or a witch who had brought the murrain upon their cattle.

"Good afternoon, John," Martha said quietly to the blacksmith.

The blacksmith did not look at her. He spat into the snow at his feet, turned his back, and walked toward the smithy.

Old Giles stepped out from the churchyard wall, leaning upon his ash stick. His face was puckered with the misery of the cold, his old eyes watery and red.

"You shouldn't have spoken against the Master, Martha," the old man said, his voice trembling with a mixture of fear and reproach. "He’s been a good master to us. If High Barn is broken up, the landlord will turn the whole combe into sheep-walks. There won't be three men kept on where there was twenty. We’ll be on the parish gravel-pits before the summer, breaking stones for sixpence a day."

"I spoke the truth, Giles," Martha said gently. "Would you have had me swear a living child was dead?"

"What is a child to sixty families?" someone shouted from the back of the crowd. It was young Tom Vowles, his face dark with anger. "The girl was a stranger from Taunton! She was nothing to this parish! If the child had died, nobody would have wept for it! But now the whole vale is ruined, and you’ve got your silver in your pocket!"

"That is a lie, Tom Vowles," Martha said, turning upon the young man with a sudden, regal authority that made him take a step back. "I have no silver. I have never taken a penny that was not lawfully earned by my hands. And there is not a man among you whose wife or child has not lived by the skill God gave me."

She looked round at the circle of sullen, frightened faces.

"If the truth destroys an honest house, Tom Vowles, it is not because the truth is wicked; it is because the house was built upon a deceit. If Gervase Finch had given that child an honest name and an honest home, there is not a magistrate in England who could have touched his lease."

She walked past them, through the churchyard gate and up the flagged path to the church porch.

No one followed her, but as the heavy oak door of the church closed behind her, she heard the murmuring begin again outside—louder now, more bitter, the sound of a community turning upon the one who had made their danger visible.

### Chapter 6

Inside the church, the air was like the interior of a tomb. The high, whitewashed walls of the nave were bare save for the painted board containing the Ten Commandments and the black-letter royal arms of King William the Third. A single brazier of charcoal burned in the middle of the aisle, sending up a thin, blue smoke that did nothing to warm the stone flags.

At the communion table sat Justice Scrope, wrapped in a greatcoat of dark beaver-fur, his clerk sitting beside him with the parish account-books open before him. 

The Reverend Mr. Harford stood by the pulpit stairs, his hands tucked into his sleeves, his face pale and distressed. Beside him stood the two churchwardens—Farmer Trask the miller, a heavy, red-faced man with flour-dust still clinging to the brim of his hat, and Farmer Crane of Lower Duntish.

"Come forward, Mrs. Garrow," the rector said, his voice echoing hollowly in the empty nave. "The Vestry is called to consider the present condition of the parish, and the representation made by Mr. Scrope to the Chancellor of the Diocese."

Martha walked up the center aisle, her boots ringing on the grave-slabs of the old vicars of Nether-Coombe. She stopped three paces from the communion rail, curtsying to the gentlemen.

Justice Scrope looked up from a letter he was reading. His expression was neither angry nor triumphant; it had the cold, bureaucratic serenity of a man who saw every human calamity purely as an adjustment in the parish ledger.

"Mrs. Garrow," the magistrate said. "The Overseers of the Poor have completed their audit of the expenses incurred by this parish during the past twelve months. By reason of the war, the price of corn, and the extraordinary number of fatherless infants charged upon the rates, the poor-rate has risen to five shillings and sixpence in the pound."

He picked up a sheet of paper.

"Under the sixty-seventh canon of the Church of England, and the ancient constitutions of this diocese, a midwife is an officer of the ecclesiastical jurisdiction. She is sworn to act for the protection of the parish, to prevent the intrusion of charges upon the common stock, and to deliver up to the churchwardens every circumstance that may fix the maintenance of a child upon its lawful father."

He set the paper down and fixed his cold grey eyes upon her.

"Yesterday morning, you appeared before me as a witness. You swore that a living male child was born at High Barn in September. That testimony was sufficient to discharge the girl Eleanor Ward from the capital charge of murder under the statute of King James."

"It was the truth, your worship," Martha said.

"It was the truth as to the birth," Scrope conceded. "But what of the child itself? Where is that child now, Mrs. Garrow?"

"I do not know, sir."

"You do not know?" Scrope leaned forward, his spectacles flashing in the grey light from the clerestory windows. "You attended the birth. You remained in the house until eight o'clock in the morning. Master Finch is now lying upon his bed, deprived of his speech by the hand of God. Julian Finch has fled the county. The mother is in the county gaol, raving of Welsh drovers and cattle-dealers. And you—the only person of credit who saw that infant—tell this Vestry that you do not know where it was taken?"

"I have told you all that I saw, your worship," Martha said steadily. "I washed the child; I laid it in the cradle; I saw Mistress Finch watching over it. When I left the house, my duty was done. I am a midwife, Mr. Scrope, not a constable. I have no authority to set a watch upon the farmer’s gate, nor to ask what horses ride the road after nightfall."

Farmer Trask, the miller, stepped forward, his heavy fist slamming down upon the communion rail with a force that made the silver chalice rattle in its baize case.

"She knew it!" Trask bellowed, his face purpling with rage. "She knew the whole business from Michaelmas to Christmas! She’s been in and out of High Barn like a pet cat these twenty years! She knew Julian was courting my Phoebe, and she knew the girl was lying-in above the cheese-press! If she had come to me or to the churchwardens in September, this disgrace would never have touched my family! My Phoebe is half out of her wits with the shame of it, and the whole market at Yeovil is laughing in my face!"

"Peace, Master Trask," the rector murmured, laying a hand on the miller’s sleeve. "This is the house of God."

"It is the house of the parish!" Trask shouted. "And the parish is paying for her silence! Mr. Scrope, I demand that her licence be taken from her! Let her go and spin tow for her bread like any other pauper! We want no midwives in this parish who keep secrets for the gentry while the rates eat the smallholders out of house and home!"

Justice Scrope raised his hand.

"The churchwardens have a right to be heard," the magistrate said. "And the Chancellor of the Diocese has already answered my representation."

He drew from his pocket a broad parchment document sealed with a heavy disc of red wax—the seal of the Consistory Court of Bath and Wells.

Martha’s eyes fixed upon that red seal. She knew that wax. Her mother had held a duplicate of it for thirty years; she had carried her own for twenty. It was the mark of her calling, the thing that distinguished her from the drunken old crones who practiced their trade in the alleys of Bristol with dirty fingernails and bottles of gin.

"The Chancellor," Scrope read, his dry voice echoing through the arches, *"having considered the petition of the Minister, Churchwardens, and Principal Inhabitants of the Parish of Nether-Coombe, and the information of His Majesty’s Justice of the Peace: Doth hereby cite Martha Garrow, licensed midwife, to appear before the Consistory Court at Wells on the twentieth day of January instant, to answer certain articles touching her conduct in the matter of an unregistered and concealed birth. And in the meantime, We do suspend the said Martha Garrow from the exercise of her office and ministry within the said diocese, under pain of the greater excommunication."*

The magistrate looked up from the parchment.

"You are suspended, Mrs. Garrow," Scrope said. "From this hour, you are forbidden to attend any woman in labour, to receive any fee, or to act in any manner as midwife within this parish or any other. If you enter a lying-in chamber before the Chancellor has heard your cause, you will be committed to the county gaol for contempt of the Bishop’s Court."

Martha stood perfectly still.

The church seemed to expand around her, the cold stone walls receding into an infinite, desolate distance. The smell of the charcoal brazier was bitter in her nostrils, like the smell of charred wheat after a fire.

Twenty-one years.

Eight hundred and forty-two deliveries.

The memory of all those nights rose before her like a cloud: the small, dark rooms; the flickering candles; the sound of women’s voices murmuring in the shadows; the first, sharp cry of the newborn soul entering the cold air; the small, red hands reaching blindly for the breast.

She had held this parish in her hands. She had held its blood, its pain, its secrets, and its life.

And now, with six lines of legal Latin and a lump of red wax, they had made her less than the beggar at the churchyard gate.

"Have you anything to say, Mrs. Garrow?" the rector asked. His voice was genuinely sorrowful; he had known Martha since he was a young curate, and he knew the goodness of her life. But he was an old man, dependent upon the Squire’s favor for his living, and he had no strength to stand against the storm.

Martha looked at the magistrate, then at the miller, and finally at the rector.

"I have nothing to say, Mr. Harford," she said. Her voice was as calm and clear as the church bell on a frost-morning. "The Bishop gave me the licence, and the Bishop may take it back. But the skill in these hands did not come from the Bishop, Mr. Scrope. It came from God, through twenty years of watching by the beds of poor women. If a woman in this parish is in her agony tonight, and she calls for Martha Garrow, I will go to her—though you put forty irons on my feet and forty locks upon your prison door."

She made her quiet, modest curtsy to the communion table, turned her back upon the gentlemen, and walked down the long, cold nave to the door.

### Chapter 7

She did not go back to her cottage on the Cross-Spur.

When she stepped from the church porch into the village street, the wind was screaming across the roofs, tearing loose wisps of thatch from the eaves and driving the dry snow in swirling white devils across the road. The street was deserted now; the cold had driven every man to his own hearth or to the tap-room of the *Sheaf and Sickle*.

Martha pulled her woollen hood tight over her face, turned her back upon Nether-Coombe, and took the north road toward the hills.

She had sixteen miles to walk.

Sixteen miles across the Mendip ridge, through the high, limestone gorges and the wild sheep-walks where the snow was already five feet deep in the hollows. 

Julian Finch had named the man: Luke Coombes, the Welsh drover.

Julian had believed the drover’s story of a sister in Glamorganshire with two cows and an empty cradle. But Martha had known Luke Coombes since he was a lad driving bullocks for his father. She knew the route the Welsh herds took: they did not cross the Severn at the Old Passage in mid-winter; the ferry was too dangerous for horses and cattle when the drift-ice was running in the estuary. In January, the drovers took the high road through the hills, crossing the river at the New Passage or heading north through Gloucester to avoid the tidal reaches.

And there was one place where every Welsh drover stopped before he crossed into the western valleys: the *Black Dog* on Mendip, an ancient, isolated stone inn that stood five miles beyond the crest, where four county tracks crossed upon the limestone waste.

If Luke Coombes had carried that child away from High Barn in September, he had not taken it to Wales on the back of a pack-horse. A newborn babe, twenty-four hours old, would have perished of the night air before the horse reached the Bristol channel.

If the child had lived three days, it was because Luke Coombes had found a woman between Nether-Coombe and the hills. A woman with milk in her breast and no questions in her heart.

The climb up the combe was an agony of cold and wind. The track was nothing more than a sheep-path between high cliffs of grey limestone that rose two hundred feet into the driving sleet. The wind came down the gorge like water through a sluice, tearing at her cloak, blinding her eyes with frozen snow, freezing her breath into icicles upon her woollen muffler.

Twice she fell upon the scree, her knees striking the sharp limestone rocks through her petticoats. Her basket was torn from her arm and went tumbling into the drift; she scrambled after it on her hands and knees, her fingers digging through the snow until she felt the rough wicker.

She had no food. She had given her bread to the girl in the prison at Ilchester. She had only her small tin cylinder, her mother’s brass-mounted horn lantern, and the fierce, unyielding purpose that burned in her heart like a piece of banked coal.

*Seven hundred and ninety-one.*

That was the number in her book. That was her charge. Every one of them was a soul she had pledged to answer for before the Bishop’s throne. She had stood by their mothers when the candle was guttering; she had washed their small, frail bodies; she had seen their eyes open to the light.

She would not let this one vanish into the dark like a dog’s litter in a ditch.

It was four o'clock in the afternoon, and the twilight was already turning to a murky, purple blackness, when she reached the high plateau of the Mendips.

Here the world was nothing but a white desert beneath a sky the color of zinc. The stone walls that divided the sheep-leazes were buried to the copings; only the tops of the gorse bushes showed through the drifts like black tufts of horsehair. The road was gone, lost under a sea of undulating white that shifted and smoked under the fury of the gale.

Half a mile ahead, through the driving snow, a faint, yellow light appeared like a star on the horizon.

The *Black Dog*.

It stood alone upon the waste: a low, two-storey building of rough mountain-limestone, without a tree or a hedge to break the wind. Around it clustered a labyrinth of rough stone cattle-pounds, where the Welsh herds were folded on their way to the southern markets.

Martha reached the yard gate, dragging her frozen feet through two feet of drift. The yard was full of the sound of cattle: fifty black Welsh runts, huddled together in the lee of the stone barn, their backs white with snow, their breath rising in a thick, steaming cloud that smelled of wet hide and sour clover.

A lantern hung from the stable-door. Under its light stood a man in a frieze coat and an oilskin cap, fork in hand, shaking down straw into a rack.

"Master Coombes?" Martha called. Her voice was cracked, reduced to a dry, harsh croak by the wind and the cold.

The man turned. He was fifty, thick-set and bowed in the legs, with a face like an old walnut shell, cross-hatched by forty years of mountain gales. A short clay pipe was stuck in the corner of his mouth, and a heavy ash cudgel hung from his wrist by a leather thong.

He held up his lantern, squinting through the snow-dust at the small, grey-hooded figure standing by the gate.

"Who's that?" he shouted in a thick, guttural Welsh accent. "The house is full! There's no beds for tramps tonight! The kitchen is packed to the hearth with the drovers from Brecon!"

Martha stepped into the circle of yellow light. She pushed back her hood, revealing her pale, drawn face, her hair white with frost, her dark eyes burning with feverish intensity.

"Luke Coombes," she said.

The drover stopped, his fork poised halfway to the rack. His jaw dropped, the clay pipe slipping from his teeth and shattering upon the stone flags.

"God Almighty," the Welshman muttered, crossing himself with his dirty thumb. "Martha Garrow."

"Yes," she said. "Martha Garrow, from the Cross-Spur."

"What... what are you doing on the mountain in such a night?" he stammered, backing toward the stable wall. "The road from the vale is blocked since noon! No woman could walk the combe in this gale!"

"I walked it," she said.

She stepped closer to him, her shadow falling across the straw like an omen.

"I have come for the child, Luke."

The drover’s face went the color of a wet turnip. He looked to the left and to the right, as if searching for a gap in the stone wall through which he might bolt into the dark.

"I know nothing of no child," he growled, trying to put a blustering defiance into his voice. "I am an honest drover, mistress! I deal in beasts, not in babes! If young Master Finch told you—"

"Julian told me everything," Martha said, her voice cutting through the wind with the sharpness of a razor. "He told me of the fifteen guineas in gold. He told me of the wicker hamper lined with sheepskin, at the inn at Cross-Hands on the night of the seventeenth of September."

Coombes swallowed hard. His thick throat moved twice before he could find his voice.

"It wasn't my doing," he muttered, dropping the fork into the straw. "Julian came to me like a madman. He had the gold in his fist and the tears running down his face. He said his father would be ruined if the child was seen in Nether-Coombe. He swore it was an orphan—that the mother had died in the bearing."

"The mother is alive," Martha said. "She is lying in the county gaol at Ilchester, waiting to be hanged for murder because the child cannot be found."

The drover gasped, his knees trembling under his frieze coat.

"Hanged?" he whispered. "Murder? As the Lord is my witness, Martha, I never laid a rough hand upon that babe! I’m a Christian man! I’ve five daughters of my own christened in the chapel at Crickhowell!"

"Then where is he, Luke?"

The drover looked at her, his eyes wild with terror.

"He isn't in Wales," he whispered.

"I know he isn't in Wales," Martha said. "Where did you leave him?"

Coombes reached out with a trembling hand and caught her sleeve, drawing her into the shelter of the stable-door, away from the howling wind.

"I took him six miles," the drover said, his voice dropping to a hurried, frightened whisper. "Six miles from Cross-Hands, on the road to the marsh. The horse was stumbling in the dark, and the little thing was crying under the sheepskin like a young lamb. I knew it would die before we reached the hills. I couldn't take the breath of a Christian child on my soul, Martha. Not for fifteen guineas, nor for fifty."

"Where did you take him?" she repeated, her fingers tightening upon his wrist with the grip of an iron clamp.

"To the Black Mill," Coombes said. "On the edge of the sedge-moor, near Meare."

"The Black Mill?" Martha frowned, her mind running through the names of the families on the marsh borders. "The miller there is old Nathan Vane. His wife has been dead these ten years."

"Not Nathan," the drover said. "His daughter. Rachel."

Martha’s breath caught in her throat.

"Rachel Vane?"

"Her man was taken by the press-gang at Bristol in the spring," Coombes whispered. "Carried off to the fleet at Portsmouth, and his ship went down in the battle with the French in June. Rachel had her own babe three weeks after the news came—a little maid, born two months before her time. The little maid died in August, but the mother’s milk hadn't left her. She was living alone in the mill-cottage, half crazed with the grief, nursing a bundle of old rags she’d tied up in a woollen shawl."

He looked at Martha, a faint, pleading hope in his rough, weathered face.

"I went to her window at midnight," Coombes said. "I knew her father; he had ground barley for my winter droves these twenty years. I told her the Lord had sent her a boy to take the place of the little maid she’d buried in the churchyard. I gave her ten of the guineas Julian had given me, to buy flour and flannel. She took the boy in her arms, Martha. She sat down by the hearth, she pulled down her bodice, and the child took her breast before I had turned my horse in the lane."

Martha stood in the dark stable, listening to the heavy stamping of the Welsh runts in the yard outside and the howling of the Mendip gale.

A great, warm wave of relief seemed to break over her heart, so sweet, so sudden, that her knees gave way beneath her. She reached out and leaned against the timber door-post to keep from falling.

"He is alive," she whispered to the darkness.

"He was alive three weeks ago," Coombes said. "I went past the mill on my way south with the Michaelmas steers. Rachel was sitting in the porch with the boy on her knee. He had a little red frock on him, and he was laughing at the mill-wheel. She’s named him Martin, after her husband’s brother."

Martha straightened herself. She pulled her hood back over her head, tied the frozen thongs beneath her chin, and reached for her basket.

"Where are you going?" the drover cried, staring at her in bewilderment. "You can't go down the combe tonight! The gale is blowing from the north; the drifts in the gorge will be ten feet deep by midnight!"

"I am going to Meare," Martha said.

"It is ten miles across the marsh!" Coombes shouted, catching her cloak. "The rhines will be frozen, but the peat-cuts are covered with drift! You'll drown in the black water before you've gone two miles!"

Martha looked at the drover. Her face, white and lined with sixty hours of vigil and toil, had a strange, luminous peace upon it that silenced the rough Welshman like a blow.

"The Lord has kept that child alive through four months of deceit, Luke Coombes," she said. "He will not let the midwife freeze in the ditch while the mother is waiting in the county gaol."

She stepped out of the stable-door into the blinding fury of the snow, turning her face toward the dark levels of the south.

### Chapter 8

The country between the Mendip hills and the Bristol channel was a great, flat basin of peat-moor and willow-beds, known to the county as the Levels. 

In summer it was a paradise of green water-meadows, intersected by hundreds of deep, reed-fringed rhines where the fat dairy cows waded to their bellies in wild mint and water-crowfoot. But in winter, when the rivers Parrett and Brue overflowed their banks, the levels became a vast, grey inland sea, dotted with isolated hillocks where the ancient villages stood like islands in the waste.

Tonight the levels were frozen into a desert of white and black. The great floods that had covered the peat-cuts in November were frozen into sheets of rough, grey ice, over which the wind drove the snow in long, serpentine ridges that hissed as they moved.

Martha walked by the guidance of the church towers. 

There were no hedges here, only the lines of pollard willows that marked the borders of the drainage rhines. Every three miles, the square, stone tower of a fen-church rose above the willow-tops: Wedmore, Mark, Meare—ancient towers of Hamstone that had stood above the peat-waters since the monks of Glastonbury had dug the first canals six hundred years before.

Her feet had long since ceased to feel the cold. They were heavy, wooden weights that she lifted and set down by a mechanical effort of her will, one pace after another, through the crusted snow. 

Her right hand was frozen to the wicker handle of her basket; when she tried to shift her grip, the skin of her palm stuck to the osier-twigs with a sharp, burning tear.

Yet her mind was curiously clear.

She thought of Eleanor Ward in the common vault at Ilchester, sitting on her wet straw, holding her hands over her breast to ease the dull ache of the milk that would never come again.

She thought of Gervase Finch lying in his great four-post bed at High Barn, his mouth twisted to his ear, his eyes watching the window to see if the landlord’s bailiffs were driving the cattle from his byres.

She thought of Julian, riding his spent mare along the turnpike road to Bristol, looking back over his shoulder at every toll-bar to see if the Sheriff’s officers were galloping through the snow behind him.

And she thought of Rachel Vane, sitting by the mill-hearth with the fatherless boy on her knee, singing some old country ballad to the rumble of the frozen water-wheel.

How many lives hung upon the breath of that one infant?

If she brought the child back to Nether-Coombe, Eleanor Ward was saved from the gallows. That was certain. The law had no power over a mother whose child was living and thriving in another parish.

But what of High Barn?

If the child was produced—if it was proven that Julian Finch had paid fifteen guineas to convey away a parish apprentice’s babe to hide his own guilt—the scandal would be ten times worse than a simple bastardy charge. Old Trask would never give his daughter to a man who had bargained with drovers on the turnpike road. The lease of High Barn would be cancelled on the next rent-day; the Finch family would be cast out upon the common; and the sixty labourers who lived upon the farm would be turned into the parish gravel-pits before the spring.

And what of Rachel Vane?

The poor, lonely widow who had taken the motherless boy to her empty breast, who had bought him his little red frock with the drover’s gold, who had called him Martin after her dead husband’s brother. 

Was she to have the child torn from her arms by the parish constables, to be thrown into the workhouse at Somerton or bound out as a parish apprentice at seven years old to the colliers of the Mendip coal-pits?

Martha stumbled over the frozen lip of a drainage rhine. She fell forward, her face striking the hard, ribbed ice of the water-course.

She lay there for a long time, the cold seeping through her woollen garments into her bones. The wind had dropped; a strange, dead silence had fallen over the moor. The sky above was clearing again, the great constellations of winter—Orion with his glittering belt, and the pale, misty cluster of the Pleiades—rising cold and bright above the dark ridge of the Polden hills.

It would be so easy to stay here.

To close her eyes in the dry snow, to let the heavy, creeping numbness steal from her knees to her hips, from her hips to her heart. In an hour it would be over. The parish would find her in the morning, when the sheep-tenders came down to inspect the willows. They would lift her into a farm-wain; they would carry her back to Nether-Coombe; they would ring the church bell ninety-two times for her years and her services; and Mr. Harford would read the burial office over her grave by the churchyard yew:

*Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth: Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.*

And the secret would be buried with her.

Gervase Finch would die in his bed, the master of High Barn to the last. Julian would return from Bristol when the storm had blown over, marry Phoebe Trask, and take the three water-mills on the Parrett. Eleanor Ward would be transported to the plantations for vagrancy—a seven years' exile across the ocean, where life was cheap and memory was short. And the boy would grow up on the marsh-borders, a stout, red-cheeked miller’s lad, knowing no mother but Rachel Vane, and no father but the memory of the sailor who had drowned in the French wars.

Why should she rise?

Why should one old woman, with her licence suspended and her name dishonoured in her own village, drag herself across ten miles of frozen fen to overturn the lives of four households?

*Because of the oath.*

The words came to her not in the dry, legal voice of Justice Scrope, nor in the Latin phrasing of the Bishop’s parchment, but in the slow, solemn voice of her mother, sitting by the peat-fire on the Cross-Spur forty years ago:

*"Martha, the life of a child is not yours to give, and it is not yours to take away. It is not yours to hide, and it is not yours to sell. When you take a newborn soul out of the water, you are God’s bailiff. You must deliver it up to the light, though the world fall to pieces about your ears."*

Martha set her palms against the ice.

Her wrists screamed with pain, but she pushed herself up. She got her knees under her; she caught the rough, gnarled stump of a pollard willow; she hauled herself to her feet.

She picked up her basket.

Two miles to the southwest, across the level expanse of the frozen peat-cuts, a small, dark shape rose above the snow. It was the high, thatched roof of the Black Mill, with its four motionless sails standing like a black cross against the rising stars.

And from the low window of the miller’s cottage beside the race, a small, yellow light was shining across the ice.

### Chapter 9

The mill-pond was frozen to the sluice-gates, a sheet of green-black ice six inches thick that groaned softly under the frost. The great water-wheel, sixteen feet across, hung motionless in its housing, bearded with five-foot icicles that glittered like crystal daggers in the starlight.

Martha crossed the frozen race on the timbers of the foot-bridge. Her boots made no sound upon the packed snow.

She reached the cottage door. It was a low, heavy door of split oak, studded with square-headed boat-nails, sheltered by a small porch made of old ship-timbers from the Bristol docks.

Through the diamond panes of the small casement beside the door, she looked into the room.

The kitchen of the Black Mill was smaller and poorer than the great hall of High Barn, but it was warm and bright. A fire of dry peat-turves burned in the low iron grate, throwing a rich, aromatic blue smoke up the wide stone chimney. 

On a low stool before the fire sat Rachel Vane.

She was twenty-two, with the pale, clear skin and dark eyes of the fen-people. She wore a simple gown of brown linsey, with a clean white kerchief pinned across her bosom. 

In her lap sat the boy.

He was four months old now, fat and sturdy, his skin glowing like a ripe apricot in the peat-fire light. He had a little frock of scarlet flannel, tied at the shoulders with black woollen ribbons. His head was covered with a thick, curly mop of dark hair, and his fat, square hands were clutching a wooden spoon with which he was beating rhythmically upon the rim of an empty pewter bowl.

Rachel was laughing. It was a low, soft laugh, full of the deep, miraculous contentment of a woman whose empty arms have been filled. She leaned down, kissed the boy’s fat neck where the flesh creased in deep rolls of healthy baby-fat, and murmured something into his ear that made the child crow with delight.

Martha stood in the snow, her hand resting against the rough oak of the door-frame.

For a long minute she did not move. She watched the mother and the child through the glass, her heart aching with a mixture of reverence and terrible sorrow.

This was the child that Justice Scrope believed was lying at the bottom of the horse-pond.

This was the child that the gossips of Nether-Coombe said had been choked with a greasy rag and buried beneath the cider-press.

This was the child that Julian Finch had given to a Welsh drover for fifteen guineas, to save his father’s lease and his own marriage settlements.

He was here. Sound, lusty, beautiful, safe in the shelter of a woman’s love.

Martha raised her hand and knocked upon the door.

Three quiet, distinct knocks, the midwife’s knock that had opened five hundred doors in twenty years.

Inside the kitchen, the laughter stopped instantly. 

Rachel Vane started to her feet, gathering the child against her breast with a swift, defensive motion, her eyes turning toward the window with sudden, instinctive dread.

In the lonely fens, a knock at midnight meant only two things: the excise officers searching for uncustomed brandy from the channel, or the press-gang coming to carry off the remaining men for the warships at Plymouth.

"Who is there?" Rachel called, her voice trembling.

"A woman from the hills," Martha answered through the keyhole. "A woman who knows Luke Coombes."

A sharp silence followed. Then came the sound of a bolt being drawn slowly, carefully, from its socket.

The door opened two inches, held by a short iron chain.

Rachel Vane peered through the gap, her candle held high. She saw only an old woman, covered from head to foot in frozen snow, her face white and drawn with exhaustion, her grey hair plastered to her cheeks with frost.

"Who are you?" Rachel whispered.

"I am Martha Garrow," the midwife said. "The midwife of Nether-Coombe. I delivered that boy from his mother’s body four months ago."

Rachel’s face went white as the snow upon the thatch. Her hand flew to her throat, the candle tipping until the hot tallow dripped down upon her sleeve.

"No," she gasped, trying to shut the door. "No! He is mine! Martin... Martin gave him to me! My husband’s brother sent him from the fleet!"

Martha set the heel of her boot against the door-post. She did not force her way in; she merely stood there, her dark, sorrowful eyes meeting the young widow’s across the iron chain.

"The girl Eleanor Ward is in the county gaol at Ilchester, Rachel," Martha said gently. "She is sitting on the cold straw with the irons on her feet, waiting to be tried for his life before the King’s judges. If I do not show this child to the magistrate before Tuesday noon, they will hang her from the gate-house of the prison."

Rachel Vane’s fingers slipped from the bolt. The candle fell from her hand, extinguishing itself upon the stone floor of the porch.

The door swung slowly open.

The young widow stood in the blue light of the peat-fire, clutching the baby against her heart so tightly that the child let out a small, fretful whimper.

"Is it true?" Rachel whispered, her eyes wide with horror. "The girl... the girl did not want him dead?"

"The girl knew nothing of Luke Coombes," Martha said, stepping over the threshold and closing the door behind her against the freezing night. "She believed the boy was safe with his father’s people. She has nearly died of her grief, Rachel, just as you nearly died of yours when your little maid was taken."

Rachel sank down upon the low stool before the fire, her head bowing over the child until her dark hair mingled with his curls. She did not speak. She only rocked the boy back and forth, her tears falling in silent, heavy drops upon his scarlet flannel frock.

The boy did not cry. He looked up at Martha with his large, dark eyes—the Finch eyes, clear and bold—and reached out a fat, sticky hand toward the iron brooch at her throat.

Martha knelt on the flagstones beside them.

She reached out her rough, calloused hand—the hand that had brought him into the world—and touched his cheek. His skin was warm, smooth as silk, smelling of clean linen, woodsmoke, and sweet milk.

"You have been a good mother to him, Rachel," Martha said softly.

"He saved my life, Mrs. Garrow," the young woman wept, her voice muffled by the child’s neck. "When Martin was drowned... when the little maid died in August... I used to go down to the mill-race at midnight and look at the black water under the wheel. I thought... I thought I would just let myself slide into the pool, and the wheel would take me down to the sea, where Martin was."

She looked up at Martha, her face convulsed with agony.

"And then Luke Coombes came to the window with the hamper. When I felt that little mouth upon my breast... when I felt him draw the milk that was choking my heart... it was as if the sun had come up through the ice. Must they take him from me, Mrs. Garrow? Must he go to the parish workhouse?"

"No," Martha said, her voice rising with quiet, immovable authority. "He will not go to the workhouse. Not while Gervase Finch has a roof over High Barn."

She took the boy from Rachel’s arms. The child gave a little kick of his sturdy legs, then settled against Martha’s shoulder with a sigh of complete contentment, his thumb finding its way into his mouth.

Martha looked at the young woman.

"Put on your cloak, Rachel," she said. "Pack his flannels and his spoons in your basket. We have sixteen miles to travel before the sun is at the noon-mark."

"Where are we going?"

"To Ilchester," Martha said. "To the County Hall. We are going to show the King’s Justice what an unregistered birth looks like when it is brought into the light."

### Chapter 10

On Tuesday morning, the fog lay thick and yellow over the valley of the Ivel. The frost had broken at dawn, replaced by a raw, clinging thaw that turned the snow-drifts into grey slush and set every gutter in Ilchester running with yellow mud.

The Great Chamber of the Court House was packed to the doors with the inhabitants of three parishes.

They had come in farm-wains, on pack-horses, and on foot through the melting slush: the smallholders of Nether-Coombe, the weavers from High Duntish, the colliers from the Mendip out-crops, and the shopkeepers of the borough. In the front benches sat the tenant farmers, their broadcloth coats smelling of damp wool and old horse-stalls, their faces grave and anxious.

At the center of the bar stood Eleanor Ward.

She had been brought from the common vault an hour before. A clean linen cap, lent her by the jailer’s wife, covered her red hair, and Martha Garrow’s grey woollen cloak was pinned tightly about her thin shoulders. Her face was the color of a blanched almond, her eyes sunken into deep, purple hollows; but she stood erect, her fingers resting lightly upon the iron rail of the bar, her gaze fixed upon the empty chair behind the magistrate’s desk.

Beside her, supported on either side by his two younger sons, sat Gervase Finch.

The old yeoman had been brought down from High Barn in his best market-cart, laid upon a bed of oat-straw with three horse-cloths over his legs. His left side was dead; his left arm hung helpless in his lap, and the left corner of his mouth was pulled down toward his collar in a frozen, grotesque grimace. But his right eye was clear, dark, and full of a terrible, lucid awareness.

A murmur ran through the crowd as the side door opened and Justice Scrope entered the chamber, accompanied by the Reverend Mr. Harford and two visiting magistrates from the Yeovil division.

Scrope took his seat beneath the royal arms. He adjusted his spectacles, opened the heavy leather portfolio before him, and struck the desk three times with an ivory mallet.

"Silence in the court," the crier called. "His Majesty’s Justices are sitting for the dispatch of the parish business."

Scrope looked down at the paper before him.

"This is the matter of Eleanor Ward, single woman, removed from the parish of Somerton upon a charge of vagrancy, and suspected of the concealment and destruction of her male bastard child under the statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First."

He looked over his glasses at the prisoner.

"Eleanor Ward. You have been examined twice by the officers of this court. You have refused to name the father of your child, and you have refused to declare where the body of that child was laid. Do you now know anything that may stay this court from committing you to the Assizes at Taunton on the capital charge?"

Eleanor looked up at the magistrate. Her lips moved, but no sound came from her throat. She shook her head once—a small, hopeless movement that seemed to surrender everything to the iron machinery of the law.

Farmer Trask, sitting among the churchwardens, leaned forward with a grim, satisfied nod.

"Commit her, your worship," the miller muttered loud enough to be heard across the bar. "She’s a hardened jade. Let the Judge deal with her at Taunton."

Scrope dipped his pen in the ink-well.

"There being no evidence produced to show that the infant lived beyond the day of its birth," the magistrate began, his clear, cold voice carrying to every corner of the crowded room, "and the said infant being nowhere to be found within the limits of this county—"

"Stop, your worship."

The voice came from the back of the chamber, near the great double doors that opened onto the bridge.

The crowd turned as one man.

Down the center aisle, between the rows of staring farmers and silent weavers, walked Martha Garrow.

She wore her second-best woollen gown, her apron clean and stiffly starched, her hair combed back into its neat, grey knot beneath her bonnet. She walked with the slow, measured stride of a woman who had walked thirty miles through the Mendip snow-drifts and felt no fatigue.

Beside her walked Rachel Vane, wrapped in a dark blue cloak, her eyes downcast, her cheeks flushed with modest dread.

And in Rachel’s arms, wrapped in a clean white flannel blanket with his little scarlet ribbons showing at the throat, lay the boy.

A great, collective gasp—a sound like a sudden squall of wind in an autumn wood—ripped through the Court House.

Men stood up on the benches to see. Women leaned across the gallery rails, their hands pressed to their mouths.

"God above," Simon Clout whispered from the constable’s box. "It's the child."

Martha walked straight to the bar. She did not look at the miller; she did not look at the staring crowd. She stopped before the magistrate’s desk, made her quiet, respectful curtsy, and laid her tin cylinder upon the green baize cloth beside the law books.

"Your worship," Martha said, her voice filling the silent hall with its quiet, unyielding authority. "I have brought the witness."

Justice Scrope had risen from his chair. His spectacles had slipped down his nose; his pen remained suspended in his fingers, a single drop of black ink falling from the nib and blotting the white parchment below.

"Mrs. Garrow," the magistrate said, his voice unsteady for the first time in sixty years. "What is the meaning of this?"

"This is the meaning of my register, sir," Martha said. "This is Martin Ward, born at High Barn at four o'clock in the morning on the seventeenth day of September last. Delivered by my hands; nourished by this good woman, Rachel Vane, at the Black Mill in Meare; and now brought back to his mother."

At the word *mother*, Eleanor Ward let out a cry that was not human—a high, piercing scream of joy and agony that made every man in the chamber catch his breath.

She reached across the iron bar.

Rachel Vane stepped forward. For the space of three heartbeats, the two young women looked into each other’s eyes: the mother who had given him her blood, and the mother who had given him her milk.

Then, without a word, Rachel laid the child into Eleanor’s trembling arms.

The boy blinked at the crowded room, looked up into his mother’s face, and reached out his fat, square thumb to touch the tears that were streaming down her cheeks.

In the front bench, Gervase Finch began to weep.

The old yeoman could not speak, but the tears rolled down the living side of his face in heavy, silent streams, falling upon his dead hand where it lay in his lap. His good right arm reached out, catching his younger son by the coat-tails, shaking him with a frantic, triumphant joy that needed no words.

Justice Scrope sat down slowly in his chair. He took off his spectacles, wiped them carefully with his silk handkerchief, and looked at the assembly.

"The charge of murder," the magistrate said, clearing his throat twice, "is dismissed. The prisoner is discharged from custody."

A cheer broke out in the back of the hall—a wild, spontaneous roar from the weavers and the labourers that made the windows of the Court House rattle. Farmer Trask jumped to his feet, his face black with fury, and pushed his way out through the side door without looking at the bench.

Scrope struck the table with his mallet until order was restored.

"There remains," the magistrate said, his eyes resting upon the Finch sons, "the matter of the child’s settlement and maintenance upon the parish of Nether-Coombe."

Eleanor Ward turned toward the bench. She was holding the boy against her heart, her face transformed, her head held high.

"He will be no charge upon the parish, your worship," a new voice said.

The crowd parted once more.

Julian Finch stood in the entrance of the hall.

He had no riding-coat; his boots were grey with dried mud, his shirt was torn at the collar, and his handsome face was haggard with three days of desperate riding. But his head was up, his eyes clear, and his hand rested upon the hilt of his hunting-whip with the old, stubborn pride of the Finches.

He walked to the bar and stood beside Eleanor Ward.

"Julian," his brother gasped from the bench.

Julian did not look at his brother. He looked at Justice Scrope, and then he looked down at the girl and the child.

"The child is mine, your worship," Julian said, his voice ringing through the chamber without a tremor. "I am his father. I have signed a bond of indemnification with the churchwardens this morning for fifty pounds, and I have five hundred pounds in my pocket from the sale of my commission in the Yeomanry and my two hunters at Yeovil."

He turned to the Reverend Mr. Harford.

"Mr. Harford," Julian said, "the banns between myself and Phoebe Trask were forbidden by the miller on Sunday. I require you to publish the banns of marriage between Julian Finch, bachelor, and Eleanor Ward, single woman, both of this parish, on three successive Sundays in the church of Nether-Coombe."

The silence that fell upon the room was deeper than the silence of the snow on Mendip.

Old Gervase Finch raised his right hand from his knee. He pointed his finger at his son, nodded his head three times with slow, solemn approval, and closed his eyes in peace.

Justice Scrope looked at the young man for a long time. The magistrate’s face was impassive, but a faint, dry twitch of the lip showed that the legal arithmetic of the parish had found an answer that satisfied the books.

"The court is adjourned," Scrope said.

***

An hour later, the crowd had dispersed into the inns and the market-place, and the great chamber was empty save for the sweepers who were clearing the melted slush from the flags.

Martha Garrow stood by the outer porch, her basket on her arm, tying the strings of her woollen bonnet.

The Reverend Mr. Harford came out from the magistrate’s room, his gown tucked up under his arm, holding the tin cylinder in his hand.

"Mrs. Garrow," the rector said.

"Sir."

The old clergyman held out the cylinder.

"The Justice has withdrawn his petition to the Chancellor," Mr. Harford said, his eyes warm with a gentle, humble respect. "The suspension is cancelled. The register is returned to you, with an endorsement from the magistrates that your conduct in this matter has been an honor to the church and to your office."

Martha took the tin cylinder. She felt the familiar, smooth weight of the metal in her hand—the weight she had carried along the lanes of three parishes for twenty-one years.

"Thank you, Mr. Harford," she said.

"They are waiting for you at the bridge, Martha," the rector added, looking through the porch door toward the river.

Martha looked out.

The sun had broken through the yellow fog, turning the wet roofs of Ilchester to gold and setting the swollen waters of the Ivel sparkling between its stone walls. 

At the foot of the bridge stood thirty men and women from Nether-Coombe: John Vowles the blacksmith, old Giles the road-mender, the weavers, the cottagers, and their wives. They were not shouting; they were not cheering. They stood in two lines along the parapet, their heads uncovered in the winter sunshine, waiting for the midwife to pass between them on her way home to the vale.

Martha tucked the tin cylinder into her basket, drew her woollen cloak about her shoulders, and walked out into the light.
### Chapter 11

The walk back from the borough of Ilchester was six miles against the thaw. 

The wind had died away into a heavy, yellow stillness that smelled of melting snow and stagnant ditch-water. Along the causeway, the great white drifts that had blocked the lanes on Sunday were slumping into grey, pitted heaps, weeping continuous rivulets of brown water that ran into the wheel-ruts. The pollard willows stood ankle-deep in slush, their black, gnarled trunks glistening with moisture, and from the eaves of every barn along the road came the steady, rhythmic dripping of the thaw.

Martha walked with her head down, her second-best shoes squelching in the mud at every stride. 

Behind her, at a distance of fifty paces, came the market-cart from High Barn. Young Robin Finch, Julian’s sixteen-year-old brother, held the rope reins, walking beside the horse’s head with his smock tucked into his belt. In the bed of the wain, nestled among dry oat-straw and wrapped in three heavy horse-cloths, lay old Gervase. His grey head was propped against an empty flour-sack, his living eye watching the slow rotation of the cart-wheel as it churned through the yellow porridge of the road.

Beside the old man sat Eleanor Ward.

She had the boy pressed inside her cloak, against the warmth of her ribs. Her face was still the color of skimmed milk, and her fingers were raw from the prison straw, but she did not take her eyes from the child’s sleeping face. Whenever the cart-wheel struck a hidden limestone boulder beneath the slush, sending a violent jolt through the bed of the wain, Eleanor’s arms tightened instinctively, absorbing the shock into her own body before it could reach the cradle of her lap.

Julian rode forty paces ahead of the team on his lathered chestnut gelding. He had refused to put on a greatcoat, though the thaw-fog was raw enough to chill a sheep through its wool. His linen shirt was open at the collar, showing the dark hollow of his throat, and he rode with his chin high, staring straight along the turnpike road toward the church tower of Nether-Coombe.

He did not look back at his father. He did not look back at the girl. 

He rode like a man who had staked his last crown upon a turn of the dice and won the pot, but who knew that every player at the table was watching him with a knife tucked into his boot.

At the parish boundary—where the stone cross stood at the entrance to the Nether-Coombe combe—a knot of women was waiting by the hedge.

They were the wives of the day-labourers: Mary Vowles from the smithy, carrying her eighteen-month-old girl upon her hip; widow Crane in her rusty black bonnet; and three of the dairymaids from the lower farms. They had not come to cheer. In the vales, women who worked the cheese-presses and the turnip-fields had little time for shouting.

As the cart reached the cross, Mary Vowles stepped out into the road, holding up her red, chapped hand to stop the horse.

Robin Finch pulled on the rope halter. The old cart-horse stopped with a heavy sigh, its flanks steaming in the damp air.

Mary Vowles walked to the side of the wain. She looked down at Eleanor Ward.

For twenty heartbeats neither woman spoke. The only sound was the dripping of the thaw from the branches of the thorn hedge and the heavy breathing of the old man in the straw.

Then Mary reached into the pocket of her coarse apron. She pulled out a small, flat cake of barley-bread, baked on the griddle that morning and wrapped in a clean dock-leaf.

"Take it, girl," the blacksmith’s wife said, her voice rough and dry as sheep-shears. "You'll have no milk in your breast if you starve your belly on the road. The child looks hearty enough, God be thanked."

Eleanor took the cake with a trembling hand. "Thank you, Mrs. Vowles."

"Don't thank me," Mary said, stepping back into the ditch. "Thank the woman who walked Mendip in the drift to fetch him. There isn't three men in this parish that would have crossed their own threshold on such a night, let alone the hills."

She turned her sharp, dark eyes upon Julian, who had reined in his horse ten paces ahead and sat looking down at them from the saddle.

"You've brought home a wife, Master Julian," Mary called out, her voice carrying clear and hard through the fog. "See that you keep her better than you kept the dairy-cows. If we see her begging her bread on the turnpike again, there's forty women in Nether-Coombe that will take their broom-staves to your shoulders, commission or no commission."

Julian’s face flushed a deep, violent crimson. He raised his hunting-whip as if to strike his boots, bit his lip until the skin went white, then touched his gelding with the spur and trotted off toward the village without answering a word.

Martha reached the gate of her cottage on the Cross-Spur half an hour before the cart.

Her thatched roof had shed the snow in great, sodden blankets that lay piled three feet high along the walls. The kale in the garden stood brown and bruised, their frozen leaves limp in the thaw; but from the tiny chimney of field-flints, a thin thread of blue smoke was rising into the mist.

Martha frowned. She had left her hearth cold when she set out for Ilchester on Monday morning.

She pushed open the wicket-gate and walked up the gravel path.

The cottage door stood on the latch. When she stepped into the low room, the smell of hot broth and burning peat met her like a blow.

Sitting on the three-legged stool by the hearth, her petticoats tucked up and her sleeves pinned to her shoulders, was Rachel Vane.

The young widow had reached the cottage an hour before on the carrier’s wain from Glastonbury. She had cleared the ash from the grate, kindled a fire with three billets of dry birch she had found in the lean-to, and set Martha’s iron pot upon the trivet to boil.

On the table stood a loaf of fresh barley-bread, a crock of salt butter, and a dozen brown eggs in a wicker basket.

"You shouldn't have stayed, Rachel," Martha said gently, setting her empty basket on the dresser.

Rachel turned round from the pot. Her eyes were red with weeping, but her face was quiet and composed, with that patient, enduring stillness that belonged to women who lived by the water-levels.

"I could not go back to the mill today, Mrs. Garrow," Rachel said softly. "The house... the house would be too wide. When the wheel goes round, it sounds like someone calling from the race. I thought... I thought perhaps you would let me sleep on the chaff-bag in the corner tonight, and I will take the carrier’s wagon to Wedmore tomorrow."

Martha walked across the flags, laid her hand upon the young woman’s shoulder, and pressed it with all the quiet strength of her long fingers.

"You may stay as long as pleases you, child," Martha said. "The room is narrow, but there is bread enough for two, and the fire is warm."

She took off her wet bonnet and sat down upon the bench by the window. Her limbs felt like leaden pipes filled with cold ditch-water. For seventy hours she had not slept more than twenty minutes at a stretch, and now that the magistrates had spoken and the register was safe in its cylinder, the exhaustion came upon her like an armed man.

"Did they... did they give him to his mother?" Rachel asked, stirring the broth with an iron spoon.

"They gave him to her," Martha said.

"Did he cry?"

"Not a peep," Martha said, a faint smile touching her cracked lips. "He had his thumb in his mouth, looking at the King’s coat of arms above the Justice’s chair as if it were a picture-book in a shop."

Rachel stared into the bubbling pot, a single tear slipping from her chin and hissing upon the hot peat.

"She is very young," the widow murmured.

"Nineteen at Michaelmas," Martha said.

"Will they... will they be good to her at the great house?"

Martha looked out through the small casement toward the combe road, where the grey mist was gathering in the hollows.

"Mistress Finch has taken to her bed," Martha said. "She has the pride of forty years of cheese-making in her blood, and she had set her heart on the miller’s daughter and the three water-mills on the Parrett. It will not be easy bread for Eleanor Ward, Rachel. A daughter-in-law brought home from the county gaol in a market-cart is a bitter herb to swallow with your bacon."

"She has the boy," Rachel said simply. "A woman can bear a great deal of salt in her porridge if she has her child at her knee."

"Aye," Martha murmured, leaning her head against the rough stone of the chimney-piece. "She has the boy."

### Chapter 12

On Sunday morning, the church bells of Nether-Coombe rang for the first time since the frost broke.

The air was mild and damp, smelling of green moss and wet sheep-pasture. The snow had vanished from the combes, leaving the red earth of the fallow fields raw and dark against the grey limestone ridges. Along the borders of the churchyard, where the sun hit the southern wall, the first yellow spikes of the winter aconites were pushing through the dead grass.

The whole parish had gathered in the churchyard by ten o'clock.

They stood in little clusters beneath the great yew tree, whispering together in low, excited voices, watching the lych-gate with an intensity that had not been seen since the old Squire died of the apoplexy ten years ago.

At a quarter past ten, the carriage from Court House arrived. 

Justice Scrope stepped down first, wearing his Sunday coat of dark plum-colored broadcloth, his gold-headed cane tucked under his arm. He walked with his usual measured, unhurried pace, looking neither to the right nor to the left, his thin lips set in their habitual line of civil indifference. Behind him walked his sister, Miss Scrope—a tall, severe lady in black silk who was known to inspect the cottages of the poor for unwashed pots and unscrubbed flagstones—and his clerk, carrying the estate ledgers in a leather bag.

Five minutes later, the wain from High Barn turned the corner by the smithy.

It was not the dung-cart that had carried Gervase Finch to the court. It was the best road-wagon, its wheels newly greased with yellow tallow, its side-boards painted a bright, clean blue.

Julian walked by the horses, wearing his yeomanry coat of dark green with silver buttons, his beaver hat brushed until it shone like an otter’s pelt. His head was held high, but there was an unnatural, rigid tension in the set of his jaw, and he looked straight between the ears of the lead horse, refusing to catch the eye of any man in the yard.

In the wagon sat Mistress Finch.

She was dressed in her best Sunday gown of black lutestring—the gown she had had made for Julian’s christening twenty-four years before—with a heavy crape shawl pinned over her bosom. Her face was pale, thin, and hard as an iron skillet. She looked neither at the people nor at the church tower; she looked straight ahead, her hands clasped tightly over her silver-clasped Prayer Book.

Beside her sat Eleanor Ward.

The girl wore a plain grey woollen gown that had belonged to Martha’s mother, altered at the waist by Rachel Vane’s needle on Saturday night, with a white cambric handkerchief pinned across her throat and a straw bonnet tied with grey ribbons. 

In her arms she carried the boy.

He was wrapped in a new white woollen blanket that Gervase Finch had ordered out of the wool-loft that morning: three yards of the finest Southdown fleece, combed and carded by the old man’s own weavers before the war began.

A dead silence fell upon the churchyard as Julian stopped the team beside the mounting-block.

He stepped to the side of the wagon, let down the tail-board, and held out his hands.

He lifted his mother down first. Mistress Finch took his arm with a stiff, formal movement, smoothed her black skirts, and walked through the lych-gate without waiting for the others.

Then Julian turned to Eleanor.

He did not look at her face. He took the child from her arms, held the boy firmly against his green coat with his left arm, and with his right hand helped the girl down to the gravel.

For a moment, Eleanor seemed to sway upon her feet. Her face went pale as linen, and she reached out blindly toward the wagon-wheel to steady herself.

From the shadow of the porch, Martha Garrow stepped forward.

She did not speak. She took Eleanor’s left hand, drew it through the crook of her own arm, and supported the girl’s weight against her shoulder.

Together—the young yeoman with the child, the parish apprentice in her borrowed gown, and the midwife in her grey cloak—they walked up the flagged path toward the church door.

The crowd of cottagers and farmers fell back on either side, making a broad lane through the grass. No one raised a hat; no one spoke a word of greeting. But as they passed the porch, old Giles the road-mender took off his greasy woollen cap and held it against his chest, and John Vowles the blacksmith nudged his wife with his elbow, muttering:

"Look at the boy’s fist, Mary. He has old Matthew’s thumb, sure enough."

***

The church was cold and damp, smelling of old lime-wash and wet coats.

The High Barn pew was the largest in the nave, situated directly beneath the pulpit: a great square box of dark oak, lined with green baize that had faded to the color of pond-weed, with six velvet hassocks and a brass rod for the curtain.

Mistress Finch was already seated in the corner, her Prayer Book open before her, her face turned toward the altar like a carved image of stone.

Julian opened the door of the pew. He stood aside, waiting for Eleanor to enter.

The girl hesitated on the threshold. Her eyes moved from the rich green baize to the face of the proud, silent woman in the corner. For six years she had entered that pew only on Saturday mornings, with a bucket of lye and a scrubbing-brush in her hand, to scour the boards for the farmer’s family.

"Go in, Eleanor," Martha whispered behind her.

Eleanor stepped over the sill. She sat down upon the low bench by the door, keeping her knees pressed together, her hands folded in her lap.

Julian followed her, sat down beside her, and laid the child into her lap. Then he turned his back upon the nave, took up his father’s heavy leather Bible, and began to turn the leaves with loud, deliberate slaps of his thumb.

The Reverend Mr. Harford entered the chancel from the vestry, followed by the parish clerk with the register under his arm.

The old rector looked weary, his powdered wig sitting slightly askew upon his grey hair, but his eyes were gentle as he mounted the three steps of the pulpit.

He read the Morning Prayer in his slow, quiet, musical voice: the confession, the absolution, the psalms for the nineteenth day of the month.

*Lord, who shall dwell in thy tabernacle: or who shall rest upon thy holy hill?*  
*Even he, that leadeth an uncorrupt life: and doeth the thing which is right, and speaketh the truth from his heart.*  
*He that hath used no deceit in his tongue, nor done evil to his neighbour: and hath not slandered his neighbour.*

When the psalms were ended, the rector laid his hands upon the velvet cushion of the pulpit and looked down upon the congregation.

"I publish the Banns of Marriage," Mr. Harford said, and his voice sounded loud and clear in the silent church, "between Julian Finch, bachelor, and Eleanor Ward, single woman, both of this parish. If any of you know cause, or just impediment, why these two persons should not be joined together in holy Matrimony, ye are to declare it. This is the first time of asking."

A sharp, rustling sound went through the church as every neck turned toward the miller’s pew on the north side of the aisle.

Farmer Trask sat alone in his high box. His face was the color of a bruised damson, his thick lips clamped shut, his heavy fists buried deep in the pockets of his coat. Beside him, his daughter Phoebe was not to be seen; the miller had sent her away to her aunt at Yeovil on Friday night, behind the closed curtains of his gig.

Trask did not move. He did not raise his hand. He sat like a stone bull in a pen, staring fixedly at the brass chandelier that hung above the chancel screen.

"This is the first time of asking," the rector repeated, waiting three heartbeats in the dead silence.

No voice answered.

Mr. Harford turned the leaf of his book, took up his sermon-case, and gave out the text from the eighth chapter of the Gospel according to Saint John:

*And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free.*

### Chapter 13

The wedding took place three weeks later, on a Tuesday morning when the sky was the color of lead and the rooks were building their nests in the parsonage elms.

It was not a village festival. 

There were no ribbons on the horses’ bridles, no firing of muskets in the lane, no barrels of cider broached upon the village green. The marriage of an apprentice girl to the heir of High Barn was not a thing that the parish could celebrate with fiddles and Morris-dancers. It was an act of reparation—a cold, solemn settlement of accounts between a proud family, an offended church, and the parish rate-books.

Julian stood before the altar-rails in his dark green coat, his boots newly blacked, his jaw set in that hard, defensive mask that had not left him since the day of the trial.

Eleanor stood beside him in her grey gown, her hand trembling so violently that Julian had to hold her wrist to keep her fingers steady while he forced the thick gold ring—his grandmother’s wedding-ring, taken from Mistress Finch’s cedar-box that morning—over her third finger.

Old Gervase Finch was not there. The doctor had forbidden him to leave his room; the raw February air would have brought the phlegm into his throat and finished what the apoplexy had begun.

Mistress Finch sat alone in the family pew, her black crape veil drawn down over her face, weeping silently into her handkerchief throughout the service.

The only witnesses who signed the book in the vestry were Robin Finch, who made a clumsy, ink-blotted cross with the goose-quill, and Martha Garrow.

When the clerk pushed the heavy parchment register toward Martha, she dipped the pen into the iron ink-stand and wrote her name in her firm, upright hand:

*Martha Garrow, Midwife.*

Justice Scrope, who had attended as churchwarden to ensure that the bond of indemnification was properly recorded, looked over her shoulder as the ink dried.

"You write a remarkably clear hand, Mrs. Garrow," the magistrate said, his voice dry and level. "Few women in this district can sign their names, let alone keep a journal."

"The old curate taught me, your worship," Martha said, setting the quill back into its stand. "He said that when a woman has the charge of souls, she should be able to read the register as well as the parson, so that no man can tell her what she saw."

Scrope’s thin mouth twitched with that faint, almost imperceptible irony that passed with him for a smile.

"An admirable principle, mistress," he said. "Though not always convenient for the parish officers."

He took up his hat and gloves, made a stiff bow to the bride and bridegroom, and walked out of the vestry without offering his congratulations.

***

The feast at High Barn that afternoon was eaten in the great kitchen, beneath the hanging flitches of bacon.

Mistress Finch had ordered two legs of mutton to be boiled with turnips, and a great suet pudding with raisins; but she did not sit at the table herself. She remained in the small parlor behind the cheese-room, with her door bolted against the company, reading the Book of Common Prayer by the light of a single candle.

Julian sat at the head of the long ash table, carving the mutton with savage, energetic strokes of the great knife, heaping the pewter plates with meat and potatoes, and pressing the cider upon the four farm-labourers who had been called in from the threshing-floor to make up the company.

"Drink, John!" Julian shouted to the blacksmith, filling his horn cup from the great brown pitcher. "The cider is from the eighty-nine press—the best fruit we ever grew on the combe. There's forty hogsheads left in the cellar, and by God, we'll see the bottom of them before the barley is sown!"

John Vowles drank, but he drank with an uneasy, guarded air, his eyes shifting toward the door that led to the old master’s bedchamber above.

"How is the Master today, Master Julian?" the blacksmith asked, wiping his mouth with the back of his sleeve.

"Mending!" Julian said, slamming the pitcher down upon the table until the salt-cellar jumped. "He had a cup of goat’s milk and two spoonfuls of broth at noon. The doctor says his right side is strong enough to knock down a bullock. He'll be in the saddle before the sheep-shearing, you mark my words!"

No one answered. Every man in the room knew that Gervase Finch would never sit a horse again.

At the other end of the table sat Eleanor.

She had the boy on her lap, feeding him tiny morsels of boiled potato mashed with sheep’s milk from a pewter spoon. She looked tired, her eyelids heavy with the long strain of the month, but there was a quiet, stubborn dignity in her face that had not been there when she was the dairymaid. She wore her wedding-ring with a quiet, unconscious pride, her left hand resting upon the table where the gold caught the firelight.

Martha sat beside her, peeling an apple with a small horn-handled knife.

Presently, the door at the foot of the stairs opened, and Robin Finch came down into the kitchen, his face pale and anxious.

He walked to Julian’s chair and whispered in his brother’s ear.

Julian set down his carving-knife. The wild, reckless color faded from his cheeks, leaving him looking suddenly ten years older.

"Martha," Julian said, his voice dropping into a quiet, subdued tone. "My father wants you."

Martha wiped her knife on her apron, rose from the bench, and followed the boy up the narrow, winding stone stairs that led to the master’s chamber.

### Chapter 14

The chamber above the kitchen was large and low-ceilinged, smelling of vinegar, camphor, and the dry, sweet scent of old lavender. 

A small fire of birch logs burned in the grate, throwing a dancing pattern of red light across the heavy curtains of the four-post bed. On the table beside the pillows stood a row of medicine phials with paper labels written in the doctor’s Latin, a pewter pap-boat, and an open Bible propped against a brass candlestick.

Gervase Finch lay propped up on four large feather pillows, wearing a clean linen night-cap tied beneath his chin. 

His face was terribly changed. The right side was still the face of the great yeoman—firm, ruddy, cross-hatched by the weather, with its sharp, intelligent grey eye; but the left side was sunken, grey, and motionless, the corner of the lip drawn down into his neck like a hook. His left arm lay upon the counterpane, lifeless and pale as a peeled willow-wand.

When Martha entered, his right eye turned toward her.

A faint, guttural sound came from his throat—a sound like a pump sucking dry at the bottom of a well.

Martha walked to the bed. She took his cold, heavy right hand in both of hers and pressed it gently.

"Good afternoon, Gervase," she said.

The old man’s fingers closed upon hers with surprising strength. The grip was hard, dry, and desperate, the grip of a man drowning in his own silence.

With his right forefinger, he pointed toward the door.

Martha looked at Robin, who was standing by the bed-post with tears in his eyes.

"Leave us, Robin," she said softly. "Wait in the lobby."

The boy nodded and went out, closing the heavy oak door behind him.

Martha drew a low chair to the bedside and sat down.

Gervase Finch did not try to speak. He had learned the bitter lesson of the apoplexy: that words came out only as a broken, meaningless babble that brought the sweat of shame to his forehead.

Instead, he reached with his right hand beneath the bolster and drew out a small, flat iron key tied to a piece of whip-cord.

He held it out to Martha.

She knew that key. It belonged to the great iron-bound oak chest that stood at the foot of the bed—the chest in which the Finches had kept their leases, their title-deeds, and their gold since the reign of King Charles.

Martha did not take the key.

"What is it, Gervase?" she asked.

The old man pointed with the key toward the chest, then pointed three times toward the floor beneath their feet—toward the kitchen where the sounds of the wedding-feast were muffled by the heavy oak beams.

"The leases?" she asked.

He shook his head with an angry, impatient jerk.

He touched his own chest, then pointed his finger toward his mouth, then made a motion with his hand as if he were rocking a child in his arms.

Martha’s eyes widened with sudden understanding.

"The boy," she said.

A long, shuddering sigh escaped the old man’s ribs. He nodded his head twice, his right eye filling with tears that overflowed and ran down into his grey beard.

He took Martha’s hand again, pressed the iron key into her palm, and closed her fingers over it with all his remaining strength.

Then he reached out and tapped the open Bible on the table beside him.

His finger rested upon the thirty-third chapter of Genesis:

*And Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept.*  
*And he lifted up his eyes, and saw the women and the children; and said, Who are those with thee?*  
*And he said, The children which God hath graciously given thy servant.*

Martha looked from the printed words to the old man’s face.

"You want me to open the chest, Gervase?"

He nodded eagerly.

Martha rose, went to the foot of the bed, and knelt before the oak chest. The lock was stiff with age and grease, but the heavy iron bolt shot back with a dull, hollow clank.

Inside lay three compartments lined with green baize. 

In the first lay the parchment leases of High Barn, bearing the great red seals of the Scrope family for eighty years. In the second lay five bags of coarse grey linen, tied with leather thongs—the gold that Gervase had hoarded through five years of high wartime prices to buy the freehold of the Lower Combe meadow.

In the third compartment lay a single folded sheet of stamped paper, written in the neat, professional hand of the town-clerk of Yeovil, dated only ten days before.

It was Gervase Finch’s will.

Martha brought the paper to the bedside, holding it where the light from the birch fire fell across the text.

The old man watched her with feverish intensity, his chest rising and falling beneath the blankets.

"Shall I read it to you, Gervase?"

He nodded once.

Martha opened the sheet. Her eyes moved rapidly down the formal legal clauses: the customary commendation of the soul to Almighty God; the bequest of forty shillings to the poor of Nether-Coombe; the life-interest in the cheese-room and six cows to his beloved wife Mary; the farm of High Barn, with all its stock, implements, teams, and crops, to his eldest son Julian, subject to the payment of two hundred pounds each to his younger sons Robin and Matthew.

And then, at the foot of the second page, written in a fresh, dark ink that had been added since the trial at Ilchester:

*"Item: I give and bequeath unto Martin, the son of Eleanor Ward, now the lawful wife of my son Julian, the sum of three hundred pounds of lawful money of Great Britain, to be paid out of the timber-sales on the hill when he shall attain the age of one-and-twenty years. And I do hereby appoint Martha Garrow, licensed midwife of this parish, to be the sole trustee and guardian of the said legacy for the use and benefit of the said child, without interference from my said son Julian or any other person whatsoever."*

Martha looked up from the paper.

Her hands were shaking. The letters seemed to dance upon the parchment in the red firelight.

"Three hundred pounds," she whispered.

In the vale, three hundred pounds was a fortune. It was the price of fifty fat bullocks; it was the lease of a sixty-acre farm; it was independence, dignity, and freedom from the parish rates for a man’s whole life.

"Gervase," she said, looking into his clear, sorrowful right eye. "Why have you done this?"

The old man reached out his hand. He touched the sleeve of her coarse woollen gown, then pointed his finger upward toward the ceiling—toward the roof, toward the sky, toward the great, silent throne before which both of them must one day stand.

He took the goose-quill that lay beside the Bible, dipped it into the ink-pot, and with a terrible, agonizing effort of his right hand, guided by Martha’s steady fingers, wrote his name at the foot of the sheet:

*Gervase Finch.*

Then he sank back upon the pillows, his breath coming in short, rattling gasps, his grey face shining with cold sweat.

He closed his eyes. The iron grip of his fingers relaxed, slipping from Martha’s hand and resting peacefully upon the white counterpane.

Martha folded the paper, carried it back to the chest, turned the key in the lock, and hung the iron key back upon the whip-cord beneath his bolster.

She stood by the bed for a long time, watching the slow, rhythmic rise and fall of the old man’s chest.

Outside the window, the February dusk was settling over the valley. 

The rooks had ceased their cawing in the parsonage elms; the mist was rising from the water-meadows, white and cold as wool, creeping up the slopes of the combe toward the sheep-cotes. From the road below came the sound of a horse trotting toward the turnpike—the carrier’s wagon returning from the market at Yeovil, its bells jingling faintly in the frosty air.

The house of High Barn was broken. 

The proud, ancient alliance with the millers was gone; the lease was precarious; the mistress was sitting in her shuttered parlor reading the burial service over her living family; and the heir had married the parish apprentice who had scrubbed his dairy floor.

Yet as Martha looked down at the old yeoman’s face, she saw a peace there that had not been there in the magistrate’s library, nor on the road from Ilchester.

The lie was dead. 

The secret that would have rotted the timber of the house from cellar to ridge-pole had been dragged into the open air and buried in the churchyard mud. The price had been paid—in gold, in pride, in tears, and in the stroke that had broken the master’s body.

But the child was alive. 

The child was sleeping in the kitchen below, with his mother’s arm about him and the parish register behind him, safe in the light of an English day.

Martha pulled her woollen hood over her hair, took her empty basket on her arm, and walked softly down the winding stone stairs to the road.

### Chapter 15

Spring came late to the Somerset vales in the year of ninety-four.

Throughout March, the wind hung obstinately in the northeast, bringing grey scuds of sleet that withered the early daffodil shoots in the churchyard and kept the sheep shivering in the lee of the limestone walls. On the high downs, the lambing was the hardest Martha had known since the great frost of eighty-three. The ewes had no milk in their udders for lack of green keep; their breath froze into white rime upon their muzzles as they dropped their lambs in the furrows, and half the twin-lambs died before they could find the teat.

Martha lived in her woollen cloak.

From Lady Day to the middle of April, she scarcely saw the inside of her cottage on the Cross-Spur save to change her wet stockings and take a fresh loaf from the bread-crock.

Her sheepskin register was filling rapidly:

*March 28. High Duntish. William Vowles, wheelwright. Sarah his wife. Twin boys. Both alive. Weak.*

*April 2. Nether-Coombe. Giles the road-mender’s daughter, Ann. Girl. Alive. Sound.*

*April 9. Saint Jude. The Miller’s Cottage at the Pool. Susan Trask. Boy. Alive. Delivered after thirty hours.*

Everywhere she went, the talk was the same: the war, the French fleet, the price of corn, and the new tax upon hair-powder and windows. 

Wheat had reached seventy-six shillings a quarter at Mark Lane, and the labourers in the villages were living on barley-dumplings and boiled nettles. In the manufacturing towns to the north—at Bath, at Frome, and at Shepton Mallet—the weavers were breaking the new spinning-jennies and parading through the streets with loaves of bread dipped in blood stuck upon the points of their poles.

In Nether-Coombe, the trouble had taken a quieter, more sullen shape.

Farmer Trask had raised the price of grist at his three water-mills, demanding two-pence a bushel for grinding the poor people’s gleanings, and refusing to take payment in kind. Justice Scrope had set a guard of four parish constables upon the estate granaries at the Court House, and had given notice that any person found wandering in the woods with a billhook or a snare would be committed to the County Gaol under the Black Act without bail.

Only at High Barn was the order of the seasons maintained.

Old Gervase Finch could no longer walk, but every morning at six o'clock, young Robin and Matthew lifted the old man into a low wicker chair mounted upon four small wooden wheels—a carriage that the wheelwright at High Duntish had built for him out of ash-poles and old cart-irons.

They wheeled him out to the great porch of the barn.

There the old master sat throughout the day, wrapped in his frieze coat with a sheepskin over his knees, his good right eye watching the work of the yard. 

Nothing escaped that eye. 

If a labourer threw down his fork before the noon-bell chimed; if a calf was scoured in the pen; if a plow-share was set too shallow in the stony ground of the Upper Combe—the old man’s ash stick would strike the flagstones with three sharp, imperious knocks, and Robin would run to carry his father’s order to the men.

And beside the wicker chair, Eleanor Ward Finch ruled the dairy.

She had taken off her borrowed wedding-finery on the morning after the marriage, folding the grey gown neatly away in Martha’s mother’s chest, and had put on her old blue linsey bedgown and her coarse linen aprons.

She was the first woman in the cheese-room at four in the morning, her skirts tucked up to her knees, her white arms plunged to the elbow in the great copper vats of milk, testing the heat of the curd with her wrist. 

Under her management, the butter from High Barn took the top price at the Yeovil market throughout the spring. She had the light, cool hand that the cheese-makers said was a gift from heaven: the curd never soured under her palm, the whey ran off clear as spring-water, and the great ninety-pound Cheddars that dried upon her racks were as smooth and yellow as wax candles.

Mistress Finch had surrendered the dairy keys without a word.

The old mistress spent her days in the parlor, spinning flax for the family sheets, her wheel humming with a low, monotonous drone that could be heard through the ceiling of the cheese-room. She never spoke to Eleanor unless the work of the house required it; she never called her *daughter*, and she never sat at the same table when the meals were served.

But every afternoon at three o'clock, when Eleanor carried the boy out to the barn porch to be kissed by his grandfather, Mistress Finch’s parlor curtain would move half an inch.

Through the narrow slit between the green baize and the frame, the old woman’s eyes would watch the child: watching his strong, straight limbs as he scrambled over the threshing-floor; watching his dark, curly hair blowing in the April wind; watching the broad, flat thumb that clutched the old yeoman’s ash stick.

She had not forgiven the shame. She would never forgive the loss of the Trask alliance and the five hundred pounds in the bank at Yeovil.

But the boy was a Finch. 

He had the blood, the bone, and the stubborn, unyielding temper of the men who had held High Barn against landlords, bailiffs, and bad harvests for a hundred and fifty years.

### Chapter 16

On the third day of May, a boy came galloping down the Cross-Spur track on an unbacked pony, his hair flying, his bare heels drumming against the beast’s ribs.

He pulled up at Martha’s gate, shouting before the pony had stopped:

"Mrs. Garrow! Mrs. Garrow! Come quick to the Black Mill! Rachel... Rachel is taken with the fever!"

Martha dropped the bundle of dried chamomile she was tying for the chimney-shelf. She snatched her basket from the table, caught her woollen cloak from the peg, and was in the road before the pony had turned.

"Who sent you, boy?" she demanded, walking rapidly beside the stirrup.

"Nathan Vane," the lad panted. "The old miller. He come back from the peat-digging at noon and found her lying on the kitchen floor, raving of the French fleet and the little maid that died in August. Her head is like a hot coal, and she can't draw her breath for the stitch in her side."

Martha felt her heart contract.

*The marsh fever.*

It was the scourge of the levels: a violent, inflammatory distemper that bred in the stagnant ditches when the May sun first heated the rotting peat-sludge. It began with an ague that set the teeth chattering until the cups rattled on the dresser, followed by a burning, delirious heat that dried the tongue like leather and filled the lungs with bloody froth within eight-and-forty hours.

She did not wait for the carrier’s wain. 

She took the short cut across the sedge-moor—a track that was still half-submerged under the spring floods, where the brown peat-water splashed to her knees at every step and the wild snipe drummed in the reeds overhead.

It was four o'clock when she reached the Black Mill.

The great sails were stopped, tied to the lower posts with ropes of twisted willow. The race was silent, the water pouring over the waste-weir with a heavy, hollow roar that sounded like distant cannon.

Inside the cottage, the air was suffocating.

Old Nathan Vane sat by the unlit grate, his grey head buried in his hands, his old coat smeared with black peat-mud. On the low bed in the corner lay Rachel.

Her face was not the pale, quiet face that had watched the peat-fire in January; it was flushed to a dark, dusky scarlet, her lips dry and blackened with crusts of fever, her eyes wide, staring, and bloodshot. Her dark hair was tangled in wild, wet curls across the bolster, and her hands were picking incessantly at the woollen threads of the counterpane.

*"Martin,"* she was whispering, the sound rattling in her throat like gravel in a tin horn. *"The water is coming under the door, Martin. The wheel has got the child... the wheel is taking him down to the sea..."*

Martha dropped her basket by the bed.

She laid her cool palm against the girl’s forehead. The heat struck through her skin like the heat of an iron plate taken from the embers. When she laid her ear against Rachel’s ribs, she heard the sound she dreaded more than any other: the dry, leathery creak of the inflamed pleura, grating with every short, agonizing breath.

"Nathan," Martha said, turning to the old man. "Bring me two buckets of cold spring-water from the upper well. Not the race-water—the spring-water from the rock."

The old man stumbled to his feet and went out with the wooden pails.

For forty-eight hours, Martha did not leave the bedside.

She bled the girl eight ounces from the arm, watching the dark, thick, inflammatory blood flow into the pewter porringer until the pulse softened beneath her fingers. She shaved the matted hair from the crown of Rachel’s head and applied cold compresses of vinegar and spring-water to cool the burning brain. 

She brewed a strong decoction of willow-bark and dried elder-flowers, feeding it to the girl spoonful by spoonful through her clenched, blackened teeth, wiping the yellow froth from her lips with clean linen rags.

Throughout the second night, the delirium raged with terrifying fury.

Rachel fought against the sheets, screaming for the child that had been taken from her arms, crying that Luke Coombes had stolen her little maid and sold her to the soldiers at Bristol. 

Martha held her down with all the quiet, patient strength of her long arms, speaking to her incessantly in that low, steady, hypnotic voice that had brought three hundred women through the dark gate of madness in the lying-in chamber.

*"Hush, Rachel. Hush, child. The boy is safe. Martin is at High Barn, in his cradle. The wheel is stopped, Rachel. There is no water on the floor. Martha is here. Martha has the watch."*

Toward dawn on the third day, the storm broke.

The violent, jerking motions of the girl’s limbs ceased. A deep, profuse sweat broke out upon her forehead, her neck, and her chest—a warm, natural sweat that smelled of life instead of rot. 

Her breathing slowed, dropping from forty shallow gasps a minute to twenty long, deep, regular respirations that filled her lungs without a rattle.

Her eyes closed. The dusky scarlet faded from her cheeks, leaving her skin pale, limp, and cool as a river-lily.

She fell into that deep, healing sleep that is the gift of nature when the fever-poison has spent its fury.

Martha stood up from the stool.

Her back was so stiff that she could scarcely straighten her spine. Her legs trembled with exhaustion, and her eyes burned as if they were full of limestone dust.

She walked to the cottage door and opened it to the morning.

The sun was rising over the Polden hills, a great ball of molten gold that burned the white mist from the surface of the peat-cuts. The willow-beds were green with their new leaves, glistening with thousands of dew-drops that sparkled like diamonds in the early light. From the reeds beside the race, a reed-warbler was singing its hurried, chattering song, full of the fierce, miraculous joy of the spring.

Nathan Vane came up the path from the mill-house, carrying an armful of dry peat-turves.

He looked at Martha’s face. He saw the dark hollows beneath her eyes, the lines of sixty hours of labor carved about her mouth—and he saw the quiet, luminous peace that rested upon her brow.

The old miller dropped the turves upon the path.

He fell upon his knees in the wet grass, caught the hem of Martha’s coarse, mud-stained apron in both of his hands, and pressed it to his lips, weeping like a little child.

"She will live, Nathan," Martha said softly, looking out across the golden marsh. "The fever has broken. God has given her back to you."

### Chapter 17

Midsummer came, and with it the great sheep-shearing at High Barn.

It was the ancient festival of the upland farms: three days of sweat, dust, cider, and the heavy, grease-laden smell of six hundred fleeces shorn from the backs of the Southdown sheep.

The great barn was cleared from end to end. 

The floor was swept until the oak planks shone; the shearers sat in two rows upon low benches, their sharp steel shears clicking rhythmically from six in the morning until the sun went down behind the combe. In the center of the floor stood Julian, his shirtsleeves rolled to the shoulder, his hands black with fleece-grease, weighing the wool on the great steelyard and marking the tallies in his pocket-book.

Eleanor sat at the long table by the granary door, serving the shearers with their noon-meat: cold boiled beef, great wedges of her own April cheese, and two-penny loaves of white wheaten bread.

On her hip she carried Martin.

The boy was nine months old now, crawling with the speed of an otter across the clean straw, his fat hands black with wool-grease, laughing at the great white sheep as they were turned out of the pens, naked and bewildered, to run back to the hills.

Old Gervase sat in his wicker chair by the mounting-block, holding a horn of cider in his right hand, watching the piles of golden fleece rise toward the rafters.

The wool-buyer from Leeds had arrived that morning on his post-chaise from Bristol. 

He was a shrewd, sharp-eyed Yorkshireman in a grey coat, who tested the staple of the wool by pulling a lock between his thumbs until it snapped like a violin string.

"It is sound wool, Master Finch," the Yorkshireman said to Julian, leaning over the tally-board. "Sound and long. There's no rot in the fleece, and the rime hasn't broken the fiber. I'll give you sixteen-pence a pound for the whole clip, delivered at the canal-basin at Taunton on Monday."

Julian looked at his father.

The old man nodded his head once with slow, emphatic satisfaction.

Sixteen-pence a pound. 

It was the highest price that had ever been paid for wool in the parish of Nether-Coombe. It meant four hundred pounds in gold: enough to pay the arrears of rent, to clear the doctor’s bill, to buy sixty sound ewes for the autumn breeding, and to leave fifty guineas in the linen bags in the oak chest.

As the wool-buyer was counting out his earnest-money upon the tail of a wagon, the clatter of a horse’s hooves sounded in the lane.

Justice Scrope rode into the yard on his tall bay hunter, accompanied by his bailiff.

The shearers stopped their clicking shears. A dead silence fell upon the great barn, the men looking at the magistrate with the old, instinctive hostility of the tenant class.

Julian stepped out from the porch. He did not take off his hat, but he touched the brim with a firm, civil finger.

"Good day, Mr. Scrope," Julian said.

The magistrate looked down from his saddle: at the piles of clean wool in the barn; at the fat cart-horses in the yard; at the old yeoman sitting in his wicker chair with his horn of cider; and at Eleanor, standing by the granary door with the boy in her arms.

His dark, intelligent eyes lingered for a long moment upon the child.

Then he reached into his coat pocket and drew out a parchment roll tied with red tape.

"Master Finch," Justice Scrope said, addressing the young man with that cold, formal courtesy that never left him. "The estate leases for the Nether-Coombe division have been reviewed by my solicitors in London."

He held out the parchment.

"This is the counterpart lease of High Barn, for the lives of Julian Finch, Eleanor his wife, and Martin their son, and the survivor of them, at the old rent of eighty-four pounds a year."

Julian took the parchment.

His hands were black with wool-grease, and they left a dark, oily smudge upon the clean white skin of the deed.

"The old rent?" Julian asked, his voice shaking slightly. "The miller said... Trask told the market that you would demand a hundred and twenty on the renewal."

"Master Trask is a miller, not my steward," Scrope said dryly. "The land at High Barn is good land, Master Finch, because your father and your grandfather made it good with their own labor and their own dung. It is not my practice to fine a tenant for his own improvements, nor to turn out an ancient family to satisfy the malice of a neighbor."

The magistrate gathered up his reins.

"I see the boy thrives," Scrope added, glancing once more at Martin.

"He does, sir," Julian said, his head held high. "He has sixteen teeth, and he can stand by the dresser without help."

"See that you teach him to sign his name, Master Finch," Scrope murmured, turning his horse toward the gate. "In these times, an unlettered man is at the mercy of every rogue who can handle a pen."

He touched his hat to the old man in the wicker chair and trotted out of the yard, his bailiff following behind him.

A cheer broke out in the barn—a great, ringing cheer from the shearers that startled the rooks from the orchard and brought Mistress Finch to her parlor window.

Old Gervase lifted his horn of cider with his good right arm, took a deep, long draught, and threw his hat into the straw with a wild, triumphant laugh that had not been heard from his lips since the day Julian was born.

### Chapter 18

Michaelmas came round again, bringing the misty autumn mornings, the smell of woodsmoke from the potato-patches, and the heavy thump of the cider-press at High Barn.

On the twenty-ninth of September, Martha Garrow sat at her small three-legged table on the Cross-Spur, her sheepskin register open before her.

The candle-light fell upon the final page of the book.

During the year of ninety-four, she had attended forty-eight births in the three parishes. 

Forty-four of them were marked with her small ink prick; four of them were marked with the cross. It had been a hard year, a year of war and dear bread, but every soul that had been born alive had been brought to the church-font and entered in the parish register under its true name.

There had been no more secret deliveries above the cheese-vats. 

There had been no more bargains with Welsh drovers on the turnpike road. 

The parish of Nether-Coombe had learned its lesson: that a lie hidden in a dark room is like a smouldering coal in a hay-rick, waiting only for a breath of wind to set the whole homestead in a blaze.

A gentle tap sounded upon the cottage door.

Martha rose and opened it.

Standing in the autumn twilight, her cheeks pink with the walk up the combe, her hair neatly combed beneath a straw bonnet, was Rachel Vane.

She carried a wicker basket covered with a clean white cloth.

"Good evening, Mrs. Garrow," Rachel said, stepping into the cottage with a bright, modest smile.

"Good evening, Rachel," Martha said. "How is your father?"

"Mending, thank God," Rachel said, setting her basket on the dresser. "The new mill-stones from Bristol are hung, and he has ground thirty sacks of barley for the parish this week. He sent you this."

She lifted the cloth from the basket.

Inside lay a stone jar of honey from the marsh-heather, two loaves of fine wheaten bread, and a pair of warm, thick stockings she had knitted herself from the wool of the High Barn shearings.

"And how is Eleanor?" Martha asked, pulling out the stool for her guest.

"She is well," Rachel said, her face glowing with quiet happiness. "I was at High Barn on Saturday with the flour. The old mistress has come out of her parlor, Mrs. Garrow. She was sitting in the kitchen with the boy on her knee, teaching him to say *Our Father* before he was put into his crib."

She looked at Martha, her dark eyes soft with a profound, unspoken gratitude.

"Julian has given my father the carting of all the grain from High Barn for the next seven years," Rachel added. "We shall have plenty of bread now, Mrs. Garrow. Even if the winter is as hard as the last."

The two women sat by the hearth for an hour, talking of the parish, of the crops, of the women whose hour was near, and of the peace that had returned to the vale.

When Rachel had taken her basket and gone down the lane toward the marsh, Martha walked to the door and looked out into the night.

The stars were rising over the Mendip ridge, cold, clear, and bright as they had been on the night she had walked the combe in the snow.

From the valley below, three miles away through the autumn mist, the lights of Nether-Coombe were shining: the yellow windows of the farms, the smithy fire glowing red against the church tower, and the steady, quiet light from the kitchen of High Barn.

Martha looked down at her hands—her long, strong, rough hands that had received eight hundred and forty lives from the dark and delivered them into the light.

She had lost the friendship of the miller; she had lost the smooth, easy favor of the great tenants; she had seen her name dragged before the Bishop’s court and her calling dishonoured in the village street.

She was forty years old, an unmarried woman living alone upon the heath, with no gold in her chest and no land to leave behind her when her own hour should come.

Yet as she looked down upon the sleeping valley, she felt no bitterness, no sorrow, and no regret.

She had kept her oath. 

She had preserved the mother’s life; she had restored the father’s house; she had kept the child from the ditch and the parish from the curse of a hidden blood.

She went back to the table, took up her goose-quill, and at the foot of the final page of her register, beneath the long list of names, wrote the words that her mother had written thirty years before when her own work was done:

*The work of the hands is nothing; the truth of the heart is all.*  
*Finis.*

Then she put out the candle, laid her head upon her chaff pillow, and slept the deep, untroubled sleep of the faithful servant who has finished her measure.
### Chapter 19

The frost that fell upon the south of England in the third week of November 1794 was not the brisk, dry rime of common winters. It began with an easterly gale that blew out of the Baltic, driving a bank of iron-colored clouds before it that shut out the sun for fourteen days, until the turf on the downs was frozen three feet deep and the water-mills along the Parrett fell silent one by one as the ice choked their wheels.

Old men who remembered the great frost of forty sat by their hearths with their knees wrapped in flannel, shaking their heads at the weather-glass. The Severn sea was white with floating ice-cakes as far south as Minehead; the mail-coaches from London were abandoned in six-foot drifts on the Wiltshire plains; and in the three villages of Nether-Coombe, High Duntish, and Saint Jude-in-the-Fens, the sheep-tenders found the ewes frozen to the hurdle-stakes where they had huddled for warmth.

At the cottage on the Cross-Spur, Martha Garrow kept her iron pot simmering upon the hearth from dawn to dark. 

It was no longer broth of mutton or beef. The price of meat in Ilchester market had risen beyond the reach of any cottager: beef was eight-pence a pound, butter was fourteen-pence, and the quartern loaf of brown bread stood at ten-pence halfpenny. Martha’s pot held only swedes, dried yellow peas, and the skin of a ham that Mistress Finch had sent down from High Barn in the bottom of a meal-sack.

Every morning at daylight, three or four women from the common would tap at Martha’s door, their aprons tucked over their heads to keep out the piercing draught. They did not come for medicine. They came for the spoonful of warm liquor that Martha ladled into their earthenware cups, and for the dried elderberry cordial she kept in two-quart stone jars beneath her bed.

"The Justice has had four sheep stolen from his home farm," Mary Vowles told her, blowing upon her red fingers as she stood beside the dresser. "The constable found the skins buried under the snow in parson’s copse. Simon Clout went to the cottages with two armed men from the borough, turning out the meal-arks and looking under the flock-beds for mutton. He found nothing in our kitchen but two dried herrings and half a peck of barley-dust."

"Simon Clout would do better to look to his own parish books," Martha said, setting a bowl of boiled peas before the blacksmith’s wife. "The poor rates are at seven shillings in the pound, and Mr. Scrope has shut the timber-gates against the women gathering dead-wood."

"He says the cottagers are cutting the living branches," Mary muttered. "Old Giles was caught with an ash-bough in his sack on Friday, and the Justice gave him fourteen days in the bridewell at Shepton. An old man of seventy-two, Martha, that has worked forty years on the parish highways. He will be dead of the cold before his time is out."

Martha did not answer. She was watching the road through the diamond panes of the window.

A two-wheeled gig with a yellow body and high, red-painted wheels was picking its way cautiously through the frozen ruts of the turnpike. The horse was a powerful, clean-limbed black cob, its nostrils white with rime, its hooves shod with sharp frost-nails that struck the ground with a rhythmic, ringing snap.

In the gig sat a man in a heavy, caped driving-coat of dark bottle-green cloth, wearing an otter-skin cap tied over his ears and spectacles of blue-tinted glass to protect his eyes from the glare of the snow.

It was not Justice Scrope. It was not the parson.

The gig turned off the turnpike into the rough track that climbed to the Cross-Spur, stopping outside Martha’s turf-wall with a sudden screech of iron brakes.

The driver descended with a brisk, agile step. He was young—no older than eight-and-twenty—with a lean, pale, scholarly face, sharp grey eyes, and hands that were protected by gloves of thick dog-skin lined with hare’s fur. He carried a flat wooden case bound with brass corners under his left arm, and an ebony cane with an ivory head in his right hand.

Mary Vowles peered through the window, her mouth dropping open.

"Lord save us," the woman whispered, crossing herself. "It is the new doctor from Bath. The one Mr. Scrope brought to the Vestry on Tuesday."

Martha rose from her bench, smoothed her woollen apron, and walked to the door.

The young man had already pushed open the wicket-gate. He walked up the flagged path between the frozen kale-stalks with the confident, proprietary air of a gentleman accustomed to stone corridors and hospital wards.

"Mrs. Martha Garrow?" he inquired, raising his hat two inches from his powdered hair. His accent was not the broad, drawling speech of Somerset; it had the crisp, clipping precision of Edinburgh and the northern colleges.

"I am Martha Garrow, sir," she said.

"My name is Prideaux," he said, stepping into the cottage without waiting for an invitation, his sharp eyes taking in the bare whitewashed walls, the low bed, the bundles of dried herbs hanging from the rafters, and the earthenware jars upon the shelf. "Evelyn Prideaux, Member of the Royal College of Surgeons, Edinburgh, and Physician-Accoucheur to the Lying-in Dispensary at Bath."

He set his brass-bound case upon the deal table, took off his dog-skin gloves, and held his long, white fingers toward the peat-fire.

"Justice Scrope informed me that I should find you here, Mrs. Garrow," Prideaux continued, glancing at Mary Vowles with a cool, dismissing inclination of his chin. "The parish officers have done me the honor of appointing me Medical Superintendent of the Nether-Coombe and High Duntish Poor Districts, under the new regulations approved by the Quarter Sessions."

Mary Vowles took up her bowl with a sullen, frightened look, curtsied awkwardly to the gentleman, and slipped out of the cottage, closing the oak door behind her.

Martha remained standing beside the dresser, her hands folded beneath her apron.

"We have had no superintendent in these parishes since I was a girl, Mr. Prideaux," Martha said quietly. "The overseers have always left the sick-poor to the apothecary at Ilchester and the women to me."

"Precisely," Prideaux said, his thin lips curving into a professional smile that had no warmth in it. "And that, Mrs. Garrow, is why the infant mortality in this vale stands at three in every ten births, and why the parish registers are filled with entries of maternal exhaustion, puerperal convulsions, and mesenteric decay."

He drew a chair toward the hearth, sat down, and unbuckled the brass clasps of his wooden case.

"The eighteenth century is drawing to its close, mistress," he said, lifting the lid to reveal two velvet-lined trays of bright, polished steel instruments: long-bladed forceps with wooden handles, curved scissors with blunt probes, silver catheters, and a row of glass phials containing tartar-emetic, laudanum, and spirit of sal-volatile. "The age of the village granny and the empirical midwife is at an end. Medicine has become an exact science, based upon anatomical demonstration and the mechanical principles of William Smellie and William Hunter."

Martha looked at the instruments in the velvet trays. 

She had seen forceps once before, thirty years ago, in the leather bag of an old surgeon from Bristol who had been called to the Court House when the Squire’s mother was dying of an obstructed labor. The old man had used them with heavy, clumsy hands; the child had come away dead, with its temples crushed like an eggshell, and the mother had died of the mortification forty-eight hours later.

"I have delivered eight hundred and forty-two women, Mr. Prideaux," Martha said, her voice dropping into that calm, immovable register that had faced down the magistrate in the library. "And I have lost four mothers in twenty-one years. One died of the smallpox that she took before her month was out; two died of the lung-rot; and one had the convulsions before the waters were broken."

The young surgeon raised his eyebrows, looking at her with a mixture of amusement and scientific condescension.

"A fortunate record, Mrs. Garrow," he murmured. "Though one that depends upon memory rather than clinical record."

"It depends upon this, sir," Martha said. 

She walked to the shelf, took down her sheepskin pocket-book, and laid it upon the table beside his brass-bound case.

Prideaux opened the book. His long, white fingers turned the leaves, his eyes running over the ruled columns, the dates, the parish names, and the tiny ink pricks that marked the living births.

A subtle change came over his face. The easy condescension faded from his eyes, replaced by the involuntary respect of an educated man who recognizes discipline and order, even when he finds it in an uncarpeted cottage on an open heath.

"You write this yourself?" he asked, looking up.

"I do, sir."

He closed the sheepskin volume with a dry tap of his knuckles.

"A remarkable document," he admitted. "And it confirms what Justice Scrope observed to me: that you are a woman of superior intelligence, though uninstructed in modern obstetrical mechanics. It is for that reason that I have called upon you before visiting the outlying farms."

He leaned forward, resting his elbows upon the table.

"The Quarter Sessions have adopted the new Somerset System," Prideaux explained. "Under these rules, no female practitioner will be permitted to attend a first delivery, nor any delivery where the presentation is preternatural, without the presence of the district surgeon. In every case where labor exceeds eighteen hours, notice must be sent immediately to my quarters at the *Red Lion* in Ilchester, and no manual version or instrumental extraction may be attempted except under my personal direction."

Martha looked at the young man’s smooth, unlined face, his spotless linen neck-cloth, and his delicate, unscarred hands.

"Eighteen hours, Mr. Prideaux?" she asked.

"Eighteen hours," he repeated. "Smellie has demonstrated that after eighteen hours of muscular exertion, the uterine fibers lose their contractile tonicity and the risk of fatal exhaustion increases in a geometric ratio."

"A girl of seventeen, bearing her first child in a ditch-cottage in mid-winter, does not consult Dr. Smellie’s clock, sir," Martha said, her voice clear and hard as frost-nails. "Some women take twenty-four hours to ripen like an apple in the sun; some take thirty. If you put steel forceps into a pelvis that has not had time to soften, you will tear the mother to ribbons and break the neck of the child."

Prideaux frowned, his pale forehead gathering into sharp, offended ridges.

"You speak of instruments you have never handled, Mrs. Garrow," he said coldly. "The English forceps, as improved by Dr. Denman, is an instrument of mercy. It saves hours of useless agony; it preserves the mother’s strength; it rescues the infant from cranial compression. Used with scientific dexterity, it is as safe as the hand of nature itself."

"The hand of nature does not cost two guineas from the parish rates," Martha said.

The surgeon stood up with a sudden, jerky movement, snapping the lid of his instrument-case shut with a loud click of the brass catch.

"I did not come here to dispute with you on the principles of physiology, Mrs. Garrow," he said, drawing on his dog-skin gloves. "I came to inform you of the law. The Overseers have made these regulations, and Justice Scrope has endorsed them. If you attend a woman contrary to these orders, your name will be returned to the Bishop’s Court for disobedience, and the parish will refuse the maintenance of any child delivered without my certificate."

He took up his ebony cane, tucked the brass-bound case beneath his arm, and walked to the door.

At the threshold he stopped, turning his sharp, intelligent face back toward the hearth.

"I am informed that Eleanor Finch at High Barn is near her time," he said.

Martha’s eyes fixed upon him. "She is in her seventh month."

"Master Finch has subscribed ten guineas to the County Infirmary," Prideaux said. "He has engaged me to attend the delivery in March. I shall expect you to act as monthly nurse, Mrs. Garrow—to prepare the linen, to boil the water, and to carry out my directions under my authority."

Martha stood tall by her deal table, the firelight catching the grey silver of her hair.

"If Eleanor Finch calls for me in her agony, Mr. Prideaux," she said, "I will go to her as I went when she was a dairymaid with nothing in her pocket. And if the child comes right, it will need neither your steel forceps nor my medicines to bring it into the light."

The young surgeon gave a short, dry laugh, touched his cap, and walked out into the frozen path.

Five minutes later, the high red wheels of the gig were spinning back toward the turnpike, leaving two sharp, parallel grooves in the crusted snow.

### Chapter 20

December brought the Great Frost to its crisis.

On the night before Christmas Eve, the mercury in the thermometer at the Court House fell to four degrees below zero. The sheep upon the Mendips froze to death in their folds by hundreds; the wild deer from the Quantock forests came down into the orchards of Nether-Coombe to eat the bark of the apple trees; and the great stone bridge over the Ivel at Ilchester was cracked from pier to parapet by the expansion of the frozen river.

In the village of Nether-Coombe, the bread-riot broke out on Saint Stephen’s Day.

Farmer Trask had stopped the mill-wheel at the Black Pool on the Tuesday before the feast, declaring that the ice had damaged the cogs and that he would grind no more grist until the spring thaw. At the same time, he sent three four-horse wains loaded with dry wheat down the canal toward Bristol, where the merchants were offering twelve shillings a bushel for shipment to the government storehouses at Portsmouth.

The women of the village met the wains at the turnpike gate.

They were forty strong, led by Mary Vowles and the weavers’ wives, armed with ash-sticks, pitchforks, and old reaping-hooks. They pulled down the toll-bar across the road, cut the traces of the lead horses, and overturned the first wagon into the ditch, spilling eighty bushels of sound white wheat into the snow.

Julian Finch arrived on his chestnut gelding within ten minutes of the riot.

He had ridden down from High Barn in his shirtsleeves, with an old cavalry sword strapped to his saddle-bow, his face dark with fury. But when he saw the women—when he saw the blacksmith’s wife standing in the road with her baby wrapped in an old woollen rag, her fingers bleeding from the frost, screaming that her children had had nothing in their bellies for three days but turnip-parings—the sword remained in its scabbard.

Julian dismounted in the slush.

He walked into the middle of the crowd, caught the halter of Trask’s second team, and turned the horses’ heads toward the village green.

"Take the corn to High Barn," Julian shouted, his voice ringing across the frozen fields. "My father’s horse-mill is running in the cider-house. We will grind every grain of it for sixpence a bushel, and any man who has no silver may pay in labor when the barley-sowing begins!"

Farmer Trask, who had galloped up on his fat cob with two constables behind him, shook his whip at the young yeoman in an ecstasy of rage.

"It is felony!" Trask screamed, his face purpling with apoplexy. "It is highway robbery under the King’s statute! Julian Finch, you will hang at Taunton for this! I’ll have the Yeomanry Cavalry out of Yeovil before the sunset!"

Julian stepped up to the miller’s stirrup, his hand resting upon the pommel of his saddle.

"The Yeomanry are my own comrades, Master Trask," Julian said, his dark eyes laughing in the frost. "Half of them are farmers’ sons who have seen their own sheep starve on the hills this month. If you bring the soldiers to Nether-Coombe, you had better see that your own thatch is well wetted, for there isn't ten men in this hundred that won't strike a tinder to your mill-tails before the night is out."

The corn was ground at High Barn.

For three days and three nights, the old stone horse-mill—which had not turned since the great drought of seventy-six—creaked and groaned in the cider-house, driven by Julian’s two stout plough-teams. 

Eleanor stood at the meal-spout with her sleeves tucked to her ears, weighing the flour into the women’s bags, taking no toll from those whose names were on the parish poor-list, while old Gervase sat in his wicker chair by the door, his right hand beating time upon his knee to the rhythmic rumble of the stones.

Justice Scrope did not send the constables.

The magistrate sat in his library with his blue-bound books, looking out upon the frozen avenue. He had received a letter that morning from the Home Office in London, warning the county lieutenancies that the miners in Cornwall and the weavers in Manchester were drilling with pikes, and that the peace of the agricultural districts must be preserved at any cost.

When Farmer Trask went to the Court House to demand a warrant of arrest against Julian Finch, the Justice did not even ask him to take a chair.

"Master Trask," Scrope said, his thin fingers turning a quill between his palms, "if the people of this parish have bread in their mouths, they will not burn my hay-ricks. If they have none, neither your constables nor my warrants will keep them from pulling your house down about your ears. Go home, sir, and see that your water-wheel is repaired before the thaw comes, or I will license the horse-mill at High Barn as a public grist-house under the statute of the Commonwealth."

The miller walked out of the library with his teeth grinding in his head, and that night a wisp of straw tied with black ribbon was found nailed to the doors of the Black Pool mill.

### Chapter 21

On the second of January 1795, the summons came for Martha.

It was two o'clock in the morning, and the frost was so intense that the stone walls of the cottage cracked with loud, pistol-like snaps as the moisture froze in the mortar.

A barefoot boy was drumming upon the window-frame with a flint-stone, screaming through the keyhole:

"Mrs. Garrow! Mrs. Garrow! For the love of God, wake up! It is Susan Vane at the Fen-Cottages! The babe is turned crosswise, and she is dying of the bleeding!"

Martha was out of her truckle-bed in an instant.

She did not strike a light; she knew every article of her clothing by the touch in the dark. She pulled on her two pairs of thick woollen stockings, her greased shoes, her linsey petticoats, and her heavy cloak. 

From the deal chest she took her wooden box of instruments: her silver catheter, her blunt-ended scissors, her bottle of tincture of opium, and the dried hog’s bladder she used for internal injections.

Beside them lay her episcopal licence in its tin cylinder. She touched it with her fingers, hesitating for the space of a heartbeat.

Under the new regulations of the Quarter Sessions, Susan Vane was Dr. Prideaux’s patient. Her husband was a day-labourer on the Scrope estate, and the Overseers had entered her name upon the dispensary list six weeks before.

To attend her without the surgeon was to risk the forfeiture of her licence, a fine of five pounds at the Petty Sessions, and the perpetual suspension of her calling.

"Where is Dr. Prideaux?" Martha shouted through the door as she drew the bolt.

The boy stood shivering on the step, his bare feet wrapped in old sheepskin rags that were soaked with frozen blood from his chilblains.

"My father ran to Ilchester at midnight," the lad wept. "The doctor was at the ball at the Assembly Rooms. He had his dancing-shoes on, and he told father he would come out in his gig at daylight. He said a woman with her fourth child was in no hurry, and that the roads were too slick for his black cob in the dark."

Martha’s eyes flashed with a cold, terrifying fury.

"His dancing-shoes," she whispered into the wind.

She snatched her basket, caught her lantern from the peg, and slammed the cottage door behind her.

The Fen-Cottages lay two miles to the south, upon the lowest shelf of the peat-waste, where the winter water was frozen into black, treacherous mirrors among the withy-beds.

The track was nothing more than a sheep-drift over the frozen marsh, where the ice cracked under Martha’s boots with sharp, hollow reports like the breaking of glass. The wind from the northeast blew directly in her teeth, driving needles of frozen sleet through the weave of her woollen hood until her eyelashes were matted with ice and her breath came in short, painful sobs that tasted of blood.

She did not slacken her pace.

For twenty years she had run this race against the cold, against the dark, and against the slow, creeping shadow of death that waited by the beds of poor women.

When she reached the cottage, the silence inside was more terrible than any scream.

It was a wretched, one-room hovel of wattle and daub, roofed with rotten sedge-thatch that was dripping frozen water upon the clay floor. A single candle, stuck in the neck of a broken bottle, guttered upon the rough stone bench beside the hearth, where three billets of green willow were smouldering with a thick, acrid smoke that filled the room with yellow gloom.

In the corner, upon a heap of dirty oat-straw covered with a torn horse-cloth, lay Susan Vane.

She was thirty years old, a small, worn creature who had borne three children in four years and had lived through the winter on potatoes and salt. 

Her face was the color of lead, her eyes rolled back into her head until only the white balls were visible, her lips blue and parted in a dry, rattling gasp. Her night-rail was soaked with bright, arterial blood that had pooled in the hollow of the straw beneath her hips and was already freezing into dark, stiff crusts around her knees.

Beside the bed knelt her husband, an ignorant, terrified ditcher, his hands buried in his hair, groaning with a monotonous, animal misery:

*"She's gone... she's gone... the doctor wouldn't come, and she's gone..."*

Martha dropped her basket on the clay floor.

She threw off her frozen cloak, pinned her sleeves to her shoulders, and knelt in the straw.

Her hand went instantly to the woman’s wrist.

The pulse was scarcely perceptible: a thin, thread-like flutter that flickered three times and then paused, like the heart of a captured bird dying in the hand.

She slid her greased fingers into the pelvis.

Her heart dropped into an abyss of cold, professional certainty.

*Placenta praevia.*

The after-birth was planted directly across the internal mouth of the womb. The powerful, opening contractions of the second stage had torn the vascular spongy tissue from the muscular wall, opening forty bleeding mouths that were pouring the woman’s life out upon the straw with every heave of the belly.

And behind the bleeding cake of the placenta, Martha’s fingertips felt something worse:

A shoulder. 

A tiny, smooth, slippery shoulder jammed hard into the superior strait of the pelvis, with an elbow wedged against the pubic bone.

The child was transverse. It was lying like an iron bar across the birth-canal. 

In such a case, nature had no remedy. The uterus would continue to beat itself against the obstruction until its muscular fibers ruptured from fundus to cervix, or until the mother bled to death in the straw before the sun was above the withies.

Martha did not hesitate for a second.

She had no steel instruments. She had no forceps, no bone-hooks, no crotchets for crushing the infant skull. She had only her two hands—her long, slender, tireless hands that knew the internal geography of the human body better than Dr. Smellie knew his books.

"William," she said, her voice cutting through the man’s groans like an iron command. "Get up from your knees."

The man looked up, his face smeared with tears and chimney-soot.

"Get to the hearth," Martha said. "Take that skillet. Put four handfuls of dried nettles from that bunch on the wall into the water, and boil it until it is black as ink. If you let that fire go out, your wife will be a corpse before the clock strikes three."

She took her phial of laudanum from the basket.

With her thumb, she forced open Susan Vane’s cold, blue lips and poured sixty drops of the dark, bitter tincture down her throat, holding her chin until the reflex of swallowing drew the medicine into her stomach.

The opium was needed to still the violent, useless contractions of the womb—to relax the rigid muscular spasm that held the child like a vice, so that her hand might pass without tearing the uterus.

Then she greased her right hand and forearm with hog’s lard from her tin pot, working the fat into her knuckles until her skin was smooth and slippery as an eel.

She knelt between the woman’s knees.

"Lord Jesus," Martha whispered into the dark straw, "who was born of a woman in the stable of Bethlehem: guide these fingers into the place of blood, and give me the life of the mother."

She passed her hand into the vagina.

The gush of hot blood struck her wrist like warm water from a pump, but she did not flinch. 

Her fingers moved with the slow, exquisite, deliberate gentleness of a master mechanic feeling for a slipped spring inside a watch. 

She reached the internal orifice. Her fingers met the spongy, bleeding mass of the placenta. 

She did not tear it. To tear the placenta was to kill the child instantly and to double the hemorrhage from the mother’s veins. 

With two fingers, she found the edge where the membrane was thin, separated it from the uterine wall for three inches, and slipped her hand through the aperture into the warm, amniotic cavity beyond.

The waters had long since broken. The uterus had contracted down upon the child like a dry leather sack, holding the little body in a rigid, unyielding embrace.

Martha’s fingers crept along the infant’s ribs.

She passed the shoulder; she passed the small, soft belly; she reached the hip.

Her fingers were cramping with the terrible, crushing pressure of the uterine contractions. The pain in her forearm was like the pain of an iron band being tightened by a screw, turning her muscles to wood, sending needles of fire through her elbow into her shoulder.

She closed her eyes. She did not breathe.

She reached out with her longest finger, feeling through the darkness of the womb for the one thing that could save two lives:

A foot.

Her fingertip brushed against something small, jointed, and soft.

A knee.

She slid her hand two inches further, hooked her forefinger over the tiny heel, and caught the two small ankles between her first and second fingers.

*The podalic version.*

It was the great operation that her mother had taught her by the light of the peat-fire on the Cross-Spur—the ancient operation of the French midwives, which Dr. Hunter had praised in his lectures at London as the highest masterpiece of the obstetric art.

Slowly, steadily, with infinite patience, using the infant’s body as a lever, Martha began to turn the child in the cavity of the womb.

Every fiber of the woman’s body resisted the change. Susan Vane let out a long, low, animal groan from the depths of her opium-stupor, her hips writhing upon the straw; but Martha held the feet with an iron grip, pressing the shoulder upward with her left hand upon the external abdomen, guiding the little limbs through their revolution until the breech slipped into the superior strait and the legs came down through the cervix into the vagina.

The version was complete.

The transverse obstruction was gone. 

The child was now an ordinary footling presentation, its own buttocks acting as an internal plug that pressed against the bleeding placenta, compressing the open blood-vessels like a tourniquet.

The hemorrhage ceased.

The bright arterial stream that had been pooling in the straw dwindled to a thin, dark trickle that stopped altogether as the breech advanced.

Martha drew her arm from the pelvis.

Her hand was covered in black, coagulated blood to the elbow; her fingers were trembling so violently from the muscular strain that she could scarcely hold her linen rollers; but her face was calm, pale, and triumphant.

"The nettle-tea, William," she whispered, without turning her head.

The man brought the hot, dark brew in a cracked cup.

Martha fed it to the woman by spoonfuls, her other hand resting upon the fundus of the abdomen, watching the return of the natural, rhythmic contractions.

At four o'clock in the morning, while the frost outside was painting the withy-branches with silver, the child was born.

It was a boy—small, thin, and blue as a bilberry from the long pressure of the labor, with a dark bruise upon its left shoulder where it had been wedged against the bone.

Martha did not wait for it to breathe.

She cleared the mucous from its mouth with her little finger, caught the child by its heels, and swung it gently twice through the cold air of the room. 

Then she laid her warm mouth over its tiny nostrils, blowing the breath of her own lungs into the collapsed air-cells of its chest, expanding the small ribs with her own life.

Once. Twice. Three times.

A faint, shuddering tremor ran through the little limbs.

The blue color faded from the lips, replaced by a pale, petal-pink that spread outward across the cheeks.

Then the boy opened his mouth and let out a small, thin, indignant wail—a cry no louder than the squeak of a field-mouse in the thatch, but sweet as the bells of paradise in the silent room.

Susan Vane opened her eyes.

The delirium was gone, leaving her face clear, pale, and infinitely weary. Her sunken eyes turned toward the sound of the cry, and a faint, trembling smile touched the corners of her cracked lips.

"Martha," she whispered.

"The boy is here, Susan," Martha said, laying the child wrapped in warm flannel against the mother’s ribs. "He has sixteen nails and a good breath. Sleep now, child. The watch is kept."

### Chapter 22

The gray dawn was breaking over the marsh when the sound of wheels rattled upon the frozen causeway.

A high-wheeled gig stopped at the ditch-bank, and Dr. Evelyn Prideaux stepped down into the slush, his brass-bound case in his hand, his green coat spotless, his beaver hat brushed to perfection.

He walked up the mud path to the cottage, pushed open the door with the ivory head of his cane, and stepped over the sill.

The smell of the room met him: the smell of boiled nettles, wet peat-smoke, carbolic vinegar, and the rich, unmistakable odor of fresh, living blood.

On the straw in the corner, Susan Vane was sleeping peacefully, her head propped upon a bundle of clean rushes, her skin cool and moist with a natural perspiration. 

Beside her, wrapped in Martha’s own woollen petticoat, lay the newborn boy, his tiny mouth working contentedly as he slept.

And beside the hearth sat Martha Garrow.

She had washed her hands in cold pump-water, but the dark stains of the delivery still showed beneath her fingernails. Her face was haggard, her eyes sunken into deep, purple hollows of exhaustion, her gray hair escaping from her cap in wild, silver wisps about her temples.

She was peeling a cold boiled turnip with her small horn-handled knife.

Prideaux stood motionless in the middle of the clay floor.

His spectacles seemed to freeze upon his nose. His eyes moved from the sleeping woman to the infant, then to the clean, tied umbilical cord, and finally to the empty pelvis that showed beneath the counterpane.

He set his brass-bound case upon the floor.

He walked to the bed, bent down, and laid his gloved fingers upon the mother’s radial pulse.

It was seventy-two beats a minute: soft, regular, and full.

Then he reached down and touched the infant’s bruised shoulder, testing the clavicle and the humeral joint with his long, educated thumbs.

"Transverse," he murmured, his voice scarcely audible above the crackle of the green willow fire. "With a placental implantation."

He turned slowly round to face Martha.

The young surgeon’s face had lost all its crisp, professional arrogance. It was pale, bewildered, and shaken to the bedrock of its scientific theories.

"How did you do it?" Prideaux whispered.

Martha did not look up from her turnip.

"I turned him, sir," she said.

"Without instruments?"

"God gave me five fingers on each hand, Mr. Prideaux," she said, "and they have had twenty years of practice in the dark. They are smaller than your steel spoons, and they do not chill the womb when they enter."

Prideaux took off his spectacles. He wiped them slowly with his handkerchief, his eyes never leaving the midwife’s face.

"Under Smellie’s rules," he muttered, more to himself than to her, "the maternal mortality in an impacted shoulder with hemorrhage is eighty in the hundred. We teach the students at Edinburgh that embryotomy is the only resource when the arm is engaged."

"Dr. Smellie may teach what pleases him in Edinburgh, sir," Martha said, slicing a piece of turnip and putting it into her mouth. "In the Somerset fens, we prefer to save both the mother and the child."

The young surgeon stood in the miserable hovel, looking at his brass-bound case with its bright, polished steel forceps, its curved scissors, and its bone-hooks.

He saw them now for what they were: instruments of power, instruments of mechanics, instruments of a male science that sought to govern nature by force rather than by understanding.

He walked to the hearth.

He did not raise his cane. He did not speak of the Quarter Sessions, nor of the Bishop’s Court, nor of the regulations of the parish vestry.

He reached into his waistcoat pocket, drew out a gold guinea—the fee that the Overseers had allowed him for an operative delivery—and laid it upon the deal table beside Martha’s sheepskin register.

"You have saved two lives this morning, Mrs. Garrow," Prideaux said, his voice quiet and genuinely humble. "And you have taught a Member of the Royal College of Surgeons that there are chapters in the book of nature that are not written in Latin."

Martha looked at the gold coin upon the table.

She reached out with her rough, scarred finger, pushed the guinea back across the wood, and let it rest against the young man’s sleeve.

"Keep your gold, Mr. Prideaux," she said gently. "Give it to William Vane to buy two sacks of meal and a side of bacon for his wife when her milk comes. A midwife does not sell the breath of a poor man’s child for twenty-one shillings."

Prideaux looked at her.

He picked up the coin, bowed his head to the midwife with a deep, silent reverence that he had never shown to the professors at Edinburgh, and walked out into the winter light.

### Chapter 23

The Great Thaw began on the fourteenth of February.

It came with a southwesterly gale that blew off the Atlantic, warm and moist with the breath of the gulf, melting the snow upon the Mendip ridges in six-and-thirty hours. 

The rivers broke their icy bonds with terrifying violence: the Parrett, the Brue, and the Tone rose ten feet above their banks, drowning the sheep-meadows, tearing down the willow-hedges, and sweeping the wooden bridges of the levels out into the Bristol Channel in a roaring torrent of yellow mud and broken timber.

In Nether-Coombe, the water reached the churchyard wall, but the village stood high upon the limestone shelf and suffered no hurt.

On the first day of March, the sun shone out clear, warm, and bright, drying the red earth of the wheat-fields and bringing the green shoots of the winter-corn through the soil in long, emerald ribbons that promised an abundant harvest.

And on the third of March, Eleanor Finch was delivered of her second child.

It was not Dr. Prideaux who was called to the great chamber at High Barn.

The young surgeon had resigned his district appointment three weeks before, returning to his dispensary at Bath with a new humility in his heart and two copies of Martha’s ruled register in his portfolio, which he intended to lay before the Medical Society of London as an example of rural obstetrical method.

It was Martha who walked up the combe road in the spring sunshine, her wicker basket on her arm, her shoes greased with neatsfoot, her heart light as the song of the larks above the down.

The delivery was easy and natural—the delivery of a healthy, vigorous young woman who had worked in the open air and had known no fear.

At four o'clock in the afternoon, while the sheep were grazing peacefully on the sunny slopes of the hill, Eleanor gave birth to a girl: a plump, fair-skinned maid with her mother’s grey eyes and the bright, copper-tinted hair of the Wards.

Julian came into the chamber as the sun was setting behind the orchard.

He had walked up from the threshing-floor, his frieze coat off, his shirtsleeves white as linen, his dark face softened by a deep, miraculous joy.

He knelt by the great four-post bed.

He took his wife’s hand in his, kissing the third finger where his grandmother’s gold ring shone in the candlelight, and then he looked down at the tiny face nestled upon Eleanor’s arm.

"What shall we name her, Eleanor?" he whispered.

Eleanor turned her eyes toward the hearth, where Martha was washing the silver pap-spoon in a bowl of warm water.

"We will name her Martha," Eleanor said softly. "Martha Finch. So that there may always be a woman of that name to remember the winter when the truth came home to High Barn."

Julian rose from his knees.

He walked across the room, took Martha’s two hands in his, and held them against his chest.

He did not speak. He had no words that could span the gulf between the young man who had bargained with Luke Coombes at Cross-Hands and the man who stood here today, the master of his land and the father of his children.

He simply pressed her fingers against his heart, and Martha felt the steady, strong, honest beating of a soul that had been redeemed by the fire.

***

An hour later, Martha walked down the winding stone stairs to the kitchen.

Old Gervase Finch sat in his wicker chair by the open door, watching the twilight settle over the valley. 

Beside him sat Martin—eighteen months old now, sturdy as an oak-sapling, wearing his scarlet frock, holding an ash-twig in his fat fist, driving an imaginary team of bullocks across the blue lias flags of the porch.

When the old man saw Martha, his right eye brightened with that clear, unfading intelligence that had defied the apoplexy.

He reached out his good right arm, caught the midwife’s sleeve, and drew her down to him.

He touched his own mouth, then pointed his finger toward the stairs, nodding his head three times with that slow, solemn joy that had become his only speech.

"She is safe, Gervase," Martha told him, laying her hand upon his white hair. "A little maid. Sound in wind and limb, with the Finch chin and her mother’s eyes."

The old yeoman closed his eyes.

A single tear rolled down his weathered cheek, losing itself in his grey beard, and he laid his right hand upon the head of the boy who played at his feet.

Martha walked through the yard gate into the lane.

The spring dusk was falling over the three parishes, purple and mild, smelling of turned earth, wild primroses, and the woodsmoke from forty cottage hearths.

From the church tower of Nether-Coombe, the curfew bell was ringing: eight slow, sweet, solemn strokes that floated out across the water-meadows toward the hills.

Martha stopped upon the ridge of the Cross-Spur.

She looked down upon the vale—her vale, the country of her mother and her grandmother, the land where she had walked four thousand miles in twenty-one years to keep the watch of life.

Her licence was safe in the tin cylinder at her side.

Her sheepskin register had forty blank leaves waiting for the names of the children who should be born of the new wheat and the summer grass.

And the village’s trust—that fragile, beautiful, enduring bond that had been strained to the breaking-point by the deceit of the great house—had returned to her, purified by the fire, strong as the Hamstone pillars of the parish church.

She took her basket upon her arm, turned her face toward her small cottage on the heath, and walked home in the starlight.
### Chapter 24

In the second week of May 1795, the magistrates of the county of Somerset assembled at the Castle of Taunton to consider the bread-scale.

The winter had left the south country broken and exhausted. Though the Great Frost had dissolved into the floods of February, the rain had continued through March and April, drowning the barley-sowing on the low grounds and turning the turnpike roads into canals of red mud through which no team could draw more than half a load. 

The price of wheat at Bristol corn-market had reached ninety-two shillings a quarter; the quartern loaf of common wheaten bread was sold at one shilling and one penny; and in the cloth-manufacturing parishes of Frome and Shepton Mallet, five thousand weavers were walking the lanes with their looms silent and their children crying for crusts.

Justice Scrope drove to Taunton in his heavy black chariot, his coachman armed with a pair of brass-barrelled horse-pistols in the holsters to protect the carriage against the mobs of unemployed colliers who were reported to be gathering at Radstock.

In his pocket, the magistrate carried a printed copy of the resolutions adopted three days earlier by the Berkshire justices at the Pelican Inn at Speenhamland.

It was an engine of legal arithmetic designed to keep the peace without raising the day-labourers’ wages: a sliding table that tied the parish allowance to the price of the gallon loaf. When the loaf was at a shilling, every poor man was to have three shillings a week for himself and eighteen-pence for his wife and every child, whether he earned it by the spade or drew it from the parish rate-box.

"It will prevent the rebellion," Scrope told the rector, Mr. Harford, as they crossed the bridge at Nether-Coombe before daybreak. "The French disease is spreading through the shires like the glanders among horses. The lower orders are reading Tom Paine’s pamphlets in the alehouses, and if we do not put bread into their bellies by the authority of the law, they will take the grain from our barns with the pike and the torch."

"And who is to pay the allowance, Mr. Scrope?" the old rector asked, wrapping his woollen scarf closer about his powdered wig.

"The land," Scrope said coldly. "The great tenants and the freeholders. The poor rates in this parish will rise to nine shillings in the pound before the wheat is in the sheaf."

He leaned back against the crimson morocco squabs of the chariot, his spectacles glittering in the grey dawn.

"And to meet the rate, Mr. Harford, we must bring the waste lands into cultivation. The commons must be enclosed. I have instructed my solicitors to prepare a private bill for the next session of Parliament: to enclose the seven hundred acres of the High Heath, the Withy-Beds on the marsh, and the Cross-Spur."

The rector turned his head, his gentle, mild face clouded with sudden distress.

"The Cross-Spur?" Mr. Harford murmured. "There are sixteen cottages on the Cross-Spur, Mr. Scrope. Some of those families have lived upon the waste since the civil wars. Old Giles’s grandfather built his chimney out of the common stones, and Martha Garrow’s cottage was built with the consent of old Matthew Finch forty years ago."

"Consent is not a title, Mr. Harford," Scrope replied, his voice clipping the words like a pair of iron shears. "The land belongs to the Manor of Nether-Coombe. It is waste of the manor, producing nothing but gorse, broom, and a few stunted sheep that rot with the fluke. Under the enclosure, it will be divided into ten-acre arable fields, ditch-drained, and let to substantial tenants who can pay sixty shillings an acre. The cottagers will receive their legal compensation in money, or they may take parish work upon the new turnpikes."

"They will not take money for their homes, sir," the rector said softly.

"They will take what the Parliamentary Commissioners award them," the magistrate said. "The law does not consult the sentiments of squatters when the nation is at war and the King’s armies are starving on the Rhine."

The chariot rolled off across the swollen river, leaving the old clergyman standing by the churchyard gate, looking up toward the green, rolling combe where the cottages on the Cross-Spur caught the first pale light of the morning.

***

The news of the enclosure reached the Cross-Spur on the following Friday, brought by Simon Clout, who had seen the surveyor’s chain-men setting out the white sighting-poles across the heath.

Martha was sitting at her door, stripping the dried leaves from a bundle of wild thyme she had gathered on the downs, when Mary Vowles and the road-mender’s daughter came running up the track.

Mary’s face was white with a terrifying, helpless anger—that dull, animal despair that falls upon cottage women when the power of the great house touches their hearthstones.

"They are measuring the garden, Martha!" Mary cried, catching the gate-post to keep from falling. "Two men from Bath with three-legged brass stands and iron chains! They told old Giles that his turf-house was inside the lord’s fence, and that every cabbage on the bank belonged to Squire Scrope!"

Old Giles followed them up the path, leaning heavily upon his ash stick, his old knees trembling beneath his patched corduroy breeches.

"Forty-two years," the old road-mender croaked, his eyes wet with tears that ran unheeded into the grey stubble of his chin. "My father died in that bed, Martha. I’ve paid the lord’s quit-rent—two-pence a year and a capon at Michaelmas—to the steward at the Court House since the old King was on the throne. Now the young clerk with the gold spectacles tells me I have no paper to show for it, and that the house must come down when the hedges are planted."

Martha set down her wicker basket of thyme.

She rose from her stool, walked to the wicket-gate, and looked out across the heath.

A quarter of a mile down the track, two young men in brown jackets and leather gaiters were dragging a heavy iron surveyor’s chain through the gorse. Behind them walked Scrope’s bailiff, carrying a bundle of red-painted wooden stakes, driving them into the turf with heavy strokes of a wooden mallet at every hundred yards.

Each stake was a line drawn through the ancient freedom of the vale.

Inside those red pegs lay the common life of forty families: the peat-stack that kept the winter hearth warm; the strip of poor gravel where the cottager’s pig was tethered in the autumn to fatten on the acorns; the gorse-bushes where the women dried their linen sheets; and the spring of clear water that rose in the limestone fissure beneath Martha’s wall.

If the enclosure passed, the red pegs would become quickset hedges of white-thorn and double ditches of clay. The cottagers would be left with nothing but their four walls, forbidden to set a foot upon the land their grandfathers had grazed, dependent upon the Speenhamland bread-scale and the sour charity of the parish rate-box.

"Where is Julian Finch?" Martha asked.

"He is at the sheep-wash in the Upper Combe," Mary said. "He has forty yearlings to wash before the shearing."

"Fetch him," Martha said.

### Chapter 25

Julian Finch arrived within half an hour, riding his chestnut gelding bareback, his shirtsleeves wet to the elbows with the soapy sheep-wash, his brow dark with sweat.

He reined in the horse beside Martha’s wall, looking at the surveyor’s stakes with eyes that smouldered with the old, dangerous fire.

"Scrope has posted the notice on the church door," Julian said, his voice thick with anger. "Application to Parliament for an Act to divide, allot, and enclose the waste grounds of Nether-Coombe. The miller has signed it, and the two farmers at High Duntish have signed it. They will take three hundred acres between them, and the cottagers will have five shillings apiece from the Commissioners to buy rope to hang themselves."

"Can the landlord take the houses, Julian?" Martha asked, looking into the young yeoman’s face.

Julian swung himself down from the horse’s back. He dropped the halter over the gate-post and walked into the cottage.

"Under the general law," he said, taking off his wet hat and throwing it upon the table, "if a man has held a cottage on the waste for twenty years without paying rent or acknowledging the lord’s title, he has a title by adverse possession against all the world. But Scrope's lawyers are too sharp for that. They have gone through the old manor rolls at the Court House. They say my grandfather had no power to grant the land to your father, because High Barn was only held upon lives under the Scrope lease, and a tenant cannot alienate the lord’s freehold."

He walked to the window, staring out at the red stakes that glittered in the sun.

"My father could have told us," Julian muttered. "He knew every acre of the manor books. When he was a young man, he was the foreman of the Homage Jury at the Court Baron. But his speech is gone, Martha. He can only nod his head and make signs with his fingers, and Scrope’s lawyers will laugh in our faces if we bring an old man with the apoplexy before the Parliamentary Committee."

Martha stood by the dresser, her fingers resting upon the cover of her sheepskin register.

"The manor rolls," she said slowly.

Julian turned round. "What of them?"

"In sixty-eight," Martha said, her mind running back through twenty-seven years of parish memory, "when the old Squire died, the Court Baron was held in the great parlor at the *Sheaf and Sickle*. My mother was called before the Homage to swear to the date of old Mrs. Trask’s delivery, so that the heir might prove his age for the copyholds. I went with her, carrying her smelling-salts. I saw the great parchment book that the old steward kept—the leather book with the brass corners and the King’s arms stamped in gold upon the back."

"The Book of the Homage," Julian said.

"Yes," she said. "The old steward was Mr. Goodford, the lawyer from Yeovil. He was an honest gentleman; he took no bribes from the farmers, and he wrote down every transfer on the parchment with his own hand. I remember his words to my father as they stood by the chimney-piece: *'Matthew Finch has surrendered the half-acre on the Cross-Spur to the lord to the use of Thomas Garrow and his heirs forever, according to the custom of the manor, by the yearly rent of one penny and suit of court.'*"

Julian’s eyes widened with sudden, electric realization.

"A copyhold!" he gasped. "A copyhold of inheritance!"

"I do not know the lawyer’s names for it," Martha said. "I only know that my father gave the steward a silver sixpence for the copy of court-roll, and that Mr. Goodford wrote his name in the great book with red ink."

"If it is a copyhold," Julian cried, catching Martha by the shoulders in his excitement, "it is an ancient estate! Scrope cannot touch it! An Enclosure Act cannot extinguish a copyhold without the tenant’s consent, unless they allot an equivalent freehold of equal value in the new fields!"

He caught up his hat from the table.

"Where is the paper, Martha? Where is the copy of court-roll your father received?"

Martha’s face fell.

"My father kept his papers in an old tin tea-canister on the high shelf," she whispered. "When he died of the pleurisy twenty years ago, my mother gave the canister to the parson to keep in the parish chest, because our cottage had no lock upon the door and the roof was leaking."

"The parish chest," Julian said, his jaw tightening into that hard, unyielding line. "And Scrope keeps one of the three keys as churchwarden, and Farmer Trask keeps the second as Overseer of the Poor."

"And Mr. Harford keeps the third," Martha said.

"Then we will go to the parsonage," Julian said, his hand already on the latch. "Before Scrope’s clerks can get their hands into the iron box."

### Chapter 26

The rectory of Nether-Coombe was an ancient, rambling house of Hamstone, roofed with mossy stone slates, that stood in a grove of dark elms beside the churchyard wall. 

In the garden, where the rector grew his winter greens and his beds of sweet-scented stocks, the bees were humming in the warm May sunshine, and a great white pear tree was shedding its blossoms like snow upon the gravel paths.

The Reverend Mr. Harford was sitting in his study—a quiet, book-lined room that smelled of old calfskin, dried rose-leaves, and pipe-tobacco—reading the *Gentleman’s Magazine* through his horn-rimmed spectacles.

When the maidservant showed Martha and Julian into the room, the old clergyman set down his paper with a nervous, uneasy flutter of his hands.

"Julian... Mrs. Garrow," the rector murmured, rising from his leather arm-chair. "I trust... I trust there is no new trouble in the parish? Eleanor and the children are well, I hope?"

"They are well, sir," Julian said, standing tall before the study table, refusing to sit. "We have come about the parish chest, Mr. Harford."

The rector’s eyes shifted nervously toward the tall iron-bound oak chest that stood in the corner of the room, beneath the portrait of Bishop Ken.

It was four feet long, made of solid English oak four inches thick, bound with heavy straps of wrought iron that ended in trefoil leaves, and secured by three massive padlocks of ancient manufacture.

"The chest?" Mr. Harford said, his voice trembling slightly. "The parish records are in the custody of the churchwardens, Julian. Mr. Scrope informed me only yesterday that the documents relating to the manor wastes would be inspected by his agents on Monday, for the purposes of the enclosure."

"Mr. Scrope has no right to inspect private titles, sir," Julian said, stepping closer to the table. "Twenty years ago, Thomas Garrow deposited a copy of court-roll in that chest for safe-keeping. "My dear boy," the old man sighed, "Justice Scrope is the lord of the manor, the patron of the living, and the chairman of the Quarter Sessions. The lawyers in London will swallow their little compensations in fees, and they will be turned out by the Sheriff’s officers without a roof to shelter their grey heads."

"We will not be turned out, Mr. Harford," Martha said. 

She walked forward until she stood opposite the clergyman, her dark, clear eyes meeting his through the dusty sunlight of the study.

"I have attended three generations of your family, sir," Martha said, her voice dropping into that quiet, solemn cadence that carried all the weight of her twenty-one years of service. "When your sister lay dying of the milk-fever in this very house, when every doctor from Bath had given her over and the church bell was waiting to toll, it was my mother who sat with her through three nights, bathing her limbs with elder-flower water until the fever broke. When your own little boy was born with the caul upon his face, it was these hands that took him from the dark and laid him in your arms."

"The law requires the three churchwardens to be present when the locks are turned," the rector said, his voice acquiring a sudden, quiet dignity that sat well upon his grey hair. "The two side locks were broken by the frost in January," Mr. Harford said, looking up with a faint, dry twitch of his eyelids. Master Trask has not yet had them repaired by the blacksmith."

He turned his key.

The ancient lock gave a loud, grinding screech of rusted iron, and the heavy bolt shot back with a dull thud.

Inside the chest lay the history of the parish of Nether-Coombe for three hundred years.

There were the great vellum baptismal registers from the reign of Queen Elizabeth, bound in rough pigskin; the account-books of the churchwardens during the Civil Wars, showing the payments for casting down the stone cross in the churchyard and the purchases of gunpowder for the Parliamentary soldiers; the parchment indentures of seventy parish apprentices bound out to the farmers; and three rows of small tin and wooden canisters, labelled with the names of the ancient tenements of the manor.

Martha knelt beside the chest.

Her fingers, which had never trembled before the bed of death, shook as she searched among the dusty boxes.

At the bottom of the second compartment, half-buried beneath an old bundle of glebe-terriers, lay an oval tea-canister of painted japanned tin, its black surface scratched and dented, bearing upon its lid the faint, faded initials:

*T. G. — 1768.*

Martha lifted the canister. Her hands left two clear marks in the twenty years of dust that covered the metal.

She opened the lid.

Inside lay three folded papers: her father’s military discharge from the Thirty-Ninth Regiment of Foot after the siege of Gibraltar; her parents’ marriage certificate from the church of Saint Mary Redcliffe at Bristol; and a broad, yellowed sheet of stamped parchment, sealed with the small wafer seal of the Manor of Nether-Coombe.

Julian caught the parchment from her hands.

He carried it to the window, holding it where the full flood of the May sunlight illuminated the faded brown ink of the text.

His eyes scanned the Latin headings, the recital of the Court Baron, and the small, neat signature of Mr. Goodford, the old steward.

"It is here," Julian whispered, his face glowing with a wild, triumphant joy. "By God, Martha, it is here!"

He read the words aloud, his voice ringing through the quiet study:

*"Ad hanc Curiam venit Thomas Garrow... To this Court came Thomas Garrow, in his own proper person, and took of the Lord of the said Manor, by the delivery of the Steward, by the rod, according to the custom of the said Manor: One messuage or cottage, with an orchard and garden containing by estimation one half-acre, situated upon the waste at the Cross-Spur... To have and to hold to the said Thomas Garrow, his heirs and assigns forever, at the will of the Lord according to the custom of the said Manor, yielding therefor yearly one penny of lawful money at the feast of Saint Michael the Archangel... And the said Thomas gave to the Lord for a fine sixpence, and did fealty, and was admitted tenant thereof."*

Julian looked at the rector.

"It is an estate of inheritance," the young yeoman said, his voice trembling with pride. "A customary freehold! Scrope can no more touch that cottage than he can seize the Duke of Bedford’s castle at Woburn!"

Mr. Harford took the parchment, laid it upon his desk, and examined the small wafer seal and the signature of the steward.

"It is perfectly regular," the clergyman said, a long, quiet sigh of relief escaping his chest. "Mr. Goodford’s hand is well known to the Court of Chancery. He was the most careful lawyer in the county."

He looked at Martha with eyes full of a gentle, affectionate benevolence.

"Take your deed, Martha," Mr. Harford said. "The church has kept it safely for twenty years, and the church now delivers it back to the daughter of the man who served his country at Gibraltar."

Martha took the yellowed parchment. She did not speak. She folded it carefully, wrapped it in her clean linen handkerchief, and tucked it into the bosom of her gown, next to the tin cylinder of her episcopal licence.

Then she made her quiet curtsy to the minister, and walked out into the sweet, blossom-scented garden with her home safe beneath her heart.

### Chapter 27

The enclosure of the Nether-Coombe wastes did not take place in the year of ninety-five.

When Justice Scrope’s solicitors in London examined the copy of court-roll produced by Julian Finch, they advised the magistrate that the cottage on the Cross-Spur was an ancient copyhold of inheritance, and that any attempt to enclose the surrounding heath without providing an equivalent allotment of common pasture for the customary tenants would be thrown out by the Parliamentary Committee upon the first reading.

The red surveyor’s stakes remained in the gorse for three weeks, weathered by the rain and bleached by the sun, until one night in June they disappeared.

No man in the parish knew who had pulled them up; but on the following morning, John Vowles the blacksmith had twenty stout ash stakes stacked neatly behind his forge, which he cut into sledge-hammer handles before the week was out.

The cottagers kept their turf-stacks; the geese continued to feed upon the green margins of the track; and the small spring of clear water flowed undisturbed beneath Martha’s wall into the valley below.

Yet the summer brought a new and heavier shadow upon the vale.

With the arrival of the hot weather in July, the great floods that had covered the levels during the spring began to dry into stagnant pools of black, putrid slime. 

The heat was tropical, oppressive, and still: a heavy, airless furnace that hung between the hills without a breath of wind, smelling of rotting weed and dead fish. By the middle of the month, the marsh ague had broken out in every cottage from Wedmore to the sea.

It was not the simple intermittent fever that the fen-people treated with willow-bark and warm ale. 

It was the *putrid typhus*—the jail-fever that had escaped from the prisons into the manufacturing towns, and had now found its way along the canal-banks into the agricultural villages.

It began with a dull, heavy stupor that blunted the senses, followed by black spots upon the breast and arms, a tongue that turned dry and black as charred wood, and a bloody looseness of the bowels that carried off the strongest farm-labourer within four-and-twenty hours.

The village of Nether-Coombe was struck on the day before the barley-harvest.

Old Giles the road-mender died first, in his little stone hovel beside the gravel-pits; then William Clout’s wife and two of his children; then the three young carters who worked at the Black Pool mill.

The people were seized with an uncontrollable, frantic panic.

The farmers shut their gates against their own day-labourers; the church bells ceased to toll for the dead, because the sexton could not dig the graves fast enough in the baking clay; and Farmer Trask fled the parish in his gig, taking his daughter Phoebe to an isolated farmhouse on the Quantocks, leaving his water-mills to be managed by an ignorant boy.

Even Dr. Prideaux did not return from Bath. 

The young surgeon wrote a long, sensible letter to Justice Scrope, advising the parish officers to sprinkle the rooms with oil of vitriol and sea-salt, to whitewash the cottage walls with hot lime, and to keep the sick separated from the healthy; but he remained in his dispensary at the hospital, where the wealthy merchants paid five guineas a visit for his advice.

Only Martha Garrow walked the lanes.

She had tied a piece of sponge soaked in camphorated vinegar into the nostrils of her hood; she carried a stone bottle of spirit of vitriol in her basket; and her hands were never dry from carbolic lye and pump-water.

From cottage to cottage she moved through the dead heat: washing the black crusts from the mouths of the dying; turning the filthy straw from beneath the bodies; brewing her teas of snake-root, Peruvian bark, and wild mint; and speaking that calm, steady, fearless word that was the only medicine that kept the breath of life in the hearts of the living.

She did not work alone.

Beside her, carrying the second basket of lime and the clean linen rollers, walked Rachel Vane.

The young widow from the Black Mill had left her cottage in the fens on the day the first death was reported. She had walked six miles through the suffocating dust of the road, presented herself at Martha’s gate, and laid her apron upon the table.

"I have had the marsh-fever, Mrs. Garrow," Rachel had said, her clear dark eyes looking into the midwife’s face with a quiet, immovable resolution. "The doctors say a woman who has once passed the fire does not take it a second time. My father can manage the mill with the boy from the village. Let me take the watch with you."

For six weeks, the two women kept the valley alive.

Martha taught the younger woman the ancient, unwritten secrets of her calling: how to test the rhythm of the sinking pulse with the tip of the second finger; how to lift an unconscious man without straining the sinews of the back; how to detect the fatal smell of mortification before the black spots appeared upon the skin; and how to close the eyes of the dead with two pennies of King George so that the lids did not spring open in the coffin.

And as Rachel watched Martha at the bedsides—watching her gentle, tireless hands, her infinite patience, her total forgetfulness of her own body—a new strength seemed to enter the girl’s soul.

The melancholy grief that had haunted her since her husband was drowned in the fleet vanished like the morning mist upon the peat-cuts. 

She was no longer the lonely, broken widow who had nursed a bundle of rags in her empty mill; she was the midwife’s scholar, the chosen vessel into which the memory and the skill of forty years of service were being poured.

### Chapter 28

The fever spent its fury with the first frosts of October, leaving sixty-four empty chairs in the three villages, but sparing the great household at High Barn.

At the homestead on the combe, the harvest had been gathered under Julian’s tireless direction: two hundred acres of wheat, barley, and oats, stacked into twelve colossal ricks in the rick-yard, every rick thatched with wheat-reed and topped with an ornamental straw cock by the old master’s own weavers.

The wheat was sold at eighty-eight shillings a quarter.

Julian paid every laborer his two shillings a day in good silver coin, refusing to take advantage of the Speenhamland allowance from the parish box.

"My father’s men shall eat the bread of their own labor," Julian told Justice Scrope at the Michaelmas vestry, "and not the bread of your charity. A man who lives upon the parish rate is only half a man, Mr. Scrope, and I will have no slaves working the fields of High Barn."

The magistrate had looked at the young yeoman with that cold, speculative glance that saw everything and said nothing; but he had signed the assessment without reducing the farmer’s valuation, and had walked out of the vestry with an unaccustomed nod to the farmer’s son.

On the twenty-fourth of October—the feast of Saint Raphael the Archangel—the call came that Martha had known must come before the winter set in.

It was not a knock at her window.

It was young Robin Finch, walking his pony slowly up the track, his hat in his hand, his eyes red with weeping.

"Martha," the lad said, as the midwife came to the wicket-gate. "My father is going."

Martha did not take her basket. She took only her mother’s Prayer Book and a clean white handkerchief, and walked down the lane beside the pony.

The great kitchen of High Barn was silent when she entered.

The work of the house was stopped. The great copper cheese-vats were covered with clean linen cloths; the fire burned low and quiet upon the hearth; and the farm-servants sat in a row upon the bench by the bread-trough, their heads bowed between their knees.

Upstairs, in the great low-ceilinged chamber, the curtains of the four-post bed were drawn back to the posts.

The afternoon sun was pouring through the south casement, filling the room with a warm, golden haze that turned the whitewashed walls to amber. Outside, the orchard was dropping its leaves, a steady, silent shower of russet and gold that carpeted the turf beneath the boughs.

Old Gervase Finch lay upon his feather pillows, his face calm, smooth, and untroubled.

The distorted, frozen look that the apoplexy had stamped upon his features had completely disappeared, relaxed by the gentle, approaching hand of the great deliverer. His skin was pale, clear, and cool as old ivory; his grey beard was neatly combed upon the white linen sheet; and his right hand rested peacefully upon the counterpane, holding the small, square Bible that had belonged to his grandfather.

Beside the bed sat Mistress Finch.

She was not weeping now. She held her husband’s dead left hand in both of hers, her head resting against the edge of the mattress, her lips moving in silent, continuous prayer.

Julian stood at the foot of the bed, his arm around Eleanor’s waist.

Eleanor held little Martha in her arms, while Martin—now two years old, a tall, broad-shouldered little lad in his scarlet woollen coat—stood on a stool beside his grandfather’s pillow, stroking the old man’s white hair with his small, square thumb.

When Martha stepped into the room, old Gervase turned his head.

His right eye met hers.

There was no darkness in that eye; there was only the clear, luminous, untroubled evening of a long life that had finished its labor and was ready for the night.

He did not try to speak. He had no need of words.

He looked at Martha; then he looked at his wife; then at Julian and Eleanor; and finally his gaze rested upon the two children.

He reached out his right arm with a slow, majestic, deliberate movement.

He took the child Martin’s small hand, placed it within Julian’s large, scarred palm, and closed his own old fingers over both of them.

Then he turned his face toward the window—toward the great combe, toward the sixty acres of winter-wheat pushing green through the red soil, toward the downs where his sheep were feeding in the autumn sun.

He drew one long, deep, quiet breath that sounded like the sigh of the wind in the orchard.

And then he ceased to breathe.

The room remained so silent that the ticking of the watch upon the mantle-piece sounded like the beat of a hammer.

Martin looked down at his grandfather’s face, then looked up at his mother with his large, dark eyes.

"Grandfather is asleep," the child whispered.

Mistress Finch raised her head from the counterpane.

She did not cry out. She rose from her chair, bent over the bed, and kissed the dead man’s forehead with that quiet, holy reverence that forty years of faithful wedlock had earned.

Then she turned to Eleanor.

For the first time since the day the apprentice girl had entered the house, the old mistress reached out her hands.

She drew Eleanor to her heart, pressing the girl’s head against her black crape shawl, weeping softly into her red hair.

"My daughter," the old woman whispered. "My good, faithful daughter."

Julian bowed his head against the bed-post, his broad shoulders shaking with those deep, silent sobs that tear the heart of strong men; and Martha knelt by the bed, took the Prayer Book from her pocket, and read the words of the ancient commendation:

*Unto God’s gracious mercy and protection we commit thee.*  
*The Lord bless thee, and keep thee.*  
*The Lord make his face to shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee.*  
*The Lord lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace, both now and evermore.*

### Chapter 29

They buried Gervase Finch on the last day of October, under the great yew tree in the churchyard of Nether-Coombe.

It was the largest funeral the vale had seen within the memory of man.

Six hundred people followed the oak coffin from the gates of High Barn down the long combe road: the magistrates, the clergy of four parishes, the tenant farmers from thirty miles around, the tradesmen from Yeovil and Ilchester, and every day-labourer, cottager, and weaver who had ever eaten the master’s bread or warmed himself by his cider-house fire.

The coffin was borne upon the shoulders of eight of his own ploughmen, wearing their clean white smock-frocks embroidered with the wheat-sheaf upon the breast.

Behind the coffin walked Julian, leading his mother by the hand; behind them came Eleanor, leading Martin, with Rachel Vane carrying little Martha in her arms; and behind the family walked Martha Garrow, alone in her grey woollen cloak.

Even Farmer Trask was there.

The miller stood by the churchyard stile, his hat in his hand, his heavy face pale and subdued. When Julian passed the gate, the older man took a step forward, held out his rough, flour-dusted hand, and spoke with a dry, choking huskiness in his throat:

"He was an honest man, Julian. A better man than I was."

Julian stopped the procession. He looked into the miller’s eyes for the space of two heartbeats, took the proffered hand in his, and gripped it with an iron grasp.

"The past is buried, Master Trask," Julian said. "Let the water run over the mill-wheel in peace."

When the service was ended and the heavy Hamstone ledger had been lowered into the vault, the mourners assembled in the churchyard to hear the reading of the will by the town-clerk of Yeovil.

The lawyer stood upon the mounting-block, his white bands fluttering in the autumn breeze, reading the formal clauses from the stamped parchment that Martha had taken from the oak chest in February.

When he reached the legacy to Martin, the crowd leaned forward with a sharp, collective intake of breath:

*"To Martin, the son of Eleanor Ward, now the lawful wife of my son Julian, the sum of three hundred pounds... to be held in trust by Martha Garrow, licensed midwife of this parish..."*

The lawyer folded the sheet.

Justice Scrope, who had stood by the vault in his black broadcloth coat, walked across the grass to where Martha stood beside the church wall.

The magistrate took off his hat with a formal, courtly gravity.

"Mrs. Garrow," Scrope said, his dry voice carrying clear and distinct to the ears of the listening churchwardens, "the Court of Quarter Sessions has directed me to inform you that your name has been placed upon the permanent roll of the county trustees for the parish of Nether-Coombe. The court recognizes that the welfare of this child, and the peace of this agricultural district, have been preserved by your uprightness, your courage, and your fidelity to the truth."

He held out his hand.

Martha looked at the long, pale hand of the man who had threatened her with the Bishop’s Court and the county gaol.

She took his fingers in her rough, work-worn palm.

"I did only what my calling required, Mr. Scrope," she said. "The law is a great thing, sir; but the life of a child is older than the statute-book, and the truth is older than the parish rate."

Scrope bowed his head with that faint, respectful irony that acknowledged a power higher than his own.

"You have the best of the argument, Mrs. Garrow," the magistrate murmured. "As you had in my library on Twelfth Night."

He turned and walked down the path to his carriage, leaving the midwife standing among the people she had served for two-and-twenty years.

### Chapter 30

A year later, on a golden afternoon in September 1796, Martha Garrow sat upon the bench outside her cottage on the Cross-Spur.

The autumn air was sweet with the scent of late honey from the heather and the dry, aromatic smell of the gorse. Below her, the valley of Nether-Coombe lay basking in the peaceful sunshine: the red fallows, the green water-meadows, the grey Hamstone roofs of the farms, and the square church tower rising above the elms like an ancient sentinel.

At the wicket-gate, a new sign had been painted upon the oak post by John Vowles the blacksmith, in neat white letters that could be read from the turnpike road:

*MARTHA GARROW & RACHEL VANE*  
*Licensed Midwives to the Parishes of Nether-Coombe, High Duntish, & Saint Jude-in-the-Fens.*

Inside the cottage, Rachel was sitting by the table, writing in a new, calf-bound register that the Reverend Mr. Harford had presented to her on her licensing day at Wells three months before.

Her hand was clear, steady, and firm—the hand of a woman who had learned her letters from the parson and her science from the bed of pain.

A shout of childish laughter came up the lane from the combe.

Martin Finch came running up the gravel path, his sturdy little legs flying, his face brown as a hazelnut from the harvest-fields, carrying a great basket of golden-drop plums from the High Barn orchard.

Behind him walked Eleanor, carrying little Martha upon her shoulder, with Julian walking beside her, leading a fat roan pony loaded with two sacks of white flour from the Black Mill.

"Martha!" the boy shouted, throwing himself against the midwife’s knees. "Look at the plums! Grandfather’s tree has borne six bushels, and mother says they are all for you!"

Martha caught the child in her arms.

She lifted him to her lap, kissing his forehead where the dark curls were damp with the heat of the walk, her fingers touching the broad, flat joint of his thumb.

Julian stopped the pony at the gate.

He stood leaning his arms upon the turf-wall, his face full of that quiet, deep, settled contentment that belongs to men who have ploughed their own fields, reaped their own harvest, and made their peace with God and man.

"The barley is all in, Martha," Julian called out, his voice rich and musical in the quiet air. "Sixty quarters to the stack, and the quality is the best we ever showed at Yeovil market."

"The earth is good to an honest plough, Julian," Martha said, smiling at him across the kale-rows.

Eleanor walked through the gate, set little Martha upon the grass beside her brother, and sat down upon the bench by the midwife’s side.

She took Martha’s rough, scarred hand in hers, pressing it against her cheek with that quiet, wordless love that needs no speech.

The four of them sat together in the autumn sunshine, watching the twilight creep up the combe from the marsh.

From the valley below, the church bell began to chime for the evening service: six slow, sweet, silver strokes that echoed off the limestone cliffs of the ridge and died away into the peaceful sky.

Martha looked down upon the two children playing at her feet; then she looked at the young yeoman and his wife; and finally her eyes turned toward the quiet room where Rachel was writing in the book of life.

Her work was done.

The measure had been filled, pressed down, shaken together, and running over.

She had walked the three villages through twenty years of winter and summer; she had seen the pride of the great house broken and healed; she had kept the mother from the gallows and the child from the ditch; and she had left a scholar behind her who would keep the watch when her own hands were laid to rest beneath the churchyard yew.

She closed her eyes.

The warm autumn wind blew across the heath, rustling the dry leaves of the thyme upon the bank, carrying the sweet scent of the turned earth and the new bread into the twilight.

And in the silence of her heart, Martha Garrow gave thanks to the Lord of life, who had given her the strength to speak the truth, to keep the trust, and to deliver the soul of her people into the light of day.
### Chapter 31

The year of ninety-seven opened with the black frost of February and the great panic of the banks.

On a Monday morning when the rime was three inches thick upon the thorn-hedges, the carrier from London brought the Gazette to the *Sheaf and Sickle*. The King in Council had issued an order prohibiting the Bank of England from paying out its notes in gold coin; the five-pound notes that the graziers had taken for their beasts at Salisbury market were suddenly worth no more than the promise of a clerk in Threadneedle Street; and in the market-place at Yeovil, the farmers refused to unyoke their teams until the corn-factors counted down the silver shillings and the spade-guineas upon the tailboards of the wagons.

Julian Finch sat in the parlor at High Barn with sixty five-pound notes spread out upon the table, looking at the water-marked paper with eyes that were dark with perplexity.

"The paper will buy neither lime nor seed, Martha," Julian said, as the midwife came in from the dairy with a bowl of warm whey for the children. "Old Trask has locked his granaries; he says he will take no promises signed by Mr. Abraham Newland for his grist. If the government cannot pay its own debts in gold, what is to become of the three hundred pounds my father left in trust for Martin?"

Martha set down her bowl upon the dresser.

She looked at the five-year-old boy who was sitting on the hearth-rug, building a cattle-pound out of ash-billets with his little sister Martha. The boy was growing tall, his square, sturdy frame showing the true Finch timber, his dark eyes watching his father with a grave, unchildish intentness.

"Your father’s gold was not lent to the Bank of England, Julian," Martha said calmly. "It was not sent to London to be turned into paper by the King’s printers. It is in the second compartment of the oak chest upstairs, in the five grey linen bags that your mother’s grandfather brought from the Glastonbury bank forty years ago. Every guinea has King George’s head stamped deep in sound guinea-gold, and there is not a lawyer between here and Westminster that can turn it into paper without my signature."

Julian let out a long, slow breath, a faint smile breaking through the anxiety of his face.

"You have a lawyer’s head under that grey hood, Martha," he said.

"I have the head my mother gave me, Julian," she answered. "A woman who has seen eighty children born into empty cottages does not need to read the London Gazette to know that a guinea in the hand is worth twenty promises on paper. Keep your corn in the rick-yard until the panic has blown over. The people must have bread, whether they pay in silver or in barley; and an acre of sound wheat on the combe is worth all the paper that ever crossed the river Thames."

Her words were proven true before the month was out.

While the smallholders who had trusted their little savings to the country banks at Bath and Taunton were brought to the parish gravel-pits, High Barn stood firm as its own Hamstone foundations. 

Julian used his father’s silver to pay his day-labourers their eighteen-pence every Saturday night; the cheese-vats in Eleanor’s dairy were filled twice a day with the rich milk of forty cows; and when the spring arrived, the sixty acres on the hill were ploughed, harrowed, and sown with barley without the borrowing of a single shilling from the gentry.

Yet with the spring came a darker and more terrifying rumor that shook the kingdom to its center.

In May, the news came that the seamen of the Channel Fleet at Spithead had turned their officers ashore, hoisted the red flag of defiance upon the ninety-gun ships, and refused to weigh anchor until their bread was weighed by sixteen ounces to the pound and their wages were paid in honest coin. 

A fortnight later, the mutiny spread to the North Sea Fleet at the Nore, blockading the mouth of the Thames, cutting off the coal-ships from Newcastle, and leaving the capital in darkness and dread.

In Nether-Coombe, the news fell like a spark into dry thatch.

There was scarcely a family in the three parishes that had not a son, a brother, or a cousin serving before the mast in the King’s ships. They had been taken by the press-gangs from the hay-fields, from the alehouse benches, from the coal-carts on the turnpike, and carried off in irons to the receiving-ships at Plymouth, to live on rotten biscuit, salt beef that was hard as mahogany, and foul water that stank of the bilge.

Every evening, when the work of the fields was done, forty or fifty men would gather by the mounting-block in the churchyard, listening to Simon Clout read the letters that had come through the post-office from the seaports.

On the third Sunday in June, a letter arrived with the Portsmouth post-mark, addressed to Rachel Vane at the Black Mill.

***

Rachel brought the letter to Martha’s cottage on the Cross-Spur before the sun was down.

She did not open it upon the road. She carried it in the bosom of her gown, her hands trembling so violently that the heavy red wafer-seal had cracked against her stays.

The young widow sat down upon the stool by Martha’s hearth, her face pale as a linen sheet, her dark eyes wide with a mixture of terror and unendurable hope.

"It is from the fleet, Mrs. Garrow," Rachel whispered. "From the *Royal Sovereign*, at Spithead. It is written in a purser’s hand, but the name... the name at the foot is Martin Vane."

Martha laid down her knitting. She drew her stool beside the girl, took Rachel’s cold hands in hers, and held them with a firm, comforting pressure.

"Read it, Rachel," Martha said gently. "Whatever is written, the truth is always better than the dark."

Rachel broke the seal with a trembling thumb. Her eyes ran down the coarse, salt-stained paper, reading the cramped lines written in the clerk’s gall-nut ink:

*"H.M.S. Royal Sovereign, Spithead. — June the 2nd, 1797.*

*"My Dear Rachel, — This comes with my duty to my father and my loving duty to you, hoping you are in good health as this leaves me at present, thanks be to God for it.*

*"I write these few lines by the hand of the ship’s corporal, to let you know that I am yet in the land of the living, though we have had a hard time of it since the battle of the First of June. When the *Marlborough* went down in the fight with the French three-deckers, I was blown into the sea by the burst of an eighteen-pounder, and was picked up by the boat of the *Culloden* after three hours in the water.*

*"They had me down in the purser’s book as drowned, and my name was returned to the Navy Office at Somerset House among the dead; which is the reason you had no letter from me these three years, being kept in the West Indies in the hospital at Antigua with the yellow fever.*

*"We have been in great trouble here with the ships, but the King has sent down the Admiral Lord Howe with his pardon, and our wages are to be raised to twenty-four shillings a month, and the wounded men are to have their smart-money without deductions.*

*"The Captain has promised me my discharge upon the peace, which they say will be signed before the winter; and I have thirty-four pounds of prize-money due to me from the capture of the Spanish galleon off Cape Saint Vincent, which I will bring home to the Black Mill as soon as my ticket is signed by the Commissioner.*

*"Give my blessing to the little maid, if the Lord has spared her to you; and keep your heart up, my dear girl, for I will be with you before the apple-crop is in the press.*

*"From your faithful and loving husband until death, — Martin Vane."*

Rachel did not speak.

She let the letter drop upon her lap, her head falling forward upon Martha’s knees, her dark hair tumbling over the midwife’s coarse woollen apron.

The tears that had been frozen in her heart through three years of widowhood broke forth in a great, warm, healing torrent—a storm of weeping that shook her slender shoulders with an agony of relief so deep that it was indistinguishable from pain.

"He is alive," she wept into the wool. "Martha... Martha... my husband is alive."

Martha stroked the girl’s hair with her rough, gentle fingers, her own eyes dim with that profound, miraculous awe that comes to those who see the dead brought back from the sea.

"He is alive, Rachel," Martha said softly. "The sea has given him back to you, just as the frost gave the boy back to his mother."

Then Rachel raised her head, her face pale and stricken with a sudden, dreadful memory.

"The boy," she whispered, her eyes searching Martha’s with a wild, frightened inquiry. "Martin Finch... what will my Martin say when he hears of the boy?"

Martha looked into the young woman’s face, her own features carved into lines of calm, unhesitating certainty.

"Martin Vane is an honest sailor, Rachel," the midwife said. "He knows what it is to be fatherless in a hard world. When he comes home to the Black Mill and sees the boy that bore his name—when he hears how you took a motherless infant to your empty breast and saved his life from the drover’s basket—there is not a true seaman in England who will not honor you for the mercy you showed to another man’s child."

She rose from the stool, walked to the shelf, and took down her small stone flask of elderberry wine.

"Drink, Rachel," Martha commanded. "Drink to the *Royal Sovereign*, and to the sailor who was picked up from the salt water. Tomorrow you and I will go to the Black Mill to put the house in order, for there must be clean sheets on the bed and fresh sand upon the kitchen flags before the carrier’s wagon brings him home from Portsmouth."

### Chapter 32

Martin Vane came home in the middle of August, on a day when the reaper’s song was loud in the wheat-fields of Nether-Coombe and the smell of the new sheaves was sweet in the valley.

He was twenty-six years old, but his skin was brown as a walnut from three years of Caribbean sun, and his left cheek was scarred by a splinter from the eighteen-pounder that had sunk his ship; but his blue eyes were clear, bright, and bold, and his broad shoulders moved with the rolling, independent stride of a man who had reefed topsails in a gale off Cape Horn.

He walked up the combe road from the coach-office at Ilchester, carrying his sea-chest upon his shoulder by a rope lashing, wearing his blue jacket with bone buttons, his white duck trousers, and a low-crowned straw hat tied with black ribbon.

When he reached the Black Mill, Rachel was standing by the sluice-gate, with old Nathan beside her.

The young sailor dropped his sea-chest into the wild mint by the race. He caught his wife in his arms, lifting her six inches from the ground in his great, sunburnt hands, kissing her lips, her eyes, and her hair with a fierce, hungry joy that brought the tears to the old miller’s eyes.

Then he turned to the house.

On the third day after his return, Martin Vane walked up the hill to High Barn.

He wore his best shore-going clothes, with a bright silk handkerchief from the Spanish Main knotted about his neck, and in his hand he carried a small, heavy box of carved cedar-wood bound with brass hoops.

Julian met him in the yard.

The two young men—the yeoman who had held the land and the sailor who had fought the King’s battles—stood looking at each other across the clean straw of the threshing-floor.

For a moment neither spoke. There was no hostility in their eyes; there was only that grave, searching scrutiny that honest men give to those whose lives have touched their own in the dark.

"Master Finch," the sailor said, touching the brim of his straw hat with a formal knuckle.

"Martin Vane," Julian said, stepping forward with his hand outstretched. "You are welcome to High Barn. You are welcome to the best horse in my stable and the best cider in my cellar."

The sailor took Julian’s hand. The grip was hard and long, the pressure of two men who understood each other without the waste of words.

"I came to see the lad," Martin Vane said simply.

Julian turned toward the kitchen door.

"Eleanor," he called.

The boy was dressed in a little smock of bleached linen that his mother had embroidered with blue thread, his dark curls brushed back from his brow, his grey eyes looking up at the strange sailor with that fearless, intelligent curiosity that was the mark of the Finches.

The sailor knelt upon one knee on the flags.

He looked into the child’s face—at the bold, handsome forehead, the firm, straight nose, and the wide, square thumb that was clutched in Eleanor’s apron.

A strange, soft light came into the seaman’s scarred face.

"He is a fine lad, mistress," Martin Vane said to Eleanor, his voice dropping into a quiet, reverent tone. "A proper, clean-built craft, sound in timber and spar. My Rachel told me the whole story, from the drover’s basket to the court-house at Ilchester."

He unbuckled the brass clasp of his cedar-wood box.

Inside lay a heavy gold doubloon, minted at Mexico fifty years before, bearing the arms of the King of Spain—the sailor’s own share of the treasure-ship taken off the Portuguese coast.

He took the gold coin, reached out, and pressed it into the child’s small, fat hand.

"Take it, little namesake," the sailor said, closing the boy’s fingers over the heavy yellow metal. "It was won in fair fight under the smoke of the *Royal Sovereign’s* guns. Keep it to buy yourself a silver watch when you are twenty-one, and remember that there was a sailor on the marsh who was proud to give you his name when you had none of your own."

The boy looked at the heavy gold piece, then looked up into the sailor’s face with a bright, fearless smile.

"I will keep it in my pocket, sailor-man," Martin said, "and when I am big, I will buy a red horse like my father’s."

Eleanor reached down, took the seaman’s hand, and pressed it to her lips with a sudden, passionate gratitude that spoke for all the silent years of her apprentice life.

"God bless you, Martin Vane," she whispered. "God bless you and Rachel for the mercy you showed to my babe."

The sailor rose to his feet. He took off his hat to the yeoman’s wife, turned to Julian with a brief, nautical nod, and walked down the combe road with his head held high in the August sun.

### Chapter 33

The autumn of ninety-seven brought the greatest flood that the Somerset levels had seen since the sea broke through the sand-dunes at Burnham sixty years before.

In the second week of November, after three weeks of continuous rain that saturated the peat-sponge to its capacity, a violent southwesterly gale coincided with the highest spring-tide of the equinox. 

The sea-water forced its way up the mouths of the Parrett and the Brue, backing up the freshwater rivers until they overflowed the dykes, drowning eighty square miles of water-meadows from Glastonbury to the foot of the Mendip hills.

The Black Mill was surrounded by five feet of water in less than six hours.

Old Nathan and Martin Vane worked until midnight, carrying the flour-sacks up to the granary floor and mooring the heavy mill-boats to the willow-trunks beside the race; while Rachel sat in the upper chamber, watching the yellow water swirl against the bedroom windows with that patient, uncomplaining endurance that belonged to the daughters of the fens.

At four o'clock in the morning, while the gale was at its height, a horseman rode his lathered beast through the flooded causeway to the foot of the Cross-Spur.

It was William Vowles, the blacksmith’s brother from High Duntish.

His horse was wet to the girths with black peat-mud, and the man’s face was distorted with terror as he shouted through the wind at Martha’s gate:

"Mrs. Garrow! Rachel Vane! For the love of heaven, come to the Island! It is Phoebe Trask—Phoebe that was—Mistress Yeo at the Pool Farm! She has been three days in her labor, and the waters have surrounded the house until no cart can reach the threshold!"

Martha was at the door in an instant, with Rachel beside her.

Phoebe Trask, the miller’s daughter, had married young George Yeo of High Duntish twelve months before—a good, substantial young yeoman who had rebuilt the old stone farmhouse on the Pool Island, where the land rose ten feet above the surrounding peat-waste.

It was her first child.

Martha looked at Rachel.

The younger woman had her woollen cloak already pinned at the throat, her small leather pocket-book in her hand, her dark eyes clear and steady.

"We must take the punt, Martha," Rachel said. "The road through the withies is under six feet of water. No horse can swim the rhines with the current running out toward the sea."

"Can you handle the pole, Rachel?" Martha asked.

"I was born in a mill-boat, Mrs. Garrow," the girl said, with a quiet confidence that settled the question. "Martin has his twelve-foot ash punt tied to the willows at the turnpike gate. We can cross the flooded meadows in half an hour."

They took their baskets—Martha’s wicker pannier with its ancient sheepskin book, and Rachel’s new calf-bound register with the silver-mounted scissors—and followed the horseman down the hill to the edge of the flood.

The scene that met them at the turnpike was terrifying.

The valley had ceased to exist. 

From the foot of the combe to the black ridge of the Poldens, the world was a great, roaring inland sea of yellow water, driven into white-crested waves by the gale, carrying floating hay-ricks, drowned sheep, broken fences, and uprooted trees out toward the Bristol Channel. 

Through the grey dawn, the square stone tower of Saint Jude-in-the-Fens rose above the flood like a lighthouse in the ocean, its bells chiming the distress-alarm with a wild, hurried clanging that was swallowed by the roar of the wind.

Martin Vane was waiting by the turnpike bar with his twelve-foot ash punt.

The sailor had stripped to his trousers and woollen shirt, his feet bare, holding the boat against the stone pier with a long ash setting-pole.

"It is a hard pull across the deep rhine, Mrs. Garrow," the sailor shouted through the gale. "The current is running at four knots toward the sea-sluice at Highbridge. But the craft is tight, and if Rachel takes the steering-paddle, we will fetch the Island before the tide turns."

Martha stepped into the boat without a word. She sat down upon the bottom-boards, holding her basket between her knees, wrapping her cloak about her instruments.

Rachel took the heavy oak steering-paddle in the stern, her hair tied tight beneath her hood, her face calm and set.

The sailor shoved off from the stone wall with a powerful heave of his arms.

The punt shot out into the yellow torrent.

For twenty minutes, the passage was a battle between the muscles of the sailor and the blind, ferocious fury of the water. 

Twice the boat was nearly swamped by the wash of a floating hay-rick that drifted past them in the foam; once the bottom-boards grated with a sickening shudder upon the submerged copings of a stone bridge; but Martin Vane’s arms were like seasoned ash-timbers, and Rachel’s paddle held the craft’s head to the sea with that miraculous, instinctive skill that belongs to people who have lived upon the rivers from their childhood.

They reached the Pool Island as the church clock at High Duntish was chiming seven.

The house stood ten feet above the flood, surrounded by eighty sheep that had been driven up from the low pastures, huddled together against the garden walls.

In the kitchen, Farmer Trask sat by the table, his grey head buried in his flour-dusted hands, weeping with that terrible, helpless agony of an old man who sees his only child dying before his eyes.

Beside him stood young George Yeo, his face white as a blanched almond, his hands clenched until the nails drew blood from his palms.

"She is sinking," the young husband whispered as Martha stepped over the sill. "The doctor from Yeovil was here yesterday; he tried to use the instruments, but he could not pass them. He said... he said the bones were too narrow, and that no living child could ever pass the pelvis."

Martha did not look at the miller. She did not look at the husband.

She turned to Rachel.

"Take off your cloak, Rachel," Martha said, her voice dropping into that quiet, commanding authority that had ruled the beds of three parishes for twenty-two years. "Wash your hands in hot water. This is your delivery."

Rachel looked up, her dark eyes wide with sudden, terrified inquiry.

"Mine, Martha?"

"Yours," the elder midwife said firmly. "You have watched me twice with the transverse births; you have studied the plates in Dr. Denman’s book; you have the small, delicate hand that can pass where an old woman’s fingers are too thick with the rheum. I will keep the watch, Rachel; but the work is yours."

They went up to the bedchamber.

Phoebe Yeo lay upon the four-post bed, her face exhausted to the color of grey ashes, her fair hair soaked with cold perspiration, her breath coming in short, shallow gasps that scarcely lifted the counterpane.

The labor had lasted forty hours. The muscular contractions of the womb had ceased, paralyzed by the long, fruitless struggle against the rigid, unyielding pelvic bones; and the girl’s pulse was rising toward that fatal, galloping frequency that marks the onset of internal exhaustion.

Rachel knelt beside the bed.

She did not touch the instruments that lay in the doctor’s leather roll upon the table.

She greased her slender right hand with warm olive-oil from the phial, slid her fingers gently into the passage, and felt for the presenting part.

Martha stood by the bed-post, her arms folded beneath her apron, her eyes watching every movement of the younger woman’s shoulder with the keen, unblinking vigilance of an eagle.

"What do you find, Rachel?" Martha asked quietly.

"The head is high," Rachel whispered, her brow furrowing with deep, concentrated thought. "The occiput is turned toward the sacrum. It is the posterior presentation, Martha—the face is looking toward the pubic bone."

"A face-to-pubis," Martha said. "And the pelvis?"

"The conjugate diameter is narrow," Rachel said. "Three inches and a half, not more. The doctor’s forceps have bruised the anterior lip of the cervix, but the bone is uninjured."

"What is the remedy, scholar?"

Rachel looked up, her dark eyes flashing with sudden, luminous certainty.

"Not the forceps," she said. "The blades would crush the parietal bones against the promontory of the sacrum. We must rotate the head manually—we must turn the occiput forward into the arch of the pubis, so that the narrow diameter of the skull may pass through the wide diameter of the outlet."

A faint, beautiful smile touched Martha’s lips.

"Do it, Rachel," the elder midwife said.

Rachel passed her two fingers behind the child’s ear.

With infinite gentleness, waiting for the return of the faint, intermittent contractions, she applied a steady, upward and rotary pressure upon the temporal bone.

For twenty minutes, the room was as silent as the churchyard.

Outside, the floodwaters were roaring against the garden wall; inside, the only sound was the breathing of the two women and the faint, rhythmic ticking of the watch upon the mantle-tree.

Slowly, millimeter by millimeter, the stubborn bone yielded to the skilled, intelligent persuasion of the woman’s hand.

The head rotated ninety degrees. 

The occiput slipped smoothly into the sub-pubic arch; the wide sagittal suture aligned itself with the long axis of the pelvic outlet; and with the next strong, natural contraction of the uterus, the head descended into the perineum with a smooth, irresistible advance that required neither steel nor force to bring it into the light.

At eight o'clock in the morning, while the sun was breaking through the storm-clouds and turning the yellow floodwaters to molten gold, Phoebe Yeo’s child was born.

It was a girl—sound, plump, and vigorous, with its grandfather’s round chin and its mother’s fair skin.

When Rachel held the child up in her warm, blood-stained hands, its first, sharp cry rang through the old stone house with a sweet, piercing clarity that brought the two men running up the stairs.

Farmer Trask stopped upon the threshold.

He looked at the living child in Rachel’s arms; he looked at his daughter, who was smiling through her tears from the pillows; and then he looked at Martha Garrow, who stood by the bed-post with her hands folded beneath her apron.

The old miller fell upon his knees upon the oak floor, his grey head bowed against the door-post, his broad shoulders shaking with those deep, cleansing tears that wash away the bitterness of forty years.

"God forgive me," the old man wept. "God forgive me for the hard thoughts I had against this house."

Martha walked across the room.

She laid her hand upon the miller’s shoulder—the shoulder of the man who had demanded that her licence be taken from her, who had locked his granaries against the starving people, who had brought the parish to the verge of ruin.

"Rise up, Master Trask," Martha said gently. "The child is alive, and the water is falling. Go down to the kitchen and bid Martin Vane broach a cask of your best ale, for the daughter of High Duntish has brought a new soul into the vale, and the watch has been kept in truth."

### Chapter 34

The century died in the winter of 1799.

It died in a season of quiet, grey cold that settled upon the three parishes like a white cloth laid over the face of the dead. 

There were no great frosts, no bread-riots, and no floods; the war in Europe had moved away to the sands of Egypt and the mountains of Switzerland; and the people of Nether-Coombe went about their winter labor—their hedging, their ditching, their thrashing, and their cheese-making—with that steady, enduring patience that had outlived four dynasties and twenty wars.

At High Barn, the old order had passed away into a new, quiet strength.

Mistress Finch had been laid beside her husband beneath the churchyard yew eighteen months before, her passing so gentle that the women who sat with her scarcely knew the hour when her breath ceased.

Julian was now thirty; a tall, grave, handsome yeoman whose voice was listened to with respect at the rent-audits and the Petty Sessions. 

He had refused the commission in the Yeomanry that Justice Scrope had offered him after the Spithead mutiny; he preferred to remain in his frieze coat, farming his six hundred acres with his own teams, paying his labourers two shillings a day in good silver, and standing between the cottagers and the enclosure commissioners.

Eleanor ruled the great house with a gentle, firm, unpretending dignity that had won the love of the whole parish. 

The old memory of her apprentice days had faded from the village mind, swallowed up by her endless kindness to the sick, her quiet modesty at the church-gate, and the reputation of her cheese-dairy, which had taken the silver medal at the Bath and West Agricultural Society two years in succession.

Martin was now seven years old: a bold, fearless, intelligent lad who could ride his roan pony without a saddle, read his chapter in the Bible with the fluency of a clerk, and write his name in a clear, upright hand that was the copy of Martha’s own.

Beside him grew little Martha, five years old, fair and delicate as a wild rose, and baby Gervase, who was just beginning to walk between his father’s knees.

On the thirty-first day of December 1799—the last night of the eighteenth century—Martha Garrow sat alone by the hearth of her cottage on the Cross-Spur.

The room was warm and bright.

A great log of split ash, sent down from the High Barn wood-shed that morning by Julian’s team, burned with a steady, quiet flame in the grate, throwing a cheerful red light across the clean flagstones and the whitewashed rafters.

On the table stood her two sheepskin registers: the old book that Bishop Moss had signed twenty-five years ago, its leaves yellow and worn with the grease of eighty lying-in chambers; and the new volume, bound in sound calf, which was already half-filled with Rachel’s neat, regular script.

Martha was forty-six years old.

Her hair was now white as the rime upon the thorn-bushes, and her knees had begun to keep that separate calendar that her mother had known before her—aching with the coming of the east wind, stiffening with the cold damp of the autumn marshes.

Yet her hands were still straight, long, and strong; her eyes were as clear and dark as the water in the limestone well beneath her wall; and her memory held the secret history of every household in the vale.

Nine hundred and ninety-nine.

That was the count at the foot of the calfskin page.

Nine hundred and ninety-nine times she had received a living soul from the dark waters of the womb and delivered it up to the light of an English day.

There was only one line left to complete the thousand.

A quiet tap sounded upon the cottage door.

Martha did not start. She rose from her chair, smoothed her apron, and drew the bolt.

Standing on the step, wrapped in a heavy blue riding-coat with silver buttons, his beaver hat dusted with the fine powder of the new snow, stood Justice Scrope.

The magistrate was seventy years old now, his tall frame bowed by the gout, his hair white as cotton beneath his hat, leaning heavily upon an ivory-headed cane.

He did not wait for an invitation. He stepped into the cottage with that slow, measured, aristocratic dignity that had not left him through forty years of judicial authority.

"Good evening, Mrs. Garrow," the magistrate said.

"Good evening, Mr. Scrope," she answered, drawing her armchair forward to the hearth. "Pray take the chair, sir. The wind from the down is bitter tonight."

The old gentleman sat down, resting his two hands upon the ivory head of his cane, his sharp grey eyes taking in the quiet room, the burning ash log, the open registers upon the table, and the dried herbs hanging from the rafters.

For several minutes he did not speak. He sat looking into the fire, his thin lips moving slightly as if he were calculating some long, intricate sum in the ledger of his life.

"Mrs. Garrow," Scrope said at last, his voice softer and lower than she had ever heard it in the library or the vestry. "I have received notice from the Bishop’s Registrar at Wells that the General Visitation of the Diocese will be held in the Cathedral on the tenth of May next."

"I know it, sir," Martha said. "Mr. Harford informed me on Sunday."

"The Chancellor has directed that the midwives’ licences throughout the Archdeaconry of Wells shall be inspected and renewed," Scrope continued. "And he has requested the magistrates of this division to make a report upon the condition of the poor, the administration of the parish rates, and the conduct of the ecclesiastical officers."

He paused, turning his spectacles toward her face.

"I have completed my report today, Mrs. Garrow."

"Yes, sir?"

The old magistrate reached into his coat pocket and drew out a folded sheet of parchment, written in his own clear, elegant clerk’s hand.

"I have thought it right to show you the entry," Scrope said, "before it is deposited in the Bishop’s archives."

He held out the sheet.

Martha took the paper, holding it where the firelight fell across the text. Her eyes ran down the formal, measured sentences:

*"To the Right Reverend the Lord Bishop of Bath and Wells, and to his Worship the Chancellor of the Diocese:*

*"I, John Scrope, one of His Majesty’s Justices of the Peace for the County of Somerset, do hereby certify that I have known Martha Garrow, licensed midwife of the parish of Nether-Coombe, for five-and-twenty years.*

*"I certify that she hath attended nine hundred and ninety-and-nine deliveries within the three parishes of Nether-Coombe, High Duntish, and Saint Jude-in-the-Fens; that she hath exercised her office with equal skill, diligence, and mercy toward the rich and the poor; that she hath used no unlawful arts or superstitious enchantments; and that she hath never concealed, switched, or made away with any infant born alive.*

*"And I do further certify that by her steadfast adherence to the truth, in a time of great public peril and private deceit, she did preserve the life of an innocent mother from the sentence of the law, did restore the peace and honor of an ancient family, and did maintain the trust of this agricultural community when the authority of the magistrates was shaken by the distress of the times.*

*"Given under my hand at Nether-Coombe Court, this thirty-first day of December, in the year of our Lord 1799. — John Scrope."*

Martha looked up from the parchment.

Her hands were shaking. A single tear—the first tear she had shed since the day she walked the Mendip snows—gathered in her eye and fell upon the magistrate’s signature.

"Mr. Scrope," she whispered.

The old man rose from his chair.

He stood tall before her, his hat in his hand, his head bowed with that grave, courtly reverence that he had once shown to the King’s judges at the Taunton Assizes.

"It is nothing more than the truth, Mrs. Garrow," Scrope said. "We have differed in our time, mistress. I looked at the parish through the rate-books and the statutes of the realm; you looked at it through the beds of women and the breath of newborn children. But as I stand upon the threshold of the grave, I see that your law was older than mine, and that your book will be remembered when my judgements are covered with the dust of the record-room."

He took her rough hand, pressed it with his cold, aristocratic fingers, and walked to the door.

At the threshold he turned, his face framed by the dark winter night.

"The new century begins at midnight, Martha," the magistrate said. "May God grant that it find as faithful a servant on the Cross-Spur as the one who has kept the watch through the century that is gone."

He opened the door and walked out into the falling snow, his servant holding the lantern before him as he descended the hill.

### Chapter 35

The church bells of Nether-Coombe did not ring at midnight.

In the agricultural parishes, men did not welcome the new century with drums and bonfires; they slept the sleep of the winter-season, waiting for the seed-time and the lambing that God should send them with the spring.

At three o'clock in the morning, while the snow was falling softly upon the thatched roofs of the village, the knock came.

It was not a hurried, terrified drumming of flint-stones upon the glass.

It was three quiet, firm, regular knocks—the knock of an honest husband who knows that his hour is come, but whose heart is steady in the faith of his house.

Martha opened the door.

Julian Finch stood upon the step, his great frieze coat covered with white flakes, his dark eyes shining with a quiet, joyous excitement in the lantern-light.

"Martha," Julian said, his voice rich and musical in the silent night. "Eleanor has called for you."

Martha took her basket from the dresser.

She took her tin cylinder from the shelf; she took her clean linen rollers, her horn-handled scissors, and her pot of fresh sheep’s tallow.

Then she walked across the heath beside the young yeoman, their boots crunching peacefully through the three inches of virgin snow.

The great kitchen of High Barn was warm, bright, and sweet with the smell of burning birch-logs and boiled elderberry wine.

Martin Vane and Rachel were sitting by the hearth, with young Martin Finch sleeping peacefully between them in the settle, wrapped in his sailor’s cloak.

Upstairs, in the great chamber where old Gervase had finished his measure, Eleanor lay upon the pillows, her face flushed with the healthy, natural effort of her labor, her grey eyes smiling as Martha stepped over the sill.

The delivery was the work of half an hour.

At twenty minutes before five o'clock on the first morning of January 1800, while the morning star was rising clear and bright above the limestone cliffs of the combe, Eleanor gave birth to her third child.

It was a boy—broad-shouldered, strong-limbed, with a thick crest of dark hair and the wide, flat thumb that had marked the Finches for five generations.

When the child slipped into Martha’s hands, he did not wait for the slap upon the feet.

He drew the cold, sweet air of the new century into his lungs, opened his bold, dark eyes, and let out a great, ringing, triumphant cry that was heard through the floor in the kitchen below, bringing a cheer from the young sailor and a prayer of thanksgiving from the mother’s lips.

Martha washed him in the warm spring-water; she bound his navel with her clean linen roller; she laid him to Eleanor’s breast; and then she sat down by the small deal table beside the casement.

She drew her calfskin register toward her.

She dipped her goose-quill into the iron ink-pot, and upon the final, blank line of the thousandth page, wrote the entry that completed her five-and-twenty years:

*1800. Jan. 1. — High Barn. — Julian Finch, yeoman, & Eleanor his wife. — Boy. — Alive. Strong and lusty. Delivered at 20 minutes before 5 of the clock in the morning. — The thousandth child.*

She set down the pen.

She walked to the window and pushed open the small diamond casement.

The snow had ceased falling.

The sky above the Mendip hills was clearing into a vast, crystal vault of pale sapphire, touched with the first rose-colored light of the rising sun. 

Below her, the valley of Nether-Coombe lay white, peaceful, and pure beneath its winter mantle: the red earth of the wheat-fields sleeping under the snow, the water-mills waiting for the thaw, the sheep-cotes safe upon the hillside, and the smoke of forty cottage hearths rising straight into the morning air.

Martha looked down upon the child sleeping at his mother’s breast; she looked down upon the young yeoman holding his wife’s hand; and she looked out across the land she had walked through all the seasons of her youth.

The trust was kept.

The mother was safe; the family was whole; the license was honored; and the village was at peace.

And as the great sun rose above the eastern ridge, turning the snow upon the church tower to molten gold, Martha Garrow drew her cloak about her shoulders and stood in the light of the new morning.
### Chapter 36

The christening of the thousandth child took place on Candlemas Day in the year 1800, when the snowdrops were hanging their white bells in the rectory garden and the rooks were wheeling high above the church tower in the pale blue winter sky.

The church of Nether-Coombe was filled from the altar-rails to the western gallery. 

They had come from the three parishes: the farmers in their blue coats and clean buckskin breeches; the weavers with their wives in their best red whittle-shawls; the cottagers from the Cross-Spur; and the peat-diggers from the borders of the marsh. In the front pews sat Justice Scrope, wrapped in his fur-trimmed cloak, with the Reverend Mr. Harford in his white surplice waiting by the ancient Norman font of carved lias stone.

Julian Finch carried the child himself up the nave, his head held high with that proud, quiet dignity that had replaced the reckless arrogance of his youth. 

Beside him walked Eleanor, wearing a new gown of lavender-colored poplin that Julian had bought for her at the Yeovil fair, her red hair dressed with a simple tortoiseshell comb, her face glowing with that serene, mature beauty that comes to women whose hearts are established in love and honor.

Behind them walked the godparents: Martin Vane in his blue sailor’s jacket, with his Mexican gold doubloon hanging from his watch-chain; Rachel in her green silk bonnet; and young Martin Finch, holding his sister Martha by the hand, wearing a little blue coat with silver buttons that had been made from his father’s old yeomanry uniform.

When the rector dipped his hand into the consecrated water, he looked round upon the congregation.

"Name this child," Mr. Harford said, his old voice trembling with emotion.

Julian looked at Eleanor, and Eleanor looked at Martha Garrow, who stood by the chancel arch with her wicker basket on her arm.

"Gervase Matthew," Julian said, his voice ringing through the ancient arches with a clear, steady strength. "Gervase Matthew Finch."

The water fell three times upon the infant’s brow.

"Brethren," the old rector said, turning his back upon the communion table and looking down upon the crowded church. "It is five-and-twenty years since I entered the cure of these three parishes. In that time, I have buried three hundred of your fathers and mothers beneath the turf of the churchyard; I have joined seventy pairs in holy wedlock; and I have baptized nine hundred and ninety of the children of this vale."

He paused, resting his hands upon the stone rim of the font.

"We have passed through great troubles together. We have seen the bread-riots, the press-gangs, the great frost, the flood, and the pestilence. We have seen the price of corn rise until the poor wept in the furrows, and we have seen the authority of the law shaken by the fear of rebellion."

He turned his eyes toward the woman who stood beside the pillar in her grey woollen cloak.

"Yet in all those years, the hand of God has preserved to us one witness whose courage never failed, whose eye never turned aside from the duty of her calling, and whose tongue never spoke a lie to save the great or to flatter the powerful."

The old clergyman reached out his hand.

"Step forward, Martha Garrow."

Martha walked up the two stone steps of the chancel. She made her quiet curtsy to the minister, keeping her hands folded beneath her apron.

Mr. Harford took from the altar a small, square book bound in dark blue morocco leather, stamped in gold upon the cover with the arms of the Diocese of Bath and Wells.

"This is the Certificate of the Chancellor of the Diocese," the rector said, placing the volume in her hands. "It records that Martha Garrow, licensed midwife, hath completed her thousandth delivery in peace and honor; that she hath trained an approved successor in the person of Rachel Vane; and that the bishop, the clergy, the magistrates, and the inhabitants of these three parishes do hereby return their humble thanksgiving to Almighty God for the mercy of her ministry."

A low, deep murmur of assent—a sound like the rising of the wind in a field of ripe wheat—ran through the church.

John Vowles the blacksmith nodded his head with a heavy, emphatic satisfaction; Simon Clout pulled his woollen comforter tighter about his neck, wiping a drop of moisture from his nose with the back of his sleeve; and old Farmer Trask, leaning upon his heavy ash stick, bowed his head toward the midwife with a tears of honest reverence in his eyes.

Martha took the blue leather volume.

She felt the smooth, cold grain of the morocco beneath her fingers—the book that would stand in the Bishop’s library at Wells beside the charters of the Norman kings and the deeds of the medieval priors.

"I thank you, Mr. Harford," Martha said, her voice clear, calm, and quiet as the church bell at dawn. "And I thank the parish. The work was heavy at times, sir; but the life was good, and the children have all grown to be honest men and women. That is the only certificate that a midwife asks to carry before the throne of God."

### Chapter 37

In the second week of May 1800, the General Visitation of the Archdeaconry of Wells was held in the Cathedral of Saint Andrew.

The day was clear, warm, and radiant with the early summer sunshine. The grey towers of the cathedral rose like cliffs of carved limestone against the dark green shoulder of the Mendip hills, their hundreds of stone saints, kings, and bishops looking down upon the green expanse of the Cathedral Green.

Through the great northern gate—the Browne’s Gate—the clergy, the churchwardens, the apparitors, and the licensed officers of seventy parishes walked two by two in their gowns, their bands, and their best Sunday broadcloth.

Martha Garrow and Rachel Vane walked together across the green.

Martha wore her best black woollen gown—the gown that Julian and Eleanor had given her for the christening—with a white lawn kerchief pinned across her bosom and her mother’s silver brooch at her throat. She walked with that slow, measured, tireless pace that had carried her four thousand miles along the turnpikes; but Rachel walked beside her, carrying the heavy wicker pannier that held the two parish registers.

Rachel was twenty-five now: a tall, handsome, dignified young woman, her dark hair combed smoothly beneath a neat bonnet of black straw, her cheeks flushed with the walk across the hills, carrying herself with that quiet, modest authority that had made her the beloved friend of every young mother on the marsh-borders.

They entered the cathedral by the north porch.

The interior of the great church was an avenue of stone columns that rose into the vaulted roof like the trunks of giant beeches in a royal forest. 

The sunshine poured through the high clerestory windows in long, slanting shafts of gold and azure, illuminating the tombs of the ancient bishops where their marble effigies lay with their hands clasped in prayer, their stone hounds resting at their feet.

In the Chapter House—that magnificent, thirty-two-sided chamber where the central stone pillar branched outward into the roof like the branches of a great palm tree—the Chancellor of the Diocese was holding his court.

The Chancellor was a venerable clergyman of seventy-five, the Reverend Dr. Moss, nephew to the Bishop who had signed Martha’s first parchment twenty-five years before. 

He sat in the great carved chair of the Dean, wearing his scarlet doctor’s gown and his powdered wig, with his registrars, his proctors, and his clerks sitting below him at a long table covered with green baize and piled with parchment rolls.

When the apparitor called the parish of Nether-Coombe, Martha and Rachel stepped forward to the bar.

The old Chancellor adjusted his spectacles. He looked down upon the two women—the elder with her white hair and her rough, capable hands; the younger with her clear dark eyes and her modest demeanor.

"Martha Garrow," the Chancellor said, his voice echoing musical and sweet in the vaulted chamber. "You were licensed by our predecessor in the year of our Lord 1775."

"I was, your worship," Martha answered.

"The registry reports that you have attended one thousand and four deliveries," Dr. Moss said, his eyes scanning the certificate that lay before him. "And that during that whole period, no complaint hath ever been exhibited against you in this court, nor hath any parish officer ever charged you with negligence, ignorance, or unfaithfulness to your oath."

He leaned forward, looking at the two books that Rachel had laid upon the table.

"Are these your registers, mistress?"

"They are, sir," Martha said. "The old sheepskin book is my mother’s and my own; the calfskin book is the work of Rachel Vane, who has been my scholar these four years, and who now holds the license of this diocese for the fen parishes."

The Chancellor opened the calfskin volume. 

He turned the pages slowly, his scholarly fingers tracing the ruled columns, the dates, the names of the mothers, and the small, regular ink pricks that recorded the living souls.

A profound silence fell upon the Chapter House. 

The forty clergymen who sat upon the stone benches around the wall—the vicars of the hill-parishes, the rectors of the rich marsh livings, and the prebendaries of the cathedral—leaned forward to listen.

"Dr. Prideaux," the Chancellor said, turning toward a gentleman who sat among the medical assessors at the end of the table. "You have examined these journals, I believe?"

Dr. Evelyn Prideaux rose from his seat.

The young surgeon was dressed in the height of the London fashion: a coat of dark blue superfine cloth with gilt buttons, a white marcella waistcoat, and his hair combed without powder in the new style of the Regency. But his sharp, pale face had a look of deep, unaffected humility as he bowed to the Chancellor.

"I have examined them with the greatest care, Mr. Chancellor," Prideaux said, his northern accent sounding crisp and clear beneath the stone vault. "And I have laid an abstract of their contents before the Royal College of Surgeons at Edinburgh and the Obstetrical Society of London."

He turned his eyes upon Martha with an expression of open, unreserved admiration.

"The records of Mrs. Garrow and her assistant, sir, demonstrate what can be accomplished by empirical patience, anatomical gentleness, and an unyielding fidelity to nature. In one thousand deliveries, the proportion of maternal deaths is less than five in the thousand—a rate of success that has not been equalled by any lying-in hospital in Great Britain or on the Continent."

The young surgeon bowed to the court.

"The mechanical instruments of our art, Mr. Chancellor, have their place in the rare extremities of malformation; but the safety of the mothers of England must always rest upon the hands, the hearts, and the consciences of women such as these."

A murmur of applause ran through the Chapter House—an irregular, spontaneous sound that was instantly checked by the apparitor’s staff, but which brought a warm, beautiful flush into Rachel’s cheeks.

The Chancellor closed the book.

He took up a broad sheet of vellum, sealed with the great seal of the Bishop of Bath and Wells in green wax—the seal of perpetual institution.

"Rachel Vane," Dr. Moss said, addressing the younger woman. "You have heard the testimony of your teacher, and the report of the district surgeon. Are you ready to take upon yourself the solemn oath of a midwife under the canons of the Church of England?"

Rachel walked to the table. She laid her right hand upon the great Latin Bible that lay before the registrar.

Her voice was low, but it did not tremble by a hair’s breadth:

"I am ready, sir," she said. "To minister to rich and poor alike with equal care; to use no secret crafts or enchantments; to preserve the life of mother and child to the utmost of my skill; to report every birth truly to the minister for lawful baptism; and never to conceal, switch, or make away with any infant born alive."

The Chancellor rose from his chair.

He placed the vellum patent in Rachel’s hands, laid his venerable palm upon her dark head, and spoke the solemn words of the ancient benediction:

"The blessing of God Almighty, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost, be upon thee, and upon the children whom thou shalt deliver into the light of his world, both now and forevermore. Amen."

***

They walked out of the cathedral into the blinding sunshine of the green.

The bells in the great central tower were chiming for the noon-service—that sweet, musical peal of twelve bells that floated out across the red roofs of the city toward the blue ridge of the Mendips.

Dr. Prideaux followed them through the Browne’s Gate, his hat in his hand.

"Mrs. Garrow," the surgeon said, stopping beside them on the gravel path. "I have a small matter of business to discharge with you, on behalf of the governors of the Bath Dispensary."

He drew from his pocket a small, flat purse of purple silk, through the meshes of which the yellow glitter of twenty gold guineas was visible.

"The governors have established an annual prize," Prideaux said, "for the best register of rural obstetrical practice kept within the county of Somerset. The committee has awarded the premium of twenty guineas to the midwives of Nether-Coombe."

Martha looked at the purple silk purse.

Her long, rough fingers did not reach out to take it.

"I have no need of twenty guineas, Mr. Prideaux," she said, with that quiet, smiling independence that had baffled Justice Scrope five years before. "Julian Finch sends my split ash to my wood-shed every Martinmas; Eleanor keeps my meal-ark full of sound flour; and Rachel’s husband brings my fish from the river. Keep your gold for the dispensary, sir. Buy blankets for the poor women who must lie-in in the cellars of Bath without a coal in their grates."

The surgeon looked at her for a long moment.

He saw the white hair beneath the black bonnet; the clear, dark eyes that had looked into the face of death fifty times without fear; and the rough, work-worn hands that had received a thousand souls from the dark.

He did not press the money upon her. He bowed low over her hand, touched his lips to her knuckles with the reverence of a courtier before a queen, and walked away toward his carriage.

Martha turned to Rachel.

"Come, child," she said, taking the younger woman’s arm. "The carrier’s wagon leaves the George at two o'clock, and we have sixteen miles to travel before the sun is down."

### Chapter 38

In the autumn of 1800, the old magistrate died.

Justice Scrope was found dead in his great leather chair in the library at Nether-Coombe Court on a sunny morning in October, with an open volume of the statutes of King William lying upon his knees and his spectacles resting upon the page.

His passing was like the falling of an ancient oak that had stood in the avenue for a hundred years: it left a great, empty space in the sky that made the surrounding country look strange and bare to the eyes of those who had lived in its shadow.

When the will was read by the family solicitors from London, it was found that the magistrate had not forgotten the battle of the Cross-Spur.

He had devised the manor of Nether-Coombe to his nephew, a young captain in the Guards who was serving with the Duke of York in Holland; but the devise was subject to a solemn, irrevocable trust:

*"Item: I do hereby give, devise, and confirm unto Martha Garrow, licensed midwife, and to her heirs and assigns forever, the messuage, cottage, garden, and parcel of land situated upon the Cross-Spur, to be held by her and them as a free and clear estate of inheritance, discharged of all rents, suits, services, and demands of the Lord of the said Manor for all time to come.*

*"And I do further give and bequeath the sum of five hundred pounds of lawful money of Great Britain, to be invested in the three per centum consolidated annuities, for the founding and maintenance of a Free School in the parish of Nether-Coombe, for the instruction of twenty poor children of the said parish in reading, writing, and the principles of the Christian religion.*

*"And I do nominate and appoint Julian Finch, yeoman, the Reverend Mr. Harford, and Martha Garrow, to be the first trustees of the said school and of the said endowment forever."*

The village received the news with that quiet, wondering awe that falls upon common people when they see the hard, rigid machinery of the law turned into an instrument of mercy and justice.

The cottage on the Cross-Spur was now a freehold: a piece of independent English soil that no landlord could enclose, no bailiff could enter, and no court could touch.

On the day the school was opened—in the old tithe-barn behind the churchyard, which Julian and his carpenters had repaired with new oak beams and leaded windows—twenty little boys and girls from the cottages sat upon the low ash benches, their faces clean, their hair combed, holding their horn-books in their laps.

At the head of the first bench sat young Martin Finch, eight years old, helping little Tom Vowles, the blacksmith’s grandson, trace the letter *A* in the sand-trough with an ash-stick.

Martha stood by the door, watching the children’s fingers move through the grey sand.

She remembered the night, five years before, when she had walked through the Ilchester drifts with the bread in her basket, wondering if that boy would live to see his name in the parish register.

Now he was the heir of High Barn, the master of his three hundred pounds trust, and the teacher of the poor children of his village.

The circle was complete.

The seed that had been sown in tears, in shame, and in the shadow of the gallows had brought forth its thirty-fold, its sixty-fold, and its hundred-fold in the rich soil of an honest life.

### Chapter 39

The year of eighteen hundred and one brought the Peace of Amiens and the great harvest of the century.

The war that had drained the blood and the treasure of Europe for eight years ceased with the signing of the preliminaries in London; the wheat that had stood at ninety shillings fell to fifty; and the great two-pound loaf was sold in the shops of Ilchester for seven-pence.

In Nether-Coombe, the harvest was the heaviest that the oldest labourer had ever seen in the valley.

The ears of wheat were so long and full that they bent the straw double; the barley on the combe was like a golden sea that rippled under the south wind from hedge to hedge; and the apple-trees in the High Barn orchards were so loaded with fruit that Julian had to prop the boughs with ash-poles to keep them from splitting to the ground.

On the tenth of September, the call came that completed the work of Martha’s life.

It was Martin Vane who came up the track on his pony, his straw hat in his hand, his blue eyes dancing with joy:

"Martha! Martha! Come down to the mill! Rachel... Rachel is taken!"

Martha did not hurry. 

She knew that the ship was in good hands. She packed her basket with that slow, deliberate, beautiful care that she had practiced for five-and-twenty years; she pinned her grey cloak at her throat; and she walked down the hill in the golden afternoon sunshine.

The delivery at the Black Mill was as natural and sweet as the turning of the great water-wheel that rumbled softly in the race outside.

At six o'clock in the evening, while the sun was setting behind the Mendip hills and the wild ducks were flighting over the peat-cuts, Rachel was delivered of her first living child.

It was a boy—sound, strong, and beautiful, with his father’s blue eyes and his mother’s dark hair.

When Martha laid the infant in Rachel’s arms, the young mother looked up into the midwife’s face with a look that had no words—a look that remembered the little maid that had died in August, the drover’s basket, the marsh-fever, and the long, faithful years of their common watch.

"What will you name him, Rachel?" Martha asked softly.

Rachel kissed the boy’s forehead, then reached out her hand and took her husband’s fingers in hers.

"We will name him Gervase," Rachel said. "Gervase Vane. After the old master who gave his life for the truth."

Martin Vane knelt by the bed, his rough, scarred face buried in his wife’s pillow, weeping with that deep, holy joy that belongs to men who have come home from the deep waters to find their haven in peace.

Martha walked out onto the wooden balcony that overlooked the mill-pond.

The evening was falling over the levels, mild, still, and fragrant with the scent of the mint and the sweet-sedge.

The water in the race was clear as crystal, reflecting the purple and gold of the western clouds; and from the cottage chimney, a thin plume of blue smoke was rising into the twilight.

Behind her, in the quiet room, she heard the sweet, soft, rhythmic sound of the child nursing at his mother’s breast—the sound that is the music of the earth, older than the laws of parliaments, stronger than the fury of kings, and eternal as the stars.

### Chapter 40

On a Sunday evening in the summer of 1802, Martha Garrow walked for the last time the circuit of the three parishes.

She was forty-eight years old.

She had retired from the active ministry of the lying-in chamber six months before, surrendering her tin cylinder and her calfskin register into the hands of Rachel Vane, who now rode her stout grey cob along the lanes with Martin Vane’s little apprentice boy running beside the stirrup.

Martha carried only her ash walking-stick and her mother’s Prayer Book.

She walked slowly, for her knees were stiff with forty years of ditch-roads, but her heart was light and her spirit was at rest.

She went first to High Duntish.

She passed the smithy, where John Vowles was sitting on the horse-block with his grandchildren about his knees; she passed the Pool Farm, where Phoebe Yeo was gathering late roses in her garden with her little maid running beside her; and she stood for a moment by the churchyard wall, looking at the stone cross where she had waited in the snow on the night of the trial.

Then she took the field-path down the combe to Nether-Coombe.

She passed the Court House, where the young Captain’s children were playing upon the gravel drive beneath the beech trees; she passed the Free School, where the twenty little desks stood clean and orderly in the Sunday quiet; and she walked through the lych-gate into the churchyard.

She stopped beneath the great yew tree.

There lay the long, grey Hamstone ledger that covered the bones of Gervase and Mary Finch:

*HERE LYETH THE BODY OF GERVASE FINCH, YEOMAN,*  
*OF HIGH BARN IN THIS PARISH,*  
*WHO DEPARTED THIS LIFE OCT. 24, 1795, AGED 57 YEARS.*  
*ALSO MARY HIS WIFE, WHO DIED MAY 12, 1798, AGED 54 YEARS.*  
*THE MEMORY OF THE JUST IS BLESSED.*

Martha laid her hand upon the rough, mossy stone.

The sun was sinking behind the western ridge, throwing long, purple shadows across the grass, turning the lichen upon the church tower to burning gold.

She could hear the sounds of the evening in the village: the cattle lowing as they came home to the milking-stalls; the laughter of the children playing upon the green; the distant clink of a bucket at the well; and the sweet, solemn chime of the church bells ringing for the evening prayer.

Julian Finch came down the churchyard path, dressed in his Sunday coat of dark blue cloth, leading Martin by the hand.

The boy was ten years old now: tall, straight as an ash-plant, his dark eyes fearless and intelligent, his face showing the noble, honest lines of the yeomen of Somerset.

Behind them walked Eleanor, with little Martha and little Gervase holding her skirts, her face calm, beautiful, and full of peace.

Julian stopped by his father’s tomb.

He looked at Martha, then looked down at the stone ledger, taking off his hat in the quiet air.

"We came to meet you, Martha," Julian said. "The table is laid at High Barn, and the children would not sit down to their supper without you."

Martha looked at the young yeoman, at the wife he had taken from the dairy-room, and at the children who carried his name.

"I will come, Julian," she said.

Martin stepped forward. The boy took the midwife’s hand—the rough, scarred hand that had received his first breath from the dark—and drew it through his arm with that gentle, protective chivalry that was his natural birthright.

"I will help you up the combe, Martha," the boy said. "The ruts are deep by the smithy, but I know where the dry stones are."

"You know the way, Martin," she said, looking down into his clear eyes. "Your father knew it before you, and his father before him."

Together, the family and the midwife walked up the long, gentle slope of the combe toward the lights of High Barn.

The twilight was deepening into that sweet, cool, fragrant summer night that brings rest to the weary fields of England.

The sheep were quiet upon the downs; the water was running clear and peaceful over the mill-wheel at the Black Pool; and from the chimney of the great house, the blue woodsmoke was rising straight toward the first white stars of the evening.

Martha looked up at the sky.

The long watch was over. 

The three villages were safe; the mother was honored; the father’s house was whole; the child was free; and the truth had triumphed over the dark.

She leaned upon the boy’s strong young arm, turned her face toward the open door of the homestead, and walked into the warmth of the home she had saved.

# The Memory of Nitrogen

### Chapter 1

The laser head did not make a sound when it burned through three hundred gigabytes of wedding photography from the summer of 2038.

There was only the dry, insectoid click of the positioning solenoid—a sound no louder than a fingernail tapping against an acrylic watch crystal—and then the amber cursor on Tamar Lind’s monitor blinked twice, stepped to the next logical sector, and began recalculating the remaining volume on Plate 4,091.

`SECTOR 09-OMEGA: CLEARED.`
`BYTES RECOVERED: 3.12 x 10^11.`
`VERIFICATION HASH: NULL.`
`RECOVERY PROBABILITY: 0.0000%.`

Tamar took a breath. The air inside the Archival Well always tasted of two things: chilled silicone grease and the faint, sweet, alkaline tang of recirculated ammonium carbonate. 

The Well was located along the dead centerline of the *Eidolon*, where the ship did not spin. Out on the agricultural rims—Ring A and Ring B, where twelve hundred people grew hydroponic soy, repaired wastewater pumps, and slept beneath LED light strips programmed to mimic the dawn over the Bay of Biscay—the centripetal acceleration was three-quarters of a standard Earth gravity. There, coffee sloshed against the rim of a mug; there, a dropped spanner hit the rubberized decking with a sharp, heavy clang that echoed through the ventilation shafts.

Here, at zero radius, there was no weight. 

Tamar was tethered to the operator’s saddle by two wide straps of faded olive nylon that buckled across her pelvis and lower ribs. If she unlatched them, she would drift upward until her head bumped the insulated liquid-helium lines that cooled the tertiary solid-state bays to four Kelvin. Her boots, soft-soled slippers with Velcro patches on the heels, rested against an aluminum kick-plate that had been polished down to the bare, bright metal by the heels of three previous archivists, all of whom were now compost in the aeration troughs of Ring A.

She was twenty-two years old. 

She had lived on Earth for eleven years, and she had lived aboard the *Eidolon* for eleven years. Her life was split into two precisely equal halves by the launch epoch: four thousand and seventeen days of sky, dirt, diesel exhaust, and cold sea spray on the pebble beach at Brighton; and four thousand and seventeen days of welded titanium, recycled sweat, sixty-cycle electrical hum, and the dead, velvet blackness that pressed against the hull from the other side of four inches of aerogel insulation.

Her title on the ship’s manifest was Junior Custodian, Third Class (Data Retention and Information Hygiene). 

Her father had been an electrical engineer who died of pericardial fibrosis during the crossing of the Martian orbital plane in Year Three. Her mother was an agronomist who currently spent nine hours a day in the humid, sulfurous heat of the fungal digestion vats in Ring B, her forearms covered in brown contact rashes from the enzyme baths. 

Tamar had been chosen for the Archives when she was sixteen because her finger tremor was less than three microns on the micrometric test rig, and because her psychological evaluations showed an \"unusually elevated tolerance for repetitive non-social task vectors.\"

The psychologist had meant that she did not get bored watching numbers disappear.

\"Batch 44-Kilo is seventy percent complete,\" she said into her collar microphone.

The circuit took two seconds to route through the central bus to the supervisory office three decks outward. The response came back with the flat, clipped cadence of Master Archivist Gordon Vane:

\"Push the threshold, Lind. The telemetry buffer on the reactor diagnostic array is spilling over into the civilian memory allocation. If we don't clear twelve terabytes before the eighteen-hundred shift change, the automated scrub will start dumping unindexed system logs from the primary coolant loop. We cannot afford another blind spot in the thermal history.\"

\"Understood,\" Tamar said. \"I’m working through the personal media partition from Launch Block Six.\"

\"Are you reading them?\"

Tamar’s fingers hovered above the trackball. 

\"No, sir,\" she lied. \"I’m running the automated criteria filter. Resolution down-sampling first, then raw video streams without biometric cross-tags, then redundant audio tracks.\"

\"Don't linger, Lind. The dead don't care about their picnics.\"

The line clicked dead.

Tamar looked back at the center display.

On the screen was an image she had paused two seconds before the burn routine engaged. It was not high-definition; to save cache space, the preview window only rendered every eighth pixel, giving the scene a soft, cross-hatched texture like a painting on rough linen.

A woman in a yellow linen dress was standing on a wooden pier that jutted into a body of water so wide that the far shore was lost in a blue, summer haze. She was laughing, her head thrown back, one hand holding a wide-brimmed straw hat against her chest to keep the wind from carrying it away. In the background, two children in red life jackets were throwing pieces of bread to a flock of white birds that hovered in the air like flakes of ash.

The file was labeled: `2038-07-14_BALATON_FAMILY_0411.MP4`.

The metadata header showed the owner was a man named Henrik Varga, who had occupied Bunk 114 in Dormitory Module C before he was crushed by a runaway pallet of lithium-hydride canisters during the Year Seven deceleration trials. He had no surviving kin on the ship. The registry listed his lineage as *Extinct / Non-Represented*.

Under the ship’s Storage Rationalization Accord—ratified by the Provisional Council during the dark months after the reactor flare of Year Two—any personal media belonging to an extinct lineage that had not been accessed for more than one thousand standard days was classified as *Category Four Ephemera*.

Category Four was burned first.

Tamar looked at the woman’s yellow dress. She looked at the water—actual water, billions of gallons of it, uncontained by pressure bulkheads, un-aerated by peristaltic injectors, lying open to the sky like a giant, shining mirror. She wondered what the wind had smelled like on that pier. The air in the archive smelled of ammonia and cold solder; the air in the mess hall smelled of boiled cabbage and yeast paste; the air in the sleeping quarters smelled of dirty wool and the vinegar stench of dried human sebum.

Did water that large smell like the hydroponic reservoirs? Did it smell of green rot and chlorine?

She reached out with her thumb and pressed `Enter`.

The laser head in the optical chassis three feet below her saddle gave another dry, microscopic *snick*. The woman in the yellow dress broke into a pattern of vertical green scan lines, melted into a smear of purple chroma artifacts, and then vanished.

`SECTOR 09-OMEGA-412: CLEARED.`
`BYTES RECOVERED: 4.88 x 10^10.`

The ship’s storage capacity was fixed at forty-eight petabytes. 

When the *Eidolon* had slipped its moorings at the Lagrange-1 assembly platform in the spring of 2035, forty-eight petabytes had seemed infinite. It was enough to hold the complete digitized holdings of the Library of Congress, the French National Archives, the genomic blueprints of four hundred thousand terrestrial species, twenty-four centuries of musical notation, and the personal communications, diaries, home movies, and medical records of the sixteen hundred men and women who had boarded the vessel to spend the rest of their lives falling through the dark between Sol and the star system designated Gliese 667C.

It was not infinite.

Silicon-germanium memory lattices did not survive the cosmic ray flux of deep space unchanged. Outside the heliosphere, where the solar wind no longer deflected the relativistic heavy ions from galactic supernovae, the ship was constantly pierced by an invisible, microscopic hail of high-energy protons and iron nuclei.

The hull armor—two meters of water-filled polyethylene cells and basalt foam—absorbed ninety-nine percent of the radiation. But the one percent that punched through the shielding struck the crystal lattices of the storage blocks like high-velocity rifle rounds striking a glass greenhouse.

Every day, four or five thousand memory cells were permanently ionized. Their gates locked into the conductive state; their oxide barriers broke down; their error-correcting codes threw unrecoverable parity faults.

The archive was shrinking.

In Year One, the ship had forty-eight petabytes.

By Year Six, it had forty-four.

Today, on the fourteenth of October, Year Eleven, the system reported the total operational storage pool as thirty-eight point two petabytes. And of that, thirty-two petabytes were locked by the ship’s primary flight systems: the nav-integrator, the reactor plasma telemetry, the environmental feedback loops, and the three-dimensional scans of every titanium weld and structural rib along the ship’s two-kilometer length.

The living needed the memory to survive the day. The dead occupied space that could not be spared.

Tamar shifted her weight in the saddle. Her tailbone ached from the nylon webbing. She reached into the pocket of her work smock, pulled out a strip of dried soybean jerky that had been flavored with synthetic smoke and salt, and chewed on it slowly, letting the saliva soften the rubbery protein.

She clicked the trackball to advance to the next partition: `SYS-ENV-HISTORICAL-2035-2038`.

She frowned.

\"Historical environmental logs?\" she muttered to herself.

Her monitor did not usually pull system files during a personal ephemera pass. System files had their own pruning pipelines, supervised directly by the Engineering Directorate and the Council’s Secretariat.

The file sat in the scratch buffer, waiting for her confirmation token.

`RECORD ID: LSS-SCHED-REV-11-FINAL.DAT`
`ORIGIN: ENVIRONMENTAL OVERSIGHT / ADVISORY SUB-COMMITTEE`
`DATE STAMP: 2042-03-03 (VOYAGE YEAR SEVEN)`
`SIZE: 1.44 MEGABYTES`
`PERMISSIONS: LEVEL 2 ARCHIVAL CULLING / UNENCRYPTED`

One point four four megabytes. A speck of dust in the ocean. It was smaller than a single frame of the woman on the pier at Lake Balaton.

Tamar hovered the cursor over the file name. The protocol was simple: check the hash, confirm there was no active pointer in the system registry, verify that the date fell outside the seven-year operational retention window, and press `F8` to commit the write-erase cycle.

She double-clicked the file to view the text header.

The screen did not display the header.

Instead, the amber text vanished. The terminal’s cooling fan, which had been humming at a steady, low four thousand RPM, suddenly whined as it dropped to a dead idle. 

In the silence of the Well, Tamar heard the liquid helium gurgling through the return line above her head—a wet, metallic sound like a small animal drinking from a tin bowl.

A single line of text appeared in the center of the dark glass:

`FILE EXECUTION ERROR: SCRIP-LOCK TRIGGERED.`
`TARGET SECTOR 04-BETA WILL BE OVERWRITTEN WITH ZEROES IN 05 SECONDS.`

\"What?\" Tamar whispered.

She lunged forward, her fingers striking the cancel key: `Ctrl + Break`.

`04 SECONDS.`

`ESC. ESC. F12. BREAK.`

The keyboard did not respond. The terminal had locked its input buffer.

`03 SECONDS.`

Down in the optical stack, the laser did not click. It let out a continuous, high-pitched, harmonic squeal that vibrated through the frame of her saddle, through her tailbone, and up into the base of her skull. The laser wasn't pulsing to read or selectively alter pits on the phase-change alloy; it was firing at maximum drive current, dumping forty watts of ultraviolet light into a single spot on the spinning glass disk, boiling the reflective tellurium layer into an invisible plume of metallic vapor.

`02 SECONDS.`

`01 SECOND.`

`SECTOR 04-BETA CLEARED.`
`CHECKSUM: DESTROYED.`

Tamar stared at the empty screen, her heart hammering against her ribs with a sudden, sharp violence that made her breath hitch in her throat.

A self-purging file.

In five years in the archives, she had never seen a self-purging file. The system didn't have self-purging files. Storage was too precious; write cycles were rationed like drinking water. Every byte erased required a recorded authorization token from an archivist and a cryptographic confirmation from the main frame’s audit loop.

Then the monitor flickered.

A block of raw, white monospace characters dropped into the volatile display register—not loaded from the disk, but held in the monitor’s local video RAM, running on the residual charge of the display controller’s decoupling capacitors.

The first line read:

`TAMAR.`

She froze.

Her hands hung in the air above the plastic keys, the knuckles white, the skin translucent under the cold fluorescent tubes.

The text was short. It did not fill twenty lines.

`Tamar.`
`If you are reading this, the culling queue has finally reached the Year Seven administrative buffers, and the laser has done what I couldn't do with my own hands.`
`Do not run a recovery pass on Sector 04. There is nothing left but melted glass, and if the supervisor flags a read failure on that drive, the Council’s watchdog script will purge your workstation’s local flash before you can unbuckle your straps.`
`Look at the scrubber replacement schedule for Year Fourteen.`
`Not the public schedule posted on the commissary boards. Not the one that says the zeolite beds in Sector C are rated for forty-two years.`
`Look at the raw chemical synthesis logs for the amine regeneration towers from the third of March, Year Seven. The vote was four to one. Helen Mercer signed the order. They voted not to record the ruthenium poisoning in the permanent engineering ledger.`
`The air isn't going to make forty years, Tamar. It isn't going to make twenty.`
`They are already thinning the mix. They tell the nurseries that the children in Cohort Two are smaller because of the low-g bone remodeling. It’s a lie. They are starving them of nitrogen and oxygen to keep the partial pressure from collapsing before we pass the halfway point.`
`The next cohort is due for conception licensing in November.`
`Ask Gordon why the nursery expansion in Ring B was canceled.`
`Do not save this. It is already fading.`

Tamar did not move.

She did not breathe.

The white characters did not wait for her. On the screen, the phosphor began to decay. Because the text was held only in the volatile frame buffer without a refresh signal from the bus, the electrons bled away into the ground plane.

First the letters turned a soft, ghostly gray.

Then the lines warped, twisting into thin, hair-like curls of green light.

The name *Tamar* hung for one more second—a pale, violet smudge on the glass—and then the screen was black again, reflecting only the twin pinpoints of the overhead LED tubes and the wide, frightened pupils of her own eyes.

`PROMPT: ENTER NEXT SECTOR FOR PRUNING.`
`CURRENT STORAGE POOL: 38,199,412,881,004 BYTES.`
`SYSTEM STATUS: NOMINAL.`

The Well was completely silent.

### Chapter 2

The journey from the zero-g core of the *Eidolon* down to the living decks of Ring B took fourteen minutes if you used the service ladders, or six minutes if you took the counter-rotating elevator car in Transit Tube Four.

Tamar took the ladder.

She unclipped herself from the operator’s saddle, checked the seal on her work smock, and pulled herself through the circular hatchway that led into Spoke Two. 

Here, three hundred meters from the ship’s rotational axis, the Coriolis force was a physical hand that pushed sideways against your shoulders with every step. When you reached for a rung on the aluminum ladder, your hand didn't move in a straight line; it curved toward the trailing bulkhead, drawn by the phantom acceleration of the ship’s fifteen-revolution-per-hour spin.

With every twenty meters she descended, weight returned to her limbs.

At first it was a gentle, buoyant sensation—the feeling of sinking into a warm, thick bath where your feet barely touched the bottom. Then her smock began to hang against her collarbones. Her hair, which had been floating in a loose, static halo around her cheeks, settled down against her neck, damp with perspiration. By the time she reached the hundred-meter landing, her knees had to bend to take her sixty kilograms of mass, and her boots gripped the aluminum rungs with a heavy, solid *clack-clack-clack*.

She stopped at the airlock for Ring B, Section Seven.

The airlock was painted an industrial yellow that had oxidized over eleven years into the color of dried mustard. Above the pressure hatch, a stencil in black block letters announced:

`HABITATION RING B - SECTOR SEVEN`
`LIFE SUPPORT PROTOCOL: LEVEL THREE STABLE`
`ATMOSPHERIC PRESSURE: 78.4 KPA`
`O2 FRACTION: 18.2% | N2 FRACTION: 80.1% | CO2: 480 PPM`
`REPORT ALL TASTE OR ODOR ANOMALIES TO ENVIRONMENTAL IMMEDIATELY`

Tamar stopped. Her hand was on the brass rotary latch of the hatch.

She looked at the numbers.

She had walked past that yellow sign twice a day for five years, ever since she had been assigned her bunk in the junior staff quarters of Section Seven. The numbers were stamped on a sheet of magnetic vinyl that had been slapped over the original factory specifications.

Beneath the corner of the vinyl sheet, where the adhesive had dried and curled back from the steel frame, she could see a sliver of the original painted lettering from the shipyards at Long Beach:

`ATMOSPHERIC PRESSURE: 101.3 KPA`
`O2 FRACTION: 20.9%`

Seventy-eight point four kilopascals.

On Earth, seventy-eight kilopascals was the pressure at an altitude of two thousand two hundred meters above sea level. It was the air on the high slopes of the Mexican plateau, or the summer pastures of the Swiss Alps. 

People lived at two thousand meters. They herded goats; they built stone villages; they had children. 

When the ship had lowered the pressure during Year Three, the Chief Medical Officer, Dr. Aris Thorne, had spoken to the entire ship over the public address system. Tamar remembered sitting on the floor of the mess hall with her mother, eating a bowl of reconstituted lentil stew, while Dr. Thorne’s voice—deep, calm, carrying the smooth, comforting vowels of an English university town—explained that the reduction in pressure was an engineering measure designed to reduce the structural fatigue on the ring seals.

*\"A lower pressure differential between the cabin and the vacuum,\"* Thorne had said, *\"means less stress on our elastomer gaskets. It means less leakage through the micrometeoroid patches. And eighteen percent oxygen at seventy-eight kilopascals provides an alveolar oxygen tension that is entirely within the human adaptive envelope. You may feel a slight shortness of breath when climbing the ladders for the first two weeks. After that, your bone marrow will produce additional erythrocytes. Your hematocrit will rise. You will adapt. Our bodies are remarkably resilient machines, my friends.\"*

And they had adapted.

Her mother’s cheeks had turned a dark, ruddy red as her blood thickened with extra red blood cells. The people in the corridors walked a little slower; they stopped at the landings to catch their breath; they didn't run down the long, curving vistas of Ring B unless there was an alarm in the irrigation bays.

Tamar turned the rotary latch.

The dogs on the hatch broke with a heavy, sucking hiss as the pressure equalized across the two-millimeter gap. She pulled the hatch open and stepped through onto the ribbed rubber floor of the corridor.

The noise of the living ship hit her like a physical blow.

In the Archival Well, there was only the hum of the cryostat and the click of the laser. Here, the world was filled with the continuous, multi-layered roar of twelve hundred people trying to stay alive inside an aluminum pipe seventy meters in diameter.

From beneath the deck plates came the deep, intestinal thrum of the main gray-water return lines, carrying twenty tons of filtered wastewater back to the treatment centrifuges in the hull. From the ceiling ducts came the whistling rush of the air blowers, moving four thousand cubic meters of gas per hour through the dust filters and the charcoal canisters.

And through it all ran the sounds of human domesticity: a child crying in one of the family modules down the hall; the rhythmic, tinny clatter of aluminum pans from the communal kitchen in Section Eight; the low, drone-like chant of an educational broadcast coming from the nursery schoolroom thirty meters away.

Tamar walked down the corridor.

The curvature of Ring B was sharp enough that you could only see about forty meters ahead before the floor seemed to rise up like the inside of a giant barrel, disappearing into the ceiling fixtures. The bulkheads were lined with gray plastic conduits carrying power cables, fiber-optic bundles, and compressed air lines, all held in place by aluminum brackets stamped with the crest of the European Space Consortium and the United States Aerospace Authority—two organizations that, as far as Tamar knew, might not even exist anymore.

Earth was eleven light-years behind them.

The last high-gain radio transmission from the tracking array at Goldstone had arrived four years ago, when the *Eidolon* was passing the outer fringe of the Kuiper Belt. It had been an automated weather summary, three compressed files of patent renewals, and an audio message from the President of the European Council congratulating the crew on achieving the fastest human transit past the orbit of Pluto.

Then the transmitter at Goldstone had gone silent.

For six months, the communication officers had listened with the twelve-meter dish on the ship’s nose, pointing the receiver back toward the yellow speck of Sol. They had heard only the hiss of Jupiter’s radiation belts, the crackle of interstellar dust striking the reflector, and the cold, random static of the cosmic microwave background.

Some people said there had been another war over the lithium extraction fields in the Bolivian Altiplano. Others said the ground stations had simply been decommissioned because the economies of Earth could no longer justify the millions of megawatts required to shout into the void after a ship that would never come back.

It didn't matter.

Whether Earth was burning or simply bored, the *Eidolon* was alone. There was no rescue craft coming up from behind; there was no supply ship carrying fresh zeolite pellets or replacement laser diodes.

Tamar stopped outside the entrance to the Section Seven nursery.

The door was an open archway covered by a green vinyl curtain that had been slit into vertical strips to allow air to circulate while keeping out the grease fumes from the kitchen.

She stood to the side, pressing her back against the cool plastic of the conduit trunking, and parted two of the vinyl strips with her fingers.

Inside, fourteen children were sitting on a blue foam mat.

They were the First Cohort—the children conceived during the first three years of the flight, born into the spinning rings when the ship still smelled of fresh epoxy and new paint. The oldest was nine; the youngest was seven.

They were listening to Sarah Vance, the schoolmistress. Sarah was forty-five, a thin, angular woman from Newcastle whose husband had been one of the structural welders on the main engine bells. She was holding a large picture book printed on stiff, laminated card stock—one of the few physical books that had been brought aboard in the cultural crates.

The page showed a painting of an elephant.

\"An elephant,\" Sarah was saying, her voice clear and patient, \"is much larger than a cow. It had four legs, like a dog, but its nose was very long, and it used its nose to pick up branches and put them into its mouth. And look at the ears. Why do you think it had such large ears?\"

A little boy named Aaron raised his hand.

Aaron was eight years old. He had been born in Bunk Module 42. Tamar had known his mother, a girl named Marta who had worked in the laundry before she was reassigned to the protein synthesis tanks.

Aaron’s hand was tiny. 

Tamar looked at his fingers. The tips were rounded, flattened like the heads of small spoons—a distinct, bulbous swelling of the soft tissue beneath the nail beds. 

Clubbing.

In the archives, Tamar had scanned sixty thousand medical textbooks from the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. She had spent two weeks in Year Nine culling redundant pediatric pathology manuals from the Johns Hopkins digital collection.

She knew what clubbed fingers meant.

It was not a genetic variation. It was not \"low-g bone remodeling.\"

Digital clubbing occurred when the peripheral tissues suffered from chronic, long-term arterial hypoxemia. When the blood did not carry enough oxygen to the fingertips, the megakaryocytes and platelet clumps lodged in the microvasculature of the nail beds, releasing platelet-derived growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor, causing the periosteal tissue to thicken, the nail to curve like the glass of a watch, and the skin to turn a dull, slate-blue around the cuticles.

Aaron spoke. His voice was thin, reedy, lacking the resonant, bell-like clarity of the children Tamar remembered from her schoolyard in Brighton.

\"It had big ears to hear things?\" he asked.

\"To keep cool, Aaron,\" Sarah said gently. \"Because on Earth, the sun was very bright, and there were no cooling ducts in the sky. The air was very hot, and the elephant pumped its blood through its ears so the breeze could cool it down.\"

Aaron nodded, but his attention was already wandering.

He rested his chin on his knees. His lips were parted, his chest rising and falling with a rapid, shallow rhythm—thirty-five breaths a minute. A healthy child at rest should have been breathing twenty.

Beside him, a little girl named Maya was leaning against the wall, her eyes half-closed, her skin pale with an odd, grayish undertone that looked like skimmed milk that had been left in a lead bucket. She had a bruise on her forearm—a purple, yellowish smudge the size of a coin that had not healed in the three weeks since Tamar had seen her drop her slate in the hallway.

*They are starving them of nitrogen and oxygen to keep the partial pressure from collapsing before we pass the halfway point.*

The words from the volatile register echoed in Tamar’s ears, as loud and distinct as if someone were standing behind her, whispering into the nape of her neck.

She looked at Sarah Vance.

Sarah’s hands were steady as she turned the page of the picture book, but her face was gaunt. Beneath her eyes, the skin was dark, stained like walnut husk, the skin stretched tight over the sharp ridges of her cheekbones. She had lived on Earth for thirty-four years. Her lungs had matured in sea-level air; her thoracic cage was broad, her diaphragm strong.

She was surviving on the eighteen percent mix because her body had twenty years of structural capital to draw upon.

These children had nothing.

They had been formed in an atmosphere that was thin, dry, and poisoned with trace gases that the failing scrubbers could no longer strip from the circulation. Their alveoli had never opened against one hundred kilopascals of pressure; their capillaries were proliferating in their nail beds and their retinas, desperately hunting for molecules of oxygen that were not there.

\"Tamar?\"

Tamar let the vinyl strips fall back into place.

She turned.

Standing behind her was Jonas Cole.

Jonas was twenty-four. He was a mechanical technician from Section Nine, his overalls stained with hydraulic oil and the green, slimy residue of the spirulina ponds. He carried an adjustable wrench in a leather holster on his hip and a portable gas detector clipped to his collar—a small, square gray box with a digital readout that blinked green every five seconds.

He was looking at her with his head tilted, his dark eyebrows pulled together in a question.

\"What are you doing down here?\" he asked. \"I thought you had the second-shift culling in the Well.\"

Tamar pulled her hands into the pockets of her work smock. Her fingers were trembling. She clamped them against her thighs, feeling the rough fabric through her trousers.

\"I finished Batch 44 early,\" she said.

Jonas looked at the nursery curtain, then back at her face. He stepped closer, his voice dropping below the roar of the ventilation blowers.

\"You look like you saw an atmospheric dump in the core, Tamar. Your skin is white as chalk.\"

\"I’m fine,\" she said.

\"Did Gordon give you more grief about the quota?\"

\"No.\" She looked at the gas detector on his collar. The LCD display read: `O2: 18.1% | CO: 4 PPM | VOC: 12 PPM`.

\"Jonas,\" she said, her voice dropping so low he had to lean his shoulder down to hear her. \"Where do the maintenance logs for the amine towers go?\"

Jonas blinked. \"The amine towers? In the scrubber core?\"

\"Yes.\"

\"They go to Environmental Central, like everything else. Then they get batched and sent up to your shop for permanent archiving every quarter.\"

\"Have you ever worked on them?\"

Jonas laughed—a short, dry bark that had no humor in it. He reached up and scratched the back of his neck, where the collar of his greasy jumpsuit had rubbed the skin raw.

\"Nobody works on the amine towers, Tamar. You don't 'work' on them. You don't touch them unless the main loop trips on high differential pressure. Those towers are four stories of stainless-steel packing rings drenched in boiling monoethanolamine. If you open an inspection port, the fumes will melt the cornea right off your eyeball before you can get your mask sealed.\"

\"When was the last time the packing was replaced?\"

Jonas stared at her. His smile died completely.

The green light on his gas detector blinked: *beep... beep... beep.*

\"What is this?\" he asked.

\"I’m asking a question, Jonas. You’re on the mechanical roster for the core. When did the ship last unpack Tower Three?\"

\"Never,\" Jonas said, his voice flat. \"We don't have replacement packing, Tamar. We don't have the stainless alloy to turn new Pall rings, and we don't have the chemical synthesis capacity to brew twenty thousand liters of fresh amine. We regenerate the beds by baking them out with steam from the secondary reactor loop every six months. You know that. It’s on the public work schedule.\"

\"And the ruthenium?\"

Jonas’s body went completely rigid.

The sound of the ventilation fans seemed to drop away, replaced by the heavy, rhythmic thrumming of the deck plates under their boots. A man in a blue maintenance smock walked past them, pushing a two-wheeled cart loaded with empty plastic water jugs, his eyes downcast, his shoulders hunched.

Jonas didn't say a word until the man had turned the corner into Section Eight.

He reached out, took Tamar by the elbow with his grease-stained hand, and pulled her three paces down the corridor, into the narrow, unlit recess where the fire suppression valves were mounted behind an emergency break-glass panel.

His grip was hard enough to leave a mark through her sleeve.

\"Where did you hear that word?\" he hissed.

### Chapter 3

The air in the fire suppression recess smelled of old dry-chemical powder and stale sweat. 

Tamar looked down at Jonas’s fingers where they gripped her arm. His thumbnail was split down the center, held together by a thin wrap of electrical tape that had curled and yellowed at the edges.

\"I saw a file,\" she said.

\"Files are your job,\" Jonas whispered, his eyes darting toward the main corridor. \"You look at them, you count the bytes, you press the button, and you turn them into heat. That’s what Gordon pays you for. You don't read them.\"

\"The file deleted itself, Jonas.\"

He stopped. His hand loosened slightly on her sleeve, but he didn't let go.

\"What do you mean, it deleted itself?\"

\"I loaded the header into the scratch buffer. It ran a self-executing scrub script. It fired the laser at maximum current and burned the tellurium right off the glass in Sector 04.\"

Jonas stared at her. His jaw was slack, his mouth slightly open, revealing his bottom teeth—two of which were crowded and crooked from an old fracture when he was twelve.

\"That’s impossible,\" he said. \"The firmware on the optical drives has a hardware interlock on the laser drive current. It’s hardwired into the controller board. You can't overdrive the diode without a supervisory shunt.\"

\"There was a note,\" Tamar said.

\"A note?\"

\"In the volatile video register. After the sector cleared. It was addressed to me.\"

Jonas didn't speak for four seconds. 

He slowly pulled his hand back from her arm, wiped his palm against the greasy denim of his thigh, and looked up at the ceiling where the aluminum conduit pipes disappeared into the darkness between the decks.

\"What did it say?\" he asked. His voice was barely a breath.

\"It said the ruthenium was poisoned. It said Helen Mercer and the Council voted on the third of March in Year Seven to suppress the engineering report. It said the air won't last twenty years.\"

Jonas closed his eyes.

He leaned his head back against the cold steel of the bulkhead, letting out a long, shuddering sigh that whistled through his nostrils. He didn't look surprised. He didn't look shocked.

He looked like a man who had been carrying a seventy-pound bag of wet fertilizer across a ploughed field, and who had just been told that the field had no boundary fence, that he was going to have to keep walking until his heart burst in his chest.

\"It’s not just the ruthenium,\" Jonas whispered.

Tamar felt the cold grease in her stomach turn over. \"What?\"

\"The ruthenium catalyst is for the Sabatier reactors,\" Jonas said, his eyes still closed. \"You know how the loop works, Tamar? Or do they just teach you how to throw away photographs up in the Well?\"

\"I know how it works,\" she said, her voice rising slightly before she caught herself and dropped it back to a whisper. \"The humans breathe oxygen and exhale carbon dioxide. The amine towers absorb the carbon dioxide from the air stream. We boil the amine with steam to release the pure CO2 gas. Then we mix the CO2 with hydrogen from the water electrolysis cells and run it over a ruthenium catalyst at four hundred degrees Celsius. The Sabatier reaction produces methane and water. We vent the methane into space, and we electrolyze the water to get the oxygen back.\"

\"Right,\" Jonas said. He opened his eyes. They were bloodshot, the whites yellowed with the chronic bilirubin buildup that affected everyone whose liver was slowly failing on the ship’s recycled water. \"That’s the diagram in the schoolbooks. That’s the clean little circle they show the children. One hundred percent recovery. A closed loop. A miracle of twenty-first-century aerospace engineering.\"

He reached out and tapped the digital gas detector on his collar.

\"The Sabatier reactors don't use pure hydrogen and pure carbon dioxide,\" he said. \"They use what comes out of the amine towers. And what comes out of the amine towers carries everything that twelve hundred people outgas into an enclosed volume for eleven years.\"

\"What things?\"

\"Siloxanes from the soap,\" Jonas said, ticking them off on his grease-blackened fingers. \"Plasticizers from the cable insulation. Freon from the cryo-chillers that leak half a gram every week. Sulfur compounds from the fungal vats. Trace carbon monoxide from the electrical motors.\"

He leaned closer to her face. She could smell the stale soy paste on his breath, the metallic odor of his sweat.

\"The ruthenium catalyst doesn't just sit there forever, Tamar. The sulfur binds to the active metal sites. The siloxanes coat the alumina pellets in a glaze of silicon dioxide—glass. Real glass, five molecules thick. Once a site is glazed, the reaction stops. You can't burn it off. You can't wash it off with acid. The only way to restore the bed is to unpack the reactor, dissolve the pellets in boiling aqua regia, separate the ruthenium with ion-exchange chromatography, and re-deposit it on fresh alumina substrates.\"

\"Can we do that?\"

Jonas gave that short, dry bark again.

\"With what? We don't have nitric acid. We have eight liters of concentrated hydrochloric acid left in the entire chemical inventory, and that’s reserved for de-scaling the primary heat exchangers in the reactor loop. If we use it on the Sabatier beds, the heat exchangers scale up with calcium carbonate in nine months, the reactor scrams, and we freeze to death in forty-eight hours.\"

\"How bad is it?\" she asked.

\"Two years ago,\" Jonas said, \"Reactor One was operating at eighty-eight percent efficiency. Reactor Two was at seventy-four. Reactor Three was already dead—they cannibalized its catalyst bed to top off One and Two during the overhaul in Year Nine.\"

\"And now?\"

\"Reactor Two tripped on low conversion efficiency three weeks ago,\" he said. \"It’s producing more carbon monoxide than water. If we run it, we’re pumping poison back into the air blowers. So Keller shut it down. We’re running the entire ship on Reactor One.\"

Tamar’s mind raced through the numbers. 

Twelve hundred people. Each person consumed roughly five hundred and fifty liters of pure oxygen every twenty-four hours. That was six hundred and sixty thousand liters of oxygen a day. More than nine hundred kilograms of O2.

To produce nine hundred kilograms of oxygen from water, you needed over a ton of water per day.

If the Sabatier loop wasn't closing—if the water wasn't being recovered from the exhaled carbon dioxide—the ship was consuming its emergency reserves.

\"The water tanks,\" she said.

\"The water tanks in the spine,\" Jonas nodded. \"The ones that are supposed to act as our storm shelter during a solar particle event. They’ve dropped thirty-two percent since the beginning of Year Ten.\"

\"Why hasn't anyone said anything?\"

\"Because what are they going to say?\" Jonas’s voice cracked. \"*Attention, citizens of the Eidolon: our math was wrong. The engineers who built this ship in California thirty years ago used laboratory rates for catalyst degradation instead of real-world multi-compound poisoning. In nine years, the water will be gone, and then the air will be gone, and there is no planet within four light-years.* Would you like that on the commissary screens tomorrow morning, Tamar? Would you like Sarah Vance to read that to the children instead of the story about the elephant?\"

Tamar stepped back out of the recess.

Her legs felt hollow, like the dried bamboo stalks they used for tomato stakes in the agricultural ring. 

Through the green vinyl curtain of the schoolroom, she could hear the children singing. It was an old song, something about a farmer and a dog, the rhythm punctuated by the clumsy, uneven clapping of fourteen pairs of small, clubbed hands.

*B-I-N-G-O.*
*B-I-N-G-O.*

\"Who wrote the note, Jonas?\" she asked.

Jonas didn't look at her. He reached down to his hip, pulled his adjustable wrench from its holster, and adjusted the thumb-screw with his calloused index finger, turning the steel jaw in and out with a soft, metallic *zip-zip-zip*.

\"Paul Ross,\" he said.

Tamar felt the breath go out of her lungs.

\"Paul is dead,\" she said. \"He died six months ago. In his bunk. Gordon said it was a stroke.\"

\"Paul didn't have a stroke,\" Jonas said. \"He was sixty-eight years old, and his heart was stronger than mine. He had the run of the Well for twenty years, Tamar. He helped write the operating firmware for the optical drives when the ship was still in the yard at Long Beach. If anyone knew how to bypass the laser interlock—how to hide a self-destruct script in an unencrypted system buffer—it was him.\"

He slid the wrench back into the leather loop on his hip.

\"He came down to the reactor bay four days before he died,\" Jonas continued. \"He had a portable terminal. He spent three hours crawling through the bilge under Reactor One, taking physical swabs of the catalyst discharge line. He looked at the color of the sludge. It wasn't gray, Tamar. It was black. Black as coal tar.\"

\"What did he do with the swabs?\"

\"I don't know,\" Jonas said. \"He went back up Spoke Two. Two days later, Gordon posted the death notice. They didn't even hold a memorial service in the commons. They put him straight into the biodigester in Ring A before the shift change.\"

Jonas looked her in the eyes. 

His face was very close. In the dim light of the emergency recess, his pupils were dilated, dark and desperate.

\"If Paul left you that file, Tamar, it means he knew he wasn't going to make it down the ladder again. It means he thought you were the only one left who could touch the records without triggering an executive quarantine.\"

\"I can't do anything,\" she whispered. \"I’m a junior custodian. I delete files about dead people’s holidays.\"

\"You have the terminal token,\" Jonas said. \"You have the physical run of the Well. The Council doesn't look at the Well, Tamar. They think the Well is a cemetery. They only look at the numbers when Gordon brings them the weekly storage totals.\"

He reached out and touched her hand. His skin was rough, cold, smelling of kerosene.

\"Find the March records, Tamar. Find the vote. Find out what they’re going to do with Cohort Two.\"

\"Cohort Two?\"

\"The conception licenses,\" Jonas said, his voice dropping into a harsh, ragged rasp. \"They’re supposed to release twenty-four embryos from the cryo-vault next month. Twenty-four new babies, Tamar. To keep the labor pool viable for the twenty-year mark.\"

He squeezed her fingers until her knuckles ground together.

\"Find out if they’re actually going to let those children breathe.\"

### Chapter 4

The Master Archivist’s office was situated at the base of Spoke One, where the spoke intersected the inner radius of Ring A. 

At this elevation, the gravity was approximately zero point three g—an airy, dream-like weight that made every movement feel slightly exaggerated, as though one were walking on a mattress made of horsehair.

The office had no windows. It had been constructed from three modular shipping containers that had carried the ship’s primary guidance gyroscopes during the launch phase. The corrugated steel walls had been painted a dull, cream-colored enamel that was yellowing around the air registers, and the floor was covered with strips of worn industrial carpet that Gordon Vane had salvaged from the officers’ mess after the restructuring of Year Four.

Gordon Vane was sixty-one years old. 

He was a tall, stooped man with a long, narrow skull that had only a few wisps of white hair clinging to the temples. He wore a cardigan sweater of thick, un-dyed Shetland wool—an authentic terrestrial garment that had belonged to his father, who had been a professor of medieval bibliography at Cambridge. The elbows were patched with squares of green vinyl cut from a discarded tractor seat.

When Tamar pushed the door open, Gordon was sitting behind his desk, examining a physical slip of paper through a large, illuminated magnifying glass.

In the archives, physical paper was rarer than platinum. 

Every sheet of cellulose aboard the *Eidolon* was counted, tracked, and recycled through the pulp digesters in Ring B to make sanitary wipes and seed-sprouting trays. The only paper that remained in existence was the *Pre-Launch Core Reference*: forty volumes of thin, acid-free rag paper bound in blue buckram, containing the mechanical blueprints, valve schematics, and chemical formulations that had been printed before the ship left Earth as an insurance policy against an electromagnetic pulse or a catastrophic digital storage failure.

Gordon did not look up when the door clicked shut.

\"You’re late with the Batch 44 report, Lind,\" he said. His voice was like dry leaves being dragged across a gravel path.

\"I had a hardware lag on Console Seven, sir,\" Tamar said. She stood with her feet together, her hands clasped behind her back, her posture mimicking the rigid, subservient stance that Gordon preferred from his juniors. \"The laser positioning head had a calibration error during the sector switch.\"

Gordon moved the magnifying glass half an inch to the left.

\"A calibration error?\"

\"Yes, sir. On Plate 4,091.\"

Gordon’s hand stopped. 

The magnifying glass remained poised above the paper. The blue-tinted fluorescent lamp inside the rim of the lens cast a harsh, circular pool of white light across the printed schematic, highlighting a complex network of pipes labeled: `HIGH-PRESSURE AMINE REGENERATION LOOP - UNIT 01`.

\"Did it throw an error code?\" he asked. His tone was casual, almost indifferent.

\"Code 409,\" Tamar said. She had spent the twelve-minute climb up Spoke One memorizing the system manual’s section on optical controller faults. \"Drive current saturation on the tracking galvanic mirror. The unit cleared the sector, but the hash verification couldn't complete before the sector was flagged as unreadable.\"

Gordon slowly lowered the magnifying glass.

He laid it on the desk with a soft, muffled click. He looked up at her, his pale, watery blue eyes framed by heavy, crepe-like folds of skin that looked like the skin on the neck of a dead turkey.

\"Did you attempt a sector restore?\"

\"No, sir,\" Tamar said immediately. \"Protocol 12-B states that if a write-erase cycle fails with a 409 code, the operator must proceed to the next contiguous block to avoid secondary thermal blooming on the adjacent tracks.\"

Gordon stared at her for five seconds. 

The clock on the wall—an ancient battery-powered Seiko quartz clock that had been mounted to the bulkhead with two strips of double-sided foam tape—ticked with a dry, hollow *tock... tock... tock*.

\"Good,\" Gordon said.

He picked up a pencil—a real wooden pencil, barely three inches long, its lead sharpened to a needle point with a razor blade—and made a small, neat checkmark on a printed ledger beside his elbow.

\"Plate 4,091 was personal ephemera anyway,\" he murmured. \"The Varga collection. Old letters, some digital video from a lake in Central Europe. Nothing of structural importance.\"

\"Yes, sir.\"

\"We have more pressing concerns than dead Hungarians, Lind.\" Gordon leaned back in his swivel chair. The steel springs in the base groaned under his weight. He pulled his Shetland cardigan tighter across his narrow chest. \"The Council has authorized an extraordinary reclamation pass.\"

Tamar felt the hairs on her arms stand up.

\"An extraordinary pass, sir?\"

\"The telemetry requirements for the next ninety days have been revised upward by eighteen percent,\" Gordon said. He took a sip of cold water from an enameled mug that sat on a silicone coaster. \"The propulsion engineers are planning an alignment burn with the cold-gas thrusters in January to correct a three-milliradian drift in our approach vector. They need four petabytes of continuous scratch memory to run the Monte Carlo simulations for the burn duration.\"

\"Four petabytes?\" Tamar’s voice rose despite herself. \"Sir, that’s... that’s ten percent of the entire active archive.\"

\"I am aware of the arithmetic, Lind,\" Gordon said sharply.

\"We’ve already cleared all the Category Four personal files from Launch Blocks One through Eight,\" she said, stepping forward, her hands unclasping from behind her back. \"If we have to find four petabytes, we’ll have to move into Category Three. That’s the cultural collection. That’s the literature database, the historical music recordings, the patent registries—\"

\"—And the university libraries,\" Gordon added smoothly. \"The digitized holdings of Heidelberg, Oxford, and Kyoto.\"

\"We can't delete Kyoto,\" she whispered.

Gordon set his mug down. 

His eyes were not watery anymore. They were cold, flat, and sharp as surgical steel.

\"Do you know what Kyoto is, Tamar?\" he asked. \"Kyoto is thirty-five million gigabytes of descriptions of buildings that were constructed from wood that rotted four hundred years ago, written in a language that only six people on this ship can still read with any degree of fluency. Kyoto is twenty thousand dissertations on the legal structures of fourteenth-century rice taxation. Kyoto is ten thousand recordings of Noh theater that have not been played through an audio speaker since we cleared the orbit of Mars.\"

He stood up. 

Even in the low gravity of Ring A, he moved with a slow, deliberate heaviness, his slippers scuffing against the carpet. He walked around the desk until he was standing twelve inches from her. He was taller than her by half a foot, his shadow falling across her face in the white light of the desk lamp.

\"Tell me, Tamar,\" he whispered. \"If the cold-gas thrusters fail to fire in January, and the *Eidolon* misses the gravitational capture window at Gliese 667C by forty thousand kilometers, will the fourteen-century rice taxation help us turn around?\"

Tamar’s throat was dry. \"No, sir.\"

\"Will a recording of an ancient Japanese flute scrub the carbon dioxide from the nursery in Section Seven?\"

\"No, sir.\"

\"Then Kyoto is mass,\" Gordon said. His voice was steady, rhythmic, devoid of anger, carrying the terrible, unshakeable weight of absolute administrative logic. \"It is digital mass that we are carrying through a vacuum that does not care about the Tokugawa shogunate. We are eighty years from our destination, Lind. Eighty years. That means two more generations must live and die in these pipes before anyone sets foot on a patch of dirt that isn't contained in an aluminum box.\"

He reached out and tapped the front of her smock, right above her heart, with the sharp point of his wooden pencil.

\"The people who built this ship had romantic notions,\" Gordon said. \"They thought they could pack the entire human species—our songs, our poems, our lawsuits, our tax returns, our holiday photographs—into a few thousand blocks of glass and carry it across forty trillion miles of nothingness without paying for it. They were fools.\"

He pulled the pencil back.

\"A generation ship is not a museum, Tamar. A generation ship is an engine. And anything that does not contribute to the operation of the engine is fuel. We have burned the coal. We have burned the wood. Now we are burning the paper. And if we must burn the memories of the people who died on the way, we will burn them too, because the only thing that matters—the only duty that you and I have to this ship—is that when the *Eidolon* reaches that star, there is someone alive on board to open the hatches.\"

He turned his back on her and walked back to his desk.

\"You will begin the Kyoto cull tomorrow morning at zero-eight-hundred,\" he said, his voice returning to its normal, bureaucratic drone. \"I have prepared the batch parameters. You will target the humanities faculties first, then the law archives, then the architectural models. Leave the agricultural and metallurgical dissertations in the scratch buffer; Engineering will review them before we commit the erase cycle.\"

\"Yes, sir,\" Tamar said.

She turned toward the door. Her hand was on the brass handle when Gordon spoke again:

\"Lind.\"

She stopped. Her heart felt like a frozen stone in her chest.

\"Yes, Mr. Vane?\"

Gordon did not look up from his ledger. He was holding his wooden pencil above the paper, his white eyebrows drawn down over his eyes.

\"Paul Ross thought like you did,\" Gordon said softly.

The room was so quiet that Tamar could hear the faint, dry rustle of the wool cardigan against Gordon’s ribs as he breathed.

\"Paul believed that if we forgot what we were, we would arrive at the destination as animals,\" Gordon continued. \"He spent forty years in the archives, and in the end, he couldn't bear the arithmetic. He thought the numbers were cruel. He thought the Council was cruel.\"

He looked up. 

His face was half in shadow, the blue light of the magnifying lamp carving deep, black hollows beneath his cheekbones.

\"The numbers are not cruel, Tamar. The numbers are simply indifferent. Paul forgot that. And because he forgot that, his heart stopped beating. Do you understand me?\"

Tamar swallowed. The taste of ammonium carbonate was thick and sour on the back of her tongue.

\"I understand, Mr. Vane.\"

\"Zero-eight-hundred tomorrow, Lind. Don't be late.\"

### Chapter 5

The seed vault was the quietest place on the *Eidolon*.

It was located in the dead-weight hull between the outer skin of Ring A and the three-meter water shield that protected the ship’s forward quarters. 

To reach it, Tamar had to crawl through three successive inspection hatches in the structural ribbing, pulling herself through spaces barely wider than her shoulders, where the air was five degrees above freezing and the only illumination came from the narrow LED beam of her headlamp.

Here, surrounded by thirty thousand frozen vials of wheat, maize, soybean, and wild grass germplasm, sat the *Analog Registry*.

It was not digital. It had no silicon-germanium gates. It had no optical phase-change tellurium alloy that could be boiled away by a laser diode.

It was sixty thousand sheets of high-density quartz microfiche, manufactured from fused silica plates two millimeters thick. The images—schematics, treaties, chemical formulas, and historical texts—had been etched into the quartz with an ion beam at a resolution of one hundred nanometers, then filled with sputtered platinum.

A quartz microfiche did not care about cosmic ray flux. It did not care about power failures. It could sit in the vacuum of deep space for ten million years, through the death of stars and the collision of galaxies, and if someone pointed a five-hundred-power optical microscope at its surface, the letters would still be there, sharp and clear as the day they were milled in the cleanrooms of Zurich.

Tamar knelt on the cold aluminum grating of the deck.

Her knees were protected by thick rubber pads she had strapped over her trousers. Her breath rose in thin, white plumes that vanished into the darkness above the storage racks.

She knew which drawer she wanted.

Paul Ross had brought her here once, during her second year in the Well, when she had broken down in tears after deleting the digitized personal journals of two hundred women who had died in the quarantine wards during the Year Two dysentery outbreak.

Paul had taken her hand—his skin warm and dry, smelling of the tobacco he still managed to grow in secret in a small plastic container behind the heat exchangers of Ring A—and had led her through the structural ribs to this freezing vault.

*\"When the machines lie to you, Tamar,\"* he had told her, his voice echoing in the narrow space between the drawers, *\"you come here. The Council can change a bit in a flash memory cell with three lines of Python code from the executive terminal. They can rewrite a law, they can delete a vote, they can change the date of an execution, and the computer will never know the difference. The computer only knows what the current voltage tells it to know.*

*\"But quartz doesn't have a voltage, little bird. Quartz only has geometry. And you can't lie to geometry.\"*

Tamar reached into the dark of Rack Nine.

Her fingers found the small, stamped brass label on Drawer 14: `LSS - ORIGINAL DESIGN BASELINE - CONTRACT 2031-A`.

She pulled the drawer. 

The steel ball bearings inside the slides had frozen into their grease, resisting her pull with a heavy, grinding groan that sounded like a saw hitting a knot in hard oak. She braced her boot against the upright frame of the rack and pulled with both hands.

The drawer slid out six inches.

Inside, arranged in vertical rows like the teeth of a giant comb, were two hundred rectangular plates of polished quartz, each one encased in a thin sleeve of mylar film.

Tamar pulled her hand-held optical reader from her smock pocket. 

It was an antique: a brass-barreled inspection loupe with a battery-powered LED ring light that Paul had assembled from an old surgical laparoscope and a camera lens. She slipped Plate 01 out of its mylar sleeve.

The quartz was cold—so cold it sucked the heat from her fingertips through her thin cotton inspection gloves.

She laid the plate on the flat top of the drawer, switched on the loupe’s ring light, and pressed her right eye against the rubber eyecup.

The microscopic world leaped into focus.

The image was a cross-sectional blueprint of the *Eidolon*’s central environmental core, dated June 12, 2032. At the bottom of the frame was the signature of Dr. Evelyn Mercer—Helen Mercer’s mother, who had been the chief life-support architect for the consortium before she died during the launch preparations.

Tamar adjusted the fine-focus ring.

The letters resolved into razor-sharp lines of black platinum against the milky translucence of the fused silica:

`SECTION 4.2: CATALYTIC LIFE-SUPPORT SUBSYSTEMS (SABATIER / BOSCH)`
`PRIMARY REACTION: CO2 + 4H2 -> CH4 + 2H2O`
`CATALYST SPECIFICATION: 5% RUTHENIUM ON GAMMA-ALUMINA EXTRUDATES (TYPE 40-B)`
`OPERATING TEMPERATURE: 380C - 420C`
`DESIGN BED LIFE: 450,000 OPERATING HOURS (APPROX. 51.3 YEARS)`

Tamar moved the plate two centimeters to the right, following the engineering notes that ran along the lower margin.

Her eye stopped at a boxed paragraph highlighted with a double-line border:

`CRITICAL CONTAMINATION SENSITIVITY NOTICE:`
`RUTHENIUM CATALYSTS ARE SUBJECT TO IRREVERSIBLE PASSIVATION IN THE PRESENCE OF VOLATILE SILICON DERIVATIVES (HEXAMETHYLDISILOXANE, OCTAMETHYLCYCLOTETRASILOXANE) AND ORGANOSULFUR FRACTIONS AT CONCENTRATIONS EXCEEDING 0.05 PPM.`

`IN A CLOSED-LOOP MULTI-DECENNIAL SYSTEM, VOC ACCUMULATION MUST BE SUPPRESSED VIA CONTINUOUS CRYOGENIC CONDENSATION TRAPS AND REGENERABLE GRAPHITIC CARBON ADSORBERS.`

`FAILURE TO MAINTAIN VOC FRACTION BELOW CRITICAL THRESHOLD WILL RESULT IN EXPONENTIAL LOSS OF CONVERSION EFFICIENCY:`
`- YEAR 05 PROJECTION: 94.2% NOMINAL`
`- YEAR 10 PROJECTION: 71.0% NOMINAL`
`- YEAR 15 PROJECTION: 38.4% NOMINAL (UNSUSTAINABLE)`

Tamar drew in a breath.

The air in the seed vault felt sharp, like tiny needles of ice piercing the lining of her lungs.

*Year 15 projection: 38.4 percent nominal.*

They were in Year Eleven. 

The engineers in 2032 had known. 

Before the first titanium plate was rolled in the shipyard; before the first colonist signed their life away on the legal indemnity forms; before Helen Mercer’s mother had even ordered the steel for the amine towers—the math had been sitting in the blueprints, stamped in platinum on fused silica.

They had known that the scrubbers would die before the ship reached the midpoint.

Tamar moved to Plate 02: `COUNCIL MINUTES - SPECIAL EXECUTIVE SESSION - MARCH 03, 2042`.

This was not a blueprint. It was a photographic reproduction of a printed typewritten document—the original paper record that Paul Ross had smuggled into the seed vault and photographed with a micro-reduction camera before Gordon Vane could burn the original sheets in the core incinerator.

The header bore the red seal of the Provisional Council of the *Eidolon*.

`PRESENT:`
`DIRECTOR HELEN MERCER`
`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER DR. ARIS THORNE`
`CHIEF ENGINEER KLAUS KELLER`
`OPERATIONS DIRECTOR MARCUS VANCE`
`MASTER ARCHIVIST GORDON VANE`

`ABSENT:`
`DR. PAUL ROSS (EXCLUDED BY EXECUTIVE ORDER 44-A)`

Tamar’s eye moved down the page, scanning the transcript of the debate.

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Klaus, give us the baseline.`

`CHIEF ENGINEER KELLER: Reactor Two is degrading thirty percent faster than the conservative model predicted. The siloxane build-up from the laundry facilities and the hygiene dispensers is cooking into a polymer coating across the first two stages of the catalyst bed. We pulled a core sample yesterday. It’s sixty percent inert. We can't clear it with steam, and we don't have the solvent chemistry to strip the silica without destroying the alumina extrudates.`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: What is the remaining duration on Reactor One?`

`CHIEF ENGINEER KELLER: If we run it alone at maximum throughput, four years. Maybe five if we reduce the total air volume in the non-essential compartments. By Year Sixteen, the Sabatier loop will be producing less than forty percent of our daily water recovery quota. After that, we are drawing down the shielding reserves at eighteen hundred liters per day.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: The shielding reserves are our only radiation barrier for the crossing of the Serpens Void. If we drop the water level below seventy percent, the cosmic ray dose in Ring B will exceed fifty millisieverts per year. We will see leukemia in the under-tens within forty months. We will have radiation-induced cardiomyopathy in forty percent of the adult workforce before Year Twenty.`

`OPERATIONS DIRECTOR VANCE: Then we reduce the metabolic load.`

A three-second pause was indicated in the transcript by a typed dash: `—`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Define 'reduce', Marcus.`

`OPERATIONS DIRECTOR VANCE: We have two hundred and fourteen people in the non-productive demographic. Elderly specialists whose contract phases expired at the Year Ten mark. Pensioners from the launch consortium. People who are no longer on the active maintenance rosters for the reactors or the agriculture.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: Marcus, no. We are not discussing euthanasia protocols. The charter is explicitly clear—`

`OPERATIONS DIRECTOR VANCE: —The charter was written on a planet with thirty-six million cubic miles of atmosphere, Aris! The charter didn't anticipate that Evelyn Mercer’s scrubbers would turn to glass eleven years out! If we don't reduce the metabolic consumption, we don't make Year Twenty. Nobody makes it. Not the children, not the engineers, not the Council. We die in the dark with our lungs full of carbon monoxide, looking at our lovely charter!`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Silence.`

Tamar could hear Helen Mercer’s voice in her head—the cold, dry, patrician tone she used during the monthly address on the ship’s video channel.

`DIRECTOR MERCER: We will not initiate euthanasia protocols. It would destroy the psychological cohesion of the crew. If the agricultural workers suspect that their parents are being selected for termination to save oxygen, we will have a general strike in Ring B within forty-eight hours. If Ring B shuts down the soy harvesters for four days, we starve before we suffocate.`

`CHIEF ENGINEER KELLER: Then what do we do?`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: We manage the atmosphere.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: What does that mean?`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: We lower the pressure in Ring B by an additional twelve percent over the next twenty-four months. We reduce the oxygen fraction to fifteen point five percent.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: Fifteen percent? Helen, that is the equivalent of four thousand five hundred meters altitude! You cannot develop a human child in fifteen percent oxygen without irreversible neuro-cognitive impairment! The vascularization of the cerebral cortex requires—`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: —The children will adapt, Aris. You told us yourself that the body is a resilient machine.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: I lied, Helen! I was speaking about adults whose lungs had thirty years of growth in standard atmosphere! If you raise a child from infancy in fifteen percent oxygen, their hematocrit will spike to sixty-five percent. Their blood will be as thick as motor oil. They will suffer pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular hypertrophy, and cognitive blunting! They won't be able to learn the calculus required to run the reactor!`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Then they won't run the reactor. The reactor will be run by automated scripts developed by Gordon’s department. The children of Cohort Two do not need to understand tensor calculus, Aris. They need to turn compost, weed the hydroponic troughs, and clean the wastewater filters. They need to live long enough to reproduce, and then they need to step out of the airlock before their hearts fail.`

`OPERATIONS DIRECTOR VANCE: And the records?`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Gordon?`

`MASTER ARCHIVIST VANE: The diagnostic reports will be re-indexed under 'Experimental High-Altitude Acclimatization Protocols.' The raw Sabatier degradation figures will be moved to the non-indexed executive buffers. Within three years, the culling schedule will reach those sectors. They will be zeroed out under standard information hygiene routines.`

`CHIEF MEDICAL OFFICER THORNE: And Ross? Paul will never sign off on the deletion of the medical baselines.`

`DIRECTOR MERCER: Paul Ross will be relieved of his supervisory clearance on the main frame. If he refuses to comply, we will treat his non-compliance as a medical emergency. A man of his age can suffer an acute cerebrovascular event at any time in this pressure regime.`

`VOTE CALLED:`
`IN FAVOR: MERCER, VANCE, KELLER, VANE.`
`OPPOSED: THORNE.`

`MOTION CARRIED. EXECUTIVE ORDER 44-B SEALED.`

Tamar stepped back from the loupe.

Her right eye was watering, stinging from the cold glass. Her vision was blurred, the black lines of the microfiche swimming across her field of view like a swarm of angry gnats.

*They need to live long enough to reproduce, and then they need to step out of the airlock before their hearts fail.*

Her boots slid on the aluminum grating as she stumbled backward against the storage rack.

Her elbow struck the edge of Drawer 14, sending a sharp, jarring pain through her ulnar nerve that flared down into her pinky finger.

In the darkness of the seed vault, someone clicked a light on.

### Chapter 6

The light was not an LED. 

It was the warm, yellow, slightly unsteady beam of an old incandescent torch—a heavy aluminum tube with a knurled grip that smelled of corroded zinc-carbon batteries.

The circle of yellow light did not hit Tamar’s face. It rested on the cold floor between her boots, illuminating the rubber soles of her shoes and the corner of the quartz plate that still sat on the edge of Drawer 14.

\"You have your father’s eyes, Tamar,\" a voice said from the shadows behind the rack. \"He always looked at a schematic as if he were trying to find where the engineer had hidden his soul.\"

Tamar didn't scream. 

Her lungs were too tight; her throat felt as though it had been packed with dry sand from the salt flats of the Mojave. She pulled her back against the steel uprights of the seed rack, her hands flat against the cold metal at her sides.

Out of the darkness between Racks Eight and Nine stepped Dr. Aris Thorne.

He was seventy-two years old. 

He was the oldest living human being aboard the *Eidolon*. His hair was completely white, cut with surgical precision straight across his forehead, and his face was covered in a network of deep, sharp wrinkles that looked like cracked porcelain on an old plate. He was wearing his blue medical officer’s tunic, the brass buttons polished until they shone in the torchlight, though the cuffs were frayed and mended with blue nylon suture thread.

In his left hand, he held a small, plastic medical nebulizer. He lifted it to his lips, pressed the trigger with his thumb, and inhaled a cloud of fine, white mist with a soft, gasping wheeze.

\"Salbutamol,\" he said, lowering the inhaler. His voice was hoarse, rattling with the wet, loose cough of chronic bronchitis. \"A bronchodilator. Very useful when one’s alveolar walls are beginning to fibrose from forty years of breathing aluminum dust and volatile amines.\"

He walked forward, his steps slow and slightly shuffling in the zero-point-three g of the outer hull.

He reached out, his hand spotted with brown liver spots, and picked up the quartz plate from the drawer. He held it up to the beam of his torch, squinting through thick, rimless reading glasses that had been taped at the bridge with clear adhesive.

\"March third, Year Seven,\" Thorne murmured. \"A very bad day. A very cold day. It was the day I learned that a medical degree from Oxford does not qualify one to argue with a woman whose grandfather owned the shipping company that built the reactors.\"

\"You signed it,\" Tamar whispered.

Thorne lowered the plate. His pale, watery eyes looked at her over the tops of his glasses.

\"I voted against it, Tamar. You read the transcript. My name is under the word *OPPOSED*.\"

\"You didn't stop them.\" Her voice was shaking now, the anger rising through the cold numbness in her chest like hot oil through a fractured pipe. \"You let them lower the pressure. You stood in the mess hall and told my mother—you told twelve hundred people—that their bodies were resilient machines! You told them their red blood cells would save them!\"

Thorne did not look angry. He looked infinitely, terribly tired.

He leaned his back against the opposite rack of seed drawers, crossing his legs at the ankles.

\"What would you have had me say, Tamar?\" he asked, his voice quiet, conversational, carrying the dry, pedagogical tone of a lecturer in a cold anatomy hall. \"Should I have told your mother that her daughter would never live to see her thirty-fifth birthday? Should I have told the men in the machine shop that their lungs would begin to fill with fluid before their apprentices were old enough to shave?\"

He tapped the quartz plate against his knuckles with a soft, ringing *tink-tink-tink*.

\"If I had told them the truth in Year Seven,\" Thorne continued, \"Marcus Vance would have had his security team declare a state of environmental martial law within ten minutes. We would have had armed men at the ladder heads. We would have had forty people dead in the corridors of Ring B before nightfall, and for what? To die five years earlier in a pool of our own blood instead of dying in our beds of cardiac failure?\"

\"They are murdering the children,\" she said.

Thorne’s face twitched. A muscle at the corner of his left eye jumped three times, then went still.

\"They are preserving the species, Tamar.\"

\"By making them idiots?\"

\"By making them smaller!\" Thorne’s voice suddenly cracked, dropping its academic smoothness, becoming harsh, thin, and desperate. \"A human child who grows to one hundred and eighty centimeters requires two hundred and fifty milliliters of oxygen per minute at rest. A child whose growth is stunted by chronic early hypoxia—whose long bones do not fully lengthen, whose muscle mass remains thirty percent below terrestrial baseline—requires one hundred and sixty milliliters! One hundred and sixty, Tamar!\"

He stepped closer, his torch beam shaking as his hand trembled with the physical exertion of standing.

\"Do you know what that ninety-milliliter difference means across six hundred people?\" he whispered, his face inches from hers. \"It means five years! It means the *Eidolon* reaches the transition zone before the water in the shielding tanks runs completely dry! It means that someone—even if they are stunted, even if their fingers are clubbed like spade handles, even if they cannot read Shakespeare or calculate an orbital transfer—is alive to pull the manual release on the landing module!\"

\"And what will they do on the planet?\" Tamar cried. \"If there is a planet! There’s no planet in sight, Dr. Thorne! We are eighty years out! We have forty trillion miles of vacuum ahead of us, and you want to fill this ship with two generations of brain-damaged dwarfs who don't even know what an elephant is!\"

Thorne did not answer for five seconds.

He reached into his pocket, pulled out a small, folded piece of paper—a scrap torn from a medical prescription pad—and held it out to her.

Tamar looked at it.

On the paper, written in faded blue ballpoint ink in a shaky, old man’s handwriting, was a line of six numbers:

`04-12-88-01-99-14`

\"What is this?\" she asked.

\"It’s the supervisory override token for Console One,\" Thorne said.

\"Console One? That’s Gordon’s terminal. The Master’s desk.\"

\"Paul gave it to me,\" Thorne said, his voice dropping so low she had to strain to hear it over the distant, metallic hum of the water shield. \"The night before he died. He knew Gordon was coming for him. He had three days of digital telemetry from the nursery environmental sensors saved in a hidden partition on the secondary guidance bus.\"

\"Why didn't you use it?\"

\"Because I am an old man, Tamar,\" Thorne said, looking down at his trembling, liver-spotted hands. \"And because I am a coward. I have lived seventy-two years. I have eaten beef that was cut from an animal that grazed on green grass. I have swum in the Mediterranean Sea. I have listened to an orchestra play in the Albert Hall while the rain beat against the glass dome above our heads.\"

He looked up at her, and in his eyes, Tamar saw a horror that was colder and deeper than the void outside the hull.

\"I wanted to die in my bunk,\" he whispered. \"I wanted to finish my rotation, take my evening dose of morphine, and close my eyes without having to watch twelve hundred people tear this ship apart with their bare hands. But Paul... Paul wouldn't let me alone. He left that note in your queue because he knew you were the only one who still remembered what the sky looked like.\"

\"I was eleven,\" Tamar said, tears finally breaking over her eyelashes, hot and stinging against her cold cheeks. \"I barely remember it at all.\"

\"You remember enough,\" Thorne said.

He pressed the scrap of paper into her palm, his fingers cold and dry as parchment, then closed her fingers over it, squeezing her fist with both of his hands.

\"The conception licenses for Cohort Two are on Gordon’s desk tonight,\" he said. \"Helen Mercer signed the authorization this afternoon. Twenty-four embryos. They will be implanted into twenty-four women from Ring B next Tuesday.\"

He turned and pointed his yellow torch beam toward the narrow inspection hatch that led back to the living decks.

\"Go to the office, Tamar. Use the code. Look at the protocol for Cohort Two.\"

\"What does it say?\"

Thorne did not answer.

He turned his back on her, switched off his torch, and stepped into the absolute, freezing darkness of the seed vault.

His voice came out of the black:

\"Look at the oxygen fraction for the incubators, little bird. And then decide if you want to burn Kyoto tomorrow morning.\"

### Chapter 7

The corridor outside the Master Archivist’s office was deserted.

It was 02:40 ship-time. 

On Ring A, the LED ceiling strips had been dimmed to five percent—a dull, purplish twilight that the ship’s chronometer called *Night Cycle*. 

Every forty meters, a single low-wattage yellow emergency light cast a narrow, circular pool of brightness across the rubber floor, leaving the spaces between the bulkheads in deep, velvet shadow.

Tamar stood in the recess by the laundry chute, her back pressed against the cold aluminum siding, watching the door to Gordon’s office.

The office was dark. 

The frosted glass panel in the upper half of the door showed only the faint, rhythmic blinking of an amber status indicator on the central server rack inside.

It used six brass pins and a coiled steel spring.

In the archives, she had scanned forty volumes of the *Locksmith’s Comprehensive Guild Guide*, printed in Sheffield in 1924. She had read the chapters on tension wrenches, shear lines, and mushroom pins while waiting for the automated verification hashes to run on the agricultural census of 2036.

*Click.*

Pin one reached the shear line. The cylinder turned one-twentieth of a millimeter.

*Click.*

Pin three.

Her hands were steady now. The finger tremor that the psychologists had measured at three microns was gone, locked into absolute immobility by the ice-cold adrenaline that was pumping through her carotid arteries.

*Click... click.*

Pins two and four.

*Click.*

Pin five.

The cylinder gave a sharp, metallic *thunk*. The brass deadbolt slid back into the housing with a sound that felt, to Tamar’s stretched nerves, like an explosion in a quiet room.

She froze.

She remained on her knees for ten seconds, her breath held behind her teeth, her ears straining into the silence of the corridor.

Nothing.

Only the distant, intestinal thrum of the gray-water pumps down in the hull, and the whistling rush of the air blowers moving their thin, eighteen-percent mix through the ducts above her head.

She stepped inside, pulled the door shut behind her until the latch clicked into the strike plate, and slid the manual privacy bolt home from the inside.

Twelve gigabytes.

### Chapter 8

Marcus Vance was fifty-two years old, but he carried his body like a man of thirty who had spent his entire life in a high-gravity centrifuge. 

He was broad-shouldered, thick-necked, his dark hair cropped so close to his skull that the gray hair at his temples looked like iron filings glued to pink leather. He had been a major in the Australian Federal Police before he was hired by the consortium to organize the ship’s internal security cadre. He was one of the few men on the *Eidolon* who still wore terrestrial leather boots—heavy, black work boots with steel toes that struck the rubber floor plates with a dull, authoritative *thud*.

He did not have his hand on his stunner.

He was holding a stainless-steel vacuum flask in his left hand, the cap unscrewed, a thin curl of steam rising from the dark liquid inside.

\"It’s late for an information hygiene audit, Lind,\" Vance said.

\"Dr. Thorne has medical authorization for the seed stocks,\" she said. \"He checks the fungal spore samples for genetic drift every quarter.\"

\"He’s seventy-two years old, sir. Old people don't sleep well in the low-g rings. His lungs hurt him when he lies flat.\"

Vance nodded slowly.

He stepped forward. 

One pace. 

His boots made that heavy, terrifying *thud* against the deck plates. In the thirty percent gravity of Ring A, he moved with an unnatural, coiled power, as though he were deliberately suppressing the spring in his calves to keep from floating off the floor.

He stopped three feet from her.

He was so close she could smell the tobacco on his uniform—real tobacco, the dark, sweet, illicit Virginia leaf that only Helen Mercer and her inner circle were allowed to harvest from the research plots in the forward hydroponic bay.

\"Your father was a good man, Tamar,\" Vance said softly.

Tamar didn't move her eyes from his neck, where the collar of his black tunic met the thick, corded tendon of his throat.

\"He was an engineer, Mr. Vance.\"

\"He was an honest man,\" Vance corrected her. \"When the plasma injector cracked on the auxiliary engine in Year Three, he didn't write an eighty-page report about how we needed to hold an inquiry. He crawled into the manifold with a tungsten-inert-gas torch while the pre-heaters were still at three hundred degrees and welded the flange himself. He had third-degree burns across his chest and arms. He couldn't hold a fork for six weeks.\"

He laid his hand on her shoulder.

The weight was immense. It was not the weight of thirty percent gravity; it was the weight of a man who held the physical keys to the life-support valves, the airlocks, and the armory.

\"Your father understood what this ship is, Tamar,\" Vance said, his voice dropping into a gentle, fatherly cadence that made her skin crawl with terror. \"He understood that the *Eidolon* is not a democracy. It cannot be a democracy. You cannot vote on the laws of thermodynamics. You cannot have a committee meeting about the partial pressure of nitrogen. When the ship is hurt, someone has to crawl into the fire and take the burn.\"

He squeezed her shoulder.

His thumb pressed against the collarbone, right above the pocket where her data slate lay hidden against her ribs.

If he reached down; if he unbuttoned the two plastic snaps of her smock; if he felt the cold, rectangular edge of the aluminum frame...

\"Do you understand what I’m saying, little bird?\" he asked.

The name.

*Little bird.*

Paul Ross had called her that. Dr. Thorne had called her that.

And now Marcus Vance was calling her that.

It was what the adults from Earth called the children who had been eleven years old when the ship launched—the children who had been small enough to slip through the forty-centimeter maintenance crawlways behind the water tanks, light enough to work in the zero-g hubs without needing safety tethers, fragile enough that a five-millibar pressure drop would give them nosebleeds that lasted for three days.

\"I understand, Mr. Vance,\" she said.

### Chapter 13

### Chapter 14

### Chapter 15

### Chapter 22

The transformation of the *Eidolon*’s engine spine did not look like the blueprints in the textbooks.

It had been built by thirty-five men and women from the agricultural and mechanical shifts, working in eighteen-hour rotations under the direction of Matteo Rossi and Klaus Keller.

They had not used consortium components.

They had used what was on the ship.

The heat exchangers were made from four hundred lengths of aluminum irrigation pipe pulled from the disused soybean troughs of Ring A. The expansion valves had been machined by hand on the big tool-room lathe in Section Four, turned from solid billets of marine bronze that had served as ballast weights in the forward trim tanks.

The system was brutally simple:

Two large centrifugal blowers, driven by eighty-horsepower electric motors salvaged from the secondary sewage centrifuges, drew forty thousand cubic meters of cabin air per hour from the returns of Ring A and Ring B.

The air was forced through the three-stage exterior loop mounted in the permanent shadow of the reaction bells.

In Stage One, the air was cooled to four degrees Celsius. 

The water vapor condensed out of the gas stream at forty gallons an hour, pouring through a clear polyurethane pipe into the forward shielding tanks—pure, distilled water that had no siloxanes, no ammonium carbonate, and no trace of human medicine.

In Stage Two, the air hit the sub-zero coils at minus eighty degrees.

The carbon dioxide did not have time to dissolve. It froze directly onto the aluminum fins in thick, white cakes of dry ice that were automatically scraped by an electric carriage every fifteen minutes, dropping into two sealed hoppers that fed the methane synthesis injectors in the propulsion bay.

In Stage Three, the stripped nitrogen and oxygen—cooled to ninety Kelvin, dense and crystal-clear as liquid glass—was passed through a counter-current heat exchanger to chill the incoming warm air, before being reheated by the waste heat from the main reactor coolant loop and blown back into the living decks at twenty-one degrees Celsius and one hundred and one kilopascals.

No ruthenium.

No monoethanolamine.

No chemical synthesis plants that required nitric acid.

The ship was using the void. 

It was using the forty trillion miles of absolute zero that surrounded its hull to wash its own lungs, running an open thermodynamic cycle that would last as long as the fusion reactor had deuterium to spin the blowers.

Ahead of them, the universe was open.

The Milky Way was a brilliant, diamond arch that stretched across forty trillion miles of pristine, dust-free vacuum, so bright that it cast the long, sharp shadows of their bodies across the white enamel of the gallery bulkhead.

And there, ten degrees above the ship’s scarred basalt snout, was Tau Ceti.

It was no longer a point of light.

It was a brilliant, golden disk half the size of a copper coin, its warm, yellow photons striking the quartz windows of the gallery, warming the glass until Tamar could feel the heat against the skin of her palms through the four inches of silica.

Around the star, visible through the optical telephoto binoculars that Aaron had mounted to the rail, were four tiny, shining pearls:

The first world: a cinder of molten iron.

The second world: a white pearl of carbon dioxide clouds.

The third world: a storm-wracked desert of red sand.

And the fourth.

Tamar leaned forward.

Her breath fogged the glass.

The fourth world was not red.

It was not white.

It was a soft, pale, luminous marble of turquoise water and deep, forest green, marbled with long, lazy spirals of white water-vapor clouds that turned in the light of the yellow star like silk ribbons on a spinning wheel.

Henrik did not speak.

He reached out his hand, his long, brown fingers touching the spot on the quartz where the fourth world was growing larger with every second.

\"Look at the water, Tamar,\" the boy whispered.

His voice was clear.

It was resonant.

It carried the full, unbroken power of a child who had never known a lie, whose lungs had been formed in the cold truth of the deep, whose blood was rich with the nitrogen of an ancient comet.

\"I see it, Henrik,\" Tamar said.

\"Is that where the grass is?\"

\"That’s where the grass is, little bird,\" Tamar whispered, tears finally running down her cheeks, hot, clean, and bright in the light of the new sun.

Maya opened the brass catalog on the rail.

She dipped her steel-nibbed fountain pen into the gall-nut ink.

She turned to the final page of the registry—the page that sat after the consortium charters, after the Council minutes, after the names of the four hundred people who had died in the long crossing of the dark.

And in her clear, strong, schoolmistress’s hand, Maya wrote the final entry of the voyage:

`VOYAGE YEAR TWENTY-FIVE, MONTH ELEVEN`
`POSITION: TAU CETI APPROACH VECTOR - PERIAPSIS IN 400 DAYS`
`HULL STATUS: SCARRED, REINFORCED, HOLDING`
`ATMOSPHERIC PRESSURE: 101.3 KILOPASCALS`
`OXYGEN: 20.9% | NITROGEN: 78.1%`
`CARBON DIOXIDE: 340 PPM`
`ACTIVE STORAGE POOL: 38 PETABYTES (NOMINAL)`
`QUARTZ SLIDES SECURED: 42,000`
`COHORT ONE: MATURE`
`COHORT TWO: TALL`
`THE NEXT COHORT IS TOLD THE TRUTH ABOUT THE AIR.`
`THE ARCHIVE IS COMPLETE.`
`WE ARE GOING DOWN TO THE WATER.`

Maya blew on the ink.

She closed the heavy brass clasps of the book with a clear, musical ring that echoed through the gallery.

Tamar Lind turned her face to the yellow light.

The ship fell forward into the dawn.

And beneath the skin of the world, through two kilometers of titanium and basalt, twelve hundred people drew in their breath together, lifted their children to the windows, and began to sing the song about the elephant that had ears large enough to cool its blood beneath an open sky.

### Epilogue: The Garden at Ryoan-ji

Three hundred miles above the turquoise sea of Tau Ceti IV, the landing module separated from the keel of the *Eidolon* with a slow, hydraulic sigh.

It did not fall like a stone.

It circled the green continent twice, its titanium aeroshell glowing a soft, cherry red as it dipped into the upper fringes of the nitrogen atmosphere at seven kilometers a second, leaving a long, white trail of ionized water vapor across the morning sky.

On the observation deck of the mother ship, Tamar Lind sat in her chair.

She was fifty-two now.

She was too old for the landing couches; her heart had adapted to the thirty percent gravity of Ring A, and her vertebrae had fused during the long crossing of the Serpens Void. 

She would remain aboard the *Eidolon* with forty other elders, tending the solar arrays, monitoring the relay transmitters, keeping the library warm for the second expedition that would come in fifty years.

Beside her on the console sat a single quartz plate:

`PLATE 42,000: THE FIFTEEN STONES`

Through the telescope, she watched the landing module’s three parachutes bloom above the green delta of the river: three giant flowers of orange and white nylon opening against the blue, uncontained sky.

The module touched the dirt.

A tiny puff of brown silt rose from the bank of the river, drifted three hundred yards in the warm sea breeze, and settled into the green grass.

The hatch opened.

Through the telephoto lens, Tamar saw a figure step down the aluminum ladder onto the earth.

It was Henrik.

He was twenty-eight years old, six feet two inches tall, his broad chest bare under the yellow sun, his feet bare, his black hair blowing wild in a wind that had never passed through a ventilation blower.

He did not look at his instruments.

He did not check the atmospheric meters on his wrist.

He dropped to his knees in the tall, green bent-grass of the riverbank.

He reached out his hands, dug his fingers deep into the black, wet, ancient silt of a world that had never known a landlord, pulled a handful of the soil to his face, and drew in a long, deep, shuddering breath.

He smelled the water.

He smelled the rain.

He smelled the green grass that had forgotten how to be stone.

And three hundred miles above his head, in the cold, silent Well where the liquid helium still hummed its sixty-cycle song, Tamar Lind picked up Gordon Vane’s wooden pencil, drew a small, neat circle on the margins of the last ledger, and let the book fall closed.

The memory was safe.

The air was clean.

And the garden had no walls.

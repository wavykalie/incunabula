# Chapter 33: The Swearing of the Witness

The eighty men in the hall turned as one body.

Hannah Thorne walked down the center aisle between the wooden benches. Her frieze cloak was dark with melted sleet; her pattens clicked with a sharp, measured cadence upon the stone flagstones. She held her osier basket firmly beneath her left arm, draped in its outer fold of coarse gray wool, keeping her right hand free to balance herself against the bench-ends.

Thomas Callow's heavy jaw twitched. His fingers tightened on the silver head of his cane until the metal bit into his palm. Beside him, Counselor Garrow sprang to his feet, adjusting his powdered tie-wig with a sharp, irritable jerk of his chin.

"Let the woman come forward," Sir William Harford commanded, laying his goose quill back upon the green baize cloth.

Hannah reached the green table. She set her basket upon the oak bench beside Mr. Tarrant, threw back her wet hood, and made a low, respectful curtsy to the three magistrates.

"Hannah Thorne," Sir William said, peering at her over his steel spectacles. "You were summoned to attend this bench at one of the clock. It is now twenty minutes past two. Why have you delayed the King's business?"

"I have ridden forty miles since yesterday noon on the King's business, your honor," Hannah replied, her voice calm and firm. "The roads across the Plain are three feet deep in snow between here and the cathedral close. Bess has done her work, and Simon Clark's trotting mare has done the rest."

Mr. Tarrant, the clerk, held out the black calfskin Bible, its brass corners gleaming in the candlelight.

"Take the book, Mrs. Thorne," said the clerk.

Hannah laid her bare right hand upon the worn leather.

"You shall true answer make to all such questions as shall be demanded of you by the court," the clerk intoned rapidly. "You shall speak the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, so help you God."

"I will," Hannah said. She lifted the Bible to her lips, kissed the calfskin, and turned to face the magistrates.

Counselor Garrow stepped around the table, placing himself between Hannah and the bench. He held a copy of the parish warrant in his left hand, tapping the sheepskin with the gold rim of his quizzing-glass.

"Now, Mrs. Thorne," Garrow began in a smooth, insinuating tone. "You are the licensed midwife of this parish?"

"I am," Hannah said. "Twenty-two years under the seal of the Bishop of Sarum."

"During those twenty-two years, Mrs. Thorne, you have attended many women in their deliveries?"

"Two thousand infants, counselor. Rich and poor alike."

"Quite so," Garrow murmured. "A large number. A very large number. And doubtless, in the course of so vast an experience, an experienced practitioner acquires many convenient receipts? Many decoctions of tansy, pennyroyal, and ergot of rye to relieve young women of their untimely burdens?"

A murmur ran through the hall.

Hannah did not blink. She looked Garrow straight in the eye.

"A woman who has handled four hundred births knows the marks of maturity, Mr. Garrow," Hannah spoke, her voice clear and resonant throughout the quiet hall. "A child born at seven months is thin-skinned, red as a boiled crab, with soft purple nails that do not reach the tips of the fingers. His lungs are weak, his cry is like the mewing of a kitten in a basket, and his skull bones move against each other under the pressure of the midwife's thumb. Mary Miller's boy was born with firm white skin, plump thighs, and dark hair an inch long. His nails were hard and curved over his fingertips, and his cry could be heard across the barnyard. That boy was conceived during the sheep-shearing at High Lea in the first week of June, exactly nine calendar months before his birth."

Squire Lambert leaned forward across the mahogany bench, his bushy white eyebrows knit in concentration. He glanced at the Warminster surgeon who sat in the second row of the bar.

"Mr. Davis," Lambert called out to the medical practitioner. "You have examined the infant in the parish house this morning. Does your observation confirm the midwife's testimony?"

The surgeon stood up, adjusting his silver spectacles.

"It does, your worship," Davis answered respectfully. "The infant is completely mature in every physical sign. The assertion that this child was conceived at Michaelmas is an anatomical impossibility."

"I have taken the bishop's licensing oath, counselor," she said, her voice cutting through the room like a cold draft. "I have sworn before God and the Chancellor never to use any herb, craft, or violence to destroy the child within the womb. If any man in Nether Bourne says otherwise, let him step to this table and swear it upon the book."

Garrow waved his hand dismissively, covering his irritation.

"We will pass that by. On the night of Friday last, the seventeenth of November, you were called to the lambing outbuilding at High Lea Farm?"

"I was called at midnight by Edward Callow," Hannah answered clearly.

Callow's counsel frowned. "Never mind who called you, woman. Attend to the facts. When you reached the outbuilding, you found the prisoner, Mary Miller, in the extremity of labor?"

"I found her on wet oat straw, shivering with the cold," Hannah testified. "There was no fire in the outbuilding; the sleet was blowing through the broken weather-boards. Her waters had broken three hours before. The head was crowning in the second stage of hard labor."

"And what did you do?" Sir William Harford asked, leaning forward over his baize desk.

"I knelt upon the straw, your honor," Hannah said, turning her face to the magistrate. "I anointed my fingers with warm sheep-tallow. I examined the presenting part. The head was engaged in the first position, occiput to the left groin. The contractions were rapid, three in ten minutes, each contraction lasting the space of fifty heartbeats."

"Was the mother conscious?" Reverend Thistlethwayte asked softly.

"She was conscious, sir, but spent with twelve hours of secret agony," Hannah answered. "She cried out for water. I gave her three swallows of warm elderberry cordial from my stone bottle. At twenty minutes past one, under the bearing-down pains, the head emerged into my hands. The face was turned toward the sacrum. I wiped the mouth and nostrils of mucus with a boiled linen cloth. With the next pain, the shoulders cleared the passage, and the child slipped into my apron."

"And the presentation?" Squire Lambert asked, leaning his heavy elbows on the baize. "Was there any unnatural obstruction? Any turning of the child required?"

"None, your worship," Hannah answered clearly. "The pelvis was spacious, the labor natural. When the head was born, the cord was loose, not twisted around the neck. The infant was covered with healthy vernix; his limbs were plump, well-formed, with ten fingers and ten toes complete. I severed the cord three finger-breadths from the navel, tying it with three-ply bleached linen thread waxed with clean tallow."

"Did you clear the airway?" Reverend Thistlethwayte asked.

"I cleared the throat with my little finger wrapped in clean boiled linen, sir," she said. "The babe had swallowed a mouthful of mucus in the passage. When the mucus was removed, he drew his breath."

"And the breath of life?" Sir William demanded. "Did the infant breathe?"

"The child cried out before the navel-string was severed, your honor," Hannah said, her voice rising so that every man in the hall heard every syllable. "He drew a great gasp of the cold air and let out a lusty wail. His chest rose and fell with regular motion. The skin, which was bluish at the crowning, turned bright pink with the circulation of the blood. He kicked with both legs, and his mouth rooted against my thumb for the breast."

A collective gasp broke from the spectators.

Garrow stepped closer, shaking his parchment brief at her face.

"Take care, woman!" Garrow barked. "You are upon your solemn oath before the King's bench! If the child was born living, why did you leave the lambing barn without reporting the birth to the parish overseer or the churchwardens?"

"Because Thomas Callow took the child from my hands," Hannah said.

The great hall went cold and still as an open sepulchre.

Counselor Garrow took a backward step, his powdered tie-wig brushing against the clerk's high candle-bracket. His legal briefs fluttered in his hands; his thin lips parted, but no sound issued from his mouth.

On the dais, Sir William Harford pushed his steel spectacles down his nose, staring directly at the tenant churchwarden.

"You swear that upon your oath, Hannah Thorne?" Sir William asked, his voice dropping to a low, terrible rumble. "You swear that Thomas Callow, a churchwarden of this parish and a commissioner of land tax, took the newborn infant from your custody?"

"I swear it before Almighty God and this bench," Hannah answered with absolute, unyielding calm. "He stood by the outbuilding door with his stable lantern. He had an oilskin sack in his left hand and his heavy horsewhip in his right. He commanded me to leave the mother on the straw and return to my cottage across the water meadows. He laid two golden sovereigns upon the meal-bin and swore that if I spoke a word of what I had seen, he would have my roof pulled down by the parish officers before the spring."

Thomas Callow leaped to his feet with a roar of fury.

"She lies!" the farmer shouted, his heavy walking cane shaking in his fist. "She is a perjured crone! She took two guineas from my pocket as a bribe, and now she comes here with a pack of malicious slanders against my house! I command this bench to commit her for perjury!"

"Silence, Master Callow!" Sir William Harford shouted, his wooden gavel coming down with a crash that made the inkstands jump. "Sit down in your place, sir! You will not interrupt the examination of a sworn witness in this court! Constable Barnes, if that man opens his mouth again without permission, remove him to the lobby!"

Callow sank back into his oak chair, his chest heaving, his face mottled with purple rage.

Sir William leaned over the bench, looking down at Hannah Thorne with stern, judicial gravity.

"Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate said. "You swear upon your oath that Goodman Callow took the living infant from the outbuilding?"

"I do, your honor," Hannah replied. "He wrapped the babe in an oilskin sack and carried him into the yard. He told me to return to my cottage and keep my clapper shut."

"And where is the child now?" Harford asked. "Under the statute of King James, unless the living child can be produced before the court, the presumption of concealment remains against the mother."

"The child is here, your honor," Hannah said.

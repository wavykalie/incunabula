# Chapter 19: The Herbs on the Beam

Hannah returned to her cottage in the dark.

The moon had risen over the chalk downs, round and silver in a sky of clear ice. The flint walls of her cottage gleamed like frozen salt beside the rushing water of the chalk bourne.

She did not light a candle. She blew upon the embers of the peat fire until a yellow tongue of flame licked the black pot-hook, then set on the kettle for a cup of mint tea.

She climbed the ladder to the low loft beneath the thatch.

The loft was cold as a meat-cellar, smelling of dry herbs, sheep tallow, and aged oak timber. From the collar beams hung hundreds of dried bundles, harvested across three parishes during the long summer days: wild thyme, agrimony for wound-wash, dried elderberries tied in calico bags, and bundles of brown tansy leaves tied with hemp twine in lots of seven.

She reached for a bundle of tansy and ran her fingers through the brittle leaves. They crumbled into fragrant, bitter dust between her palms.

For twenty years, these plants had been her pharmacopoeia and her defense. When a poor mother lay burning with the weed in an unheated cot, it was not the physician from Salisbury who came with his silver carriage and his five-guinea fee; it was Hannah Thorne with her kettle of tansy tea and her earthenware pot of elder fat.

She had built her life upon the ground of this parish. She knew every hedge, every sheep-wash, every family secret whispered behind labor curtains.

And now, with a single oath before the magistrate, she stood to lose everything.

If Thomas Callow carried his threat at the Easter vestry, her pension would cease. Her cottage would be let to a strange laborer; her gray pony Bess would have to be sold to the knacker or the carrier to pay for bread; and she herself would become what she had spent two decades preventing other women from becoming: a parish pauper, dependent upon the grudging charity of the overseer's bowl.

She looked out through the small dormer window in the thatch. Across the frozen water meadows, the lights of Bourne Hall shone bright and steady on their gravel terrace.

She thought of Mary Miller in the stone cell at Warminster, clutching her empty, inflamed breasts in the dark. She thought of the living boy who had cried in the straw, with the strawberry mark above his collarbone.

A child's breath against a widow's bread.

She took down a bundle of dried chamomile, climbed down the ladder to the kitchen, and laid her episcopal license upon the deal table.

Hannah sat by her hearth until the parish clock struck midnight.

The deal table was covered with the dried harvest of her calling: bundles of agrimony, jars of elder-tallow, and the framed episcopal licence with its red wax seal.

She reached for her iron bodkin and scraped a hardened fleck of candle grease from her thumbnail. The familiar, rhythmic click of iron against horn calmed the beating of her heart.

For twenty years she had lived by the rule of the parish: deferring to the squire, curtsying to the rector, accepting the tenant farmer's bacon with a quiet word of thanks. She had thought that by keeping her head down and her eyes upon the laboring bed, the storms of the world would pass over her flint roof without touching her.

She had been a fool.

The world was not a neutral place where a woman could hide behind a basket of boiled linen. When the masters of the land chose to lie, their lies crushed the helpless beneath them like iron wagon wheels crushing chalk flints in the lane.

She picked up her episcopal licence and read the Latin words once more by the dying firelight: *In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti...*

The Bishop had not granted her this authority to protect the tenant farmer's purse. He had granted it to protect the breath of the newborn child.

She rose, packed her leather bag with clean linen and her pewter bodkin, and blew out the rushlight.

The quiet of the cottage loft was like a church after the service had ended.

Hannah touched the bundles of dried chamomile hanging from the oak rafters. Each plant had been gathered with prayer and patience: picked at the full moon along the riverbanks, dried in the shade of the apple loft, tied with clean flax cord.

For twenty years, these humble weeds had been her armor against the cruelties of the world. She had cured milk-fever with tansy tea; she had stopped uterine flooding with powdered ergot; she had soothed the agony of crowning with elder-tallow and boiled water.

She took down her mother's calfskin bag from the peg.

The leather was dark with age, polished smooth by forty years of carrying across the downland combes. Inside lay the tools: the bone scissors, the pewter bodkin, the linen tapes.

They were clean. They had no stain of blood or corruption upon them.

She knelt upon the cold floorboards of the loft, pressed her face against the fragrant herbs, and prayed for strength to face the morrow.

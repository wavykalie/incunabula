# Chapter 22: The Sickness of Mistress Callow

The great bedchamber smelled of elderberry wine, wet wool, and the sour breath of slow fever.

Martha Callow lay beneath four heavy woolen quilts in the carved tester bed. She was fifty-two years old, but her face was shrunk into dry yellow skin, her teeth long and loose in her gums, her hair gray as unwashed fleece beneath her dirty linen mob-cap. Her chest rattled with every breath, a wet whistling sound like wind passing through broken reeds in the bourne.

Hannah set her basket on the oak chest by the hearth.

The fire had burned down to gray embers; someone had thrown two green ash sticks onto the coals, filling the chimney corner with bitter smoke that made the sick woman cough.

Hannah took the iron poker, raked away the choking ash, and laid three dry hazel splits from her own bundle across the hearth. A clean yellow flame leaped up, warming the flagstones.

"Martha," Hannah said gently, leaning over the bedside.

The woman opened her eyes. They were yellowed around the rims, cloudy with dropsy, but when she saw Hannah Thorne, her fingers clawed at the patchwork quilt.

"Hannah," she whispered. Her voice was a dry rattle. "Is it you?"

"It is Hannah," the midwife answered.

She took the woman's right wrist between her thumb and two fingers. The pulse was thin, fluttering at ninety beats in the minute, like a trapped bird in a net.

Hannah drew the horn phial of squill vinegar from her pocket, uncorked it, and poured twenty drops into a pewter spoon. She mixed it with two spoons of boiled rainwater from her stone flask and held it to the woman's lips.

"Take this down," Hannah commanded. "It will loosen the phlegm in your throat."

Martha Callow swallowed with a grimace. The acid squill made her shudder, but after two minutes her chest eased; she brought up a dark, ropy phlegm into a linen rag Hannah held ready.

Hannah wiped the woman's chin with cold elder water, then turned back the quilt to examine her limbs.

The woman's feet were swollen, the skin pitted when Hannah pressed her thumb against the ankle-bone. The dropsy was rising toward the belly; three weeks at most remained before the fluid choked the heart.

"Where is Thomas?" Martha asked, her eyes darting toward the closed oak door.

"In the upper meadows," Hannah said.

Martha reached out, catching Hannah's woolen sleeve in a claw-like grip. Her breath came hot against the midwife's wrist.

"He told me it was a lamb," the woman whispered.

Hannah did not pull her arm away. "What was a lamb, Martha?"

"On the Friday night," Martha said. Her voice sank until Hannah had to bend her head close to the pillow to hear the words. "The wind was roaring off the high down. I was lying here, shivering with the ague. I heard a baby cry down in the yard. Not a lamb's bleat. A newborn child, crying for the breast."

The clock on the landing ticked with a heavy, deliberate stroke.

"What did you do?" Hannah asked.

"I called to Thomas," Martha whispered. "He was pulling on his boots by the hearth. He had his greatcoat on, and his wool gloves. I said to him, *Thomas, whose child is that crying in the yard?* And he cursed me. He told me I was light-headed from the fever. He said it was an ewe that had dropped early in the ditch, and he was carrying it down to the lower barn."

A tear slipped from the corner of Martha's yellow eye, running into the deep groove beside her mouth.

"He went down the stairs," she continued. "Through the window, I saw the stable lantern moving toward the turnpike gate. Then I heard John Pike's cart rumbles on the gravel. Pike's bells were muffled with rags, but I know the sound of his four gray mares."

She looked straight into Hannah's eyes.

"Whose child was it, Hannah?"

Hannah looked down at the dying woman. On the oak chest beside the bed lay the Callow family bible, its leather binding cracked with age. On its flyleaf were written the names of five dead infants buried in Nether Bourne churchyard before Edward was born:

*Thomas, born 1768, died of the thrush.*

*Sarah, born 1770, died of the croup.*

*William, born 1773, died of the spotted fever.*

*Martha, born 1776, died in the birth.*

*John, born 1778, died of the convulsion.*

Hannah had laid out three of those babes herself; she had washed their small limbs in elder water and tied their chin-cloths while Martha wept beside the cold hearth.

"It was Mary Miller's child, Martha," Hannah said softly. "And Edward is the father."

The sick woman let out a long, shuddering sigh that whistled through her throat. Her fingers relaxed their grip on Hannah's sleeve, falling limp upon the patchwork quilt.

"I knew it," Martha whispered. "The girl was a good maid. She scrubbed the cheese vats until they shone like pewter plates. She sang at the milking. When she took sick in September, Thomas wanted to turn her out onto the parish road. I begged him on my knees to let her stay till the winter."

She closed her eyes, two fresh tears tracing the lines of her hollow cheeks.

"Thomas has an iron heart, Hannah. He cares for nothing but the sheep, the lease, and the Earl's favor. He would sell his own flesh to save forty shillings on the poor-rate."

"Where did Pike take the child?" Hannah asked.

"Salisbury," Martha breathed. "Pike has a brother in the city who weaves broadcloth. Thomas told Edward that fifty shillings would clear the parish of the brat forever."

Hannah took a small piece of refined loaf-sugar from her pocket, touched it with three drops of peppermint water, and slipped it between the woman's dry lips.

"Rest now, Martha," Hannah said. "The child breathes, and God will judge the rest."

She smoothed the woolen quilts over the woman's thin shoulders, picked up her basket, and walked quietly out onto the landing.

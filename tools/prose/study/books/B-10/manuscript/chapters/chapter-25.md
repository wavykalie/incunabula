# Chapter 25: The Parish Tavern

The George Inn at Bishopstrow stood at the junction where the downland track joined the Salisbury turnpike.

It was an ancient, low-built house of flint and timber, its thatched eaves hanging low over leaded casements that glowed with the red light of a sea-coal fire. Outside the door, six carrier wagons were drawn up beneath the dripping horse-chestnut trees, their horses munching chopped clover from wicker nosebags while the drivers warmed their shins within.

Hannah tied Bess to the mounting post beneath the inn sign and pushed open the heavy oak door.

The taproom was packed with men. The air was thick with the reek of stale ale, shag tobacco, wet frieze coats, and the greasy sheep-salve that clung to every drover's boots. At the long trestle table near the hearth sat a dozen tenant farmers and smallholders from the Wylye parishes, their pewter pots thumping on the oak boards as they argued over the news from Nether Bourne.

Hannah stood in the shadow of the porch, shaking the snow from her hood.

"I tell you the girl is guilty under the statute," shouted Martin, the tanner from Heytesbury, slamming his fist onto the table. "The 1624 Act is plain as print! If an unmarried maid conceals the birth of her bastard, and the child cannot be produced alive, the law presumes the child was murdered! It is capital felony, without benefit of clergy!"

"Ay, that is the lawyer's talk," retorted Giles, the village smith, his leather apron still tied around his waist. "The law is an iron collar for the poor, but a silk ribbon for the rich! Who was the father, Martin? Answer me that! Why does the constable keep Edward Callow's name out of the parish warrant?"

A murmur ran around the taproom.

"Edward was at the grammar school," Martin sneered. "He is an educated young gentleman. You think an educated young gentleman would meddle with a common dairy maid who sleeps on a straw pallet in the out-house?"

"He meddled with her right enough," called out an old carter from the corner. "I saw him behind the hay-rick at Michaelmas, giving her a ribbon from Warminster fair. And Goodman Callow knows it! Why did Callow send forty sheep to the magistrate's bailiff on Monday morning? To buy a hanging for the girl, that's why! To keep the parish rate off High Lea Farm!"

"Silence your clapper, Giles!" shouted a tenant farmer in a broadcloth coat. "Goodman Callow is churchwarden of Nether Bourne! He pays fifty pounds a year to the poor-rate! If every bastard dropped in a ditch was laid to the tenant farmers, the whole valley would be bankrupt before the spring shearing!"

The argument raged back and forth across the table, spoons banging against pewter mugs.

The poor cottagers and day-laborers knew the girl was innocent of murder; they knew the Callow family had driven her into the barn to hide their shame. But the tenant farmers who held leases under the Earl feared the parish rate. A single bastard child maintained by the parish from infancy to seven years cost twenty-five pounds in nursling fees, clothing, and bread; and that money came straight from the pockets of the rate-payers at the Easter vestry.

Hannah stepped forward into the firelight.

The iron pattens on her shoes clicked sharply against the flagstones.

The shouting in the taproom ceased as if a knife had cut the air. The tanner froze with his pewter tankard halfway to his mouth; the carter stared; the tenant farmer turned his back to the hearth, his face flushing dark red.

Hannah looked around the circle of men.

"Good day, neighbors," she said.

No man answered for three heartbeats. Then old Giles pulled off his woolen cap, his soot-streaked forehead bowing toward the midwife.

"Mistress Thorne," the smith said, his voice dropping into rough respect. "We did not look to see you on the turnpike in this weather."

"I am on the King's business," Hannah said.

She walked to the tap-bench. The landlord, a fat man with an ale-stained apron, wiped the wooden counter with a dirty cloth.

"A cup of hot cider, Mrs. Thorne?" he asked nervously. "Or a bowl of mutton broth?"

"A cup of boiled water for my stone flask," Hannah answered. "And two quarts of crushed oats for my mare at the post."

She laid a silver threepenny piece upon the timber counter.

The tenant farmer in the broadcloth coat cleared his throat, leaning his shoulder against the chimney breast.

"The magistrates meet tomorrow at Bourne Hall, Mrs. Thorne," he said, trying to make his voice sound casual. "Goodman Callow has retained Counselor Garrow from Salisbury to represent the parish. The counselor says the deposition of the midwife is all that is required to send the baggage to Fisherton."

Hannah took the warm flask from the landlord's hand. She corked it with a birch plug and slid it into her basket.

Then she turned and looked the farmer full in the face.

"Counselor Garrow may say what pleases his fee, Master Barnes," Hannah said, her voice clear and hard as bell-metal. "But Counselor Garrow was not in the lambing barn when the child was born. I was."

She picked up her basket, walked past the silent table, and stepped out into the freezing snow.

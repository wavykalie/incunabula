# Chapter 6: The Cry in the Straw

The crowning was swift and fierce.

As the next contraction crested, the hairy crown of the infant's head broke through the vulva. Mary let out a high, ragged shriek that rose into the timber rafters and was swallowed by the gale outside. Her heels drummed against the wet straw; her hips bucked upward in the involuntary expulsion reflex.

"Steady, girl!" Hannah commanded, her voice dropping into the low, hypnotic cadence of her craft. "Pant now. Do not push. Pant like a hound in the harvest sun! Let the mouth stretch!"

She pressed the flat of her palm firmly against the perineum, guarding the fragile margin of flesh with the warm boiled linen. With her other hand, she guided the infant's occiput downward, flexing the head so the smallest circumference passed the pubic bone. The crown slipped forward; the forehead followed, then the nose, mouth, and rounded chin, covered in a thick white coating of vernix caseosa.

The child's face was purple, bloated from the compression of the birth canal. Its eyes were sealed tight, its tiny mouth filled with gray mucus and amniotic fluid.

Hannah did not pause. Instantly she hooked her forefinger into the corner of the infant's mouth, sweeping the throat clear of phlegm. She ran her finger around the neck. There was no cord entangled about the throat—the navel string lay free, pulsing thick and blue between the child's thighs.

"One more breath, Mary!" Hannah cried. "Give me the shoulders!"

Mary gave a short, sobbing grunt. Hannah supported the head with both hands, gently depressing it toward the straw. The anterior shoulder slipped beneath the pubic arch with a soft, wet release. Hannah guided the torso upward, and in a rush of warm amniotic brine and blood, the child slid completely into her hands.

It was a boy.

He lay across Hannah's wool-covered lap, limp as a wet hare, his skin mottled with gray-blue shadows. He did not breathe.

Behind her, Edward Callow let out a choked sound and turned his face to the timber wall.

Hannah did not look at him. She had twenty seconds before the brain starved in the cold air. She snatched the pewter bodkin from the linen cloth. She used the blunt, rounded head to stroke the infant's spine vigorously from base to neck. With a clean scrap of linen moistened with brandy from her flask, she rubbed the child's chest and soles with fierce, circular friction.

Still no breath.

She bent her head, placed her mouth over the infant's tiny nostrils and parted lips, and blew three gentle puffs of warm air into the chest. The little ribcage rose and fell.

She stroked the soles of his feet with the edge of her fingernail.

A twitch ran through the boy's limbs. His hands clenched into miniature fists. His chin trembled, his chest heaved in a sudden convulsive spasm, and then he let out a sharp, sneezing gasp.

A second later, his lungs opened fully. A loud, vigorous cry rang through the empty lambing barn—clear, angry, and resonant as a trumpet call.

"He lives, Mary," Hannah said, her breath catching in her throat as she laid the crying boy upon his mother's bare breast. "You have a living son."

Mary looked down at the child. Tears cut clean tracks through the grime on her cheeks. Her shivering hands reached out to touch the wet, black hair plastered to his scalp.

"My boy..." she whispered, laughing and weeping at once. "My sweet boy..."

Near the door, Edward Callow stared at the crying child as if an adder had uncoiled from the straw. His jaw worked, but no sound came from his mouth. He looked at Hannah, then at the girl, then out into the black rain of the lane.

Without a word, he turned, caught the reins of his gelding, and fled into the darkness, the hooves of his horse pounding away down the ridge.

The sound of the galloping horse faded quickly into the roar of the wind. Hannah knelt over the mother and child, listening to the steady, rhythmic wail of the newborn infant. The child's skin was flushing pink now, the blue shadows retreating from his fingertips as his small heart drove the oxygen through his veins.

Hannah took the clean flannel square from her basket and wiped the vernix from the boy's chest and belly. His limbs were sturdy, his thighs plump despite Mary's months of privation in the dairy. He was a nine-month child, carried to full term, with well-formed fingernails reaching the tips of his digits and dark eyelashes glistening against his cheeks.

She laid her warm hand upon Mary's abdomen once more. The womb had contracted to the size of a cannonball, firm and hard beneath the navel. There was no profuse hemorrhage; the bleeding was the normal dark seepage of a healthy third stage.

Mary lay back against the saddle blanket Edward had left behind. Her eyes were heavy, her breathing slow and shallow as the feverish flush began to subside into the cold exhaustion of postpartum relief.

"Hold him close to your smock, Mary," Hannah instructed softly, drawing the dry corners of the woolen horse blanket over the child's back. "Keep him warm against your heart while I tend to the cord."

Hannah knelt in the trampled straw between Mary's feet. From her wicker basket she took a fresh roll of narrow linen tape, boiled four days earlier in rainwater with dried lavender sprigs, and two iron clamps wrapped in oiled silk.

With steady fingers, she measured three finger-breadths from the infant's navel, tying the first linen knot with a double turn that bit deep into the gelatinous blue cord. She waited two full pulse-beats, watching for any tell-tale bead of arterial blood, then tied the second knot an inch further along. Taking her shear-steel scissors from the horn cup of sweet oil, she snipped the cord cleanly between the ligatures.

The severed stalk shrank back against the boy's belly. Hannah dusted the stump with finely powdered dry burnt alum and crushed lycopodium spores from her box to prevent the wet mortification that killed so many cottage infants before their ninth day. She smoothed a soft linen square over the dressing and pinned the woolen binder snug with a bone bodkin.

Mary watched every motion with shining eyes, her hand resting upon the crown of her baby's head.

The boy nuzzled against the stained linen of his mother's shift, his tiny mouth rooting for the nipple. Mary gave a faint, broken sigh, her arm tightening around the bundle as the storm rattled the hazel hurdles overhead.

Hannah knelt on the damp straw and bowed her head in the shadow of the pen. For a long minute she did not move, letting the adrenaline drain from her wrists. The crying had softened to a rhythmic, snuffling whimper.

In twenty years, she had never grown accustomed to the miracle of that first breath. A minute before, there was only darkness and the blind struggle of flesh; now there was a boy, endowed with an immortal soul, crying for warmth in an unheated lambing barn on the high downs of Wiltshire.

She reached for the boiled linen tape resting in her wicker basket. She cut two lengths with her bone scissors, measuring each against the palm of her hand.

"A good boy, Mary," Hannah said, her voice gentle in the dim firelight. "A strong boy with his father's shoulders and his mother's dark hair."

Mary touched the infant's cheek with her cracked thumb. "Edward will come back for us, won't he, Hannah? He only went to fetch a cart from the yard."

Hannah did not answer. She dipped the linen cord into the warm water kettle, shook off the droplets, and prepared to tie the navel string.

The final stage came upon them like a rising tide.

Mary's body was seized by an irresistible expulsive reflex. She grasped the hemp ropes of the empty sheep hurdles with both hands, her fingernails tearing into the bark, her chin pressed against her collarbones as she threw the entire weight of her young body into the downward effort.

"Pant now, Mary!" Hannah commanded, kneeling firm between the trembling thighs. "Pant, girl! Do not tear!"

The crown of the infant's head bulged through the vulva. The scalp was covered in thick, dark hair that glistened with vernix under the flickering lantern light. Hannah's left hand guarded the perineum, pressing a warm square of boiled linen against the stretching skin to support the tissues, while her right hand guided the occiput downward beneath the pubic arch.

With a soft, wet slip, the head was born.

The face was turned toward Mary's left thigh: swollen, purple, the eyes sealed tight with white vernix.

"Stop pushing, Mary!" Hannah called out. "Breathe! Only breathe!"

Swiftly, Hannah ran her forefinger into the infant's mouth, hooking away a thick clot of gray mucus that clogged the throat. She slipped her finger around the neck; the navel cord was loose, pulsing with thick, dark venous blood.

With the next gentle breath, the infant's head rotated to the right, aligning the shoulders with the pelvic outlet. Hannah gently depressed the head toward the straw; the anterior shoulder slipped under the pubic bone, followed instantly by the posterior shoulder, and in a warm, rushing flood of liquor and blood, the child slipped into Hannah's outstretched hands.

It was a boy.

A sturdy, full-term boy with broad shoulders and plump limbs. But he was limp as a wet towel, his skin mottled with gray-blue shadows, his chest motionless.

Hannah did not waste a heartbeat. She wrapped the infant in a warm flannel cloth from her basket, snatched her pewter bodkin, and vigorously stroked the blunt, rounded head up and down the spine from sacrum to neck. She splashed two drops of French brandy from her flask onto her palm and rubbed the soles of his feet with fierce, circular friction.

"Breathe, little one," she whispered under her breath, reciting the Latin words of her licensing oath. "In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost, breathe."

She placed her lips over his tiny mouth and nose, blowing two gentle, warm puffs of air into his lungs. The chest expanded, then sank.

She rubbed his ribs with the rough boiled linen.

Suddenly, a spasm ran through the child's frame. His eyelids fluttered. His chin twitched, his chest hitched in a sharp, convulsive gasp, and then his mouth opened wide.

A loud, furious cry rang through the empty lambing barn—a sound so clear, vibrant, and alive that it seemed to banish the cold from the stone walls.

Mary let out a ragged sob of joy, reaching out her trembling arms from the straw. "My boy... my little boy..."

In the doorway, Edward Callow took one look at the crying child, turned white as ash, and fled into the rain. His horse's hooves pounded away down the ridge, swallowed by the roar of the downland storm.

# Chapter 9: The Empty Hearth

Dawn came to the Wylye valley not with sunlight, but with a slow, gray lifting of the fog off the chalk water meadows.

Hannah led Bess into the lean-to stable behind her cottage. She rubbed the mare down with a wisp of dry oat straw, checked her hooves for picked stones, and gave her a double measure of bruised beans. The pony lowered her muzzle into the wooden manger, chewing with a rhythmic, comforting crunch.

Hannah carried her leather bag into the dark kitchen.

The room was cold. The fire in the hearth had died to white embers during the night, leaving the smell of damp soot and old hearthstones. On the deal table, the two golden sovereigns sat where she had dumped them from her apron pocket. They looked strange and alien upon the scrubbed ash boards: bright, polished, stamped with the King's profile, bought with a newborn life.

She took down an earthenware crock from the shelf, took a handful of dry birch kindling, and blew upon the embers until a small flame caught. She set the iron kettle upon the trivet.

While the water heated, she unpacked her kit. She dropped the bone scissors and the pewter bodkin into an iron skillet of boiling water, letting the scalding steam scour away the traces of blood and vernix. In twenty years, she had never laid an unboiled tool against a woman's flesh. Her mother had taught her that: *Keep your iron hot as the hearth, Hannah, or you will carry the rot from house to house.*

She sat on the wooden settle by the chimney, her hands clasped in her lap.

Her thumbs ached. In the quiet of the morning, the memory of her own dead child rose before her like a shadow upon the wall. She remembered the cold November day twenty-two years ago: John carrying the little deal box under his arm, walking across the sodden grass of Nether Bourne churchyard. There had been no bells, no priest in a white surplice, no prayers from the book. The parish vicar had refused to read the burial service over an unbaptized infant born before its time.

*"He was born alive,"* she had wept to the vicar at the lych-gate. *"He drew three breaths! I heard him cry!"*

*"A cry is not a Christian soul, woman,"* the vicar had answered, turning back to his warm rectory. *"The law of the Church is plain."*

Now another boy had cried in the dark of a lambing shed. A living, vigorous boy with black hair and a strawberry mark on his collarbone.

And where was he now?

Was he in the straw with his mother? Or had Thomas Callow's cart already rumbled down the high ridge toward the Salisbury turnpike?

She reached out her thumb and pressed it against the edge of the golden sovereign. The milled edge was sharp, biting into her calloused flesh.

A bushel of barley and a side of bacon. That was the price of her silence. That was what kept an old widow warm when the winter storms swept the Wiltshire downs.

She pushed the gold coins into an earthenware mustard pot and shoved it deep into the dark recess of the chimney breast.

She poured the boiling water from the iron skillet into a pewter basin. She took a cake of hard tallow soap and washed her arms to the elbows, scouring the dried blood from her skin with a wisp of coarse hemp tow. The skin turned bright red from the heat, tingling in the cold room.

Through the window, she watched the milk cows moving slowly along the lane toward the lower water meadows. Old Giles, the cowman, plodded behind them in his patched smock, his hazel stick tapping against the chalk flints. The village was waking to its ordinary labor: smoke rose from the forge chimney, and the iron hammer began its dull, regular clang against the anvil.

She dried her hands upon a clean linen roller. She took down her bible from the shelf, laid it upon the table, and sat staring at the calfskin cover until the morning bells began to toll for church.

The gray pony carried her down the winding chalk track as the first pale light of dawn filtered through the frozen fog.

In the valley below, the village of Nether Bourne was slowly stirring. Smoke rose from the thatched chimneys of the farm laborers; cocks crowed in the fold-yards; and the sound of iron buckets clanking at the village well traveled clearly through the cold, still air.

Hannah dismounted by her garden gate, her limbs so stiff from the ride that her knees buckled as her boots struck the frozen gravel. She led Bess into the stable, rubbed down her sweating flanks with dry oat straw, and filled her manger with sweet meadow hay.

Inside the cottage, the silence was heavy as lead.

The two golden sovereigns in the earthenware mustard pot seemed to burn through the brickwork of the chimney breast. Two pounds of gold: enough to buy two winter petticoats, a sack of flour, and three loads of peat. Blood money, paid to buy the silence of a licensed midwife while a tenant farmer robbed an unwed mother of her child.

She stripped off her stained apron and dropped her pewter bodkin and bone shears into the iron pot of boiling water on the hearth. The steam rose, carrying the sharp, clean scent of boiled vinegar and lye.

She sat upon the oak settle and closed her eyes.

In the darkness behind her eyelids, she saw again the face of her own dead child in the winter of 1774: the tiny, perfect fingernails, the blue lips that never learned to smile, the cold clay of the churchyard falling upon the deal lid.

For twenty-two years, she had carried that sorrow in secret, pouring her maternal love into two thousand parish babes. She had sworn an oath before the Bishop of Salisbury that she would protect the innocent and speak the truth without fear of worldly power.

She looked at her hands, red and scalded from the hot water.

Thomas Callow thought his gold had bought her silence. He thought his threats of eviction and ruin would keep her tongue behind her teeth.

He did not know Hannah Thorne.

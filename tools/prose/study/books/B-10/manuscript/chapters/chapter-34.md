# Chapter 34: The Living Evidence

Hannah turned to the oak bench beside the clerk's table.

She lifted the wicker basket with both hands, set it upon the green baize cloth directly beneath Sir William Harford's spectacles, and untied the hempen strings that secured the osier lid.

The entire courtroom stood up on the benches. Men in the back row climbed upon the window-sills to see; Constable Barnes forgot his staff and leaned over the table, his mouth open; Counselor Garrow backed away two paces, his gold quizzing-glass dangling from his fingers.

Hannah folded back the outer gray frieze cloak. Beneath it lay the clean white woolen blanket she had boiled in lavender water. She drew back the blanket fold by fold.

In the center of the basket, lying upon a pillow of clean carded fleece, lay the baby.

The sudden glare of the eighty candles and rushlights struck the infant's face. The child stirred, screwed up his small features, sneezed twice, and then let out a piercing, indignant yell that rang through every corner of the great hall.

*Waaah! Waaah! Waaah!*

The cry was strong, lusty, and unmistakable. It was the full-throated cry of a five-day-old infant whose lungs were clean, whose heart was vigorous, and whose belly demanded milk.

At the bar of the court, Mary Miller gave a wild, choking scream.

She broke past the constables, throwing herself across the baize table, her handcuffed arms reaching blindly into the wicker basket. She caught the child to her bosom, burying her face against the warm flannel wrap, her tears falling in a hot rain upon the baby's dark curls.

"My boy!" she cried, rocking the infant against her shoulder. "My little boy! Oh, God be praised! God be praised in heaven! You are alive! You are alive!"

Constable Barnes stepped forward to lay hands upon the girl's shoulder, but Sir William Harford struck the table with his open palm.

"Stand back, Barnes!" the magistrate commanded, his voice shaking with sudden, fierce emotion. "Leave the mother alone! Stand back, all of you!"

Sir William stood up from his velvet chair, his gout forgotten for a moment. He leaned over the green baize table, staring down into the basket at the crying child. Beside him, the Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte leaned his white head forward, his aged eyes filling with tears as he looked upon the newborn infant.

"A living soul," the clergyman whispered, crossing himself. "Born into the parish of Christ, and preserved by the hand of the Almighty."

Squire Lambert rose from his chair, his apple forgotten upon the table. He leaned his heavy shoulders over the bench, peering down at the child with keen, rustic curiosity.

"By the living God, Harford," Lambert muttered, "that is a stout lad! Look at the breadth of his chest! That is no seven-month weakling!"

Sir William turned his hawk-like gaze upon Hannah Thorne.

"Where did you recover this infant, Mrs. Thorne?"

"In a broadcloth weaver's cellar in Sheep Street, behind Saint Thomas's church in Salisbury," Hannah answered clearly, her voice reaching the furthest corners of the gallery. "He was carried thither on Friday night by John Pike, the drover, who was paid twenty shillings by Thomas Callow to take him from the parish."

"How do you identify this child?" Counselor Garrow cried out, his voice shrill with desperation as he saw his case collapsing. "Any beggar's brat from the Salisbury gutters might be brought into this hall to deceive the court! The law requires strict, incontrovertible proof of identity!"

Hannah looked at Garrow with cool contempt.

She stepped to Mary Miller's side, laid her warm hand upon the girl's trembling shoulder, and gently took the crying child from her arms. She laid the baby upon the green baize cloth between the clerk's inkstands.

"I have delivered two thousand children into this county, Counselor Garrow," Hannah said. "A midwife knows the child she has brought through the crowning."

She unbuttoned the little shift of bleached linen at the infant's left shoulder, pulling the cloth back to expose the collar-bone.

"Examine his left shoulder, your worships," Hannah commanded.

Sir William Harford adjusted his spectacles and bent low over the table. Squire Lambert put down his pocket knife and leaned his red face forward.

There, two inches above the beating heart, was the raised, purple-red birthmark the size of a hazelnut, shaped like a wild strawberry with three distinct lobes.

"I noted that mark within five minutes of the birth," Hannah testified. "I showed it to Edward Callow while he held the stable lantern in the lambing shed; I bade him remember the mark for the rest of his life. And examine the navel-string, Sir William."

She turned back the linen swaddling at the infant's belly.

"The umbilical cord is tied with three-ply bleached linen thread, waxed with mutton tallow, and knotted with a double clove-hitch three finger-breadths from the flesh. The ends are cut square at half an inch with bone-handled shears. No surgeon in Salisbury ties that knot; no parish nurse in Wiltshire waxes that thread. That is the seal of my craft."

Squire Lambert touched the waxed linen thread with the tip of his finger.

"The woman speaks the truth, Harford," the squire declared, looking up at his fellow magistrate. "I know Hannah Thorne's work. She delivered three of my own grandchildren at Heytesbury Manor. She ties that identical clove-hitch on every child she brings into the world. That is no city foundling."

Reverend Thistlethwayte adjusted his spectacles and peered closely at the infant's shoulder.

"The strawberry mark is identical to the mark carried by the late old Goodman Callow, Thomas Callow's father," the vicar remarked in a quiet, thoughtful voice. "Old John Callow had that self-same red mark upon his collar-bone when I ministered to him on his deathbed twenty years ago. It is a family token well known in this parish."

A murmur of assent ran through the elderly tenant farmers on the front benches.

Thomas Callow sat frozen in his oak chair, his broadcloth coat buttoned to his chin, his fleshy face drained of all color. His hands gripped the arms of the chair until the walnut wood creaked beneath his weight.

Counselor Garrow gathered his parchment briefs with trembling hands, trying to maintain his professional bluster.

"Even if the identity be admitted, your worships," Garrow stammered, "the legal charge under the statute remains to be considered! The child was concealed from the parish officers! The mother made no declaration to the churchwardens! The parish rate is threatened with the burden of maintenance!"

Sir William Harford straightened his back. He looked down from the bench, his eyes resting upon Counselor Garrow with icy, judicial disdain.

"Counselor Garrow," Sir William said, his voice ringing through the hall like an iron bell. "The statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First was enacted to punish the murder of infants, not to serve as an instrument of extortion for wealthy rate-payers. The statute applies only where the child is destroyed or concealed so that it cannot be known whether it was born alive. The child is here before our eyes. He breathes; he kicks; he cries in the presence of the King's commission. The accusation of capital murder against Mary Miller is utterly groundless, vexatious, and dismissed."

The magistrate turned to Constable Barnes.

"Barnes, take the irons from this girl's wrists instantly."

The constable drew his iron key from his leather pouch, inserted it into the brass padlock, and struck off the handcuffs. The heavy iron bands clattered upon the stone floor with a sharp, ringing crash.

Mary Miller sank to her knees, gathering her crying child back into her arms, pressing her lips to his soft forehead, weeping tears of pure, unburdened deliverance.

She buried her face in the child's soft linen wrap, kissing his cheeks, his chin, and his tiny curled fingers. The infant ceased his crying, his dark eyes gazing up into his mother's weeping face with a calm, untroubled wonder.

Hannah knelt beside the girl on the cold stone flags.

She adjusted the woolen swaddling around the baby's feet, tucking the warm corners beneath Mary's trembling arms.

"He is safe, Mary," Hannah whispered, her weathered hand resting upon the young mother's shoulder. "The King's bench has heard the truth. No man can take him from you now."

Mary lifted her face, her tears wetting Hannah's wool sleeve.

"You rode across the Plain for us, Mrs. Thorne," the girl wept. "You rode through the blizzard to save my boy."

"I took the bishop's oath, Mary," Hannah replied softly. "A midwife does not leave her children in city cellars."

Women in the gallery leaned over the railings, wiping their eyes with their aprons; day-laborers in the back row stamped their heavy boots upon the floorboards in noisy approval.

Old Martha Gane, the blacksmith's wife, leaned from the gallery front, shouting down at the dock:

"God bless you, Mary! God bless your boy! The poor of Nether Bourne will stand by you!"

Constable Barnes struck the railing with his iron-tipped staff to restore order, but his own face was red with excitement. At the table, Mary sat upon the low oak bench, unlacing her linsey-woolsey bodice with trembling fingers. Her milk, which had been dried and checked by the cold of the Warminster cell, began to flow; she guided the infant's rooting mouth to her breast. The child took the nipple with a deep, hungry gasp, his tiny hands kneading against her skin, his cries turning to soft, contented murmurs of nourishment.

Reverend Thistlethwayte looked upon mother and child, his lips moving in silent thanksgiving.

"The natural law of God," the vicar whispered to Squire Lambert. "Stronger than all the penal statutes of Parliament."

Squire Lambert nodded his heavy head, wiping his eyes with his silk pocket handkerchief.

"By God, Thistlethwayte, you are right," the squire muttered. "I'd rather have delivered that babe from the gallows than won fifty guineas on the Salisbury races."

Women in the gallery leaned over the railings, wiping their eyes with their aprons; day-laborers in the back row stamped their heavy boots upon the floorboards in noisy approval.

Sir William Harford raised his hand to restore order. He looked across the crowded hall toward the deep shadows beneath the gallery stairs.

"Constable Barnes," Harford commanded. "Bring Edward Callow to the bar."

# Chapter 32: The Magistrate's Bench

The great hall of Bourne Hall was packed with eighty men, smelling of wet frieze coats, stale beer, horse-sweat, and coal smoke.

At the upper end of the room, upon a raised dais beneath the carved royal arms of King George, sat the bench of magistrates.

Sir William Harford presided in the center, seated in a high-backed chair of dark oak lined with crimson velvet. He wore a dark blue coat with silver-gilt buttons, a powdered bag-wig that sat slightly crooked upon his forehead, and steel-rimmed spectacles perched on his hawk-like nose. His left foot, wrapped in bandages of flannel to ease his gout, rested upon a low stool covered with sheepskin.

Beside him sat two junior justices: the Reverend Arthur Thistlethwayte, the aged vicar of Nether Bourne, whose thin white hands rested upon an open Latin testament; and Squire Lambert of Heytesbury, a red-faced gentleman in hunting boots who was paring an apple with a horn-handled pocket knife.

Below the dais, at a long table covered with green baize, sat Mr. Tarrant, the magistrate's clerk, his quills scratching furiously across ruled sheets of legal foolscap.

In the front row of the spectators' benches sat Thomas Callow.

The tenant farmer wore his finest Sunday suit of superfine broadcloth, a scarlet waistcoat with brass buttons, and a clean white neckcloth tied with a lawyer's knot. His silver-headed cane rested between his knees; his heavy face was calm, settled into an expression of patient, righteous civic duty. Beside him sat Counselor Garrow, a sharp-featured barrister from Salisbury with a powdered tie-wig and a sheaf of parchment briefs tied with red tape.

At the bar of the court, guarded by two parish constables with brass-headed staves, stood Mary Miller.

The girl looked shrunk to half her former size. Her linsey-woolsey bodice hung loose upon her shoulders; her wrists were bruised from the iron handcuffs she had worn in the Warminster lock-up; her dark hair fell in tangles around her white, sunken cheeks. She kept her eyes fixed upon the floorboards, her lips moving in a soundless, continuous prayer.

Counselor Garrow was standing at the baize table, holding a sheepskin warrant in his left hand.

"May it please your worships," Garrow began, bowing low to the three justices. "The matter before the bench this afternoon touches the peace of our Sovereign Lord the King and the good order of the parish of Nether Bourne. Under the statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First, chapter twenty-seven—an act entitled *An Act to prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children*—the law of England speaks with unmistakable severity."

He opened a calfskin volume of Dalton's *Country Justice* and read with relish:

"'If any woman be delivered of any issue of her body, male or female, which being born alive, should by the laws of this realm be a bastard, and that she endeavour privately, either by drowning or secret burying thereof, or any other way, either by herself or the procuring of others, so to conceal the death thereof, that it may not come to light, whether it were born alive or not, but be concealed, she shall suffer death as in case of murder.'"

Garrow closed the volume with a heavy thud that echoed through the silent hall.

"The statute, your worships, creates a presumption of capital murder from the single fact of concealment. The mother who hides her delivery must produce at least one witness to prove that the child was dead at the birth; or the law presumes she destroyed the fruit of her body to avoid the shame and the parish burden."

He turned, gesturing toward Thomas Callow.

"On the night of Friday, the seventeenth of November, Goodman Callow, the principal tenant of this manor and a churchwarden whose family has tilled the soil of Nether Bourne for three generations, was aroused by cries from his lambing outbuilding. Upon repairing thither with a lantern, he found the prisoner lying in the straw, having privily delivered herself of an infant. When questioned by Master Callow and the parish officers, she refused to declare the father's name, and she could produce no living child."

Sir William Harford shifted his bandaged foot upon the sheepskin stool, wincing with pain.

"Was the body of the infant discovered upon the premises, Mr. Garrow?" the magistrate asked.

"It was not, Sir William," Garrow replied smoothly. "The parish constables have dragged the sheep-wash, searched the dung-pits, and probed the hay-stacks for three miles around High Lea Farm. No living child has been found; no dead body has been recovered. The conclusion in law is inescapable: the infant was destroyed and secretly conveyed away."

The attorney spread his black silk gown with a practiced sweep of his forearms, turning to face the full assembly of magistrates upon the elevated bench.

"Consider the statutory mischief, gentlemen," Garrow argued, his sharp, nasal voice echoing into the high timbered rafters of the courtroom. "If every casual pauper who drops a child in a field byway is permitted to fix that offspring upon the wealthiest township in the hundred merely by naming a reputable yeoman, no tenant farmer between Salisbury and the Bristol Channel could safely hire a maid-servant. The Act of King William was framed for the explicit protection of property. The statute requires an examination taken before two justices of the peace, under oath, prior to the quarter sessions. Where is that examination? The churchwardens of Nether Bourne have produced neither an affidavit of bastardy nor an order of filiation signed by Sir William Harford."

Harford shifted painfully in his cushioned chair, his bandaged right foot elevated upon the velvet hassock. He struck the table with the flat of his palm, rattling the leaden inkstand.

"The girl was detained in the Warminster cellar without warrant, Mr. Garrow," Harford said coldly. "She was denied access to her friends, denied the attendance of a licensed midwife, and locked up within ten days of her delivery. Do you call that the King's justice, or the private persecution of a parish ratepayer?"

He turned a sharp, disdainful eye upon Mary Miller.

"The prisoner has attempted to cast the blame of this unnatural crime upon the respectable son of our churchwarden, asserting that Edward Callow carried the child away to a carrier's wagon. But where is the proof of this monstrous accusation? Edward Callow is a dutiful son, educated at Warminster grammar school, who denies upon his honor that he ever had unlawful commerce with this hired servant."

Squire Lambert cut off a slice of his apple with his pocket knife, chewing it slowly as he looked down at Garrow.

"A young man's honor is easily asserted in a petty session, Counselor," the squire remarked dryly. "I have heard fifty yeomen's sons swear upon their honor that they never touched a dairy maid, until the affiliation order was sealed and the weekly four shillings demanded from their fathers' pockets."

Garrow smiled with oily condescension.

"Squire Lambert's worldly experience is well known," Garrow countered. "Yet the law requires evidence, not conjecture. Who was present in the outbuilding? The parish constable has taken the deposition of Thomas Callow. Master Callow swears that when he entered the barn at half-past one, he saw blood upon the straw, but no living infant. He questioned the girl; she was sullen, mute, and desperate."

Counselor Garrow smoothed the lace ruffles at his wrists, looking around the crowded room with theatrical assurance.

"The law of England, your worships," Garrow continued, his voice ringing off the oak paneling, "does not depend upon sentiment or country gossip. In the third volume of Sir Edward Coke's *Institutes of the Laws of England*, at the hundredth and seventy-fifth page, the great oracle of our jurisprudence lays down the maxim: *Concealment of the death is an evidence of the murder.* When an unmarried female retires into a private outbuilding, away from the eyes of matrons and neighbors, and there brings forth an infant which is never again seen by human eye, the common law presumes the destruction of the babe."

Reverend Thistlethwayte shifted his spectacles on his nose, looking troubled.

"Yet Coke speaks of concealment of the *death*, Counselor," the old clergyman observed gently. "If the child were born dead, or if it were conveyed away alive by other hands without the mother's consent, where is the malice prepense that constitutes murder?"

"The statute of King James supplies the malice, Reverend Sir!" Garrow replied sharply. "The statute was passed expressly to cure the defect of the common law. Under King James's act, the burden of proof is shifted to the prisoner. She must prove by one witness that the babe was born dead; otherwise, the law adjudges her guilty of capital felony without benefit of clergy."

The barrister turned to Mr. Tarrant.

"Mr. Tarrant, read the sworn information of the churchwarden."

The clerk took up a sheet of foolscap and read in a clear, nasal tone:

"'The information of Thomas Callow of High Lea Farm, yeoman, taken upon oath this twentieth day of November, 1792, before Sir William Harford, Knight. Who saith: That on Friday night last, about the hour of one in the morning, hearing a disturbance among his sheep in the lower fold, he proceeded to the outbuilding called the lambing barn. That he there found Mary Miller, single woman, recently delivered of an infant. That upon demanding where the infant was, she answered not a word. That he saw neither living child nor dead corpse. That he verily believes the said child was destroyed or privily buried in the river meadows by the said Mary Miller or some person acting by her procurement.'"

Mary Miller lifted her face from her hands, her eyes wide with horror.

"It is false!" she cried, her voice echoing through the timbered roof. "Master Callow took the boy with his own hands! He wrapped him in an oilskin sack! He swore if I spoke a word, he would have me whipped at the cart's tail from Nether Bourne to Warminster!"

Sir William Harford struck the table with his wooden gavel.

"Silence, prisoner," Harford said sternly, though his hawk-like eyes lingered upon Callow's impassive countenance. "You will have your turn to answer when the information is closed."

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte leaned forward, his spectacles resting upon his open Bible.

"Was no midwife called to the outbuilding, Counselor?" the old clergyman asked. "In sixty years of pastoral care in this valley, I have never known a mother deliver alone in a winter gale without seeking the aid of her neighbors. Did no woman attend her?"

"The prisoner claims she was attended by Hannah Thorne, the licensed midwife of this parish," Garrow answered with a thin, mocking smile. "A summons was duly issued by Constable Barnes for Mrs. Thorne to attend this bench at one of the clock. It is now half-past two. The midwife has failed to appear."

Garrow spread his white hands with theatrical gravity.

"The midwife is absent. The child is absent. There is no evidence of a living birth known to the law of England. On behalf of the churchwardens and overseers of Nether Bourne, I move that Mary Miller be committed to the county gaol at Fisherton, there to remain until the next general Assizes for the county of Wilts, upon the capital charge of wilful murder."

Sir William Harford took his quill from the lead inkstand. He laid the tip of the feather to the sheepskin warrant lying before him on the baize cloth.

"Mary Miller," Harford said, looking down at the weeping girl. "Have you any witness to call before this warrant is signed?"

Mary lifted her hands, the iron handcuffs clinking against her waist.

"I have no witness, your honor," she sobbed. "Only the Lord God in heaven, who saw my baby breathing upon the straw."

Sir William dipped the quill in the black ink.

A clear, steady voice rang out from the rear of the hall:

"The midwife is not absent, Sir William."

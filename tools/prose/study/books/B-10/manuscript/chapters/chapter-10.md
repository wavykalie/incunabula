# Chapter 10: The Parish Rumor

On Sunday morning, the bells of Saint Mary's Nether Bourne tolled for the eleven o'clock service.

The village gathered in the church porch beneath the ancient Norman arch. It was a cold, bright morning; the frost of the past three days had melted under a pale sun, leaving the gravel paths soft with yellow mud. The men stood in their best frieze coats and gaiters, scraping their iron-shod boots against the iron scrapers by the door, while the women gathered in clusters near the yew tree, their woolen shawls pinned tight beneath their chins.

Hannah stood near the porch pillar, her hands tucked in her cloak pockets. In a village of two hundred souls, nothing moved without eyes upon it.

"They say old Miller's girl has gone from High Lea," whispered Martha Gane, the blacksmith's wife, leaning close to Hannah's ear. Martha's eyes were sharp, darting toward the lych-gate where the farmers were assembling. "Vanished between Thursday night and Friday morning. My boy Tom was hauling hurdle poles past the lower barn at dawn on Friday and saw Master Callow's light cart heading down the Warminster road with a canvas tilt tied over the back."

"She had kin in Somerset," Hannah said evenly, keeping her eyes on the path.

"Somerset!" snorted Goodwife Povey, whose husband farmed ten acres of water meadow. "A dairy maid does not leave four months of unpaid wages behind to visit Somerset kin in the middle of winter! And she was swelling under her aprons since Lammas, as everyone in the parish knows. When a maid gets that shape, she does not walk to Somerset—she goes to the ditch, or she goes to the parish cage."

Down the path came Goodman Thomas Callow.

He walked with the heavy, deliberate tread of a man who owned fifty dairy cows and six teams of working oxen. He wore his finest Sunday coat of dark blue broadcloth with gilt buttons, a spotted neckcloth, and polished top boots with yellow cuffs. Beside him walked his son Edward. The young man looked pale and sickly, his eyes bloodshot, his hat pulled down to his brows as he stared fixedly at the churchyard gravel.

The farmers touched their hats as Callow passed.

"Good day to you, Master Callow," called out George Finch, the parish overseer of the poor. Finch was a small, sour-faced tailor who spent his days checking settlement certificates and driving vagrants across the parish brook. "A sharp frost for the wheat, sir."

Callow stopped beneath the porch archway. He took a pinch of snuff from an ivory box, inhaled it with a loud snort, and dusted his waistcoat with a red handkerchief.

"Good day, Finch," Callow said, his booming voice carrying across the entire churchyard. "The frost will do no harm to the early sown. It sweetens the ground."

Finch stepped closer, lowering his voice, though not enough to prevent Hannah from hearing every word.

"I hear talk about your dairy maid, Master Callow. Mary Miller. The Warminster constable was in the Red Lion yesterday asking questions. They say a wench was taken up by the town watch on Friday night, wandering near the chalk pit on the common, raving with the fever and her linen covered in blood. They have her in the lock-up."

Edward Callow flinched as if a whip had struck his shoulders. He took a step toward the church door, his hands trembling against his coat skirts.

Thomas Callow did not flinch. His face remained calm, solid as a block of Chilmark stone.

"The girl was discharged from my service a week ago for insolence and thieving milk," Callow said loudly, looking directly at the crowd of cottagers. "Where she went is her own concern. If she has fallen into bad company at Warminster, the magistrates will deal with her. The parish of Nether Bourne has no responsibility for a runaway servant who broke her yearly hiring contract."

"Aye, but the child, Master Callow?" Finch pressed, his ferret-like eyes blinking. "The constable says she was delivered of an infant within the day, but no child was found upon her."

Callow's jaw hardened. He turned his head slowly, and his small, dark eyes met Hannah's across the crowded porch.

"Then she has made away with it," Callow said, his voice dropping into a cold, flat cadence that silenced the porch. "And if a mother conceals the death of her bastard, the statute of King James will give her what she deserves at the end of a hempen rope."

He turned and strode into the dim nave of the church, his heavy boots echoing on the ancient flagstones.

The congregation parted before him like water before a barge. Edward followed in his father's wake, his head bowed, his hands shoved deep into his pockets.

Hannah remained by the Norman pillar. She felt the cold stone through the wool of her cloak. Across the churchyard, the jackdaws circled the square tower, their harsh cries echoing over the leafless elder trees that shadowed the graves of the unbaptized.

The parish beadle struck his iron-tipped staff against the flagstones, calling the people to prayer.

Hannah stepped into the south aisle, walking past the carved oak pews where the tenant families sat in their broadcloth and furs. Near the chancel step, on the north wall, was the brass memorial plate of the Callow family: three generations of tenant farmers who had held the land since the Great Plague, buried beneath Latin mottoes and heraldic wheat sheaves.

She took her seat on the back bench of rough deal, where the parish widows and paupers sat beneath the bell-ropes. Beside her, old Widow Barnes pulled her tattered wool shawl over her coughing chest.

The rector, Mr. Thistlethwayte, climbed the three steps of the oak pulpit. He was eighty years old, with shaking hands and watery eyes that looked past the heads of his flock toward the colored glass of the east window. He opened his leather sermon book and gave out the text:

*"Deliver him that suffereth wrong from the hand of the oppressor; and be not fainthearted when thou sittest in judgment."*

Hannah folded her hands inside her apron pockets. Beneath her right palm lay the bone scissors, wrapped in their boiled linen. She bowed her head, listening to the dry voice of the old priest droning above the winter coughs of the village.

Across the aisle, Edward Callow sat with his shoulders hunched, staring at the stone flagstones between his boots.

The congregation rose as the old rector finished his sermon and gave the blessing from the chancel steps.

Hannah remained in her seat beneath the bell-ropes until the tenant farmers and their wives had filed down the central aisle in their heavy broadcloth and beaver hats. Thomas Callow walked with his head held high, his silver-topped cane striking the flagstones with arrogant authority, acknowledging the bows of the cottagers with curt nods of his chin. Behind him walked Edward, pale and hollow-eyed, shrinking from every glance.

In the churchyard, the crowd lingered beneath the bare branches of the great yew tree.

"Did you hear what Callow said in the porch?" whispered Martha Gane, catching Hannah's arm as they walked toward the lych-gate. "He swears the girl was discharged for thieving! But my brother Tom says he saw Callow's cart driving toward the Salisbury turnpike on Friday morning with an oilskin bundle in the back!"

"People see many things in the fog, Martha," Hannah cautioned quietly.

"Aye, but where is the babe?" pressed Goodwife Povey, her red face pinched with winter cold. "If the girl made away with it, as Callow claims, the constable will find the little body in the chalk pit! And if she did not... if someone took it from her... then there is blood on the hands of the masters of this parish!"

Down the road, George Finch, the overseer of the poor, was talking earnestly with Constable Barnes beside the village stocks.

"We must have the midwife's deposition," Finch was saying, his shrill tailor's voice carrying over the frosted hedge. "Sir William will hold a Petty Sessions at Bourne Hall before the week is out. If there is a bastard chargeable to Nether Bourne, we must know who got it, so the parish rates do not bear the cost!"

Hannah did not wait to hear more. She drew her cloak around her shoulders and walked swiftly down the lane toward her cottage beside the bourne.

The lines were drawn. The overseer cared only for the poor rates; the tenant farmer cared only for his lease and his name; and the magistrate cared only for the King's statute.

Tomorrow, the writ would come. And when it came, she would be ready.

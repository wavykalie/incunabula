# Chapter 24: The Drover's Track

The north wind off the Salisbury Plain had stripped the downland beeches bare as bleached bones.

Hannah Thorne rode Bess along the ancient ridgeway that ran from Heytesbury toward the cathedral spire eighteen miles to the south-east. It was noon, but the winter sky was the color of hammered lead, heavy with the dull, bruised promise of fresh snow. Below her in the winding valley, the river Wylye lay hidden beneath rolling banks of white river-fog that crept through the willow beds like smoke from a burning lime-kiln.

The ridgeway was not a turnpike road; it was the old British track, five hundred cubits broad between the chalk ditch-banks, where sheep, cattle, and packhorses had been driven to the Wiltshire fairs since the time of the ancient heathen kings. The turf was frozen into ridged waves, hard as iron slag, with crusts of granular snow filling every sheep-track and cart-rut.

Hannah sat upright in her side-saddle, her frieze cloak pinned tightly around her shoulders with two iron bodkins.

Under her stays, the square of stained linen from the granary loft pressed cold against her ribs. In her basket, wrapped in three layers of boiled flannel, lay a horn lantern, an iron flint-box, a loaf of rye bread, a wedge of hard skim-milk cheese, and two stone bottles of elderberry cordial wrapped in thick woolen stockings.

Bess picked her way with steady deliberation, her iron shoes ringing against the flint nodules that cropped out through the thin chalk turf.

Every half-mile, an ancient earthen barrow rose beside the track—the resting places of forgotten chieftains who had hunted deer upon these hills before the Romans built their roads. Around the barrows grew stunted thorn bushes, their crooked black branches hung with withered red berries that rattled like dry shot in the gale.

Hannah had known this downland track for thirty winters.

As a young woman, she had ridden these high sheep-walks with her husband John, carrying ash timber from the Grovely copses to his wheelwright's shop. In twenty years of midwifery, she had traversed these ridges by night and day, in harvest heat and winter frost, answering the call of shepherds' wives who labored in solitary turf huts beside the sheep-folds.

She knew the signs of the Plain.

A kestrel hovering motionless against the gray clouds; a flock of fieldfares chattering in the gorse; the distant sound of church bells rising from the river villages—these were the companions of her solitary journeys.

A mile past the Heytesbury boundary stone, an old shepherd in a sheepskin coat stood leaning upon his crook beside a fold of wattle hurdles. His sixty ewes were huddled together in the leeward corner of the bank, their fleece yellow with grease, their warm breath curling into the freezing air like white steam.

Hannah checked Bess's trot.

"Good day, shepherd," she called out against the wind.

The old man turned his weathered face, his rheumy eyes peering from beneath a battered woolen cap. He touched his forehead with a thumb calloused by fifty years of shearing.

"Good day, Mrs. Thorne," the shepherd answered, his voice cracked with the frost. "You're far from Nether Bourne in this weather."

"Did John Pike's cart pass this way on Friday night?" Hannah asked.

The shepherd spat a brown stream of tobacco juice onto the frosted grass.

"Ay, mistress. He passed an hour past midnight. He had forty steers on the hoof, heading for the city shambles, and two heavy carthorses in his wain. The steers were blowing hard in the sleet, and Pike was cursing his near-horse with every stride."

"Did you hear a child cry in the cart?"

The old man looked at her keenly, his bushy white eyebrows drawing together over his hooked nose.

"I heard a sound like a young lamb bleating under the cart-tilt," he said slowly. "I shouted to Pike: *Have you got an early yeanling in the straw, John?* And Pike shouted back: *I have a passenger for Salisbury, shepherd, and none of your business!* Then he cracked his whip, and the team rumbled down into the combe."

The shepherd stepped closer to the pony's shoulder, pointing with his crook toward the chalk cutting below Yarnbury rampart.

"Look at the tracks where the wheel rut cuts the ditch," the old man said, his voice dropping to a gravelly rasp. "Pike was not driving his usual broad-tired carrier wagon with the six-horse team. He had a light two-horse van with iron tires, built for speed on the turnpike. The off wheel threw gravel into the juniper bushes because he took the bend at a trot. A carrier does not trot his cattle on Salisbury Plain in December unless he is paid in gold to beat the night."

Hannah leaned down from her saddle, studying the fresh indentations in the crust. The iron tires had cut through two inches of rime into the white marl beneath, leaving distinct crescent marks where the caulkins of the off-wheeler had struck the flint.

"How long ago, Jacob?" she asked.

"Two hours before midday," the shepherd replied. "The snow has partly drifted into the ruts, but you can follow them easily enough as far as the crossways at Deptford Inn. If he turned south for Fisherton, he will find the valley road blocked by the sheep-drift near Steeple Langford."

"I thank you, neighbor," Hannah said.

She touched Bess with her heel, riding down the slope into the dry bottom of Dead Man's Combe.

Here the north wind was cut off by the high turf ramparts of an ancient ditch; the air was dead, cold, and still, smelling of wet flint, damp moss, and the sour sheep-wash of fifty lambing seasons. At the bottom of the combe stood a ruined sheep-shelter of unmortared chalk stones, its thatched roof long since rotted away, its hazel hurdles gray with lichen.

Beside the ruined shelter, the cart ruts turned sharply off the ridgeway.

Hannah pulled Bess up. She swung down from the side-saddle, her knees cracking with the cold, and led the pony by the bridle into the shelter of the stone wall.

In the leeward corner of the wall, where the snow had not drifted, the ashes of a small fire lay between two large chalk boulders. Beside the hearth lay three empty beer-corkings, a gnawed mutton bone, and a torn scrap of coarse sacking stained with black tar.

Hannah knelt upon the frosted grass.

She picked up the scrap of sacking. It was coarse, six-thread hemp, marked with the red ochre circle that Thomas Callow used to brand his yearling wethers at Michaelmas. The inner side of the cloth was lined with dry oat straw.

She brought the straw to her nose.

It smelled of sour cow's milk and the warm, sweet musk of a young infant's skin.

John Pike had halted here on Friday night. The drover had pulled his cart behind the stone wall to wait out the worst of the midnight sleet. He had built a fire of gorse roots; he had warmed his beer; and he had taken the child from the cart-tail to feed it.

Hannah examined the ground with obstetrical care.

Beside the hearth lay an empty sheep-horn, its narrow tip cut off and plugged with a soft quill. It was a rustic sucking-bottle, such as downland shepherds use to rear motherless cade-lambs in the winter folding. On the frozen earth beside the horn lay two drops of yellow cow's milk, curdled into hard white beads by the frost.

The child had been alive here.

He had sucked the cow's milk from the horn; he had swallowed; his little heart had been beating two hours after Thomas Callow carried him out of the lambing shed.

Hannah rose from her knees, her eyes burning with a fierce, quiet certainty.

She walked out from the shelter to examine the wheel tracks where the cart had turned back onto the ridge.

The tracks were fresh, cutting straight through the frozen grass. Beside the ruts were the hoof-prints of two heavy draught horses, their shoes calked with iron studs for the ice. A man's boots had walked beside the near-side wheel: broad, square-toed cowhide boots with iron heel-plates, walking with the heavy, rolling limp of John Pike, who had broken his knee twenty years ago under a timber wain.

Beside the wheel track, half-covered by drifting snow, lay a small object that caught the pale light.

Hannah bent down and picked it up.

It was a strip of bleached linen, six inches long and two fingers broad, torn roughly from the hem of a child's swaddling band. It was pinned together with a small brass bodkin—one of the bodkins Hannah had left in her basket in the lambing shed on Friday night.

John Pike had re-swaddled the child here in the combe. The babe had wet his linen in the cart; the drover had taken the dry strip from Hannah's bundle and pinned it tight before facing the blizzard on the high plain.

Hannah held the small brass bodkin in her palm.

She looked at the ruined dry-stone wall where the sheep-hurdles lay half-buried in the snow. Ten years ago, in the great frost of 1783, she had ridden into this same combe to attend old goody Barnes's daughter. The girl had been caught in a snow-drift while bringing butter from the sheep-fold; she had dropped her child upon a bed of frozen gorse. Hannah had arrived with two stone bottles of hot water, wrapped the newborn babe in her own flannel petticoat, and carried mother and child three miles through the drifts to the parish almshouse.

Both had lived. That child was now a stout lad of ten, working the oxen at the lower water meadows.

The soil of this downland was hard, sour with chalk flints and beaten by winter gales, but the folk who tilled it had iron in their bones. They did not perish easily; they clung to life like the dwarf juniper bushes on the ditch ramparts. And this child—Mary Miller's son—had survived forty miles of winter road because the stubborn life of the Wiltshire soil was in his blood.

The metal was cold against her skin, but it was an iron warrant written by the hand of Providence. It linked the lambing barn at High Lea to the drover's cart, and the drover's cart to the child in Salisbury.

She looked out across the white downland plateau.

The wind was freshening from the east, carrying pellets of ice that struck her wool hood like bird-shot. To the left, the ancient ditch of Grim's Ditch stretched across the chalk for three miles, a black line cutting through the white snow. She knew that beyond that ditch lay the boundary of the cathedral parish of Fisherton Anger. If the drover had cleared the ditch before sunrise on Saturday morning, the child had entered the city before the morning market opened.

She smoothed Bess's neck. The pony turned her head, nudging Hannah's shoulder with her frosted muzzle, asking for her oats.

"We have sixteen miles to Salisbury, Bess," Hannah said softly. "And the child was breathing when they crossed this combe."

She climbed back into the side-saddle, settling her skirts over Bess's flank.

Bess snorted, tossing her white mane, and scrambled out of the combe back onto the high ridge.

The wind met them on the crest like an open hand. It blew straight from the east, carrying fine, needle-like grains of frozen sleet that stung the skin of Hannah's cheeks until her lips were numb. But she did not turn her face away.

Across the great white plateau of the Plain, the horizon stretched empty and vast for miles. There were no trees, no cottages, no church towers; only the long, swelling lines of the chalk downs, rising and falling like the waves of a frozen sea under the darkening winter sky.

In the far distance, twelve miles to the south, the thin gray spire of Salisbury Cathedral rose above the winter haze, sharp as a needle against the clouds.

Hannah gathered the leather reins into her numb fingers and urged the mare into a steady, reaching trot.

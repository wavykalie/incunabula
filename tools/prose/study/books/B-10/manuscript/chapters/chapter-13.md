# Chapter 13: The Statute of James

The magistrate laid down his quill and turned the heavy calfskin folio that lay open beside his elbow.

"Listen to the statute, Mrs. Thorne," Sir William said. His voice echoed off the book-lined walls with the dry authority of the law. "*Act of the Twenty-First Year of King James the First, Chapter Twenty-Seven: For the prevention of the murder of bastard children.*"

He ran his bony forefinger down the printed lines of black-letter type:

"*Whereas many lewd women that have been delivered of bastard children, to avoid their shame, and to escape punishment, do secretly bury or conceal the death of their children, and after, if the child be found dead, the said women do allege that the said child was born dead: Be it enacted, that if any woman be delivered of any issue of her body, which being born alive, should by the laws of this realm be a bastard, and that she endeavor privately, either by drowning or secret burying thereof, or any other way, either by herself or the procuring of others, so to conceal the death thereof, the said mother so offending shall suffer death as in case of murder, except such mother can make proof by one witness at the least, that the child (whose death was by her so intended to be concealed) was born dead.*"

Harford closed the heavy volume with a dull thud that raised a puff of dry leather dust.

"You understand the gravity of what you have sworn, Mrs. Thorne," Harford said, leaning his chin against his laced cravat. "If you swear the child was born dead, Mary Miller is guilty only of lewdness and vagrancy. She will be set in the stocks at Warminster market cross and returned to her parish of legal settlement."

He fixed his cold, gray eyes upon her face.

"But if you testify in open court at the Assizes that the child was born alive, and no living infant is produced before the grand jury, the law of England presumes murder. Mary Miller will hang by the neck from the gallows at Fisherton Anger before the spring assize is ended."

Hannah stood motionless. The air in the study smelled of ink, wax, and the dry paper of dead centuries.

"And what of the men who took him from the straw, Sir William?" she asked.

Harford tapped his ivory cane against his footstool. "The law demands evidence, Mrs. Thorne. Not village whispers, not rumors from the church porch. If Thomas Callow took the child, where is it? If Edward Callow carried it away, to whom did he deliver it? Unless a living babe is brought before this bench, your testimony will not save Mary Miller. It will hang her."

Hannah bowed her head. "The truth is what God heard in the lambing shed, Sir William. I can swear to nothing else."

Harford waved his hand toward the door. "Go home, Mrs. Thorne. You will be summoned to the Petty Sessions on Friday. Until then, speak to no one of this deposition."

Hannah stood before the magistrate's desk as the winter afternoon faded into blue dusk.

Through the tall sash casements of the study, the shadow of the great cedar tree on the lawn stretched across the snow like a giant hand. The silence in the room was heavy, broken only by the ticking of the bracket clock and the muffled coughing of the old footman in the corridor outside.

Sir William turned the heavy calfskin page of the statute book with a dry rustle.

"The law of England is an ancient engine, Mrs. Thorne," he said softly, staring into the red embers of the coal fire. "It was framed by men who knew that human nature is corrupt, that passions are wild as mountain water, and that without the terror of the rope, the countryside would be filled with the unburied bones of murdered innocents. It is a harsh law. Sometimes an unjust law in its particular operation. But it is the only shield that preserves the peace of this realm."

"The peace of the realm was not broken by Mary Miller, Sir William," Hannah said. "It was broken by the man who put her in an unheated lambing pen to save his family from parish disgrace."

Harford sighed, a sound that seemed to come from the depths of his aching bones. He closed the book, took his ivory cane, and pushed himself upright from his chair.

"The world is not governed by midwives, Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate whispered. "It is governed by land, lease, and statute. Bear that in mind when you take the oath on Friday."

The magistrate turned back to the legal folio, his fingers tracing the margin of the King James statute.

"A statute of blood," Harford whispered, almost to himself. "Framed when the Puritans ruled the Parliament, to terrify unwed wenches into holy marriage. In sixty years of practice, I have seen twenty women hanged under its provisions. Half of them were simple dairy girls whose babes were stillborn in the ditches, with no witness to swear to their innocence."

He looked up at Hannah, his eyes reflecting the orange glow of the grate.

"You are that witness, Mrs. Thorne. But your testimony cuts both ways. If you swear the child lived, you clear Mary Miller of having dropped a dead babe in secret; but you bind her to produce the living soul, or hang as a murderess. You carry a two-edged sword into this court."

"The truth is never a sword against the innocent, your honor," Hannah said.

Harford closed the book with a heavy sigh. "We shall see on Friday, Mrs. Thorne. We shall see."

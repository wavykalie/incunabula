# Chapter 35: The Examination of Edward

Edward Callow did not wait for the constable's staff to touch his shoulder.

He had been standing in the deep shadow beneath the gallery stairs, his frieze coat collar turned up to his ears, his hat clutched in both hands against his chest. When the magistrate spoke his name, he stumbled out into the center aisle as if an invisible rope were dragging him toward the green baize table.

His face was the color of sour curd. His knees knocked together beneath his corduroy breeches; he stopped two paces from the bench, staring at the floorboards between his muddy boots.

"Edward Callow," Sir William Harford said, leaning his elbows upon the baize cloth. "You have heard the testimony of the sworn midwife of this parish."

Edward gave a ragged gasp, but no words came from his throat.

Thomas Callow sprang to his feet once more from the front bench, his walking cane brandished in the air.

"Do not speak, Edward!" the father shouted, his voice cracking with fury. "You are not bound to criminate yourself before this bench! Garrow, advise him! Tell him to hold his tongue!"

"Sit down, Master Callow!" Harford thundered, his wooden gavel coming down with a tremendous crack upon the table. "If you utter one more word in this court, I will commit you to Fisherton gaol for contempt of the King's commission! Constable Barnes, take that cane from his hand!"

Barnes stepped forward, wresting the silver-headed cane from Callow's grasp. The farmer sank back into his oak chair, his teeth grinding together in helpless rage.

Sir William turned back to the young man.

"Edward Callow," the magistrate said, his voice dropping into quiet, measured gravity. "You are upon the threshold of your manhood. You have seen this young woman stand at this bar, loaded with felon's irons, facing the gallows at Salisbury for the murder of your child. Look at her."

Edward lifted his head.

He looked at Mary Miller. The girl was standing three feet away, holding her baby against her chest, her dark eyes looking at him not with hatred, but with a terrible, sorrowful pity.

The child in her arms let out a soft, contented whimper, his little hand reaching out to touch the brass buttons of Edward's waistcoat.

That small sound broke the last remnant of the young man's defiance.

Edward dropped to his knees upon the stone floor. He buried his face in his hands, his whole body shaking with long, convulsive sobs that echoed through the quiet hall.

"It is my child, your honor!" he cried, the words tearing from his throat. "I am the father! I lay with Mary in the dairy loft after the sheep-washing! I swore to her by the Holy Gospel that I would take her to the church and make her an honest wife!"

A great sigh, like the wind passing through an autumn wheat field, rose from the laborers in the gallery.

Squire Lambert leaned over the bench, his face flushed with rustic indignation.

"You promised her marriage, young man?" Lambert barked. "And why did you not carry her to the church like an honest Englishman, instead of leaving her to drop your child in a sheep-shed?"

"Father swore he would turn me out, your worship," Edward cried, his voice breaking into a shrill wail. "He said Mary was a common vagrant whose family had taken parish relief in Bishopstrow. He said if I took her to church, he would burn the lease and leave High Lea to our cousins in Devizes. He kept me locked in the parlor on Friday morning; he watched every road with his horsewhip; he swore if I went near the lambing barn, he would strike me down like a dog!"

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte shook his white head in profound sorrow.

"And you valued forty acres of pasture above a human soul, Edward?" the clergyman asked. "You valued your father's broadcloth above the life of your own flesh?"

"I was afraid, Mr. Thistlethwayte," Edward sobbed, beating his breast with his fists. "God help me, I was a coward! I thought Father would find a good nurse in Salisbury; he told me the child would be reared by honest folk and that Mary would take service in another valley where no one knew her shame!"

"And what of Friday night?" Harford demanded. "Who carried the child to John Pike's cart?"

"Father did!" Edward sobbed, lifting his wet face to the bench. "I wanted to fetch Hannah Thorne at dusk, but Father locked me in the stable. He said if the parish heard of the child, the Earl's steward would give the farm lease to our cousins in Devizes. At midnight, when Mary was screaming in the barn, he let me go for Hannah; but he swore if I spoke a word of paternity, he would turn me out onto the turnpike without a shilling!"

He turned and pointed a trembling finger at Thomas Callow.

"He paid John Pike twenty shillings to take the boy to Salisbury! Pike was to leave him with his cousin in Sheep Street! Father said the babe would die of the thrush before the week was out, and no one would ever know the difference!"

Edward reached into the breast pocket of his coat. He pulled out a worn leather pocketbook tied with a whipcord thong and laid it upon the table before the clerk.

His hands shook so violently that the whipcord knot took two minutes to untie.

"Look at the third leaf, your worship," Edward cried, pointing with a trembling finger. "There is the memorandum of the twenty pounds. Father made me write the entry myself in the parlor on Michaelmas night: *Received of Edward Callow, twenty pounds of shearing money, to be held against his marriage or good behavior.* And underneath, in Father's own hand: *Cancelled upon the agreement with J.P. for the removal of the child from Nether Bourne.*"

Squire Lambert leaned over the table, taking the pocketbook from Tarrant's fingers. He examined the coarse paper beneath his reading glass, comparing the brown ink with the signature on Callow's lease.

"The signature is Thomas Callow's beyond all doubt," Lambert declared, his voice heavy with contempt. "I have fifty receipts in my rent-chest signed with that identical flourish. The father took the boy's savings to buy the carriage of his own grandchild."

"There is the proof, Sir William," Edward wept. "That is my account book. On the flyleaf is written the agreement with John Pike, witnessed in my father's own hand: *To John Pike, carter, twenty shillings to convey the child to Salisbury, and two shillings a week for the keeping.* Father kept the book in his iron chest, but I took it this morning before I rode to court."

Mr. Tarrant took the pocketbook, opened the pages, and handed it to Sir William Harford.

Sir William read the entry through his spectacles. His face grew dark as a thundercloud.

"The entry is dated the sixteenth of November," Harford said, reading aloud. "'Agreed with J.P. to take the child from the parish for twenty shillings down, and two shillings weekly till Michaelmas.' It is signed with the initials *T.C.* in the well-known hand of Thomas Callow."

The magistrate looked down at Edward.

"Why did you not speak when Mary was arrested at Warminster, Edward?"

"Father threatened to disinherit me, your worship," the young man answered, tears streaming down his face. "He said he had thirty pounds lodged with Counselor Garrow to defend the parish, and that the girl would be transported to Botany Bay where she could do no harm to High Lea Farm. But when I saw Hannah Thorne in the stable yesterday, my heart failed me. I could not sleep for thinking of Mary in the cold cell."

He turned to Mary Miller, sinking lower on his knees until his forehead touched the hem of her russet skirt.

"Forgive me, Mary," he sobbed. "I was a coward. I listened to my father's pride instead of my own heart."

Mary looked down at him. She did not draw her skirt away, but her hand remained tight upon her sleeping son.

"I forgive you, Edward," she said in a low, trembling whisper. "For the sake of the child."

Sir William Harford laid the pocketbook upon the baize cloth beside the Bible.

"Rise, Edward Callow," the magistrate said. "You have played the part of a coward and a villain; yet by speaking the truth today, you have saved an innocent woman from the gallows and preserved your soul from the guilt of blood. You will remain in court to answer the affiliation order under the statute."

Sir William Harford turned to his clerk.

"Mr. Tarrant, enter this confession upon the minutes: Edward Callow, upon his examination before this bench, doth voluntarily confess and acknowledge that he is the true and reputed father of the male bastard child born of the body of Mary Miller on the seventeenth of November last past."

The magistrate looked at Edward, who remained kneeling on the flagstones.

"Under the act of the sixth year of His present Majesty, chapter thirty-one, you are hereby ordered to stand committed until you shall give security to indemnify the parish of Nether Bourne, or enter into a recognizance with two sufficient sureties to appear at the next quarter sessions."

Edward lifted his face, wiping his wet cheeks.

"I will give the security, Sir William," the young man said. "I have thirty pounds from my sheep-shearing. I will pay every penny that the court orders for Mary and the boy."

He turned his eyes upon Thomas Callow.

The courtroom fell into an ominous, breathless silence as Sir William reached for his gavel.

# Chapter 31: The Return to Bourne Hall

The cathedral bells of Salisbury were striking seven through the morning mist when the two-wheeled gig rolled out through the Castle Gate.

Simon Clark, the carrier, drove with his ash whip between his knees, his woolen mittens gripping the leather reins. Beside him on the horse-hair cushion sat Hannah Thorne, wrapped in two heavy frieze cloaks. In her lap rested the wicker basket, lined with sheep's fleece and warmed by two stone bottles filled with boiling spring water. Deep inside the wool folds, the infant slept, his face flushed with the dry heat of the blankets.

Behind the gig, tied to the rear axle by a hempen halter, the gray pony Bess trotted with steady, rhythmic strides.

The snow had ceased during the night. A thin, watery sunlight broke through the winter clouds over Old Sarum, turning the frosted downland turf into a sheet of diamond-sparkle. But the air was biting cold; icicles two feet long hung from the thatched eaves of the wayside cottages along the Wilton turnpike.

"We have eighteen miles to Bourne Hall, Mrs. Thorne," Simon Clark remarked, flicking the mare's shoulder with the whip-lash. "If the cutting at Barford is choked with drifts, we'll lose half an hour on the lower meadow road."

"We cannot lose half an hour, Simon," Hannah answered quietly. "Sir William takes the bench at two o'clock. If Counselor Garrow moves the committal before we reach the hall, Mary Miller will be remanded to the Assizes without a hearing."

Clark nodded. He shifted his whip to his left hand and pulled a horn snuff-box from his waistcoat pocket.

"Old Sir William is a hard man on the bench, Mrs. Thorne," the carrier said, tapping the lid with his thumbnail. "He knows the statutes backward, from the Magna Charta down to the latest game act. When a poacher is brought before him, Sir William gives him twelve months in the house of correction without blinking an eye. But he has no love for tenant pride. He remembers when Thomas Callow's father was an unlettered carter on the Pembroke estate; and he knows that Callow has been buying up the small copyholds in the valley to make himself a great man at the county elections."

Clark took a pinch of brown rapee snuff, sneezing loudly into his red handkerchief.

"If you can produce that child alive before Sir William's eyes," the carrier chuckled, "Callow will find that forty sheep and a broadcloth coat won't save him from the King's bench. The old knight loves the law better than he loves any tenant in Wiltshire."

Clark nodded. He leaned forward over the dashboard, whistling a sharp country tune between his teeth, and urged the chestnut mare into a brisk, rolling canter.

The road wound along the floor of the Wylye valley.

To their left, the river ran swift and gray between black willow stumps; to their right, the steep chalk ramparts of Grovely Wood rose like frozen cliffs against the pale sky. They passed through Wishford and Steeple Langford without stopping. At each toll-bar, Clark shouted his name to the gatekeeper, threw a penny into the road, and drove straight through without checking his speed.

The infant stirred beneath the blankets as the iron tires rumbled across the stone bridges.

Hannah slipped her hand into the basket. Her fingers found the child's cheek. It was warm, soft as carded wool, his small mouth turning instinctively toward her palm to suck. She drew out her stone bottle, dipped a linen rag into the warm milk, and let him take the moisture without lifting him into the freezing wind.

"Hold fast, little soul," she whispered against the wicker rim. "You have eighteen miles to travel before you look upon your mother's face."

The journey was a continuous struggle against the winter road.

Between Wishford and Steeple Langford, the snow had drifted four feet deep in the sunken lane where the limestone walls trapped the wind. Twice the chestnut mare stumbled into a hidden ditch, the carriage shafts groaning as the near-wheel sank to the hub in freezing slush.

Simon Clark leaped down into the snow, his cowhide boots splashing through the yellow mud. He gripped the near-side spoke with both hands, shouting encouragement to the mare:

"Hup, Duchess! Hup, my hearty girl! Pull, you old rogue!"

Hannah held the wicker basket tight against her breast, shielding the wicker lid with the thick fold of her frieze cloak. Inside, the infant gave a sharp, fretful whimper as the gig tilted sideways; she slipped her warm finger into his mouth, murmuring quiet words until the wheel crested the rut and righted itself upon the gravel.

At noon they halted for ten minutes at the blacksmith's forge in Wylye.

While the smith cooled the chestnut mare's steaming bit with a bucket of snow, Hannah carried her basket into the warm smithy. By the roaring hearth of pit-coal, she unwrapped the baby. The child was awake, his dark eyes blinking at the shower of orange sparks that flew from the anvil. Hannah dried his limbs, changed the inner linen cloth, and warmed his feet before the coals.

"A fine boy, mistress," the blacksmith said, leaning upon his sledgehammer. "Whose lad is he?"

"He is the King's witness, master," Hannah said.

"He'll need to be stout of heart if he stands before Sir William," the smith remarked with a rough grin. "The old knight has the gout in his left foot today. When the gout takes him, he commits men to Fisherton for sneezing in the churchyard."

Hannah smiled grimly. She wrapped the infant tight, tucked the freshly heated stone bottle beneath his feet, and climbed back into the gig.

At twenty minutes before two, the carriage turned off the turnpike into the gravel avenue of Bourne Hall.

The long driveway was choked with vehicles: light gigs belonging to tenant farmers, heavy broad-wheeled farm wagons, and twenty saddle-horses tied to the iron railings of the deer park. Laborers from Nether Bourne, Bishopstrow, and Upton Lovell were gathered in small, excited clusters beneath the frosted beech trees, talking in low, urgent voices.

When they saw Simon Clark's gig rattling up the avenue with the midwife beside him, an electric hush fell across the yard.

"There she is!" whispered young Tom Gane, stepping aside to let the wheels pass. "The midwife has come!"

"She's too late," muttered an old carter from High Lea. "Callow's counselor has been inside with the magistrates for half an hour. The warrant is drawn."

"Warrant or no warrant," shouted young Giles the blacksmith's prentice, stepping up to the gig, "the midwife is here! Make way for Mrs. Thorne! Stand aside, you High Lea men!"

A dozen cotters and water-meadow laborers formed a rough lane through the press of horses and carts, their heavy ash sticks clearing a path to the portico. They knew the woman who walked between them. There was not a cottage in Nether Bourne where Hannah Thorne had not watched through the small hours of the night beside a sick woman's bed, holding the cold hands, brewing the chamomile decoctions, and receiving the new life into the light of the valley.

"God speed you, Hannah!" called out old goody Povey from the mounting block. "Speak for the poor girl!"

Hannah did not answer with words. She touched the brim of her hood, holding the basket closer against her ribs.

Hannah stepped down from the gig before the wheels had fully stopped.

She unfastened the halter of her pony Bess, handing the rope to Simon Clark.

"Rub her down with a dry sack, Simon," she said. "And give her a warm mash at the stables."

She drew her dark frieze cloak around her shoulders, tucking the wicker basket firmly beneath her left arm so that only the thick woolen fringe of the outer blanket showed above her elbow.

She walked up the broad stone steps of the hall.

At the carved oak doors stood Constable Barnes, his tricorn hat cocked over one ear, his five-foot iron-tipped staff barred across the entrance. When he saw Hannah Thorne mounting the steps, his jaw dropped, his small ferret eyes bulging in alarm.

"Mrs. Thorne!" Barnes stammered, his voice cracking. "You cannot enter now! Sir William has already taken his seat on the bench! Callow's attorney is speaking!"

Hannah did not check her stride. She walked straight at the constable's staff.

"Take down that timber, Barnes," she said, her voice dropping to a low, commanding rasp. "The King's witness does not wait upon a parish attorney."

She pushed the iron-tipped staff aside with her right hand and stepped across the threshold into the great hall.

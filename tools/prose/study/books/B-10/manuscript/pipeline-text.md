# Chapter 1: The Pewter Bodkin

The peat coals in the hearth had burned down to gray ash and a dull red eye. On the scrubbed deal table beside the chimney, Hannah Thorne laid out the instruments of her calling one by one upon a square of boiled bleached linen.

First came the bodkin. It was cast of dull pewter, six inches long, flattened at the eye and blunt at the tip. Her fingers knew every nick along its shank. For twenty-one years, through three reigns and two epidemics of the spotted fever, that blunt rod had cleared mucous from newborn throats and pressed down swollen tongues when infant breath hitched in the freezing air of downland cottages. Beside the bodkin lay the bone shears, their horn handles yellowed with age, their short blades honed on an Arkansas oilstone until they severed flax thread without fraying.

The wind off the chalk ridge rattled the leaded casement. November had come to the valley of the Wylye with sleet and early frost. Outside the flint walls of the cottage, the water of the chalk bourne ran swift and milky white between the watercress beds.

She touched the earthenware pot of mutton tallow. The grease was hard, smelling faintly of elder leaves and sulfur. In the bitter months, when sheep were brought down from the downs into the valley folds, cold chapped the hands of cottagers until the flesh split to the raw meat. A midwife whose palms were rough as an oak rasp had no business touching the tender thighs of a laboring woman. Every night before sleep, Hannah scoured her fingers with hot water and coarse lye soap, then worked the elder tallow into her skin until her thumbs were supple.

On the shelf above the hearth stood eight stoneware crocks. Each jar held its dried harvest, gathered during the long summer twilights along the sheep tracks of Upton Lovell and Bishopstrow. There was pennyroyal for lingering afterbirths, dried chamomile flowers to steep for maternal cramps, powdered ergot of rye tied in oiled bladder skins to stem hemorrhages, and bundles of dried tansy leaves tied with hemp twine in lots of seven.

Seven was the number. Hannah did not hold with the papist superstitions that lingered in the high hamlets, but seven sprigs of tansy in a pint of boiled well-water made an infusion that eased the shivering fits after a third-stage labor.

She reached into her apron pocket and drew out an iron bodkin to scrape a drop of hardened tallow from her thumbnail. The sound of the iron against horn was dry and sharp in the quiet room.

In twenty years across the three parishes of Nether Bourne, Upton Lovell, and Bishopstrow, two thousand infants had drawn their first breath beneath her hands. Two thousand umbilical cords tied with boiled linen string and severed three finger-breadths from the belly. Two thousand times she had knelt on cold flagstones or damp chalk floors, waiting for the expulsion of the placenta, listening for the sudden, sharp gasp that marked the transition from water to air.

And thirty-four dead.

She kept their count not in ink, but in the small notches cut into the inner rim of her wicker basket. Thirty-four babes who came forth blue, or twisted, or strangled by their own navel strings before the daylight touched their faces. And one of her own.

Twenty-two years ago, in the wet Martinmas of 1774, when she was eighteen and married to John Thorne, the wheelwright. The child had come six weeks early, tiny as a plucked pigeon, its skin translucent as lard paper. John had dug the grave himself behind the north wall of Nether Bourne churchyard, where the unbaptized lay beneath the dripping elder trees. Two winters later, John took the lung ague after hauling ash timber from the woods in a December gale, leaving her with the flint cottage, three pewter spoons, and her mother's calfskin bag.

The churchwardens had granted her the parish licence five years later. She still carried the parchment folded inside the calfskin lining of her basket: *Johanna Thorne, vidua, examinata et approbata per Reverendum in Christo Patrem...* signed with the purple ink and paper wafer of the Bishop of Salisbury.

She folded the linen corners over the pewter bodkin and the shears. She tied the parcel with a thong of greased leather.

The flint walls of the cottage were two feet thick, built by her husband's grandfather in the reign of Queen Anne. When the winter gales drove down from the Plain, the draft found every crevice around the oak doorframe, carrying the scent of dead bracken and damp chalk sheep pens. She took a twist of dry moss from the hearth box and pressed it firmly into the gap beneath the lintel.

From the rafter hooks hung bunches of sweet woodruff and dried lavender, gathered to sweeten the bed straw of laboring women whose cottages smelled of wet wool and turnip greens. In the corner by the copper boiler sat three earthenware crocks of coarse salt, stored to rub into postpartum hides and preserve sheep bladders for dressings.

She ran her thumb along the edge of the deal table. The wood was white, bleached by two decades of boiling vinegar and lye water. On this table she had wrapped the corpses of five mothers whose bellies had turned to fire from the childbed rot; on this table she had dressed the severed cords of three sets of twins born to tenant cotters on the High Lea estate.

She listened to the wind. It was rising from the north-east, veering toward the bitter quarter that froze the watercress ditches before midnight. When the weather set in from the Plain, the sheep huddled against the thorn hedges, and women heavy with child took their pains early, shocked by the falling frost.

She checked the oil in her tin dark-lantern. The reservoir was full of clear colza oil, the braided cotton wick trimmed square with her shears. Beside the lantern lay her leather purse containing two silver shillings, a bone comb, and her steel lancet for opening arm veins in cases of sudden eclampsia.

She took down her frieze cloak from the oak peg behind the door. It was heavy, double-milled cloth from the Bradford-on-Avon mills, stiffened by grease and downland rain, its deep hood lined with rabbit fur. She shook out the folds, checked the bone buttons at the throat, and laid it across the back of the settle.

Her gray pony, Bess, kicked once against the weatherboarding of the lean-to stable outside. The sound was muffled by the turf bank between the buildings. Hannah took a pinch of dried chamomile from the earthen crock, dropped it into a pewter cup of hot well-water, and drank the bitter brew in three swallows.

She buckled the two pigskin saddle-bags across the wooden tree of the pack-saddle. The leather was dark with years of neat's-foot oil, supple against the frost, smelling of salt and dried pennyroyal. In the near pocket lay her secondary roll: six extra yards of coarse linen toweling, a ball of hempen string rubbed with beeswax, and an elderberry-wood quill for blowing crushed sneezing-root into a swooning woman's nostrils to bring back the breath. In the off pocket she placed two dried mutton collops wrapped in clean grease-paper, a crust of barley bread, and an iron hoof-pick. A woman could ride twelve miles into the high down at midnight and find neither candle nor peat on the hearth; a midwife who rode without her own tinder-box and grease was a fool who gambled with two lives.

She lifted the latched horn lantern from the iron hook. The yellow flame guttered once against the tin draft-holes, then burned steady and clear, casting round yellow discs of light across the chalk floor. Outside in the lane, the north wind was rising, driving loose dry snow from the sheep barrows against the kitchen shutters.

She knew every family between the chalk downs and the river gravels. She knew which cotter beat his wife when the barley crop failed, and which dairy maid hid her swelling stays beneath a pinned wool kerchief until the seventh month. In three villages, no child was born without Hannah Thorne's hands upon the crown; no woman died in childbed without Hannah washing the clay-cold thighs and tying the linen chin-cloth before the parish sexton arrived with his spade.

It was an austere trade. The parish vestry paid her twenty shillings a year out of the poor rates, augmented by sixpence from a poor husband or half a crown from a prosperous tenant farmer. When winter set in and the chalk ruts froze into iron ridges, Goodman Callow would send a cart of split ash logs from High Lea wood, and Sir William Harford's bailiff would deliver two bushels of malt barley from the manor granary.

That was the contract of the parish. She kept her silence, delivered the babes, buried the stillborn behind the nettles, and in return the great houses allowed the widow to live beside the bourne without begging for parish bread.

She had learned the art of patience from her mother, who had carried the same basket across the same parishes when King George the Second sat upon the throne. Her mother used to say that a midwife's eyes must be like well-water: reflecting everything, holding nothing, and never rippling when a stone was dropped into the deep.

"If a farmer gives you a side of pork, eat it with thanksgiving," her mother had told her. "If a squire gives you half a guinea, curtsy and bless his hearth. But never ask whose seed is growing under the girl's shift. The seed belongs to the man; the pain belongs to the woman; and the breath belongs to God."

Hannah walked to the small casement and looked out into the night.

The chalk bourne ran black between its frozen banks, reflecting the pale scud of clouds that raced across the face of a waning moon. Across the valley, the high ridge of Upton Lovell rose like a dark wall against the sky. Up there, in the lonely downland sheep folds, three thousand Dorset ewes were huddled in the straw hurdles, their breath rising in gray clouds beneath the stars.

The wind sighed in the chimney breast. Somewhere in the village, a dog barked once, a short, sharp yap that died away into the rushing sound of the stream.

She reached for her iron bodkin once more, ran her thumb over the blunt point, and laid it into the calfskin pocket of her basket.

The church clock struck one. The deep brass tone traveled down the water meadows, muffled by the falling sleet. Hannah banked the hearth coals with dry turf, blew out the rushlight, and sat down upon the oak settle to wait for whatever summons the night might bring.

# Chapter 2: The Knock on the Shutter

The knock came two hours past midnight. It was not the gentle tap of a neighbor's boy sent with a message, but the heavy, rhythmic pounding of an iron crop handle against the oak shutter.

Hannah was out of her feather pallet before the third stroke fell. In twenty years of parish practice, a night summons never brought good news. Labor did not wait for the sun to rise over the downs; it took women in the dark watches when hearths went cold and water pails froze solid inside cottage kitchens.

She thrust her feet into her sheepskin pattens, threw a thick frieze cloak over her shift, and took the iron rushlight holder from the mantel. She lifted the heavy ash bar from the door.

The freezing gale tore into the room, blowing fine sleet across the flagstones and nearly extinguishing the smoking rush. A man stood in the doorway, his broadcloth riding coat dripping with sleet, his tricorn hat pulled low over his eyes. Behind him, a tall bay gelding stamped its iron shoes against the flint paving of the lane, breath steaming white from its nostrils.

It was Edward Callow.

He was twenty-four, the only surviving son of Goodman Thomas Callow of High Lea Farm. His face was flushed red with liquor or haste, his neckcloth crooked beneath his chin. In his right hand he clutched a horn lantern whose candle sputtered against the wind.

"Get your kit, Hannah," he said. His voice was breathless, rattling with a petulant tremor. "You are needed at High Lea. At once."

Hannah did not step aside to let him enter. She stood square in the doorway, her hands tucked inside the woolen pockets of her cloak.

"Who is in labor, Master Edward?" she asked calmly. "Your mother is sixty and past childbearing. Your sisters are both married in Devizes."

Edward shifted his weight from boot to boot. The leather lash of his riding crop twitched against his thigh. He looked past her shoulder toward the dark chimney, avoiding her eyes.

"One of the sheep-shearers' women," he muttered, wiping sleet from his forehead with his sleeve. "A traveling wench from the Somerset borders. Father let her shelter in the lambing shed behind the upper fold. Her time has come upon her. She is screaming loud enough to wake the valley."

Hannah looked at his boots. They were of fine cordovan leather, polished to a deep mahogany sheen, though now splashed with downland chalk slurry to the silver buckles. A gentleman farmer's son did not ride through a November blizzard at two in the morning to fetch a midwife for an Somerset vagrant. For a shearer's wench, Thomas Callow would have sent an under-carter on an old cart mare, or left the woman to drop her litter in the straw as cottagers often did.

"If she is in the high lambing shed," Hannah said, "she has neither hearth nor hot water. A woman in the second stage of hard labor cannot be delivered on frozen dung, Master Edward. Bring her down to the farm kitchen."

"No!" Edward snapped, his voice rising in sudden panic. He stepped closer, smelling of stale malt spirits and horse sweat. "Father gave strict orders. She stays where she is. The house is full of barley buyers from Salisbury who arrived for yesterday's market. Father will not have screaming wenches disturbing the guests. Fetch your basket and mount behind me, or by God I will carry you across the saddle!"

Hannah's jaw tightened. She looked him in the face until the young man dropped his gaze to the wet threshold stones.

"You will carry nothing, Edward Callow," she said in a low, level voice that had quieted drunken laborers in labor rooms for twenty years. "I ride my own pony, or I do not stir from this chimney. Step back into the lane while I dress."

She closed the heavy door in his face and dropped the iron bar into its staple.

Inside the dark cottage, her movements were swift and precise. She drew on her woolen stockings, tied her thick flannel petticoats over her linen smock, and laced her calfskin stays. She took her leather midwifery bag from the table. Into its deep side pouches she thrust two rolls of boiled linen tape, the pewter bodkin, the bone-handled scissors, an earthenware pot of lard ointment, a flask of French brandy, and three packets of dried chamomile and ergot.

From beneath the brick floor of the pantry, she drew out her lantern, struck a spark from flint and steel into a tin of charred linen tinder, and blew the spark into a yellow flame.

She went out the back door to the lean-to stable. Her gray pony, Bess, whickered softly in the darkness, turning her broad head toward the lantern light. Bess was fourteen hands, thick-legged and sure-footed on the chalk ruts. Hannah threw a woolen pad across the mare's back, cinched the hemp girth, and hung the leather basket securely across the pommel.

When she led the pony into the front lane, Edward was pacing back and forth beside his gelding, cursing the cold under his breath.

"You took your time, old woman," he snarled, swinging into his leather saddle.

"A child takes its own time, Master Edward," Hannah said, mounting Bess from the mounting block by the gate. "And the road to High Lea is three miles uphill in the dark."

The young man swore under his breath, wrenching the gelding's curb-chain so sharply that the beast threw up its lathered head and stamped against the chalk wall.

"My father will be at the barn before us," Edward muttered, his voice ragged with tension. "If the girl has cried out or roused the dairy hands, he will turn her out onto the turnpike before the child is half-born."

"If your father turns an unsevered mother into a December ditch, Master Edward," Hannah said, settling her woolen skirts over the pommel, "he will answer for felony murder before the coroner in Salisbury. And you will stand beside him in the dock for procuring the abandonment."

Edward went white in the moonlight. He stared at her through the bitter wind, his lower lip quivering with a mixture of terror and petulance.

"I never asked for the girl to be brought to the high barn," he protested, his fingers fumbling with his leather reins. "Father planned the business. Father arranged with the carrier from Warminster to take her north into Somerset once the work was finished. I only wanted to give her ten pounds of my shearing wages to go quietly to her aunt at Frome."

"Ten pounds buys no settlement in Somerset," Hannah replied, reining Bess out into the lane. "A parish constable turns a pregnant stranger back at the hundred stone."

Edward wheeled his horse and dug his rowel spurs into its flanks. The gelding broke into a sharp trot, its iron shoes sparking against the flint stones as they turned toward the high ridge.

The wind met them at the edge of the village, howling down through the beech hangers of Upton Lovell. Water from the overflowing road gutters swirled around the pony's hocks, brown with washed chalk and rotted oak leaves. Above them, the chalk ridge loomed like a great black wall against the racing storm clouds, blocking out the stars.

Hannah leaned forward in the saddle, pulling her hood down until only her eyes were exposed to the biting sleet. Edward's tricorn hat was plastered flat against his skull by the rain, but he rode without checking his mount, his whip hand rising and falling against the gelding's quarter with cruel, nervous rhythm.

She watched the twitch of his shoulders. She had known the boy since he was delivered from his mother's womb in the great wainscot bedroom at High Lea twenty-four years ago. A breech presentation, long and agonizing, that had left his mother barren for the rest of her days. Thomas Callow had stood in the doorway then, counting golden guineas into his palm while his wife groaned in the bed, demanding a living son to inherit the tenant lease.

And here was the son. Riding through the dark with liquor on his breath, running from something that terrified him more than his father's belt.

Edward turned in his stirrups to look back at her. The sleet glistened on his cheeks like greasy tallow.

"Faster, Hannah!" he shouted back into the wind. "If she dies before we reach the fold, my father will have your cottage pulled down before noon!"

Hannah kept her hand firm upon Bess's reins. "A woman does not die of labor in an hour, Edward," she called back. "She dies of cold and neglect. Put your crop away before you lame that beast on the flints."

He cursed her, but he slackened his pace as the slope steepened into the hanging woods. The mud here was thick as brewer's barm, sucking at the horses' hooves with wet, hollow groans. Overhead, the naked boughs of the beech trees groaned against one another in the dark, creaking like the timber frames of a watermill.

She knew every turn of this road. On the left stood the ruined lime-kiln where old gaffer Miller had burned chalk when the turnpike was first laid; on the right rose the steep bank of the parish coppice, where the tenant farmers cut hazel hurdles every seven years. In summer, the lane was white with dust and sweet with honeysuckle, but tonight the chalk slurry was deep as a ditch, washing over the pony's knees.

Edward kept his head down, refusing to look at the dark woods. He was a boy who loved the tavern benches at Warminster and the cock-fights at the George, spending his father's silver on silver buckles and silk cravats while his father counted sheep fleeces by candlelight. A boy who had never faced a reckoning in his life.

She looked at the lantern swinging from his saddle D-ring. The horn panes were yellow, clouded with soot, throwing a dim, greasy light across the drenched hedgerows.

"How long has she been in travail?" Hannah asked, pulling Bess alongside the gelding where the road widened at the top of the wood.

Edward did not look at her. "Since noon yesterday. She began moaning in the dairy behind the cheese presses. Father sent her up the hill before the Salisbury carters arrived."

"Without a fire?"

"She had straw," Edward muttered defensively, his voice cracking. "And an old horse rug. A dairy wench does not need goose down."

Hannah's fingers tightened on the reins until her knuckles ached. "She is a human creature, Edward. Bearing your father's grandson, if village talk has any truth in it."

Edward jerked on the reins so violently that the bay gelding reared, its iron shoes slipping on the wet chalk rock.

"Hold your tongue!" he screamed, his face contorted with panic. "Do you want to ruin us both? If Father hears you say that, he will turn you into the ditch to freeze with the sheep!"

Hannah did not blink. She let Bess move ahead into the sleet, her gray head pointing steadily toward the crest of Gallows Hill.

The frost crackled beneath Bess's hooves as they cleared the village boundary. Hannah looked back once at the dark flint cottages of Nether Bourne, where only a single rushlight flickered in the window of the blacksmith's shop. The rest of the parish was asleep, buried beneath thatch and heavy wool coverlets, oblivious to the drama unfolding on the high downs.

Edward rode ahead like a man pursued by devils. His horse was a prime four-year-old hunter, bred for the chase, but in the heavy chalk slurry the beast slipped and stumbled against the hidden flints. Every time the gelding faltered, Edward brought his crop down across its flank with a cruel, nervous crack that echoed through the bare beech trees.

"Hold your hand, Edward!" Hannah called out again, her voice sharp as an east wind. "You will break that horse's knees on the rock, and then where will your dairy wench be? Ride with a loose rein and let him feel the ground!"

Edward did not turn his head, but his whip hand sank to his thigh. He leaned over the pommel, his shoulders hunched against the gale, his breath whistling through his teeth in short, gasping sobs. He was trembling from head to foot, not with the cold of the downland night, but with the sick, hollow dread of a boy who had sown his wild seed in summer and was now reaping the harvest in the winter dark.

Hannah touched the wicker basket at her knee. The leather strap was cold, stiffened by the freezing drizzle. Inside lay the linen, the tallow, and the bone shears. In twenty years, she had delivered the bastards of squires, tenant farmers, and ditch-side beggars; and in every case, it was the woman who bore the sorrow while the man rode away in the morning mist.

She set her jaw, leaned over Bess's mane, and urged the gray mare up the steep ascent toward the ridgeway.

# Chapter 3: The Ridge in Sleet

The track from Nether Bourne to High Lea rose two hundred cubits above the valley floor, following the ancient ridgeway that skirted the chalk barrows of Upton Lovell. In summer, the ridge was thick with wild thyme and yellow rock-rose, sweet with the scent of downland turf and sheep dung. Tonight it was a knife edge of freezing wind.

Hannah drew her woolen hood tight beneath her chin. Sleet blew sideways off the Salisbury Plain, stinging her cheeks like thrown sand. The gray pony lowered its head and leaned into the gale, its hooves crunching through the crusted ruts where cartwheels had churned the chalk slurry into deep gutters during the autumn rains.

Fifty yards ahead, the red circle of Edward Callow's lantern bobbed against the darkness. He rode hard, urging the tall bay gelding through the drifts, his horse's tail streaming like a black banner in the wind. Whenever the pony fell behind, Edward pulled up his mount and waited in the shadow of the gorse bushes, cursing the animal's pace and glaring back down the track.

"Keep up, Hannah!" he shouted above the howl of the storm. "The woman was half-spent when I left the fold!"

Hannah did not answer. To speak in such wind was to swallow sleet and freeze the lungs. She kept her heels pressed against Bess's ribs, letting the mare find her own footing along the ditch bank. A pony born on the chalk knew the treacherous hollows where old sheep-dips had caved in; an over-eager horse could break a cannon bone in a rabbit burrow before a rider could draw rein.

As they reached the crest of Gallows Hill, Hannah looked for the lights of High Lea Farm. The farmstead was the largest holding in the three parishes: four hundred acres of arable down and water meadow rented by Thomas Callow from the Earl of Pembroke's estate. The manor house, built of coursed Chilmark stone with tiled roofs and six brick chimneys, sat in a sheltered combe a quarter of a mile below the ridgeway.

On a night like this, the high chimneys of High Lea should have been pouring birch smoke into the gale, and the dormer windows of the granary should have shown the yellow glow of rushlights where the farm servants slept.

The manor was completely dark.

Not a single candle flickered in the casements. Not a dog barked from the kennel yard. Instead of turning down the sunken road that led to the manor gates, Edward swung his horse sharply to the left, taking a narrow, overgrown cart track that climbed toward the sheep folds on the open down.

Hannah pulled Bess to a halt beside a gnarled thorn bush.

"This is not the road to the house, Edward!" she called out, her voice cutting through the hiss of sleet.

Edward turned his gelding in the narrow lane. The lantern light caught the sweat running down his neck and the white glint of his eyes.

"I told you she was in the lambing barn!" he shouted. His voice was ragged, cracking with a thin, desperate edge. "The sheep-shearers' shed behind the chalk pit! The house is shut for the night!"

Hannah sat motionless in the saddle. The lambing shed on High Lea stood half a mile beyond the farmyard, perched on the windswept rim of an abandoned marl pit. It was a rough timber hovel, walled with unmortared flint rubble and roofed with rotten hurdles of hazel thatch. In March, when the Dorset ewes dropped their lambs, the shepherds kept a turf brazier burning to keep the frost from the newborn fleeces. In November, it was an icehouse: no hearth, no chimney, and no water except the brackish rainwater caught in a rotting oak butt beneath the eaves.

"No woman gives birth in that ditch, Master Edward," Hannah said, her hand resting firmly on the reins. "If you have brought a laboring girl up here to hide her from your father, turn about this instant. We take her down to the kitchen, or I turn back to Nether Bourne."

Edward rode his horse back until the gelding's warm shoulder bumped against her knee. He leaned down from the saddle, his breath sour with brandy.

"Father knows she is there," he hissed, his face inches from hers. "It was Father who put her in the barn! Now move that nag, Hannah, or as God is my witness, you will answer to the vestry for every shilling of your parish pension!"

Hannah felt the cold settle deep into her breast. It was not the sleet that chilled her, but the sudden certainty of what lay ahead. Thomas Callow did not lodge Somerset vagrants in the high lambing shed. A strange laborer who fell sick on the highway was turned out by the parish constable under the Poor Laws, carted beyond the parish boundary to die in the next tithing so the church rate would not bear the cost of her burial.

If Thomas Callow was sheltering a pregnant woman on the high ridge in secret, the girl was no stranger.

She touched Bess with her heels. The pony moved forward through the sleet.

They skirted the dark rim of the chalk pit. The wind shrieked through the leafless thorns. Ahead, a low, black shape rose against the snow-streaked grass: the rough timber gables of the lambing barn. Through the chinks in the weatherboarding, a faint, amber thread of lantern light leaked into the dark.

The chalk pit yawned fifty yards to their right, a pale amphitheater carved from the downland turf by three centuries of parish lime-burners. In the pitch blackness, the sheer drop of the pit was invisible, marked only by three rotten oak posts and a sagging strand of wire. Edward did not slacken his speed, riding within three paces of the lip.

Hannah guided Bess along the upper grass margin, where the turf was firmer. The sleet was turning to hard, pelting graupel that stung the pony's ears.

Beneath the eaves of the lambing shed, an ancient hazel hedge caught the drifts, creating a sheltered bank where old sheep hurdles lay rotting in the nettles. As Hannah swung her leg over the saddle and dropped to the slushy ground, she heard a sound from within the hovel.

It was not a sheep's cough.

It was a woman's voice, high and thin, breaking into a ragged, gasping sob that rose and fell with the rhythmic fury of an unassisted labor.

Hannah took down her leather bag from the pommel. She looped Bess's reins over a hazel post, turned her back on Edward Callow, and pushed open the sagging hurdle door.

The wind tore at the hurdle as she slipped inside, nearly ripping the hazel frame from her fingers. Edward caught his gelding by the bit, tying the horse to the lee side of the hay-rack. He did not follow her into the pen. He stood in the sleet, shivering beneath his greatcoat, his boots sinking into the sodden mixture of mud and sheep dung that rimmed the threshold.

Hannah smelled the sheep tallow and the stale smoke of a smothered turf fire. The darkness within was thick, parted only by the yellow slit of the lantern resting on the trough.

In the corner, the straw rustled with a violent, shuddering tremor. A groan followed, bubbling through phlegm and dry lips, the sound of a creature driven past the limits of human endurance.

Hannah knelt, drew her tin lantern from her bag, and opened the brass shutter.

The yellow circle of light fell upon a pair of bare, gray feet, caked with dry sheep droppings and mud, shivering against the flint foundation wall. Above the feet, beneath a sodden frieze petticoat, rose the great rounded mound of a laboring womb, shuddering as the muscular wall tightened into an iron dome.

Hannah drew a deep, steadying breath. She unbuttoned her cloak, rolled her wool sleeves to her elbows, and moved into the shadow of the timber pen.

The wind screamed across the open downs like a pack of hounds. Here on the high ridgeway, eight hundred feet above the sea, there were neither trees nor drystone walls to break the fury of the gale blowing off the Salisbury Plain. The sleet had turned to needles of driving ice that coated Hannah's frieze cloak in a brittle, rattling crust.

To their left, the ancient barrows of the pagan kings rose against the purple-black sky: smooth, grassy mounds where British warriors had been buried with their bronze spears two thousand years ago. In the summer heat, the sheep grazed upon the barrows among wild thyme and blue hare-bells; tonight the mounds looked like frozen corpses lying beneath the snow.

"Keep away from the ditch, Hannah!" Edward shouted, his voice whipped away by the wind. "The sheep-dip has caved in by the thorn bush! The drift is six feet deep!"

Hannah guided Bess along the crown of the turf track. The gray mare snorted, lowering her muzzle until her nostrils almost brushed the frozen ruts. A downland pony knew the treachery of chalk country: the subterranean fissures where rain had dissolved the soft limestone, creating blind sinkholes covered by a thin skin of turf that would swallow horse and rider without warning.

Ahead, the black amphitheater of the old chalk pit yawned in the darkness.

It was an eerie place, shunned by the parish cotters after dusk. Fifty years ago, a traveling peddler had been robbed and murdered on the lip of the pit, his body thrown into the lime kiln at the bottom. The old people said his ghost walked the rim on winter nights, crying out for Christian burial.

Edward did not look toward the pit. He kept his eyes fixed upon the distant hurdles of the sheep fold, urging his horse through the slush with frantic kicks of his heels.

"There it is!" he gasped, pointing with his whip.

A low, turf-covered hovel clung to the sheltered side of the chalk bank. It was walled with flint rubble picked from the arable fields, roofed with rotten hurdles of hazel thatch that had turned black with winter wet. In the lee of the wall, forty ewes were huddled together, their gray fleeces crusted with snow, their breath rising like mist in the lantern light.

From within the shed came a groan that seemed to rise from the earth itself: a low, guttural cry of agony that was torn from a woman's throat by the crushing fist of an unassisted contraction.

Hannah swung her leg over the saddle before Bess had even come to a full halt. Her boots sank ankle-deep into the freezing slush of mud and sheep dung that surrounded the hurdle door.

"Hold the horses, Edward," she said, her voice dropping into the quiet, commanding tone that brooks no hesitation. "Take the blankets from your saddle and bring them inside. And do not stand there shaking like an aspen leaf. A woman is dying in that straw."

She pushed aside the rotten hazel hurdle and stepped into the darkness.

# Chapter 4: The Lambing Barn

The door of the lambing shed hung crooked on a single iron strap. Hannah pushed aside a hanging hurdle of gorse and stepped into the damp, dung-scented gloom.

The smell struck her first: sour straw, wet sheep fleeces, old tallow, and the unmistakable metallic reek of fresh blood and broken amniotic waters.

On the dirt floor in the corner of the pen, curled upon a heap of mouldering barley straw, lay a woman. She wore only a stained linen shift and a tattered frieze petticoat. Her bare legs were drawn up toward her belly, her feet gray with cold and encrusted with sheep droppings. Beside her on an overturned feed trough sat a single horn lantern, its tallow candle guttering in the draft that whistled through the timber slats.

Hannah dropped her basket onto the trough and went down on her knees in the straw.

"Let me see you, child," she said softly, laying her bare palm against the woman's forehead.

The skin burned like hot brick. The woman groaned, rolling her head to the side. Her dark hair was matted with sweat and dried chaff, plastered across her temples in tangled curls. When she opened her eyes, the pupils were wide with fever, gazing blindly into the gloom.

It was Mary Miller.

Hannah's breath caught in her throat. Mary was twenty-one, the daughter of old Miller who had tended the water meadows below Bishopstrow until his drowning three years ago. For two summers, Mary had worked as head dairy maid at High Lea Farm, scouring the wooden butter churns and skimming the broad cream pans in the cool north dairy. Hannah had seen her at Michaelmas fair: a tall, quiet girl with rosy cheeks and clean calico aprons, carrying two pails of skimmed milk on an ash yoke.

Now her cheeks were hollowed to the bone, her collarbones standing out like dry sticks beneath her shift. Her belly rose beneath the wet linen, tight and rounded as a tavern cask, shuddering with the fierce, rhythmic contraction of the uterine muscle.

"Hannah..." Mary whispered, her dry lips cracking as she spoke. She reached out a trembling hand, her fingers gripping Hannah's woolen cuff with surprising strength. "The pains... they have not stopped since the sun went down. It is tearing my back in two."

"Hush now, Mary," Hannah said, slipping her arm beneath the girl's shoulders to raise her from the wet straw. "I am here. The pain has its work to do."

Through the doorway, Edward stood in the sleet, holding his horse's bridle. He peered into the shed, his face pale and nervous in the lantern light.

"Is she... is it coming?" he stammered.

Hannah turned upon him with eyes like cold flint.

"Bring in the saddle blanket from your horse, Edward!" she commanded. "And fetch the iron kettle from the shepherd's locker! There is an oak cask of water beneath the eaves. Build up a fire with the dry hurdles in the corner, or this girl will freeze to the floor before the child crowns!"

Edward hesitated, glancing back into the darkness of the lane as if expecting someone to emerge from the night.

"There is no firewood here," he muttered.

"Then smash an empty hurdle with your boots!" Hannah cried, her voice ringing off the timber beams. "Do you want two corpses on your hands before dawn? Move, boy!"

Shamed by her fury, Edward turned and kicked apart a dry hazel hurdle with his heavy riding boots. He piled the split laths near the doorway, struck a spark from his tinderbox, and blew a smoky flame into life.

Hannah turned back to the laboring girl. She turned back the stained shift. Mary's belly was low, dropped between her thighs in the unmistakable posture of the second stage. As Hannah laid her hand upon the abdomen, the womb hardened into a rigid, stony mound. Mary arched her spine against the straw, her throat emitting a long, low moan that ended in a sharp, choking gasp.

Hannah slipped two fingers between the labia. The cervix was fully dilated, thin as an onion skin, and pressed firmly against the pelvic outlet was the hard, round crown of an infant's head.

The skull bones were overlapping slightly, molded by the pressure of the pelvic brim, but there was no obstruction. The amniotic membranes had ruptured hours before; the presenting caput was covered in thick dark hair that felt cool to the touch from the drafts in the shed.

"You have done well, Mary," Hannah whispered, wiping the girl's sweating brow with a corner of clean boiled linen. "The head is down. Two or three good pains, and you will see your child."

Mary's breath hitched. She stared up into the darkness of the rafters where cobwebs hung thick with lime dust.

"Will Edward stay?" she gasped, her teeth chattering with milk-fever. "He swore... he swore he would take me to Devizes..."

Hannah smoothed the matted hair from the girl's temples. She did not answer.

She took her flask of brandy, wet her linen kerchief, and pressed it against Mary's lips. The spirit brought a gasp from the girl's dry throat, but her eyes cleared slightly.

"Do not think of Devizes now, child," Hannah murmured. "Think only of the breath in your lungs. When the next cramp takes your hips, do not fight the bed. Pull your knees to your shoulders and bear down with the weight of your body."

Mary nodded weakly, her fingers closing tight over Hannah's wrist.

Outside, the wind hurled a barrage of sleet against the timber weatherboards. Edward returned with an iron kettle filled with black water from the rain butt. He hung it over the crackling hazel fire upon an iron hook, then stepped back into the doorway, his eyes darting between the girl in the straw and the dark downland track.

Hannah took the earthen pot of mutton tallow, scooped a lump onto her fingers, and lubricated the perineum with gentle, outward strokes. The flesh was hot, swollen, but pliable under the grease.

"Breathe deep, Mary," Hannah murmured. "Here it comes."

The girl's belly hardened to stone once more. Mary drew in a great, shuddering gasp of air, clenched her teeth, and bore down into Hannah's waiting hands.

Hannah knelt in the wet straw, gathering Mary's shivering shoulders into the crook of her arm.

The girl's shift was soaked through with sweat and broken water, clinging to her emaciated ribs like a shroud. Her teeth were chattering with such violence that Hannah could hear the clicking of enamel in the quiet shed. On the dirt floor beneath the straw, a dark pool of lochial discharge and amniotic fluid had frozen into slush around Mary's bare ankles.

"How long have the waters been broken, Mary?" Hannah asked, keeping her voice low and steady as she smoothed the damp hair from the girl's temples.

"Since noon... yesterday," Mary whispered, her throat dry as parched grain. "They broke while I was skimming the morning pans in the dairy... Master Callow saw the water on the flagstones... he swore at me... he said if I dropped the bastard in his dairy he would have me whipped at the cart-tail..."

"Hush now, child," Hannah murmured, resting her hand upon the rounded dome of the womb. "Do not speak of Callow. Think only of the child."

Beneath her palm, the uterine muscle tightened like a curved sheet of iron. It was a true second-stage contraction, powerful and sustained. Mary's back arched from the straw, her heels digging into the frozen dung as she stifled a scream against Hannah's shoulder.

Hannah slipped her fingers between the labia. The head had descended deep into the hollow of the sacrum; the anterior fontanelle was rotating toward the pubic arch. The child was moving with nature, obedient to the ancient laws of birth, despite the bitter cold and the mother's fever.

"You are doing well, Mary," Hannah said. "The gate is open. When the next pain comes, do not hold back. Bear down into the bed."

Edward came stumbling through the doorway, carrying the damp saddle blanket and a wooden noggin of black water from the rain butt. He dropped the blanket into the straw, his eyes wide with horror as he looked upon Mary's contorted face.

"Is she... is she going to die, Hannah?" he stammered, his fingers trembling against his riding coat.

"She will not die if you keep that fire burning, Edward Callow," Hannah said without looking up. "Put on more hazel sticks. And blow the smoke out through the thatch before we are all choked in the dark."

# Chapter 5: The Bishop's Oath

The hazel sticks crackled in the smoky fire, throwing dancing shadows across the unmortared flint walls. The shed was still freezing, but near the hearth a circle of warmth thawed the frozen dung on the floor.

Hannah knelt between Mary's drawn-up knees. In her mind, clear as the parish church bell tolling across the valley, ran the solemn words of the oath she had sworn in Salisbury Cathedral before the Chancellor of the Diocese:

*"I, Hannah Thorne, do swear upon the Holy Evangelists that I will faithfully and diligently exercise the office of a midwife... I will not permit nor suffer any child to be murdered, maimed, or hurt... and in the time of labor and travail of any woman, I will not urge nor counsel her to name any other for the father of the child than him that is the true father thereof, nor will I certify any false name..."*

Under the statute of the realm and the canon of the Church, a woman's declaration of paternity made in the agony of crowning was received by the courts of law as solemn truth, carrying the weight of an oath sworn before the King's Bench. In that terrible hour, when death walked beside the bed and the veil between world and eternity was thin as linen thread, no woman was presumed capable of perjury.

A fresh pain seized Mary. The girl cried out, her fingernails digging deep into Hannah's forearm. The muscles of her thighs trembled with violent strain.

"Mary," Hannah murmured, leaning close to the girl's ear so that her words did not carry beyond the straw. "Listen to me, child. The child is at the threshold. You must tell me the truth before God and this unborn soul. Who got this child upon you?"

Mary's eyes rolled upward. Her teeth chattered against her tongue.

"Edward..." she sobbed, a string of saliva trailing from the corner of her mouth. "Edward swore... he swore at midsummer by the hayrick... he said he would buy a cottage in Upton... he swore his father would give consent..."

Near the door, Edward dropped a bundle of hazel twigs. The wood clattered against the dirt floor. His face went white as chalk in the firelight.

"She is raving!" Edward gasped, taking a step backward into the rain. "The fever has turned her brain! She does not know what she speaks!"

Hannah did not look back at him. Her fingers remained steady upon the perineum, supporting the thin, stretching flesh with a warm square of folded linen to prevent a tear.

"The child has its father, Mary," Hannah said softly. "Hold your breath now. Push into my hands."

Mary buried her chin into her chest. The cords of her neck stood out like hemp ropes as she bore down with all the strength left in her fevered frame.

The crown advanced another half-inch, parting the swollen vulva. A thin trickle of dark venous blood ran down the inner thigh into the straw. Hannah pressed the folded linen cloth upward against the posterior commissure, guarding the fragile tissue against the shearing force of the occiput.

Edward stood frozen in the doorway, his chest heaving, staring at the bed of straw with horror in his eyes. He reached into his coat pocket, his fingers fumbling with a silver pocket watch, checking the hour by the firelight as if counting the minutes until an execution.

Hannah kept her hands upon the emerging head. Her Latin oath rang in her blood: *non timebo homines neque potentiam saecularem...* She would not fear the power of tenant masters. The child was at the door of life, and she was its only keeper.

The fire of hazel twigs crackled on the dirt floor, sending up a thin plume of fragrant blue smoke that drifted through the holes in the hazel thatch.

In the quiet between contractions, Hannah sat back upon her heels and looked at the girl. Mary's face was bathed in sweat, but her lips were blue, her fingers plucking nervously at the frayed edges of her frieze petticoat.

Under the statutes of the realm, this was the hour of judgment. The 1624 Act was merciless: if an unwed mother concealed the death of her issue, the law presumed murder, and the gallows was her end. But the ancient common law of England provided one great sanctuary for the innocent: the midwife's oath.

A declaration of paternity made while the child's head was crowning between the thighs had the standing of an unshakeable deposition. The law recognized that in the extremity of labor, when the fear of God was upon the soul and the pains of death compassed her about, no woman had the art or malice to invent a falsehood.

"Mary," Hannah said, leaning close to the girl's ear, her voice carrying the solemn cadence of the church altar. "Look upon me. In a few moments, your child will draw its first breath. Before God and his holy angels, who is the true father of the babe within your womb?"

Mary's dark eyes opened, clear and bright through the haze of fever. She turned her head toward the doorway where Edward stood shivering in his greatcoat.

"Edward," she whispered, her voice carrying across the small shed. "Edward Callow. He gave me his mother's silver thimble by the hayrick at Lammas. He swore before the Lord he would make me his lawful wife."

Edward shrank back against the timber post, his face gray as a tombstone. He did not speak. He buried his face in his hands, unable to meet the gaze of either the midwife or the girl he had ruined.

Hannah bowed her head. The oath was recorded in her memory, carved deeper than any parish register.

# Chapter 6: The Cry in the Straw

The crowning was swift and fierce.

As the next contraction crested, the hairy crown of the infant's head broke through the vulva. Mary let out a high, ragged shriek that rose into the timber rafters and was swallowed by the gale outside. Her heels drummed against the wet straw; her hips bucked upward in the involuntary expulsion reflex.

"Steady, girl!" Hannah commanded, her voice dropping into the low, hypnotic cadence of her craft. "Pant now. Do not push. Pant like a hound in the harvest sun! Let the mouth stretch!"

She pressed the flat of her palm firmly against the perineum, guarding the fragile margin of flesh with the warm boiled linen. With her other hand, she guided the infant's occiput downward, flexing the head so the smallest circumference passed the pubic bone. The crown slipped forward; the forehead followed, then the nose, mouth, and rounded chin, covered in a thick white coating of vernix caseosa.

The child's face was purple, bloated from the compression of the birth canal. Its eyes were sealed tight, its tiny mouth filled with gray mucus and amniotic fluid.

Hannah did not pause. Instantly she hooked her forefinger into the corner of the infant's mouth, sweeping the throat clear of phlegm. She ran her finger around the neck. There was no cord entangled about the throat—the navel string lay free, pulsing thick and blue between the child's thighs.

"One more breath, Mary!" Hannah cried. "Give me the shoulders!"

Mary gave a short, sobbing grunt. Hannah supported the head with both hands, gently depressing it toward the straw. The anterior shoulder slipped beneath the pubic arch with a soft, wet release. Hannah guided the torso upward, and in a rush of warm amniotic brine and blood, the child slid completely into her hands.

It was a boy.

He lay across Hannah's wool-covered lap, limp as a wet hare, his skin mottled with gray-blue shadows. He did not breathe.

Behind her, Edward Callow let out a choked sound and turned his face to the timber wall.

Hannah did not look at him. She had twenty seconds before the brain starved in the cold air. She snatched the pewter bodkin from the linen cloth. She used the blunt, rounded head to stroke the infant's spine vigorously from base to neck. With a clean scrap of linen moistened with brandy from her flask, she rubbed the child's chest and soles with fierce, circular friction.

Still no breath.

She bent her head, placed her mouth over the infant's tiny nostrils and parted lips, and blew three gentle puffs of warm air into the chest. The little ribcage rose and fell.

She stroked the soles of his feet with the edge of her fingernail.

A twitch ran through the boy's limbs. His hands clenched into miniature fists. His chin trembled, his chest heaved in a sudden convulsive spasm, and then he let out a sharp, sneezing gasp.

A second later, his lungs opened fully. A loud, vigorous cry rang through the empty lambing barn—clear, angry, and resonant as a trumpet call.

"He lives, Mary," Hannah said, her breath catching in her throat as she laid the crying boy upon his mother's bare breast. "You have a living son."

Mary looked down at the child. Tears cut clean tracks through the grime on her cheeks. Her shivering hands reached out to touch the wet, black hair plastered to his scalp.

"My boy..." she whispered, laughing and weeping at once. "My sweet boy..."

Near the door, Edward Callow stared at the crying child as if an adder had uncoiled from the straw. His jaw worked, but no sound came from his mouth. He looked at Hannah, then at the girl, then out into the black rain of the lane.

Without a word, he turned, caught the reins of his gelding, and fled into the darkness, the hooves of his horse pounding away down the ridge.

The sound of the galloping horse faded quickly into the roar of the wind. Hannah knelt over the mother and child, listening to the steady, rhythmic wail of the newborn infant. The child's skin was flushing pink now, the blue shadows retreating from his fingertips as his small heart drove the oxygen through his veins.

Hannah took the clean flannel square from her basket and wiped the vernix from the boy's chest and belly. His limbs were sturdy, his thighs plump despite Mary's months of privation in the dairy. He was a nine-month child, carried to full term, with well-formed fingernails reaching the tips of his digits and dark eyelashes glistening against his cheeks.

She laid her warm hand upon Mary's abdomen once more. The womb had contracted to the size of a cannonball, firm and hard beneath the navel. There was no profuse hemorrhage; the bleeding was the normal dark seepage of a healthy third stage.

Mary lay back against the saddle blanket Edward had left behind. Her eyes were heavy, her breathing slow and shallow as the feverish flush began to subside into the cold exhaustion of postpartum relief.

"Hold him close to your smock, Mary," Hannah instructed softly, drawing the dry corners of the woolen horse blanket over the child's back. "Keep him warm against your heart while I tend to the cord."

Hannah knelt in the trampled straw between Mary's feet. From her wicker basket she took a fresh roll of narrow linen tape, boiled four days earlier in rainwater with dried lavender sprigs, and two iron clamps wrapped in oiled silk.

With steady fingers, she measured three finger-breadths from the infant's navel, tying the first linen knot with a double turn that bit deep into the gelatinous blue cord. She waited two full pulse-beats, watching for any tell-tale bead of arterial blood, then tied the second knot an inch further along. Taking her shear-steel scissors from the horn cup of sweet oil, she snipped the cord cleanly between the ligatures.

The severed stalk shrank back against the boy's belly. Hannah dusted the stump with finely powdered dry burnt alum and crushed lycopodium spores from her box to prevent the wet mortification that killed so many cottage infants before their ninth day. She smoothed a soft linen square over the dressing and pinned the woolen binder snug with a bone bodkin.

Mary watched every motion with shining eyes, her hand resting upon the crown of her baby's head.

The boy nuzzled against the stained linen of his mother's shift, his tiny mouth rooting for the nipple. Mary gave a faint, broken sigh, her arm tightening around the bundle as the storm rattled the hazel hurdles overhead.

Hannah knelt on the damp straw and bowed her head in the shadow of the pen. For a long minute she did not move, letting the adrenaline drain from her wrists. The crying had softened to a rhythmic, snuffling whimper.

In twenty years, she had never grown accustomed to the miracle of that first breath. A minute before, there was only darkness and the blind struggle of flesh; now there was a boy, endowed with an immortal soul, crying for warmth in an unheated lambing barn on the high downs of Wiltshire.

She reached for the boiled linen tape resting in her wicker basket. She cut two lengths with her bone scissors, measuring each against the palm of her hand.

"A good boy, Mary," Hannah said, her voice gentle in the dim firelight. "A strong boy with his father's shoulders and his mother's dark hair."

Mary touched the infant's cheek with her cracked thumb. "Edward will come back for us, won't he, Hannah? He only went to fetch a cart from the yard."

Hannah did not answer. She dipped the linen cord into the warm water kettle, shook off the droplets, and prepared to tie the navel string.

The final stage came upon them like a rising tide.

Mary's body was seized by an irresistible expulsive reflex. She grasped the hemp ropes of the empty sheep hurdles with both hands, her fingernails tearing into the bark, her chin pressed against her collarbones as she threw the entire weight of her young body into the downward effort.

"Pant now, Mary!" Hannah commanded, kneeling firm between the trembling thighs. "Pant, girl! Do not tear!"

The crown of the infant's head bulged through the vulva. The scalp was covered in thick, dark hair that glistened with vernix under the flickering lantern light. Hannah's left hand guarded the perineum, pressing a warm square of boiled linen against the stretching skin to support the tissues, while her right hand guided the occiput downward beneath the pubic arch.

With a soft, wet slip, the head was born.

The face was turned toward Mary's left thigh: swollen, purple, the eyes sealed tight with white vernix.

"Stop pushing, Mary!" Hannah called out. "Breathe! Only breathe!"

Swiftly, Hannah ran her forefinger into the infant's mouth, hooking away a thick clot of gray mucus that clogged the throat. She slipped her finger around the neck; the navel cord was loose, pulsing with thick, dark venous blood.

With the next gentle breath, the infant's head rotated to the right, aligning the shoulders with the pelvic outlet. Hannah gently depressed the head toward the straw; the anterior shoulder slipped under the pubic bone, followed instantly by the posterior shoulder, and in a warm, rushing flood of liquor and blood, the child slipped into Hannah's outstretched hands.

It was a boy.

A sturdy, full-term boy with broad shoulders and plump limbs. But he was limp as a wet towel, his skin mottled with gray-blue shadows, his chest motionless.

Hannah did not waste a heartbeat. She wrapped the infant in a warm flannel cloth from her basket, snatched her pewter bodkin, and vigorously stroked the blunt, rounded head up and down the spine from sacrum to neck. She splashed two drops of French brandy from her flask onto her palm and rubbed the soles of his feet with fierce, circular friction.

"Breathe, little one," she whispered under her breath, reciting the Latin words of her licensing oath. "In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost, breathe."

She placed her lips over his tiny mouth and nose, blowing two gentle, warm puffs of air into his lungs. The chest expanded, then sank.

She rubbed his ribs with the rough boiled linen.

Suddenly, a spasm ran through the child's frame. His eyelids fluttered. His chin twitched, his chest hitched in a sharp, convulsive gasp, and then his mouth opened wide.

A loud, furious cry rang through the empty lambing barn—a sound so clear, vibrant, and alive that it seemed to banish the cold from the stone walls.

Mary let out a ragged sob of joy, reaching out her trembling arms from the straw. "My boy... my little boy..."

In the doorway, Edward Callow took one look at the crying child, turned white as ash, and fled into the rain. His horse's hooves pounded away down the ridge, swallowed by the roar of the downland storm.

# Chapter 7: The Warm Caudle

Hannah let the young man ride. She had more urgent business than chasing a craven boy through the sleet.

From the embers of the hazel fire, she took the small iron kettle Edward had brought. She poured boiling water into a wooden noggin, stirred in two spoonfuls of oatmeal from her pocket, added a pinch of powdered cinnamon, and laced the gruel with a generous measure of French brandy.

"Drink this, Mary," she said, holding the noggin to the girl's pale lips.

Mary drank greedily, the warm caudle bringing a faint touch of color back into her hollow cheeks. While the mother swallowed the gruel, Hannah turned her attention back to the third stage of labor.

She waited five minutes until a gentle contraction signaled the detachment of the placenta. With steady, practiced hands, she applied gentle traction to the cord while guarding the fundus of the womb through the abdominal wall. The afterbirth slipped into the wooden trencher with a soft, heavy slither. Hannah examined the dark mass under the lantern light: intact, four pounds of dark cotyledons without tear or missing fragment. Had a piece remained behind, childbed fever would take the girl within three days.

She tied the umbilical cord three finger-breadths from the infant's navel with a length of boiled linen tape, knotted it twice with a surgeon's knot, and tied a second cord two inches beyond. With her bone-handled scissors, she severed the string between the ties.

No blood seeped from the stump. She wrapped the cut end in a clean linen rag smeared with elder tallow.

She lifted the boy, dried his skin with a warm flannel, and swaddled him tightly in the woolen saddle blanket Edward had left behind. As she wrapped his left shoulder, the lantern flame illuminated a distinct mark upon the skin: a raised, dark red strawberry birthmark, the size of a sixpence, perched directly above the left collarbone.

"Look here, Mary," Hannah said, showing the mark to the mother. "God has set His seal upon him."

Mary touched the mark with her thumb, smiling through her exhaustion.

Hannah laid the swaddled baby into the crook of Mary's arm. The child nuzzled against the mother's smock, seeking the breast with eager, snuffling breaths.

Hannah stood, her knees cracking in the silence. She stepped toward the doorway to rinse her blood-stained hands in the rain butt.

In the shadows outside the door, standing motionless beneath the dripping hazel hurdle with an oilskin sack slung over his shoulder, stood Goodman Thomas Callow.

The farmer had not ridden up the track; his boots were coated in chalk slurry from walking the sheep gutters on foot. He stood six feet tall, broad as a barn door in his sheepskin waistcoat, his breath curling white into the sleet. In his right hand he carried an ash walking staff shod with an iron ferrule.

He looked past Hannah into the dim interior of the shed. His small, gray eyes fixed upon the bundle in Mary's arms with a cold, predatory stillness.

Hannah wiped her wet hands upon her linen apron. She stepped back into the doorway, filling the narrow opening with her body, her chin lifted high.

The wind drove a spray of sleet against Callow's sheepskin collar. He did not blink. His heavy, weathered face showed neither anger nor surprise, only the blunt, calculating patience of a man who looked upon a stray ewe or a flooded hayrick.

"The girl lives, then," Callow said.

"She lives," Hannah answered. "And the child is whole."

Callow shifted his weight from boot to boot. The iron ferrule of his walking staff grated against a loose flint on the threshold.

"Edward met me at the lime-kiln," Callow said in his low, gravelly rumble. "The boy was shaking like a dog passing peach stones. Blubbering about magistrates and church bells. A weak vessel, Hannah. Like his mother before him."

He looked through the doorway at the girl lying in the straw. Mary had pulled the saddle blanket up to her chin, watching him with wide, terrified eyes.

"Step out into the lee of the wall, Hannah," Callow commanded. "I have a word for your ear alone."

Hannah looked back at Mary, then pulled her frieze cloak close and stepped out into the freezing mud.

Hannah knelt in the straw beside the mother, watching the steady rise and fall of the newborn's chest.

The boy's cries had softened to a hungry, snuffling murmur. His skin was turning from mottled purple to a healthy, ruddy pink, warm against his mother's bare breast. Mary lay back against Edward's saddle blanket, her face pale and exhausted, but her dark eyes were shining with a tender, protective light that transformed the squalor of the sheep shed.

"He has your eyes, Mary," Hannah said softly, reaching into her basket for the boiled linen tape.

"He has Edward's chin," Mary whispered, her cracked lips curving into a faint, defiant smile. "The Callow cleft in the bone. Master Callow will not be able to deny his own blood when he looks upon this face."

"Master Callow will deny what suits him, child," Hannah replied grimly. "Keep your thoughts on the boy."

Hannah turned her attention back to the third stage of labor. She laid her hand upon Mary's abdomen, feeling the fundus of the womb harden into a firm, round ball beneath the navel. A slight gush of dark blood signaled the separation of the placenta from the uterine wall.

"A gentle push now, Mary," Hannah instructed. "Just to clear the bed."

Mary bore down once, and the afterbirth slipped smoothly into the wooden trencher Hannah had placed between her feet. Hannah examined the mass under the horn lantern: the liver-colored cotyledons were whole and unbroken, the gray amniotic membranes trailing intact behind. No fragment remained in the womb to cause the dreaded childbed rot.

She tied the umbilical cord three finger-breadths from the infant's belly with double knots of boiled linen flax, severed it with her bone scissors, and dressed the stump with mutton tallow and a clean rag.

As she swaddled the child in a dry woolen flannel, the lantern light caught the skin above his left collarbone. There, distinct as a red wax seal upon a legal writ, sat a dark strawberry birthmark, perfectly round and raised above the flesh.

"Look upon this, Mary," Hannah said, showing the mark to the mother. "God has marked him so that no man can ever mistake him."

Before Mary could answer, a shadow fell across the threshold.

Goodman Thomas Callow stood in the doorway, his massive frame blocking the sleet, an oilskin sack slung over his broad shoulder, his ash staff planted firmly in the mud.

# Chapter 8: The Farmer's Gift

Thomas Callow was a broad-shouldered man of fifty-eight, thick in the chest and heavy in the jaw, with bushy iron-gray eyebrows that shadowed small, sharp eyes. He wore a heavy coat of Wiltshire frieze with horn buttons and a waistcoat of greasy sheepskin. He smelled of horse dung, pipe weed, and wet wool.

He did not look at Hannah as he entered the shed. He walked past her to the corner where Mary lay in the straw with the infant.

The girl shrank back against the timber wall, pulling the blanket tight over the child's head.

"So," Callow said. His voice was deep, gravelly as river stones grinding in a mill race. "The wench has dropped her burden."

"She has delivered a healthy boy, Master Callow," Hannah said, stepping between the farmer and the straw. "Both mother and child are alive, by the grace of God."

Callow grunted. He reached into the deep pocket of his breeches and drew out two golden coins. He held them out in the palm of his calloused hand: two George III golden sovereigns, gleaming bright and yellow in the lantern light.

"You have done your work, Hannah Thorne," he said. He did not raise his voice, but every word fell with the heavy finality of an iron weight. "Take these two sovereigns. When the Martinmas slaughter is done next week, my carter will deliver a whole side of salted bacon and two sacks of ground oats to your cottage. That will keep your pot boiling through the winter."

Hannah looked at the gold, but did not reach out her hand.

"The child must be registered, Master Callow," she said quietly. "The parish book requires the name of the mother and the date of birth. And the girl cannot walk down the ridge in this frost."

Callow's eyes narrowed. He took half a step forward, towering over her in the narrow pen.

"The parish book is the business of the churchwardens and the vestry," he said softly. "I am churchwarden of Nether Bourne, Hannah. I keep the keys to the chest. The girl will be cared for. My sister has a dairy at Warminster; Mary will go there in the covered cart before daybreak."

"And the boy?" Hannah asked.

Callow dropped the two sovereigns into Hannah's apron pocket. His hand lingered on the cloth, his fingers tightening against her hip with a slow, deliberate pressure.

"The boy has an honest foster-mother waiting for him in Salisbury," Callow said. "A decent weaver's wife whose own child died of the croup last month. He will be fed and raised to a trade. That is more mercy than the Poor Law gives to bastards born in the parish ditch."

He nodded toward the open door.

"Take your pony and ride home, Hannah. Before the sun rises. Forget you rode up the ridge tonight. Speak of it to no one. If you value the roof over your head, keep your tongue behind your teeth."

Hannah looked down at Mary. The girl was asleep, her fever-flushed face resting against the straw, one arm draped protectively across the blanket.

Hannah picked up her wicker basket and walked out into the sleet.

The farmer stepped into the shed, his iron-shod boots crunching through the frozen straw. He did not remove his hat; he stood over the bed like a bailiff inspecting seized goods for an auction.

"You have a fine tongue, Hannah Thorne," Callow said, his voice dropping into a low, threatening rumble that made the lantern flame flicker. "Talking of God's marks and legal registers. Let me tell you how the world turns in this valley."

He took a step closer, towering over the midwife.

"The girl was my servant. She ate my bread and slept in my barn. If she drops a bastard, the law of the parish gives the churchwardens the right to remove her beyond our borders before the child becomes chargeable to our poor rate. I am saving her from the county bridewell."

"You are stealing a mother's child, Master Callow," Hannah said, her voice steady as the stones beneath her feet.

Callow's hand dropped the two sovereigns into Hannah's apron pocket, his fingers gripping the wool of her skirt with sudden, brutal force.

"I am placing the boy where he will be fed," Callow hissed, leaning down until his sour breath washed over her face. "A weaver in Salisbury will take him for twenty shillings. He will learn a trade and earn his bread. If he stays here, the vestry will put him out to the parish sweep at seven years old to climb chimneys until he dies of the soot-cancer."

He looked toward the open door where the gray light of dawn was beginning to break over the chalk ridge.

"Mount your pony, Hannah. Ride home. Forget you were ever in this shed. If I hear one whisper in the village—one word about Edward, or High Lea, or this night's work—you will find yourself without bread, without licence, and without a roof over your head before the new year is out."

Hannah looked at Mary, who was clutching her sleeping child with trembling arms. She knew that to resist Callow here, alone in the high sheep fold with no witnesses, would only bring violence upon them both.

She gathered her basket, turned her back upon the master of High Lea, and walked out into the bitter sleet.

# Chapter 9: The Empty Hearth

Dawn came to the Wylye valley not with sunlight, but with a slow, gray lifting of the fog off the chalk water meadows.

Hannah led Bess into the lean-to stable behind her cottage. She rubbed the mare down with a wisp of dry oat straw, checked her hooves for picked stones, and gave her a double measure of bruised beans. The pony lowered her muzzle into the wooden manger, chewing with a rhythmic, comforting crunch.

Hannah carried her leather bag into the dark kitchen.

The room was cold. The fire in the hearth had died to white embers during the night, leaving the smell of damp soot and old hearthstones. On the deal table, the two golden sovereigns sat where she had dumped them from her apron pocket. They looked strange and alien upon the scrubbed ash boards: bright, polished, stamped with the King's profile, bought with a newborn life.

She took down an earthenware crock from the shelf, took a handful of dry birch kindling, and blew upon the embers until a small flame caught. She set the iron kettle upon the trivet.

While the water heated, she unpacked her kit. She dropped the bone scissors and the pewter bodkin into an iron skillet of boiling water, letting the scalding steam scour away the traces of blood and vernix. In twenty years, she had never laid an unboiled tool against a woman's flesh. Her mother had taught her that: *Keep your iron hot as the hearth, Hannah, or you will carry the rot from house to house.*

She sat on the wooden settle by the chimney, her hands clasped in her lap.

Her thumbs ached. In the quiet of the morning, the memory of her own dead child rose before her like a shadow upon the wall. She remembered the cold November day twenty-two years ago: John carrying the little deal box under his arm, walking across the sodden grass of Nether Bourne churchyard. There had been no bells, no priest in a white surplice, no prayers from the book. The parish vicar had refused to read the burial service over an unbaptized infant born before its time.

*"He was born alive,"* she had wept to the vicar at the lych-gate. *"He drew three breaths! I heard him cry!"*

*"A cry is not a Christian soul, woman,"* the vicar had answered, turning back to his warm rectory. *"The law of the Church is plain."*

Now another boy had cried in the dark of a lambing shed. A living, vigorous boy with black hair and a strawberry mark on his collarbone.

And where was he now?

Was he in the straw with his mother? Or had Thomas Callow's cart already rumbled down the high ridge toward the Salisbury turnpike?

She reached out her thumb and pressed it against the edge of the golden sovereign. The milled edge was sharp, biting into her calloused flesh.

A bushel of barley and a side of bacon. That was the price of her silence. That was what kept an old widow warm when the winter storms swept the Wiltshire downs.

She pushed the gold coins into an earthenware mustard pot and shoved it deep into the dark recess of the chimney breast.

She poured the boiling water from the iron skillet into a pewter basin. She took a cake of hard tallow soap and washed her arms to the elbows, scouring the dried blood from her skin with a wisp of coarse hemp tow. The skin turned bright red from the heat, tingling in the cold room.

Through the window, she watched the milk cows moving slowly along the lane toward the lower water meadows. Old Giles, the cowman, plodded behind them in his patched smock, his hazel stick tapping against the chalk flints. The village was waking to its ordinary labor: smoke rose from the forge chimney, and the iron hammer began its dull, regular clang against the anvil.

She dried her hands upon a clean linen roller. She took down her bible from the shelf, laid it upon the table, and sat staring at the calfskin cover until the morning bells began to toll for church.

The gray pony carried her down the winding chalk track as the first pale light of dawn filtered through the frozen fog.

In the valley below, the village of Nether Bourne was slowly stirring. Smoke rose from the thatched chimneys of the farm laborers; cocks crowed in the fold-yards; and the sound of iron buckets clanking at the village well traveled clearly through the cold, still air.

Hannah dismounted by her garden gate, her limbs so stiff from the ride that her knees buckled as her boots struck the frozen gravel. She led Bess into the stable, rubbed down her sweating flanks with dry oat straw, and filled her manger with sweet meadow hay.

Inside the cottage, the silence was heavy as lead.

The two golden sovereigns in the earthenware mustard pot seemed to burn through the brickwork of the chimney breast. Two pounds of gold: enough to buy two winter petticoats, a sack of flour, and three loads of peat. Blood money, paid to buy the silence of a licensed midwife while a tenant farmer robbed an unwed mother of her child.

She stripped off her stained apron and dropped her pewter bodkin and bone shears into the iron pot of boiling water on the hearth. The steam rose, carrying the sharp, clean scent of boiled vinegar and lye.

She sat upon the oak settle and closed her eyes.

In the darkness behind her eyelids, she saw again the face of her own dead child in the winter of 1774: the tiny, perfect fingernails, the blue lips that never learned to smile, the cold clay of the churchyard falling upon the deal lid.

For twenty-two years, she had carried that sorrow in secret, pouring her maternal love into two thousand parish babes. She had sworn an oath before the Bishop of Salisbury that she would protect the innocent and speak the truth without fear of worldly power.

She looked at her hands, red and scalded from the hot water.

Thomas Callow thought his gold had bought her silence. He thought his threats of eviction and ruin would keep her tongue behind her teeth.

He did not know Hannah Thorne.

# Chapter 10: The Parish Rumor

On Sunday morning, the bells of Saint Mary's Nether Bourne tolled for the eleven o'clock service.

The village gathered in the church porch beneath the ancient Norman arch. It was a cold, bright morning; the frost of the past three days had melted under a pale sun, leaving the gravel paths soft with yellow mud. The men stood in their best frieze coats and gaiters, scraping their iron-shod boots against the iron scrapers by the door, while the women gathered in clusters near the yew tree, their woolen shawls pinned tight beneath their chins.

Hannah stood near the porch pillar, her hands tucked in her cloak pockets. In a village of two hundred souls, nothing moved without eyes upon it.

"They say old Miller's girl has gone from High Lea," whispered Martha Gane, the blacksmith's wife, leaning close to Hannah's ear. Martha's eyes were sharp, darting toward the lych-gate where the farmers were assembling. "Vanished between Thursday night and Friday morning. My boy Tom was hauling hurdle poles past the lower barn at dawn on Friday and saw Master Callow's light cart heading down the Warminster road with a canvas tilt tied over the back."

"She had kin in Somerset," Hannah said evenly, keeping her eyes on the path.

"Somerset!" snorted Goodwife Povey, whose husband farmed ten acres of water meadow. "A dairy maid does not leave four months of unpaid wages behind to visit Somerset kin in the middle of winter! And she was swelling under her aprons since Lammas, as everyone in the parish knows. When a maid gets that shape, she does not walk to Somerset—she goes to the ditch, or she goes to the parish cage."

Down the path came Goodman Thomas Callow.

He walked with the heavy, deliberate tread of a man who owned fifty dairy cows and six teams of working oxen. He wore his finest Sunday coat of dark blue broadcloth with gilt buttons, a spotted neckcloth, and polished top boots with yellow cuffs. Beside him walked his son Edward. The young man looked pale and sickly, his eyes bloodshot, his hat pulled down to his brows as he stared fixedly at the churchyard gravel.

The farmers touched their hats as Callow passed.

"Good day to you, Master Callow," called out George Finch, the parish overseer of the poor. Finch was a small, sour-faced tailor who spent his days checking settlement certificates and driving vagrants across the parish brook. "A sharp frost for the wheat, sir."

Callow stopped beneath the porch archway. He took a pinch of snuff from an ivory box, inhaled it with a loud snort, and dusted his waistcoat with a red handkerchief.

"Good day, Finch," Callow said, his booming voice carrying across the entire churchyard. "The frost will do no harm to the early sown. It sweetens the ground."

Finch stepped closer, lowering his voice, though not enough to prevent Hannah from hearing every word.

"I hear talk about your dairy maid, Master Callow. Mary Miller. The Warminster constable was in the Red Lion yesterday asking questions. They say a wench was taken up by the town watch on Friday night, wandering near the chalk pit on the common, raving with the fever and her linen covered in blood. They have her in the lock-up."

Edward Callow flinched as if a whip had struck his shoulders. He took a step toward the church door, his hands trembling against his coat skirts.

Thomas Callow did not flinch. His face remained calm, solid as a block of Chilmark stone.

"The girl was discharged from my service a week ago for insolence and thieving milk," Callow said loudly, looking directly at the crowd of cottagers. "Where she went is her own concern. If she has fallen into bad company at Warminster, the magistrates will deal with her. The parish of Nether Bourne has no responsibility for a runaway servant who broke her yearly hiring contract."

"Aye, but the child, Master Callow?" Finch pressed, his ferret-like eyes blinking. "The constable says she was delivered of an infant within the day, but no child was found upon her."

Callow's jaw hardened. He turned his head slowly, and his small, dark eyes met Hannah's across the crowded porch.

"Then she has made away with it," Callow said, his voice dropping into a cold, flat cadence that silenced the porch. "And if a mother conceals the death of her bastard, the statute of King James will give her what she deserves at the end of a hempen rope."

He turned and strode into the dim nave of the church, his heavy boots echoing on the ancient flagstones.

The congregation parted before him like water before a barge. Edward followed in his father's wake, his head bowed, his hands shoved deep into his pockets.

Hannah remained by the Norman pillar. She felt the cold stone through the wool of her cloak. Across the churchyard, the jackdaws circled the square tower, their harsh cries echoing over the leafless elder trees that shadowed the graves of the unbaptized.

The parish beadle struck his iron-tipped staff against the flagstones, calling the people to prayer.

Hannah stepped into the south aisle, walking past the carved oak pews where the tenant families sat in their broadcloth and furs. Near the chancel step, on the north wall, was the brass memorial plate of the Callow family: three generations of tenant farmers who had held the land since the Great Plague, buried beneath Latin mottoes and heraldic wheat sheaves.

She took her seat on the back bench of rough deal, where the parish widows and paupers sat beneath the bell-ropes. Beside her, old Widow Barnes pulled her tattered wool shawl over her coughing chest.

The rector, Mr. Thistlethwayte, climbed the three steps of the oak pulpit. He was eighty years old, with shaking hands and watery eyes that looked past the heads of his flock toward the colored glass of the east window. He opened his leather sermon book and gave out the text:

*"Deliver him that suffereth wrong from the hand of the oppressor; and be not fainthearted when thou sittest in judgment."*

Hannah folded her hands inside her apron pockets. Beneath her right palm lay the bone scissors, wrapped in their boiled linen. She bowed her head, listening to the dry voice of the old priest droning above the winter coughs of the village.

Across the aisle, Edward Callow sat with his shoulders hunched, staring at the stone flagstones between his boots.

The congregation rose as the old rector finished his sermon and gave the blessing from the chancel steps.

Hannah remained in her seat beneath the bell-ropes until the tenant farmers and their wives had filed down the central aisle in their heavy broadcloth and beaver hats. Thomas Callow walked with his head held high, his silver-topped cane striking the flagstones with arrogant authority, acknowledging the bows of the cottagers with curt nods of his chin. Behind him walked Edward, pale and hollow-eyed, shrinking from every glance.

In the churchyard, the crowd lingered beneath the bare branches of the great yew tree.

"Did you hear what Callow said in the porch?" whispered Martha Gane, catching Hannah's arm as they walked toward the lych-gate. "He swears the girl was discharged for thieving! But my brother Tom says he saw Callow's cart driving toward the Salisbury turnpike on Friday morning with an oilskin bundle in the back!"

"People see many things in the fog, Martha," Hannah cautioned quietly.

"Aye, but where is the babe?" pressed Goodwife Povey, her red face pinched with winter cold. "If the girl made away with it, as Callow claims, the constable will find the little body in the chalk pit! And if she did not... if someone took it from her... then there is blood on the hands of the masters of this parish!"

Down the road, George Finch, the overseer of the poor, was talking earnestly with Constable Barnes beside the village stocks.

"We must have the midwife's deposition," Finch was saying, his shrill tailor's voice carrying over the frosted hedge. "Sir William will hold a Petty Sessions at Bourne Hall before the week is out. If there is a bastard chargeable to Nether Bourne, we must know who got it, so the parish rates do not bear the cost!"

Hannah did not wait to hear more. She drew her cloak around her shoulders and walked swiftly down the lane toward her cottage beside the bourne.

The lines were drawn. The overseer cared only for the poor rates; the tenant farmer cared only for his lease and his name; and the magistrate cared only for the King's statute.

Tomorrow, the writ would come. And when it came, she would be ready.

# Chapter 11: The Red Wax Seal

The parish constable arrived on Tuesday morning while the hoar frost was still thick on the thatch.

He did not dismount from his cob. He leaned down over the flint garden wall, his broad red face framed by the collar of a greasy roquelaure cloak, and struck the gate post with his painted parish staff. On the staff, beneath the royal cipher of King George, the words *Nether Bourne Tything* were painted in faded yellow lead.

"Hannah Thorne!" he called out, his voice fogging in the freezing air. "Come forth in the King's name."

Hannah dried her hands upon her coarse linen apron and stepped out of the lean-to pantry. She had been washing roots of winter elecampane in a tub of ice-skimmed water. Her fingers were red and stiff, aching in the knuckles.

"What is the matter, Constable Barnes?" she asked, stopping by the rosemary bush.

Barnes reached into the deep breast pocket of his coat and drew out a folded slip of heavy paper. It was sealed with a wafer of scarlet wax stamped with an eagle's talon—the private seal of Sir William Harford, Baronet, Justice of the Peace for the County of Wilts.

"A summons to Bourne Hall," Barnes said, thrusting the paper toward her over the flint coping. "Before two o'clock this afternoon. You are to attend upon Sir William in his private study."

Hannah did not take the paper immediately. She looked at the scarlet seal.

"Under what warrant?" she asked quietly.

"A warrant of Petty Sessions," Barnes said, wiping his nose with the back of his woolen glove. "Concerning the body of a male child delivered in this parish within the week and now missing. Mary Miller is in the county lock-up at Warminster, taken up on suspicion of felony under the statute of King James. Sir William has the deposition of the town watch, and he requires the testimony of the midwife who delivered her."

Hannah took the paper. The parchment felt stiff and cold, the wax brittle beneath her thumb.

"Did Master Callow send you?" she asked.

Barnes's eyes shifted toward the chimney of High Lea visible across the bare water meadows. He spat into the frosted nettles by the ditch.

"Master Callow is churchwarden, Hannah," the constable muttered, lowering his voice. "He was at Bourne Hall yesterday with Sir William for two hours behind locked doors. What was said between them is not for a parish constable to know. But I will tell you this as an honest neighbor: speak carefully before the magistrate. Sir William's gout is fierce this frost, and when his foot burns, he commits every vagrant and wench to Fisherton gaol without a second thought."

Hannah looked down at the writ. The black clerk's ink was neat and formal: *To Hannah Thorne, widow, licensed midwife of the parish of Nether Bourne: These are in His Majesty's name to command you...*

"I will be there at two, Barnes," she said.

The constable nodded, turned his cob in the narrow lane, and trotted back toward the bridge, the iron shoes of his mount ringing against the frosted gravel.

Hannah stood by the flint wall. The wind off the Plain blew cold and thin, carrying the dry rattle of dead beech leaves from the wood. She broke the red wax seal with her thumbnail and read the words twice through.

The ink was sharp, signed with Harford's trembling signature at the foot. A summons from the King's magistrate was not an invitation to be declined. Under the statutes of the county, a refusal to answer a magistrate's subpoena in matters of felony resulted in an immediate warrant of contempt and conveyance to the county jail.

She folded the paper into four and placed it in her pocket.

She re-entered the cottage, washed her face in cold water from the pitcher, and combed her hair with her horn comb. She drew on her cleanest linen coif, pinned her dark gray shawl over her chest with an iron bodkin, and checked her shoes for clean leather. A midwife appearing before the Petty Sessions had to present the outward appearance of sober godliness and parish deference.

She took her leather basket from the settle. Into its pocket she tucked her episcopal licence, folded inside its oiled bladder wrap. If the magistrate questioned her authority to practice or sought to treat her as an uncertified village crone, the seal of the Bishop of Salisbury would speak for her.

She looked at the clock. It was half-past twelve. The walk along the water meadows to Bourne Hall would take three-quarters of an hour through the slush.

She locked the heavy oak door, slipped the iron key onto the hemp string at her waist, and set out along the frozen bourne.

The walk from Nether Bourne to Bourne Hall followed the water meadows of the Wylye.

The frost had stiffened the rushes along the riverbank into brittle silver lances. Watercress cutters were at work in the gravel shallows, their bare legs red as boiled lobsters, their wooden scoops splashing in the clear chalk spring. They looked up as Hannah passed along the raised causeway, touching their wool caps in silent greeting. Every cutter on the river had had a child delivered by her hands; three of their wives had been sustained through winter fevers by her elderberry cordials and dried tansy teas.

"A sharp day, Mistress Thorne," called out old Dan'l from the cress ditch. "The wild geese were flying south over the Plain at sunrise. A three-week freeze, if the old signs hold."

"Keep your flannel dry, Dan'l," Hannah answered. "The lung-rot loves wet wool."

As she approached the wrought-iron gates of Bourne Hall, she saw two horsemen dismounting by the mounting block. One was Thomas Callow; the other was Mr. Christopher Tarrant, the clerk to the magistrates. Callow was speaking in low, vehement tones, his whip hand gesturing toward the high windows of the library, his heavy brow furrowed like plowed land. When he caught sight of Hannah on the causeway, he ceased speaking instantly, turning his back and striding through the courtyard toward the stables.

Tarrant waited by the iron gates, tucking his leather case under his arm.

"A punctilious arrival, Mrs. Thorne," the clerk said, his dry lips parting in a formal smile that reached nowhere near his eyes. "Sir William is in the library. The apothecary from Warminster has just departed; the gout is in the great toe, and his honor's temper is like dry kindling."

"A magistrate's temper does not alter the King's statute, Mr. Tarrant," Hannah said calmly.

Tarrant gave a thin, whistling chuckle through his teeth. "The statute is an iron bar, Mrs. Thorne. But it is the magistrate who swings it."

He led the way across the gravel courtyard, where three peacocks perched shivering upon the stone balustrade, their long tails dusted with hoar frost.

The frost crackled beneath her pattens as she reached the stone bridge over the Wylye.

The water below ran swift and gray, swirled with slush from the higher chalk streams. Beside the mill race, old Miller the sawyer was stacking ash planks under a timber shed, his breath rising in white clouds like a lime kiln. He looked up, touching his knitted cap.

"Going to the Hall, Hannah?" he called out above the roar of the waterwheel.

"I have business with Sir William, Goodman Miller," Hannah answered, pausing on the stone arch.

The sawyer stepped closer, wiping sawdust from his leather apron with a calloused thumb. "Speak plain before the bench, mistress. Callow's carts have been hauling grain to Warminster all week, but he hasn't paid a penny to the poor fund since Michaelmas. If the masters hang Mary Miller for dropping her babe in the straw, there won't be an honest maid left in the three parishes willing to hire herself to the great farms."

Hannah nodded, her mouth set in a firm line. "The truth will be spoken, master."

She crossed the bridge, turned into the gravel avenue of Bourne Hall, and mounted the broad steps between the stone urns.

# Chapter 12: The Study at Bourne Hall

Bourne Hall stood on a rise of gravel above the river, a great square house of red brick with stone dressings built in the Dutch style after the Glorious Revolution.

Hannah was admitted by an elderly footman in powdered hair and faded livery of yellow broadcloth. He led her down a stone corridor smelling of beeswax, damp coal, and vinegar wash, stopping before an arched mahogany door at the back of the house.

"The midwife, Sir William," the footman announced, bowing low.

The study was warm, almost stifling, heated by a massive grate of sea-coal that glowed with fierce, orange heat. Heavy curtains of crimson damask shut out the winter daylight, leaving the room illuminated by two brass desk lamps and the fire. The walls were lined with calfskin folios: statutes of the realm, Dalton's *Country Justice*, Burn's *Justice of the Peace*, and rows of parish rate books bound in dark buckram.

Behind a broad mahogany writing desk sat Sir William Harford.

He was fifty-six years old, but his frame was bowed and shrunken by chronic disease. His right leg was propped upon an embroidered velvet footstool, wrapped in thick folds of white flannel as large as a bolster. His face was gray, lined with deep furrows of pain, but his eyes beneath his curled peruke were sharp and cold as polished steel. Beside his inkstand lay an ivory-headed walking cane and an open box of dark rappee snuff.

"Come forward, Mrs. Thorne," Harford said. His voice was dry, clipped with the formal cadence of the Salisbury quarter sessions. "Close the door, Thomas. Stand by the window, woman. The draft from the hall chills my marrow."

Hannah stepped to the edge of the Turkey carpet, her hands folded over her gray apron. She dipped a small, respectful curtsy.

"Sir William."

Harford took a pinch of snuff between his thumb and forefinger, carried it to his right nostril, and inhaled with a sharp, wheezing breath. He brushed a stray brown speck from his lace cravat with a flick of his lace handkerchief.

"You know why you are summoned," the magistrate said, tapping a quill pen against a sheet of foolscap covered in fresh handwriting. "The Warminster town watch apprehended Mary Miller, lately dairy maid at High Lea Farm, wandering in the turnpike ditch three miles outside the town on Friday night. She was in a state of violent fever, her garments saturated with lochial blood. When examined by the parish apothecary, she was found to have been delivered of a child within twenty-four hours."

He leaned forward, his dark brows drawing together.

"No infant was in her possession. When questioned by the town constable, she wept, raved, and declared her baby was dead. When questioned again yesterday morning before the Warminster bailiffs, she altered her story and declared her baby was taken from her by persons she refused to name."

He paused, letting the silence fill the warm room. The sea-coal popped in the grate, throwing a shower of yellow sparks against the fender.

"You attended her, Mrs. Thorne."

It was not a question. It was a statement of legal fact, laid upon the table like an iron weight.

"I attended a woman in labor on Thursday night, Sir William," Hannah said evenly.

"In the sheep-shearers' hovel behind the chalk pit on High Lea," Harford said, reading from the foolscap. "A peculiar lying-in chamber for a Christian parish, Mrs. Thorne. Why was she not brought to the farmstead?"

"Master Edward Callow summoned me at two in the morning," Hannah replied. "He instructed me that his father required her delivered where she lay, on account of visitors in the manor house."

Harford's mouth tightened into a thin, bitter line. "Goodman Callow was here yesterday. He informed me that the girl had been dismissed from his service on Wednesday morning for misconduct, and that he had no knowledge of her whereabouts until the constable brought word from Warminster. He swears on his oath that neither he nor his son entered that barn on Thursday night."

Hannah's heart hammered against her ribs, but her face remained smooth as an uncarved gravestone.

"Goodman Callow was in that barn before dawn, Sir William," she said quietly. "He stood in the doorway while I severed the cord."

Harford reached for his ivory cane and gripped the carved head with a claw-like hand. "Take care, Mrs. Thorne. Goodman Callow is a principal tenant of this manor and churchwarden of Nether Bourne. You are a parish pensioner whose license to practice is held at the pleasure of the diocesan ordinary and the vestry. A charge of false oath against a churchwarden is a grave matter before the King's Bench."

"I speak what my eyes saw and my ears heard, your honor," Hannah said. "I took an oath upon the Gospels in Salisbury Cathedral when my licence was granted. I cannot unswear it for the vestry."

Harford leaned his heavy, powdered wig against the high back of his red leather armchair. The fire in the hearth snapped, sending a shower of sparks across the polished brass fender.

"The bishop's oath governs your soul, Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate said with weary precision. "The statutes of the realm govern the parish rates. If Mary Miller's infant is charged upon Nether Bourne, the cost of the order of filiation, the weekly maintenance of two shillings until the child reaches eight years, and the eventual apprenticeship fee of five pounds will fall squarely upon the rateable occupiers of this manor. Callow pays forty percent of the assessment. If he contests the order at the quarter sessions, the litigation will consume twenty guineas of parish money before the lawyers have filed their replications."

He pointed his silver quill pen at Hannah.

"The churchwardens of Warminster have already issued a summons against us under the ninth of George the First," Harford continued. "They allege that the woman was unlawfully removed into their hundred while in labor, to avoid the charge of her delivery. If that complaint is upheld by the bench, Nether Bourne will be fined forty shillings for the contempt, and the infant returned upon our overseers with double costs."

"The girl was not removed into Warminster while in labor, Sir William," Hannah said steadily. "The child was born in High Lea barn on Thursday at four in the morning. I washed the babe and bound the navel with my own hands before the carrier wagon ever touched the parish boundary."

Harford stared at her for a long moment. The pain in his foot seemed to throb; he winced, his fingers clenching the arm of his leather chair until the knuckles showed white through the parchment skin.

"The statute of King James is rigorous, Mrs. Thorne," Harford said in a lower, colder tone. "If a mother conceals the death of her issue, the law presumes she murdered it, unless she can prove by one witness at the least that the child was born dead. Was the child born dead?"

Hannah looked into the fire. The flames licked the black lumps of sea-coal, turning them to glowing embers.

If she said the child was born dead, the law's presumption evaporated. Mary Miller could not be hanged for murdering a child that never lived; she would be discharged with a whipping or three months in the bridewell for bastardy, and Thomas Callow's secret would be buried under the parish nettles.

And if she spoke the truth? If she swore the boy was born alive, vigorous and crying?

Then where was the child?

If the living child could not be produced, Mary stood before the Salisbury Assizes accused of willful infanticide, with the midwife's own words hanging the noose about her neck.

"Sir William," Hannah said, her voice dropping into the quiet dignity of her craft. "The child drew breath. He cried aloud in the straw. He was a living boy."

Harford let out a slow, hissing breath through his teeth. He picked up his quill, dipped it into the black inkstand, and wrote three lines upon the foolscap.

The scratching of the quill against the rough paper sounded loud in the stifling room. Behind the desk, an eight-day bracket clock ticked with a heavy, brass beat.

Harford looked up, his face grim. "If you swear to that upon the book, Mrs. Thorne, you seal the girl's fate. Unless Mary Miller can produce a living infant before the grand jury, she will be tried at the Lent Assizes and hanged before Easter. The statute leaves the court no discretion."

"The child was living when I left the barn, Sir William," Hannah repeated. "Thomas Callow took him from the straw. Edward Callow was in the lane with the horse. Let the court examine them."

Harford struck his desk with his open palm. "A magistrate cannot indict the principal tenant of the Earl of Pembroke upon the bare word of a parish midwife, woman! Where is the child? Can you lay your hand upon it? Can you swear where it was carried?"

"I cannot, your honor," Hannah whispered. "Not yet."

Harford leaned back in his chair, rubbing his forehead with his handkerchief. "Then God help Mary Miller. For the law of England will have her neck."

Sir William Harford stared at the foolscap sheet beneath his fingers for a long, silent minute.

The coal fire hissed in the grate, throwing an orange glare across the gilt lettering of the law books. On the wall behind the desk hung the portrait of his son: Captain Charles Harford of the 33rd Regiment of Foot, painted in his scarlet coat and gold epaulets before he sailed for Ostend in 1793. The boy had his father's high cheekbones and clear gray eyes, untouched by the bitter cynicism that forty years on the magistrate's bench had carved into the old baronet's brow.

"Do you know what happens to a woman committed for child-murder at the Assizes, Mrs. Thorne?" Harford asked, his voice dropping into a dry, quiet cadence.

"I have seen the Salisbury cage, Sir William," Hannah answered.

"You have seen the outside of the cage," Harford corrected her, leaning back against his cushions and grimacing as the gout twinged in his bandaged foot. "I have sat on the commission of oyer and terminer for twenty years. A woman committed under the statute of King James is lodged in the dungeon beneath Fisherton bridge. The floor is river silt; the water rises to the straw when the Avon floods; and the jailer charges fourpence a day for dirty water and moldy barley bread. If she does not die of the jail fever before the judges ride in from Winchester, she is brought into court in irons, half-naked and raving with terror."

He pointed his bony finger at her.

"Twelve honest shopkeepers and tenant farmers sit in the jury box. They look upon the girl; they hear the parish overseer swear that the bastard would have cost the rate-payers five pounds a year; they hear the midwife swear that the infant was born alive; and in ten minutes they return a verdict of willful murder. Two days later, the sheriff conducts her to the gallows hill behind the city, and she hangs until she is dead."

Hannah felt the blood run cold in her veins, but she did not drop her eyes.

"And is that justice, Sir William? When the father who got the child sits warm in his churchwarden's pew, and the master who stole the babe walks free upon his four hundred acres?"

Harford's eyes flashed with sudden, aristocratic fury. "Justice is the execution of the statute, woman! It is not for a parish midwife to lecture the King's Commission on equity! Dalton is plain, Burn is plain, Hale is plain: the law presumes murder where concealment is proved. If Mary Miller concealed the birth, she is guilty under the act."

"She did not conceal the birth, your honor," Hannah said firmly. "She summoned a licensed midwife. I am here before your bench. That is no concealment."

Harford leaned his head back against the leather chair, closing his eyes in sheer weariness. "Go home, Mrs. Thorne. The court sits on Friday. And pray that you have not spoken a girl's death warrant."

Sir William leaned his head against the high morocco back of his chair, watching the flames curl around the lumps of sea-coal.

"You speak of your licensing oath, Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate said softly, his voice dry as dead leaves. "Do you know what the Chancellor of the Diocese would say if Goodman Callow brings a formal petition against you at Salisbury? The churchwardens have the power to certify that a midwife is negligent, that she attends unlawful births, or that she conceals the sins of lewd wenches. A bishop's parchment is easily revoked upon the testimony of two respectable rate-payers."

Hannah folded her hands upon her apron. "The Bishop of Salisbury granted my licence after examination before three sworn physicians, Sir William. It is not Goodman Callow who gave me my hands, and it is not Goodman Callow who can take them away."

Harford gave a short, bitter chuckle that ended in a wheezing cough. He picked up his ivory snuff-box, tapped the lid twice, and placed a pinch of rappee upon his wrist.

"You have a stout spirit, woman," Harford murmured, inhaling the snuff with a sharp snort. "A rare thing in a parish where every cottager pulls his forelock to the tenant masters. But spirit does not pay for winter fuel. When Friday comes, Tarrant will take your deposition upon the Gospels in open court. If you swear the child was born alive, the law will have its course."

Hannah dipped a respectful curtsy. "I ask only for the King's law, Sir William. The true law, that protects the living child as well as the landlord's rate."

# Chapter 13: The Statute of James

The magistrate laid down his quill and turned the heavy calfskin folio that lay open beside his elbow.

"Listen to the statute, Mrs. Thorne," Sir William said. His voice echoed off the book-lined walls with the dry authority of the law. "*Act of the Twenty-First Year of King James the First, Chapter Twenty-Seven: For the prevention of the murder of bastard children.*"

He ran his bony forefinger down the printed lines of black-letter type:

"*Whereas many lewd women that have been delivered of bastard children, to avoid their shame, and to escape punishment, do secretly bury or conceal the death of their children, and after, if the child be found dead, the said women do allege that the said child was born dead: Be it enacted, that if any woman be delivered of any issue of her body, which being born alive, should by the laws of this realm be a bastard, and that she endeavor privately, either by drowning or secret burying thereof, or any other way, either by herself or the procuring of others, so to conceal the death thereof, the said mother so offending shall suffer death as in case of murder, except such mother can make proof by one witness at the least, that the child (whose death was by her so intended to be concealed) was born dead.*"

Harford closed the heavy volume with a dull thud that raised a puff of dry leather dust.

"You understand the gravity of what you have sworn, Mrs. Thorne," Harford said, leaning his chin against his laced cravat. "If you swear the child was born dead, Mary Miller is guilty only of lewdness and vagrancy. She will be set in the stocks at Warminster market cross and returned to her parish of legal settlement."

He fixed his cold, gray eyes upon her face.

"But if you testify in open court at the Assizes that the child was born alive, and no living infant is produced before the grand jury, the law of England presumes murder. Mary Miller will hang by the neck from the gallows at Fisherton Anger before the spring assize is ended."

Hannah stood motionless. The air in the study smelled of ink, wax, and the dry paper of dead centuries.

"And what of the men who took him from the straw, Sir William?" she asked.

Harford tapped his ivory cane against his footstool. "The law demands evidence, Mrs. Thorne. Not village whispers, not rumors from the church porch. If Thomas Callow took the child, where is it? If Edward Callow carried it away, to whom did he deliver it? Unless a living babe is brought before this bench, your testimony will not save Mary Miller. It will hang her."

Hannah bowed her head. "The truth is what God heard in the lambing shed, Sir William. I can swear to nothing else."

Harford waved his hand toward the door. "Go home, Mrs. Thorne. You will be summoned to the Petty Sessions on Friday. Until then, speak to no one of this deposition."

Hannah stood before the magistrate's desk as the winter afternoon faded into blue dusk.

Through the tall sash casements of the study, the shadow of the great cedar tree on the lawn stretched across the snow like a giant hand. The silence in the room was heavy, broken only by the ticking of the bracket clock and the muffled coughing of the old footman in the corridor outside.

Sir William turned the heavy calfskin page of the statute book with a dry rustle.

"The law of England is an ancient engine, Mrs. Thorne," he said softly, staring into the red embers of the coal fire. "It was framed by men who knew that human nature is corrupt, that passions are wild as mountain water, and that without the terror of the rope, the countryside would be filled with the unburied bones of murdered innocents. It is a harsh law. Sometimes an unjust law in its particular operation. But it is the only shield that preserves the peace of this realm."

"The peace of the realm was not broken by Mary Miller, Sir William," Hannah said. "It was broken by the man who put her in an unheated lambing pen to save his family from parish disgrace."

Harford sighed, a sound that seemed to come from the depths of his aching bones. He closed the book, took his ivory cane, and pushed himself upright from his chair.

"The world is not governed by midwives, Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate whispered. "It is governed by land, lease, and statute. Bear that in mind when you take the oath on Friday."

The magistrate turned back to the legal folio, his fingers tracing the margin of the King James statute.

"A statute of blood," Harford whispered, almost to himself. "Framed when the Puritans ruled the Parliament, to terrify unwed wenches into holy marriage. In sixty years of practice, I have seen twenty women hanged under its provisions. Half of them were simple dairy girls whose babes were stillborn in the ditches, with no witness to swear to their innocence."

He looked up at Hannah, his eyes reflecting the orange glow of the grate.

"You are that witness, Mrs. Thorne. But your testimony cuts both ways. If you swear the child lived, you clear Mary Miller of having dropped a dead babe in secret; but you bind her to produce the living soul, or hang as a murderess. You carry a two-edged sword into this court."

"The truth is never a sword against the innocent, your honor," Hannah said.

Harford closed the book with a heavy sigh. "We shall see on Friday, Mrs. Thorne. We shall see."

# Chapter 14: The Tenant's Warning

Hannah walked back along the river path from Bourne Hall. The afternoon sun had sunk behind the high downs of Upton Lovell, leaving a pale green sky streaked with cold crimson.

When she reached the turn of the lane beside her cottage, she saw a heavy farm cart standing before her garden gate. Two shaggy carthorses stood in the shafts, their breath rising like steam boilers into the twilight. In the bed of the cart lay a half-cord of split ash logs, dry and white, and on the tailboard rested a wooden firkin of salt butter and a woven hamper covered with clean canvas.

Beside the gate, his thumbs hooked into the armholes of his sheepskin waistcoat, stood Goodman Thomas Callow.

He was chewing a wisp of dry clover, his heavy shoulders leaning against the gatepost. When he saw Hannah approaching along the bourne, he spat the grass into the road and stepped forward to meet her.

"A cold walk from the great house, Hannah," Callow said. His voice was jovial, loud as a church bell, but his eyes were hard and fixed.

"The air is clean on the water meadows, Master Callow," Hannah said, stopping three paces from his boots.

Callow gestured toward the cart with his ash stick. "I have brought your winter firing. Good seasoned ash from the high copse, split fine for your small hearth. And there is a side of bacon in the hamper, cured in salt and brown sugar, and twenty pounds of summer butter from the dairy pans."

"I did not order ash logs from High Lea, Master Callow," Hannah said.

Callow stepped closer. He smelled of tobacco juice, malt liquor, and the greasy wool of unwashed sheep. He lowered his voice until it was a low, vibrating rumble that did not carry beyond the garden wall.

"A widow does not order winter fuel, Hannah. A widow receives it from the parish vestry. And who is the vestry in Nether Bourne? Who pays forty pounds a year to the poor rates? Who rents four hundred acres of down and water meadow from my Lord Pembroke?"

He tapped the end of his staff against the flint stones of her garden wall.

"Sir William sent for you today," Callow murmured. "The old man is half-dead with the stone and the gout. His mind wanders back to his dead boy in Flanders. He has notions about King's statutes and Assize trials. But Sir William does not live in this valley in January when the snow drifts six feet deep against the cottage thatch. I live here. My carters clear the lanes; my granaries feed the parish cotters when the flour mills freeze."

He leaned down, his face inches from her hood.

"You were always a sensible woman, Hannah. You delivered my boy Edward when the doctor in Warminster had given my wife up for dead. I have never forgotten that. I want no harm to come to you."

"What do you want, Master Callow?" Hannah asked, her hands steady inside her cloak.

Callow smiled, showing yellow, broken teeth. "The girl was out of her senses with milk-fever in that barn. You know how fever takes a young maid: she imagines sights and sounds that never were. If you tell Sir William that the babe was born dead—strangled in the navel cord, as many are—the whole business is ended. The girl goes to the house of correction for a month, and the parish is quiet."

"And if I say the child was born alive?" Hannah asked.

Callow's smile vanished. His jaw set like an iron vise.

"Then you will have no ash logs to burn this winter, Hannah," he said softly. "The parish vestry will find another midwife for the three villages. A younger woman from Warminster who knows how to respect the tenant masters. And the roof of this cottage belongs to the churchwardens. When the thatch rots in the spring rains, no carter of mine will bring straw to mend it."

He turned, caught the reins of his lead horse, and clicked his tongue. The heavy cart rumbled into motion, its iron-shod wheels groaning against the frosted flints of the lane.

Hannah watched the cart until it disappeared behind the hedge. The wind whistled through the bare twigs of the elder bush by the gate, scattering dry leaves across the frosted grass. She unlatched her gate, walked past the pile of ash logs without touching a single stick, and entered her quiet kitchen.

The cart rumbled away down the lane, leaving Hannah standing by her flint garden wall in the freezing twilight.

The smell of horse sweat, tobacco, and cold dung hung in the air, slowly dispelled by the sharp north wind blowing off the downs. She looked down at the pile of split ash logs Callow had dumped before her gate: white, straight-grained wood, seasoned in the High Lea copse for two summers, dry as bone. It would have burned with a clear, steady flame, throwing out the fierce heat that her drafty cottage so desperately needed.

She turned her back upon the wood.

She took her iron key, unlocked the low wicket gate, and walked up the gravel path to her door. On either side of the path, her winter herbs stood stiff with rime: bushes of dark rosemary, stalks of lavender encased in ice, and the frosted rosettes of winter savory.

In the kitchen, the peat fire had burned low. She did not light a candle; the pale moonlight filtering through the casement threw the diamond shadows of the leaded panes across the scrubbed deal floor.

She thought of Thomas Callow's words: *A spark will fall upon your thatch.*

It was no idle boast. In rural Wiltshire, rick-burning and barn-firing were the ancient weapons of the downland class war. A tenant farmer whose laborers were insolent would wake to find his wheat ricks ablaze; a laborer who defied his master would find his cottage thatch burning over his sleeping children. The parish constables were powerless against a bundle of dry brimstone matches dropped in the dark.

She knelt by the hearth, blew upon the turf embers, and added two sods of dark bog peat from her own small stack. The peat caught with a crackle, filling the room with its rich, pungent smoke.

She sat upon the wooden stool, folded her hands in her apron, and listened to the wind rising over the chalk ridge.

The wind off the chalk ridge whistled through the elder bush beside Hannah's wicket gate.

She watched the dark silhouette of Thomas Callow's cart disappear past the watercress beds, the red tail-lantern swinging with the uneven motion of the rutted lane. The threat hung over her flint cottage like a storm cloud: *A spark will fall upon your thatch.*

In fifty years, the Callows had bought up or leased every arable acre in Nether Bourne. They had pulled down nine ancient cottages on the common to turn the land into sheep pastures; they had enclosed the high downland turf where the cottagers had cut peat since the days of King Alfred. Those who resisted were driven to the county workhouse or forced to emigrate across the seas.

Hannah turned back into her dark kitchen.

She took down her iron rushlight holder, struck a spark from her flint into the charred linen tinder, and blew the spark into a steady yellow flame. The room smelled of dried pennyroyal, tallow, and the damp flint of the walls.

She laid her hand upon the deal table.

This was her sanctuary. Here she had prepared her tinctures, boiled her linen, and prayed through the long night watches for the laboring mothers of the valley. She would not surrender it to a tenant farmer's threat.

She set the rushlight upon the hearthstone, drew her bible to her lap, and read the ninety-first psalm by the flickering flame.

# Chapter 15: The Downland Wind

The hard frost settled upon the valley like an iron lid.

By Wednesday morning, the Wylye was frozen over from bank to bank at the shallow sheep-dips. The watercress beds beneath Hannah's garden wall were stiff with rime, the dark green leaves encased in milky ice. On the high downs, the wind screamed through the bare thorn hedges, driving dry, powdery snow into the sunken lanes until the chalk tracks were obliterated.

In the village, the sheep were brought down from the open ridge into the valley fold-yards. Hannah could hear their dry, hacking coughs carried on the north wind: four hundred breeding ewes huddled together in the hurdles behind High Lea, their wool gray with dirt and powdered snow.

The cold entered the cottages like an enemy through an unbarred door.

In the hovel of Martha Gane, the blacksmith's wife, a child lay sick with the croup. Hannah spent two hours by the hearth, holding the choking infant over a basin of hot vinegar and boiling chamomile, listening to the harsh, crowing breath that signaled the closing of the windpipe.

"Will he die, Hannah?" Martha wept, her hands black with charcoal from the forge.

"He will live if we keep the chest warm," Hannah said, rubbing elder-tallow into the little ribcage and wrapping the child in warm flannel. "Keep the kettle boiling, Martha. Do not let the fire go out."

As she walked home through the village lane, she met old Giles the carter hauling a load of dry peat from the bogs.

"A bitter season, Mistress Thorne," the old man said, pulling his wool cap over his ears. "They say bread flour has risen to fourteen pence the quartern loaf at Salisbury market. The cotters are eating turnip tops and boiled bran."

"The masters have grain in the granaries," Hannah said.

Giles spat into the snow. "Master Callow has three hundred quarters of prime barley locked in the high granary at High Lea. He is holding the sacks for fifteen shillings a bushel. He says he will not sell a peck to the parish until the price reaches sixteen at Warminster."

Hannah looked up at the high ridge. The clouds hung low and yellow over the downs, heavy with another snowstorm.

The power of Thomas Callow was not a phantom. It was the food in the children's mouths and the fuel in their grates. If she defied him before the magistrates on Friday, she would not only bring ruin upon herself; she would turn the master's wrath upon every poor family in the parish that looked to High Lea for winter charity.

She entered her cottage and barred the door. The ash logs Callow had brought still lay in the garden, untouched by her hearth. She had left them in the frost, burning only her own dry peat turf.

She sat by the chimney, her coat still wrapped about her shoulders, and listened to the downland wind rattling the leaded panes.

The house was cold, but the peat fire gave off a steady, aromatic heat that warmed her shins. On the table lay her wooden trencher and a piece of dry barley bread soaked in warm milk. She ate slowly, her thoughts traveling back across twenty years of winter vigils.

How many times had she stood between a tenant master and a helpless maid?

In every parish in Wiltshire, the story was the same. A master's son cornered a girl in the hay-barn; a bastard was born in secret; and if the child died or disappeared, the law laid the whip upon the woman while the young squire rode to hounds in his red coat. The Poor Laws were designed by landlords to protect the parish rates, not to protect the women who dropped their babes in the ditches.

She looked at her work-hardened fingers. The callouses were deep, yellowed by years of scouring lye and holding hemp reins. Those fingers had brought two thousand lives into the light.

She would not let one death be hidden in the dark to save Thomas Callow's lease.

The frost held the Wylye valley in an unyielding grip through Thursday.

In the village lane, the ruts were frozen so hard that iron wagon tires rang against the stones like church bells. At the village forge, John Gane was shoeing farm horses with frost-corks—sharp iron studs welded to the shoes to keep the heavy beasts from slipping on the icy chalk slopes.

Hannah walked to the parish well at noon to fetch her water.

The oak windlass was coated in clear ice; she had to break the surface of the well with an iron crowbar before the bucket could sink into the dark water sixty feet below. Around the well-head, half a dozen village women were gathered with their wooden pails, their shawls wrapped tight around their heads, talking in hushed, urgent whispers.

"They say Mary Miller has taken the lung-rot in the Warminster cage," said young Ann, whose husband worked on the High Lea threshing floor. "My sister was at Warminster market yesterday and heard the turnkey say the girl cannot stand upon her feet. Her stays are black with dried milk."

"And what of the master?" asked Goodwife Povey, looking over her shoulder toward the ridge. "Callow was in the Red Lion this morning with two attorneys from Devizes. They say he has hired counselor Garrow from London to defend Edward if the matter comes before the Quarter Sessions."

"Gold can buy London counselors," Hannah said quietly, lifting her brimming pail from the stone coping. "It cannot buy a clean conscience before the throne of God."

The women fell silent, looking at Hannah with a mixture of awe and fear. In a village where every cottager depended upon tenant farmer favor for gleaning rights, winter firewood, and parish pensions, the midwife's open defiance of Thomas Callow seemed like madness.

Hannah carried her water back along the frozen lane.

She knew what the village was thinking. They thought she was throwing her bread away for the sake of a fallen dairy maid. They did not understand that if the licensed midwife of Nether Bourne bowed to Thomas Callow's lie, no poor woman's child would ever be safe in the three parishes again.

She set her pail upon the bench, took down her basket, and prepared for her journey to Warminster.

The third night of the freeze brought the temperature down to zero.

Inside Hannah's cottage, the water in the stoneware pitcher on the dresser froze solid, splitting the glazed clay with a sharp, pistol-like report at two in the morning. She woke to find her breath freezing in white crystals upon the wool of her blanket.

She rose, rekindled the peat fire with dry birch twigs, and wrapped her sheepskin coat around her shoulders.

From the window, the valley looked like a world carved from white bone. The Wylye was completely silent, its rapid current imprisoned beneath three inches of clear river ice. On the high downs, the sheep had ceased their coughing; they stood huddled together in the lee of the thorn barrows, their fleeces frosted white, waiting for the dawn.

Hannah took a cup of hot chamomile tea and sat by the embers.

She thought of Mary Miller in the stone lock-up at Warminster. Without fire, without warm food, with breasts hard as stones from unsucked milk, the girl could not survive another forty-eight hours of such cold. The jail fever or the chest-rot would take her before the magistrates could convene the Petty Sessions.

And the child?

If Edward had carried him to Salisbury, who was tending him? A city foster-mother paid five shillings by an unfeeling drover? A woman who had children of her own starving in the weaving cellars?

Hannah set down her cup.

She could not wait for Friday's court. She had to know where the child lay. She had to look upon his face with her own eyes.

She pulled on her pattens, took her calfskin basket, and prepared to climb the hill to High Lea.

# Chapter 16: The Cell at Warminster

On Thursday morning, Hannah took the carrier's wagon to Warminster.

The town lock-up stood in the yard behind the market house, an ancient stone building of rough flint rubble with a low slate roof and narrow slit windows barred with iron straps. The air in the courtyard smelled of rotting cabbage stalks, horse urine, and the greasy wash from the nearby wool dyeworks.

The town constable, a fat weaver named Higgins with a stained leather apron, unlocked the heavy oak door with a massive iron key.

"Ten minutes, Mrs. Thorne," Higgins grunted, holding out his greasy hand for the fourpence toll. "Sir William's clerk said you might see her to dress her breasts, but you are not to carry any letters or tools into the cage."

Hannah stepped into the cell.

The room was six paces square, entirely paved with cold flagstones that sweated moisture from the river gravel below. There was neither hearth nor chimney. In the corner, upon a heap of mouldering rye straw covered with a single horse rug, sat Mary Miller.

The girl looked up as the iron door creaked shut. Her face was hollow, gray as chalk dust, her dark eyes sunken deep into their sockets. Her linen shift was filthy, stiffened with yellow serum and dark blood across the bosom.

"Hannah..." Mary whispered, her dry lips barely parting.

Hannah knelt beside the straw. The cell was freezing, colder than the open street, but Mary's body was shivering with the unmistakable tremblings of milk-fever.

"Let me see your breasts, child," Hannah said gently, unbuttoning the front of her smock.

The girl's breasts were swollen as round as loaves, hard and inflamed to a deep purple red. The nipples were cracked, weeping yellow pus and curded milk that had had no infant mouth to draw it down. The skin was burning to the touch, stretched so tight that the blue veins stood out like cords.

Hannah reached into her pocket and drew out a pot of elder ointment and a clean square of linen. With firm, practiced strokes, she began to express the hardened milk from the swollen glands. Mary cried out in pain, biting her lip until the blood ran, but as the thick, curded milk began to flow into the linen, the agonizing tension in her chest eased.

"Cry if you must, Mary," Hannah murmured, working her thumbs in circular sweeps from the shoulder toward the nipple. "The milk must come away, or the fever will enter the blood and turn your brain to fire."

Mary buried her face in Hannah's wool cloak, her thin shoulders shaking with silent, racking sobs.

"Where is he, Hannah?" she wept. "Where did they take my boy?"

The turnkey's iron key grated in the lock of the Warminster cage with a sound like a pistol shot.

Hannah knelt in the foul straw beside Mary Miller, supporting the girl's trembling shoulders against her knee. The cell was dark, lit only by a gray slit of winter daylight high in the flint wall, through which powdery snow drifted onto the flagstones.

Mary's face was fevered, her skin hot and dry as parchment, her lips covered in brown fever crusts. She clung to Hannah's woolen cloak like a drowning sailor to a spar.

"They took him, Hannah..." Mary sobbed, her voice breaking into a dry, gasping cough. "They wrapped him in the horse rug... I heard him cry as Edward went through the hurdle... A little, angry cry... He wanted his milk, Hannah... And I had two breasts full of sweet milk, and no mouth to drink it..."

"He is alive, Mary," Hannah whispered firmly, rubbing the girl's cold hands between her own warm palms. "Do you hear me? The child lives. Edward gave him to John Pike to carry to Salisbury."

Mary's eyes widened, a sudden, desperate hope flaring in their sunken depths. "Salisbury? But... but the drovers' carts are open to the frost! An infant will die on the Plain in an hour!"

"A child wrapped in wool and fed with warm milk can live through three frosts," Hannah said. "I am riding to Salisbury today. I will find the weaver's cellar. Keep your heart strong, Mary. Tomorrow you must stand before the magistrates and name Edward Callow as the father."

The heavy oak door swung open, and Constable Higgins thrust his fat face into the cell.

"Time is up, Mrs. Thorne," the constable grunted, jangling his iron keys. "The Warminster bailiff is coming across the yard. Out you go."

Hannah pressed Mary's forehead once more with her lips, rose from the straw, and walked out into the freezing court.

The stone walls of the Warminster cage were weeping moisture that froze into glistening sheets of rime near the floor.

Hannah gently fastened the front of Mary's stained shift, pinning the linen with her iron bodkin. The girl's breathing had slowed, the agonizing tension of the milk-fever easing as the hard caking of the breasts was relieved.

"Listen to me, Mary," Hannah whispered, leaning her face close to the fevered cheek. "I am going to High Lea today. I will find where Edward carried your boy. If he is in Salisbury, I will ride to the city myself."

Mary reached out her trembling hand, her fingers closing over Hannah's sleeve with the desperate grip of a child.

"Bring him back, Hannah," she sobbed, her dark eyes wide and pleading in the gloom. "Do not let them hang me without seeing his face once more. He had Edward's chin... and the little strawberry mark upon his shoulder... God's mark, you called it..."

"God's mark," Hannah repeated softly. "And God does not forget his own."

The turnkey rattled his keys outside the iron grating. Hannah kissed the girl's forehead, pulled her hood over her face, and stepped back into the freezing courtyard.

# Chapter 17: The Father's Name

Hannah did not stop her hands. She worked the hard caking from the left breast until the purple knot softened beneath her fingers.

"Tell me what happened after I left the barn, Mary," she whispered, her mouth close to the girl's ear. "Every word. As you hope for God's mercy."

Mary choked back a sob, wiping her nose on the sleeve of her stained shift.

"Master Callow came into the shed," she whispered, her voice trembling in the damp gloom of the cell. "He had the oilskin sack. He took the baby from my arms while I was too weak to lift my hands. I screamed for Edward, but Edward was standing by the cart in the lane, holding his head in his hands."

"What did Callow say to you?" Hannah pressed.

"He said... he said the child was going to a decent foster-mother in Devizes," Mary sobbed. "A respectable woman whose husband was a carrier on the London road. He swore he had paid five golden guineas to have the boy apprenticed when he came of age. He said if I held my tongue and went quietly to my aunt's house in Warminster, Edward would come for me when the spring sowing was done."

"And did you go to your aunt's?"

Mary shook her head, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on her hollow cheeks.

"My aunt died at Michaelmas, Hannah! Master Callow knew that! He put me in the light cart with his under-carter, but when we reached the Warminster turnpike, the carter told me to get down in the road. He said Master Callow had given him orders to leave me at the parish boundary."

Her voice rose in a thin, desperate wail that echoed against the damp flint walls.

"I had no money, Hannah! No shoes, and my milk running like water through my stays! I wandered in the ditch until the town watch found me by the chalk pit! I fell in the mud, crying for my baby! And now... now the magistrates say I murdered him! They say they will hang me at Fisherton!"

Hannah drew Mary's head against her breast, wrapping her frieze cloak around the girl's trembling body.

"Listen to me, Mary Miller," Hannah said, her voice hard and clear as an iron chime. "The magistrate is Sir William Harford. Tomorrow is the Petty Sessions at Bourne Hall. You must stand before the bench and speak the truth. You must name Edward Callow as the father, and you must swear before the King's Justice that Thomas Callow took your living child from the straw."

Mary looked up, her dark eyes wide with terror.

"They will never believe a dairy maid against Master Callow, Hannah! He is churchwarden! He owns the bread in every mouth!"

"I will be there, Mary," Hannah said, smoothing the matted hair from the girl's brow. "I will stand beside you at the bar. And I will swear that your son was born alive, with God's mark upon his collarbone."

The carrier's wagon rumbled back along the Warminster turnpike through the gathering dusk.

Hannah sat on the wooden bench beneath the canvas tilt, her hands tucked deep inside her sheepskin muff, her eyes fixed upon the vast, white curves of the downland ridges. The snow had begun to fall again, fine and dry as sifted flour, swirling across the road in snaking white ribbons.

Beside her sat an old wool-comber from Heytesbury, his wooden comb-box strapped between his knees.

"A bad business in Warminster today, mistress," the old man muttered, pulling a clay pipe from his pocket. "The magistrates are committing every vagrant to Fisherton. They say the French have landed in Ireland, and the King's ministers have sent orders to the county lieutenancy to clear the roads of masterless men and lewd wenches."

"The King's ministers would do better to look to the price of corn," Hannah remarked.

The comber spat into the straw. "Aye! Callow and his sort are holding the wheat till it rots in the sacks. They want twenty shillings a bushel before they sell to the mills. A working man must spend his whole week's wage for two loaves of black barley bread."

He looked at Hannah's leather basket.

"You are the midwife from Nether Bourne, are you not? My daughter was delivered by you five years ago at Upton. A fine boy he is now, running the sheep hurdles for his grandfather."

"I remember him," Hannah said softly. "Born at Michaelmas, in the middle of the barley harvest."

"A good memory," the old man said, nodding with satisfaction. "A midwife who remembers the names of the children is an honest woman. Keep clear of Thomas Callow, mistress. The man is hard as an iron horseshoe. He broke two tenant cotters at Heytesbury last Michaelmas for gathering dead wood in his copses."

Hannah looked out into the snowy dusk. The lights of Nether Bourne were beginning to glimmer across the water meadows: small, yellow sparks of home in a vast and indifferent landscape.

The carrier's wagon dropped Hannah at the crossroads below Nether Bourne as the twilight deepened into blue darkness.

The air smelled of snow and woodsmoke from the cottage chimneys. As she walked up the lane toward her gate, she met old Giles the carter leading his heavy team into the High Lea fold-yard.

The carter stopped, his wool cap pulled down over his frosty eyebrows, looking around cautiously before speaking.

"Master Edward was in the stable this afternoon, Hannah," the carter whispered, his hand resting on the shaft-horse's collar. "He was drinking gin from the bottle, crying like a maid. He told young Tom he was going to enlist in the King's cavalry at Salisbury tomorrow to escape his father's wrath."

"He will not enlist before Friday," Hannah said firmly.

"The master has locked the granary," Giles continued, lowering his voice still further. "He had two drovers from Fisherton in the kitchen at noon. There is talk in the village that the master is sending fifty wethers to Salisbury market on the Friday cart. John Pike is hauling the wethers."

Hannah's heart gave a sudden, fierce throb.

"John Pike?" she asked.

"Aye. The Fisherton drover. A rough fellow who sleeps under his wagon with an ash cudgel."

Hannah nodded, her mind working with swift, cold clarity. "Thank you, Giles. Keep your team warm."

She walked on to her cottage, her boots crunching sharply in the frozen snow.

# Chapter 18: The Vestry Meeting

On Thursday evening, the monthly vestry meeting of the parish of Nether Bourne was held in the church nave.

It was an austere gathering. The churchwardens, the overseer of the poor, the parish surveyor of highways, and four substantial tenant farmers sat around the long oak communion table beneath the flickering light of three tallow dips in tin sconces. The stone nave was freezing; the breath of the men rose in white plumes toward the timber roof, where painted Norman angels looked down upon the accounts of the parish poor.

Thomas Callow sat at the head of the table.

He had laid his silver-mounted riding whip and his pigskin account book upon the oak boards. Beside him sat George Finch, the overseer, his spectacles balanced on the tip of his pointed nose as he turned the pages of the relief book.

"We have sixteen paupers on the weekly rate," Finch whined, running his ink-stained finger down the column of figures. "Widow Barnes at two shillings; old Peter the thresher at eighteen pence; and three bastard children chargeable to the tithing at ninepence each. The poor rate must be raised by threepence in the pound, or the parish chest will be empty before Candlemas."

Callow struck his fist upon the table. The pewter inkstand jumped, splashing black drops across the sheepskin register.

"Not a penny more!" Callow roared, his voice ringing through the empty aisles. "We are eaten up by idleness and lewdness! The cottagers spend their wages at the Red Lion and come to the vestry with their hands out when the frost sets in! I propose we cut Widow Barnes to twelvepence, and as for old Peter, let his son in Upton keep him, or let him go to the county workhouse!"

A murmur of assent ran around the table from the tenant farmers. None dared cross Thomas Callow when the lease renewals for the Earl's estate were coming before the steward at Lady Day.

Callow leaned forward, his small eyes fixing upon the churchwarden's chest in the corner.

"And there is another matter," Callow said, dropping his voice into a harsh, deliberate cadence. "The parish midwife. Hannah Thorne."

The men around the table fell silent. George Finch looked up from his figures, blinking nervously.

"Mrs. Thorne receives twenty shillings a year from the church rate," Callow continued, his thick fingers drumming against his riding whip. "And she occupies the cottage beside the bourne rent-free by grant of the vestry. But I am informed that her conduct of late has been unseemly. She has been carrying tales between the parishes, stirring up strife, and consorting with felonious wenches who stand charged with child-murder before the King's bench."

He looked around the table, his gaze challenging every man present to meet his eye.

"A midwife must be a woman of sober conversation and respect for the masters of the parish. At the Easter vestry, I shall propose that her annual pension be withdrawn, and that notice of quit be served upon her cottage. We can find an honest woman from Devizes who knows her place."

From the dark shadow of the south aisle, near the Norman font, a woman stepped forward into the circle of candlelight.

It was Hannah Thorne.

She wore her gray cloak, her hood thrown back, her hair pinned neatly beneath her linen coif. Her face was calm, pale in the yellow tallow light.

"Good evening, gentlemen of the vestry," Hannah said clearly.

The farmers stared at her as if a ghost had risen from the churchyard vaults. Thomas Callow's face darkened to the color of a bruised plum.

"This is a closed meeting of the rate-payers, woman!" Callow snarled, half-rising from his oak armchair. "Midwives have no seat at this table! Get you home!"

Hannah did not retreat an inch. She walked to the foot of the communion table and laid both hands flat upon the oak boards.

"I have served this parish for twenty-one years, Goodman Callow," she said, her voice steady and resonant in the stone church. "I delivered twelve of the children belonging to the men sitting at this board. I have asked for nothing from the vestry but my bread and my roof."

She looked directly into Callow's furious eyes.

"Tomorrow at two o'clock, the Petty Sessions meet at Bourne Hall. If you have an account to settle with me, Master Callow, bring it before Sir William Harford and the King's Justice. We shall see then whose name is written in the book of truth."

She turned her back upon the table and walked down the central aisle, her pattens clicking firmly against the stones until the heavy oak door closed behind her.

The church nave of Saint Mary's was cold as a burial vault after the vestrymen departed.

The three tallow candles on the communion table had guttered down to smoky pools of grease in their tin sockets, throwing long, trembling shadows across the Norman pillars. Hannah stood by the ancient stone font where every child of Nether Bourne had been baptized since the days of King Stephen.

In the dark aisle, the sound of boots echoed on the flagstones.

George Finch, the overseer of the poor, lingered near the south door, wrapping his threadbare woolen scarf twice around his thin neck. He looked at Hannah with his small, sharp tailor's eyes, hesitating before he spoke.

"You should not have spoken so to Master Callow, Hannah," Finch whispered, glancing nervously back toward the church porch. "He is churchwarden. He holds the keys to the parish chest. If he moves the vestry to stop your pension, I have no power to pay you from the rate."

"I did not ask you for your power, George Finch," Hannah replied. "I asked only that the truth be spoken."

"Truth does not fill the parish pot," Finch said sourly. "The poor rates are forty pounds in arrears. If Mary Miller's bastard becomes chargeable to this tithing, it will cost the rate-payers two shillings a week until the child is seven years old. That is thirty-six pounds of parish money! Where is it to come from? From Callow's rent! And if Callow refuses to pay his assessment, the parish will be sued at the Quarter Sessions!"

Hannah stepped closer to the little overseer, her eyes holding his in the gloom.

"And to save thirty-six pounds of parish money, you would let an innocent girl hang from the gallows at Fisherton?"

Finch flinched, pulling his wool scarf over his mouth. He did not answer. He turned, opened the heavy oak door, and scurried out into the churchyard like a rat escaping a flooded cellar.

Hannah remained alone in the dark church. Above her, in the belfry tower, the great tenor bell groaned slightly in the wind, its hemp rope swinging like a pendulum in the dark.

The cold in the church nave deepened as the vestrymen argued over the poor rates.

"The cottagers can eat potatoes," Thomas Callow was shouting, his fist banging the communion table. "My father lived on barley bread and water in Fisherton gaol, and he died an honest tenant! I will not have my rent money squandered on lazy scoundrels who won't work twelve hours a day for sixpence!"

Reverend Thistlethwayte adjusted his spectacles, his voice thin and quavering against the farmer's roar. "The Christian duty of the parish, Goodman Callow... The scriptural injunction to feed the hungry..."

"The scriptures do not pay the Earl of Pembroke's rent, parson!" Callow snarled, turning upon the trembling rector. "And the scriptures do not keep the King's taxes from our doors! If the churchwardens cannot govern the rates, I will surrender my seat and let the county magistrates lay an assessment upon the whole tithing!"

The tenant farmers muttered anxiously. None wanted the magistrates from Salisbury looking into the parish accounts, where three hundred acres of newly enclosed downland had been quietly omitted from the poor rate assessments for five years.

Hannah stood in the shadows of the south aisle, watching the men squabble over their pence and shillings.

These were the rulers of the rural world: hard, grasping tenant farmers who looked upon human life as a tally of profit and loss, weighing a child's breath against a bushel of seed corn.

She stepped forward into the candlelight, her voice cutting through their noise like an iron bell.

"You speak of gold, Goodman Callow," Hannah said, her eyes flashing in the gloom. "Speak of it tomorrow before Sir William Harford. We shall see what the King's Bench says to a man who trades an infant's life for thirty pieces of silver."

# Chapter 19: The Herbs on the Beam

Hannah returned to her cottage in the dark.

The moon had risen over the chalk downs, round and silver in a sky of clear ice. The flint walls of her cottage gleamed like frozen salt beside the rushing water of the chalk bourne.

She did not light a candle. She blew upon the embers of the peat fire until a yellow tongue of flame licked the black pot-hook, then set on the kettle for a cup of mint tea.

She climbed the ladder to the low loft beneath the thatch.

The loft was cold as a meat-cellar, smelling of dry herbs, sheep tallow, and aged oak timber. From the collar beams hung hundreds of dried bundles, harvested across three parishes during the long summer days: wild thyme, agrimony for wound-wash, dried elderberries tied in calico bags, and bundles of brown tansy leaves tied with hemp twine in lots of seven.

She reached for a bundle of tansy and ran her fingers through the brittle leaves. They crumbled into fragrant, bitter dust between her palms.

For twenty years, these plants had been her pharmacopoeia and her defense. When a poor mother lay burning with the weed in an unheated cot, it was not the physician from Salisbury who came with his silver carriage and his five-guinea fee; it was Hannah Thorne with her kettle of tansy tea and her earthenware pot of elder fat.

She had built her life upon the ground of this parish. She knew every hedge, every sheep-wash, every family secret whispered behind labor curtains.

And now, with a single oath before the magistrate, she stood to lose everything.

If Thomas Callow carried his threat at the Easter vestry, her pension would cease. Her cottage would be let to a strange laborer; her gray pony Bess would have to be sold to the knacker or the carrier to pay for bread; and she herself would become what she had spent two decades preventing other women from becoming: a parish pauper, dependent upon the grudging charity of the overseer's bowl.

She looked out through the small dormer window in the thatch. Across the frozen water meadows, the lights of Bourne Hall shone bright and steady on their gravel terrace.

She thought of Mary Miller in the stone cell at Warminster, clutching her empty, inflamed breasts in the dark. She thought of the living boy who had cried in the straw, with the strawberry mark above his collarbone.

A child's breath against a widow's bread.

She took down a bundle of dried chamomile, climbed down the ladder to the kitchen, and laid her episcopal license upon the deal table.

Hannah sat by her hearth until the parish clock struck midnight.

The deal table was covered with the dried harvest of her calling: bundles of agrimony, jars of elder-tallow, and the framed episcopal licence with its red wax seal.

She reached for her iron bodkin and scraped a hardened fleck of candle grease from her thumbnail. The familiar, rhythmic click of iron against horn calmed the beating of her heart.

For twenty years she had lived by the rule of the parish: deferring to the squire, curtsying to the rector, accepting the tenant farmer's bacon with a quiet word of thanks. She had thought that by keeping her head down and her eyes upon the laboring bed, the storms of the world would pass over her flint roof without touching her.

She had been a fool.

The world was not a neutral place where a woman could hide behind a basket of boiled linen. When the masters of the land chose to lie, their lies crushed the helpless beneath them like iron wagon wheels crushing chalk flints in the lane.

She picked up her episcopal licence and read the Latin words once more by the dying firelight: *In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti...*

The Bishop had not granted her this authority to protect the tenant farmer's purse. He had granted it to protect the breath of the newborn child.

She rose, packed her leather bag with clean linen and her pewter bodkin, and blew out the rushlight.

The quiet of the cottage loft was like a church after the service had ended.

Hannah touched the bundles of dried chamomile hanging from the oak rafters. Each plant had been gathered with prayer and patience: picked at the full moon along the riverbanks, dried in the shade of the apple loft, tied with clean flax cord.

For twenty years, these humble weeds had been her armor against the cruelties of the world. She had cured milk-fever with tansy tea; she had stopped uterine flooding with powdered ergot; she had soothed the agony of crowning with elder-tallow and boiled water.

She took down her mother's calfskin bag from the peg.

The leather was dark with age, polished smooth by forty years of carrying across the downland combes. Inside lay the tools: the bone scissors, the pewter bodkin, the linen tapes.

They were clean. They had no stain of blood or corruption upon them.

She knelt upon the cold floorboards of the loft, pressed her face against the fragrant herbs, and prayed for strength to face the morrow.

# Chapter 20: The Second Deposition

At nine o'clock on Friday morning, while the hoar frost was still sparkling on the gorse bushes, a horseman rode into Hannah's garden.

It was not Constable Barnes. It was Mr. Christopher Tarrant, the clerk to the magistrates, a thin, sharp-featured gentleman of forty with a powdered wig, an ink-stained coat of bottle-green wool, and a leather portfolio strapped across his saddle.

He dismounted with stiff, deliberate movements, tied his mare to the gate, and entered the kitchen without knocking.

"Mrs. Thorne," Tarrant said, bowing with formal legal courtesy. He took a chair by the hearth, blew upon his cold fingers, and opened his portfolio upon the deal table.

He drew out a large sheet of ruled sheepskin parchment, covered in close, beautiful legal script, with three red wax seals affixed along the lower margin.

"Sir William has directed me to prepare your formal deposition for the Petty Sessions this afternoon," Tarrant said, dipping a bone-handled pen into a portable brass ink-pot. "The hearing will convene at two o'clock in the great hall of Bourne Hall. Mary Miller will be brought from Warminster under armed escort."

Hannah stood by the dresser, her hands folded over her apron. "What does the paper say, Mr. Tarrant?"

Tarrant adjusted his spectacles and read from the parchment:

"*The Examination and Deposition of Hannah Thorne, widow, taken upon oath this twenty-fifth day of November, in the thirty-seventh year of the reign of our Sovereign Lord George the Third... Who saith that on the night of Thursday, the seventeenth instant, she was summoned to a certain outbuilding on High Lea Farm, where she delivered one Mary Miller of a male child... Which said child was born alive, but of whose subsequent custody or disposal this examinant hath no certain knowledge...*"

Tarrant looked up from the sheet, his eyes shrewd and questioning.

"Sir William has added a rider, Mrs. Thorne," the clerk said in a low voice. "At the request of Goodman Callow, who attended upon the bench this morning with his attorney from Devizes."

"What rider?" Hannah asked.

Tarrant turned the sheet toward her and pointed with the feather of his pen.

"*Provided always, that this examinant doth admit that her recollection of the signs of life in the said infant may have been mistaken, by reason of the darkness of the shed and the suddenness of the travail...*"

Hannah looked at the neat, black words. A single signature beneath that rider, and the truth was undone. Thomas Callow would be satisfied; Mary Miller would be acquitted of murder; the child would remain lost forever in the alleys of Salisbury; and Hannah Thorne would keep her cottage and her parish bread.

She reached out her hand, took the bone-handled pen from the clerk's fingers, and laid it deliberately back upon the table.

"I will sign no rider, Mr. Tarrant," Hannah said calmly.

Tarrant's eyebrows rose. "Do you know what you do, Mrs. Thorne? If you swear without qualification that the child was born alive, and no living babe is produced, Sir William has no choice under the statute. He must commit Mary Miller to the county gaol for trial on the capital charge."

"Then let Thomas Callow produce the child," Hannah replied, her voice firm as seasoned oak. "I will sign the truth, and nothing but the truth, when I stand before the magistrates' bench at two o'clock."

Tarrant stared at her for a moment, then let out a dry, whistling breath. He picked up his pen, returned the parchment to his leather portfolio, and buckled the straps with a sharp snap.

"As you will, Mrs. Thorne," the clerk said, standing and taking his hat. "The court convenes at two."

Mr. Christopher Tarrant stood by the garden gate, his green wool riding coat whipped by the morning wind, watching Hannah Thorne with cold judicial curiosity.

"You are a stubborn woman, Mrs. Thorne," the clerk said, his hand resting upon his leather portfolio. "I have known parish midwives who would have taken Callow's two sovereigns and sworn the child was born with two heads if it pleased the vestry."

"I am licensed by the Bishop of Salisbury, Mr. Tarrant," Hannah answered from the doorway. "Not by the vestry."

"The Bishop is sixty miles away in his palace," Tarrant remarked dryly. "Thomas Callow is six hundred yards away at High Lea. Which of the two do you think will provide your winter firewood when the snow drifts four feet high against your door?"

"God will provide my firewood, Mr. Tarrant," Hannah said. "Or I will burn my own turf."

Tarrant gave his thin, whistling laugh. "God is a poor fuel merchant in January, Mrs. Thorne. But as you will. At two o'clock the court sits in the great hall. Be prompt. Sir William does not like to be kept waiting when his foot is in flannel."

He mounted his bay mare, wheeled the beast in the lane, and rode away toward Bourne Hall, his green skirts flapping against the horse's flanks.

Hannah watched him go. She looked at the sky: the clouds were gathering again from the east, heavy with another blizzard.

She had five hours before the court sat. Five hours to ride to Salisbury, find John Pike's cousin in the alleys of Saint Thomas, and bring the living child back to the magistrate's bench.

She turned into the lean-to stable and threw the saddle across Bess's back.

Mr. Christopher Tarrant stood in the lane, adjusting his tricorn hat against the rising wind.

"One final word, Mrs. Thorne," the clerk said, turning back at the garden gate. "Goodman Callow has retained counselor Garrow from London. He will be at Bourne Hall this afternoon. Garrow is an iron-jawed barrister who has broken three dozen witnesses at the Old Bailey. If you stand in the box and swear against the master of High Lea, he will tear your character to shreds. He will ask about your dead child in 1774; he will ask about every unbaptized infant you ever buried; he will make you look like a venomous crone pursuing a private grudge."

Hannah looked the clerk in the eyes.

"Let counselor Garrow ask what he pleases, Mr. Tarrant," she said calmly. "A lie needs forty lawyers to prop it up. The truth needs only one woman who was there in the straw."

Tarrant gave a brief, reluctant bow. "We shall see, Mrs. Thorne. The court sits at two."

# Chapter 21: The Search of the Granary

The frost held the ruts of High Lea lane like iron teeth.

Hannah Thorne rode Bess through the yard gate at ten in the morning. A skiff of fresh snow lay over the dung-heap, graying where the warm straw steamed into the bitter air. By the stone granary, two carters were throwing sacks of seed-barley into a low wagon, their breath puffing white past their woolen mufflers. They touched their caps to the midwife, but their eyes shifted toward the farmhouse windows.

"Good day, mistress," the older carter muttered. "The master is up in the top meadows with the shearers."

"I am here for the mistress," Hannah said. "She had a cough in her chest on Tuesday."

She swung down from the gray pony, looping the reins to an ash post by the dairy wall. Her basket hung from her left forearm, the woven osier stiff with cold. Beneath her frieze cloak she wore her second-best linsey-woolsey gown, pinned high at the throat with a plain brass pin. In the side pocket rested her horn phial of squill vinegar and a lump of refined loaf-sugar wrapped in brown paper.

The kitchen porch was cluttered with the iron gear of an eighty-cow dairy: ash milk yokes, wooden pails bound with copper hoops, and broad cheese-vats drying in the frost. Inside, the flagstones smelled of boiled bacon, wood-ash, and sour whey. A dairy girl was scouring milk pans by the copper, her arms red to the elbows from cold pump-water and lye.

"Is your mistress in the parlor?" Hannah asked.

"She took a turn before daylight, Mrs. Thorne," the girl whispered, glancing over her shoulder at the closed staircase door. "The master made her stay abed. He said no one was to climb the stairs but him and Edward."

"I will see her now," Hannah said.

She did not wait for the girl to curtsy. She crossed the flagstones, her pattens clicking with a hard, deliberate beat. She opened the latch and climbed the dark staircase. The wood was cold underfoot, smelling of dry rot and sheep grease.

At the head of the stair the landing branched in two. To the right lay the great bedchamber where Thomas Callow slept beneath heavy wool curtains; to the left, a narrower passage ran toward the apple loft and the granary steps.

Hannah turned left.

The granary formed the upper floor of the stone outbuilding that adjoined the dairy. Here the winter grain was stored above the damp: two hundred quarters of wheat, barley, and winter rye, winnowed and binned in heavy timber chests. A passage of rough elm planks ran along the wall, covered in a fine yellow dust of wheat chaff that rose in puffs beneath Hannah's boots.

The door to the inner loft was fastened with an ash peg thrust through an iron staple.

She pulled the peg. The iron hinges shrieked with a dry, piercing screech that hung in the silence of the rafters. Hannah held her breath, counting ten slow pulses at her wrist. No footstep followed from the bedchamber below. Only the wind whined through the stone slits in the gable, blowing fine drifts of snow across the floorboards.

She slipped into the inner loft.

The air was frigid, smelling of dry straw, mouse droppings, and old wool. Long rows of grain sacks stood against the chalk-masonry walls, labeled with black sheep-tar: *T.C. 1792 — BRENT WHEAT*.

Hannah moved along the row of sacks.

Mary Miller had worked this dairy for two summers. A girl in her ninth month of labor does not walk three miles across downland without stopping; she drops where she finds straw or wool. In the lambing barn down by the combe, Hannah had delivered the child on damp bedding. But Mary had been seen near the granary at dusk on the Friday night, carrying a basket of dried pease.

She reached the far corner beneath the low eaves, where the roof descended to within three feet of the floor.

Here the old wool fleeces were stacked in oilskin wraps, waiting for the spring shearing to fill the gaps. Behind a pile of empty rye bags lay a low wicker hamper used for sorting fleece.

Hannah dropped to her knees in the chaff.

She pulled back the top bag. The coarse hemp scratched her skin through her woolen mittens. Beneath it lay an old shepherd's coat of coarse frieze, stained yellow with grease from the sheep-wash. Folded beneath the coat was a bundle of cloth.

She drew it out into the gray light from the gable.

It was an old calico neckerchief, faded blue with white spots, such as dairy maids buy at Warminster autumn fair for fourpence. Inside the neckerchief lay a square of bleached three-thread linen, a yard wide, torn roughly at the hem. Near the center lay the dark, unmistakable stain of dried vernix and infant blood, crusted brown against the pale flax.

Hannah touched the cloth with her bare thumb.

She knew that linen. It was three-thread flax from her own copper boiler. She had bleached it over her garden rosemary hedge in October; she had laid it beneath Mary's hips in the lambing shed when the child's head was crowning under the lantern beam.

The cloth had not been washed. Someone had brought it here from the barn, crumpled it into the wicker hamper, and covered it with sheep-frieze.

Beside the hamper, half-buried in the chaff, lay a pair of small leather shoes.

They were women's shoes of rough calfskin, worn thin at the soles, the pewter buckles missing. The leather was stiff, caked with dry chalk mud from the water meadows. In the toe of the left shoe was stuffed a scrap of paper.

Hannah unfolded the paper with stiff fingers.

It was a page torn from an old printed almanac: *The Western Farmer's Remembrancer for 1792*. In the margin, written in a clerk's brown ink, were three lines in a clumsy, youthful hand:

*To M.M. — Ten shillings for the fair. Meet me by the five-acre combe when the milking is done.*

The hand was Edward Callow's. Hannah had seen his signature on the parish rate-book when he witnessed his father's accounts before the vestry.

She folded the paper, slipped it into the pocket of her stays beside the bodkin, and tucked the stained linen square deep into her basket beneath her herb phials.

Footsteps sounded on the lower stairs.

A heavy boot hit the second tread, the timber groaning beneath solid weight.

"Who is up there?" a voice shouted.

It was Edward Callow. His voice broke on the final syllable, high and ragged with fear.

Hannah rose from the chaff. She brushed the yellow dust from her apron with both hands, picked up her basket, and walked toward the loft door.

Edward stood at the head of the steps, a stable lantern swinging in his fist. His coat was unbuttoned, his linen neckcloth twisted to one side, his face pale as sheep-fat in the gray light from the gable. When he saw Hannah emerge from the granary door, his jaw slacked.

"Mrs. Thorne," he stammered. "What are you doing in the grain loft?"

"I was looking for dry sage," Hannah said. Her voice was flat, steady as an anvil. "The dairy maid told me the herbs were hung beneath the apple rafters."

Edward stared at her basket. His fingers twitched on the iron wire of the lantern bail.

"There are no herbs here," he said. "My father keeps the loft locked. You have no business above the kitchen."

"Your mother has business with me," Hannah replied. "And your mother is spitting blood into a pewter bowl while you stand here shaking like an ash leaf in a frost."

She stepped forward. Edward did not move at first; then his shoulders sank, and he backed down three steps to let her pass into the lower hallway.

Hannah walked past him without turning her head.

# Chapter 22: The Sickness of Mistress Callow

The great bedchamber smelled of elderberry wine, wet wool, and the sour breath of slow fever.

Martha Callow lay beneath four heavy woolen quilts in the carved tester bed. She was fifty-two years old, but her face was shrunk into dry yellow skin, her teeth long and loose in her gums, her hair gray as unwashed fleece beneath her dirty linen mob-cap. Her chest rattled with every breath, a wet whistling sound like wind passing through broken reeds in the bourne.

Hannah set her basket on the oak chest by the hearth.

The fire had burned down to gray embers; someone had thrown two green ash sticks onto the coals, filling the chimney corner with bitter smoke that made the sick woman cough.

Hannah took the iron poker, raked away the choking ash, and laid three dry hazel splits from her own bundle across the hearth. A clean yellow flame leaped up, warming the flagstones.

"Martha," Hannah said gently, leaning over the bedside.

The woman opened her eyes. They were yellowed around the rims, cloudy with dropsy, but when she saw Hannah Thorne, her fingers clawed at the patchwork quilt.

"Hannah," she whispered. Her voice was a dry rattle. "Is it you?"

"It is Hannah," the midwife answered.

She took the woman's right wrist between her thumb and two fingers. The pulse was thin, fluttering at ninety beats in the minute, like a trapped bird in a net.

Hannah drew the horn phial of squill vinegar from her pocket, uncorked it, and poured twenty drops into a pewter spoon. She mixed it with two spoons of boiled rainwater from her stone flask and held it to the woman's lips.

"Take this down," Hannah commanded. "It will loosen the phlegm in your throat."

Martha Callow swallowed with a grimace. The acid squill made her shudder, but after two minutes her chest eased; she brought up a dark, ropy phlegm into a linen rag Hannah held ready.

Hannah wiped the woman's chin with cold elder water, then turned back the quilt to examine her limbs.

The woman's feet were swollen, the skin pitted when Hannah pressed her thumb against the ankle-bone. The dropsy was rising toward the belly; three weeks at most remained before the fluid choked the heart.

"Where is Thomas?" Martha asked, her eyes darting toward the closed oak door.

"In the upper meadows," Hannah said.

Martha reached out, catching Hannah's woolen sleeve in a claw-like grip. Her breath came hot against the midwife's wrist.

"He told me it was a lamb," the woman whispered.

Hannah did not pull her arm away. "What was a lamb, Martha?"

"On the Friday night," Martha said. Her voice sank until Hannah had to bend her head close to the pillow to hear the words. "The wind was roaring off the high down. I was lying here, shivering with the ague. I heard a baby cry down in the yard. Not a lamb's bleat. A newborn child, crying for the breast."

The clock on the landing ticked with a heavy, deliberate stroke.

"What did you do?" Hannah asked.

"I called to Thomas," Martha whispered. "He was pulling on his boots by the hearth. He had his greatcoat on, and his wool gloves. I said to him, *Thomas, whose child is that crying in the yard?* And he cursed me. He told me I was light-headed from the fever. He said it was an ewe that had dropped early in the ditch, and he was carrying it down to the lower barn."

A tear slipped from the corner of Martha's yellow eye, running into the deep groove beside her mouth.

"He went down the stairs," she continued. "Through the window, I saw the stable lantern moving toward the turnpike gate. Then I heard John Pike's cart rumbles on the gravel. Pike's bells were muffled with rags, but I know the sound of his four gray mares."

She looked straight into Hannah's eyes.

"Whose child was it, Hannah?"

Hannah looked down at the dying woman. On the oak chest beside the bed lay the Callow family bible, its leather binding cracked with age. On its flyleaf were written the names of five dead infants buried in Nether Bourne churchyard before Edward was born:

*Thomas, born 1768, died of the thrush.*

*Sarah, born 1770, died of the croup.*

*William, born 1773, died of the spotted fever.*

*Martha, born 1776, died in the birth.*

*John, born 1778, died of the convulsion.*

Hannah had laid out three of those babes herself; she had washed their small limbs in elder water and tied their chin-cloths while Martha wept beside the cold hearth.

"It was Mary Miller's child, Martha," Hannah said softly. "And Edward is the father."

The sick woman let out a long, shuddering sigh that whistled through her throat. Her fingers relaxed their grip on Hannah's sleeve, falling limp upon the patchwork quilt.

"I knew it," Martha whispered. "The girl was a good maid. She scrubbed the cheese vats until they shone like pewter plates. She sang at the milking. When she took sick in September, Thomas wanted to turn her out onto the parish road. I begged him on my knees to let her stay till the winter."

She closed her eyes, two fresh tears tracing the lines of her hollow cheeks.

"Thomas has an iron heart, Hannah. He cares for nothing but the sheep, the lease, and the Earl's favor. He would sell his own flesh to save forty shillings on the poor-rate."

"Where did Pike take the child?" Hannah asked.

"Salisbury," Martha breathed. "Pike has a brother in the city who weaves broadcloth. Thomas told Edward that fifty shillings would clear the parish of the brat forever."

Hannah took a small piece of refined loaf-sugar from her pocket, touched it with three drops of peppermint water, and slipped it between the woman's dry lips.

"Rest now, Martha," Hannah said. "The child breathes, and God will judge the rest."

She smoothed the woolen quilts over the woman's thin shoulders, picked up her basket, and walked quietly out onto the landing.

# Chapter 23: Edward in the Stable

The stable smelled of ammonia, sweet meadow hay, and neatsfoot oil.

Three heavy plow horses stood in the stalls, their great hindquarters twitching as they munched their midday clover. At the far end of the gangway, beneath a horn lantern hung from a beam, Edward Callow sat upon an upturned half-bushel tub, an oily woolen rag in his hand. Across his leather-aproned knees lay a set of brass-mounted cart-gear, the black leather cracked with salt-sweat from the plowing.

Hannah stepped through the half-door into the straw.

The stable dog, a rough-coated lurcher, lifted its head from an empty sack and gave a low, questioning growl.

"Down, Hector," Edward said without looking up. He thought it was the carter returning with the seed-corn. "Did you finish the ditching in the five-acre?"

"The ditching is not finished," Hannah said.

Edward started. The oily rag dropped from his fingers into the straw. He scrambled to his feet, knocking the wooden half-bushel tub over onto its side. His head nearly struck the hanging lantern, setting the tin frame swinging so that long yellow shadows rocked across the stall partitions.

"Mrs. Thorne," he whispered. He backed against the manger of the third stall. The plow horse shifted its hooves with a dull thump against the oak bedding boards. "What are you doing down here?"

"I am looking at you, Edward," Hannah said.

She stood in the center of the gangway, her basket set upon a feed-bin, her hands folded over her white apron. In the dim lantern light her gray eyes were fixed upon his face.

"You have lost your color," she remarked. "A man who owns two hundred sheep on the down should not look like a felon in Fisherton lock-up."

Edward swallowed. He wiped his palms on his leather apron, but the grease only smeared darker across his breeches.

"I have work to do," he said, his voice rising thin and defensive. "Father will be back from the five-acre before noon. If he finds you talking to me—"

"Your father is not here," Hannah interrupted. "And your mother has just told me what she heard from her bed on Friday night."

The young man's breath caught in his throat. He reached out blindly, gripping the top rail of the horse stall to steady himself.

"She is light-headed," he muttered. "She has the spotted fever. Dr. Tarrant said she does not know her own sister when the fit comes on."

"She knows the sound of John Pike's wagon bells," Hannah said. "Even when they are muffled with sheep-grease and rags."

She took two steps toward him. The stable was quiet save for the heavy, rhythmic chewing of the horses and the drip of thawing snow from the eaves outside the hay-door.

Hannah watched the young man's hands. His fingers were shaking as he gripped the trace-leather, his knuckles white against the blackened cowhide.

"I delivered you into this world, Edward Callow," Hannah said in a low, measured voice that seemed to fill the dim gangway. "Twenty-two years ago, on a Michaelmas night, your mother lay upon her bed in the front chamber. The labor was hard; the cord was twined twice around your neck. I worked for two hours with sweet oil to slip the loop over your chin before you took your first breath. Your father knelt by the hearth, weeping into his wool gloves, praying that God would spare his only son."

Edward flinched as if a whip had touched him.

"You were spared, Edward," she continued. "You were given broad acres, dry timber, and eighty milking kine in the water meadows. And now, when a poor girl bears your firstborn in an unheated lambing outbuilding, you hide behind your father's broadcloth coat and let your child be carried off like an unchristened pig."

"Where is the boy, Edward?"

Edward did not answer. He lowered his head, staring at the dirty straw between his boots. A tremor ran through his shoulders, shaking the brass buttons of his waistcoat.

"I never meant for him to go," he whispered. "I swore to Mary by the church wall that I would take her to Warminster. I told her I would speak to the vicar. I had twenty pounds saved from the fleece money in my chest under the floorboards."

"And your father took the twenty pounds?"

"He took the whole chest," Edward cried, lifting his face. Tears were cutting clean tracks through the grease on his cheeks. "He said if I married a pauper's dairy girl, he would strike my name from the Earl's lease. He said he would leave High Lea to his brother's boy in Devizes. He called her a ditch-born drab who would drag the family name into the parish vestry."

"So you gave your son to a drover?"

"It was not my doing!" Edward shouted, then choked his voice to an urgent whisper as the dog stirred. "Father brought John Pike to the lower gate at midnight. Pike was driving forty steers toward the Salisbury shambles. Father gave him two sovereigns and an old saddle-cloth. He told Pike to take the bundle to his cousin's wife in the city."

"Which cousin?" Hannah pressed, her voice sharp as an awl. "What is the street?"

"Sheep Street," Edward sobbed, wiping his sleeve across his running nose. "Behind Saint Thomas's church. The man is a weaver named Giles Pike. He has a broadcloth loom in the cellar."

Hannah drew a slow breath. Her fingers touched the cold bodkin beneath her stays.

"Did the child live when Pike took him?"

"He cried," Edward whispered. "When Father handed the bundle up over the wagon-tail, the babe cried out. Father struck the tilt with his whip to drown the sound. Then Pike lashed the horses, and the wagon went down the turnpike into the sleet."

He sank down onto the upturned tub, burying his face in his greasy apron.

"They will hang Mary," he wept. "They will hang her at Fisherton, and her ghost will walk the dairy."

"Mary will not hang if you speak the truth," Hannah said.

Edward looked up through his swollen eyelids. "Father will kill me."

"Your father will answer to the Assizes," Hannah said. "Sir William Harford holds the Petty Sessions at Bourne Hall tomorrow at two o'clock. If you are not in that great hall to declare yourself the father, you will carry a woman's death upon your soul until the judgment day."

She reached into her basket and pulled out the slip of printed almanac she had found in Mary's shoe.

"Do you know your own writing, Edward?"

Edward stared at the brown ink on the yellowed margin: *To M.M. — Ten shillings for the fair.*

He went whiter still. His mouth worked silently, his hand trembling as he reached toward the paper. Hannah drew it back, sliding it safely into her pocket.

"The magistrate will see this paper tomorrow," Hannah said. "You have twenty-four hours to decide whether you stand before Sir William as an honest man who sinned and repented, or as a coward who helped murder the mother of his child."

She stepped closer to the young man. She laid her hand upon his shoulder. Her touch was not harsh, but it carried the weight of twenty years of parish authority.

"Your mother gave birth to six children in this house, Edward," she said softly. "Five of them are asleep beneath the north church wall. You are the only one left to carry the name. If you buy your farm with the blood of an innocent girl and the starvation of your child, you will rot in your bed like an old hollow willow."

Edward shuddered. He bowed his forehead against the manger rail, his tears wetting the dry clover.

"I will come," he whispered. "I will come to Bourne Hall."

Hannah turned on her heel and walked toward the stable door.

"Hannah!" he called after her.

She stopped at the threshold, her hand upon the latch.

"Does the boy live?" he asked.

"I am riding to Salisbury to see," Hannah answered, and dropped the latch.

# Chapter 24: The Drover's Track

The north wind off the Salisbury Plain had stripped the downland beeches bare as bleached bones.

Hannah Thorne rode Bess along the ancient ridgeway that ran from Heytesbury toward the cathedral spire eighteen miles to the south-east. It was noon, but the winter sky was the color of hammered lead, heavy with the dull, bruised promise of fresh snow. Below her in the winding valley, the river Wylye lay hidden beneath rolling banks of white river-fog that crept through the willow beds like smoke from a burning lime-kiln.

The ridgeway was not a turnpike road; it was the old British track, five hundred cubits broad between the chalk ditch-banks, where sheep, cattle, and packhorses had been driven to the Wiltshire fairs since the time of the ancient heathen kings. The turf was frozen into ridged waves, hard as iron slag, with crusts of granular snow filling every sheep-track and cart-rut.

Hannah sat upright in her side-saddle, her frieze cloak pinned tightly around her shoulders with two iron bodkins.

Under her stays, the square of stained linen from the granary loft pressed cold against her ribs. In her basket, wrapped in three layers of boiled flannel, lay a horn lantern, an iron flint-box, a loaf of rye bread, a wedge of hard skim-milk cheese, and two stone bottles of elderberry cordial wrapped in thick woolen stockings.

Bess picked her way with steady deliberation, her iron shoes ringing against the flint nodules that cropped out through the thin chalk turf.

Every half-mile, an ancient earthen barrow rose beside the track—the resting places of forgotten chieftains who had hunted deer upon these hills before the Romans built their roads. Around the barrows grew stunted thorn bushes, their crooked black branches hung with withered red berries that rattled like dry shot in the gale.

Hannah had known this downland track for thirty winters.

As a young woman, she had ridden these high sheep-walks with her husband John, carrying ash timber from the Grovely copses to his wheelwright's shop. In twenty years of midwifery, she had traversed these ridges by night and day, in harvest heat and winter frost, answering the call of shepherds' wives who labored in solitary turf huts beside the sheep-folds.

She knew the signs of the Plain.

A kestrel hovering motionless against the gray clouds; a flock of fieldfares chattering in the gorse; the distant sound of church bells rising from the river villages—these were the companions of her solitary journeys.

A mile past the Heytesbury boundary stone, an old shepherd in a sheepskin coat stood leaning upon his crook beside a fold of wattle hurdles. His sixty ewes were huddled together in the leeward corner of the bank, their fleece yellow with grease, their warm breath curling into the freezing air like white steam.

Hannah checked Bess's trot.

"Good day, shepherd," she called out against the wind.

The old man turned his weathered face, his rheumy eyes peering from beneath a battered woolen cap. He touched his forehead with a thumb calloused by fifty years of shearing.

"Good day, Mrs. Thorne," the shepherd answered, his voice cracked with the frost. "You're far from Nether Bourne in this weather."

"Did John Pike's cart pass this way on Friday night?" Hannah asked.

The shepherd spat a brown stream of tobacco juice onto the frosted grass.

"Ay, mistress. He passed an hour past midnight. He had forty steers on the hoof, heading for the city shambles, and two heavy carthorses in his wain. The steers were blowing hard in the sleet, and Pike was cursing his near-horse with every stride."

"Did you hear a child cry in the cart?"

The old man looked at her keenly, his bushy white eyebrows drawing together over his hooked nose.

"I heard a sound like a young lamb bleating under the cart-tilt," he said slowly. "I shouted to Pike: *Have you got an early yeanling in the straw, John?* And Pike shouted back: *I have a passenger for Salisbury, shepherd, and none of your business!* Then he cracked his whip, and the team rumbled down into the combe."

The shepherd stepped closer to the pony's shoulder, pointing with his crook toward the chalk cutting below Yarnbury rampart.

"Look at the tracks where the wheel rut cuts the ditch," the old man said, his voice dropping to a gravelly rasp. "Pike was not driving his usual broad-tired carrier wagon with the six-horse team. He had a light two-horse van with iron tires, built for speed on the turnpike. The off wheel threw gravel into the juniper bushes because he took the bend at a trot. A carrier does not trot his cattle on Salisbury Plain in December unless he is paid in gold to beat the night."

Hannah leaned down from her saddle, studying the fresh indentations in the crust. The iron tires had cut through two inches of rime into the white marl beneath, leaving distinct crescent marks where the caulkins of the off-wheeler had struck the flint.

"How long ago, Jacob?" she asked.

"Two hours before midday," the shepherd replied. "The snow has partly drifted into the ruts, but you can follow them easily enough as far as the crossways at Deptford Inn. If he turned south for Fisherton, he will find the valley road blocked by the sheep-drift near Steeple Langford."

"I thank you, neighbor," Hannah said.

She touched Bess with her heel, riding down the slope into the dry bottom of Dead Man's Combe.

Here the north wind was cut off by the high turf ramparts of an ancient ditch; the air was dead, cold, and still, smelling of wet flint, damp moss, and the sour sheep-wash of fifty lambing seasons. At the bottom of the combe stood a ruined sheep-shelter of unmortared chalk stones, its thatched roof long since rotted away, its hazel hurdles gray with lichen.

Beside the ruined shelter, the cart ruts turned sharply off the ridgeway.

Hannah pulled Bess up. She swung down from the side-saddle, her knees cracking with the cold, and led the pony by the bridle into the shelter of the stone wall.

In the leeward corner of the wall, where the snow had not drifted, the ashes of a small fire lay between two large chalk boulders. Beside the hearth lay three empty beer-corkings, a gnawed mutton bone, and a torn scrap of coarse sacking stained with black tar.

Hannah knelt upon the frosted grass.

She picked up the scrap of sacking. It was coarse, six-thread hemp, marked with the red ochre circle that Thomas Callow used to brand his yearling wethers at Michaelmas. The inner side of the cloth was lined with dry oat straw.

She brought the straw to her nose.

It smelled of sour cow's milk and the warm, sweet musk of a young infant's skin.

John Pike had halted here on Friday night. The drover had pulled his cart behind the stone wall to wait out the worst of the midnight sleet. He had built a fire of gorse roots; he had warmed his beer; and he had taken the child from the cart-tail to feed it.

Hannah examined the ground with obstetrical care.

Beside the hearth lay an empty sheep-horn, its narrow tip cut off and plugged with a soft quill. It was a rustic sucking-bottle, such as downland shepherds use to rear motherless cade-lambs in the winter folding. On the frozen earth beside the horn lay two drops of yellow cow's milk, curdled into hard white beads by the frost.

The child had been alive here.

He had sucked the cow's milk from the horn; he had swallowed; his little heart had been beating two hours after Thomas Callow carried him out of the lambing shed.

Hannah rose from her knees, her eyes burning with a fierce, quiet certainty.

She walked out from the shelter to examine the wheel tracks where the cart had turned back onto the ridge.

The tracks were fresh, cutting straight through the frozen grass. Beside the ruts were the hoof-prints of two heavy draught horses, their shoes calked with iron studs for the ice. A man's boots had walked beside the near-side wheel: broad, square-toed cowhide boots with iron heel-plates, walking with the heavy, rolling limp of John Pike, who had broken his knee twenty years ago under a timber wain.

Beside the wheel track, half-covered by drifting snow, lay a small object that caught the pale light.

Hannah bent down and picked it up.

It was a strip of bleached linen, six inches long and two fingers broad, torn roughly from the hem of a child's swaddling band. It was pinned together with a small brass bodkin—one of the bodkins Hannah had left in her basket in the lambing shed on Friday night.

John Pike had re-swaddled the child here in the combe. The babe had wet his linen in the cart; the drover had taken the dry strip from Hannah's bundle and pinned it tight before facing the blizzard on the high plain.

Hannah held the small brass bodkin in her palm.

She looked at the ruined dry-stone wall where the sheep-hurdles lay half-buried in the snow. Ten years ago, in the great frost of 1783, she had ridden into this same combe to attend old goody Barnes's daughter. The girl had been caught in a snow-drift while bringing butter from the sheep-fold; she had dropped her child upon a bed of frozen gorse. Hannah had arrived with two stone bottles of hot water, wrapped the newborn babe in her own flannel petticoat, and carried mother and child three miles through the drifts to the parish almshouse.

Both had lived. That child was now a stout lad of ten, working the oxen at the lower water meadows.

The soil of this downland was hard, sour with chalk flints and beaten by winter gales, but the folk who tilled it had iron in their bones. They did not perish easily; they clung to life like the dwarf juniper bushes on the ditch ramparts. And this child—Mary Miller's son—had survived forty miles of winter road because the stubborn life of the Wiltshire soil was in his blood.

The metal was cold against her skin, but it was an iron warrant written by the hand of Providence. It linked the lambing barn at High Lea to the drover's cart, and the drover's cart to the child in Salisbury.

She looked out across the white downland plateau.

The wind was freshening from the east, carrying pellets of ice that struck her wool hood like bird-shot. To the left, the ancient ditch of Grim's Ditch stretched across the chalk for three miles, a black line cutting through the white snow. She knew that beyond that ditch lay the boundary of the cathedral parish of Fisherton Anger. If the drover had cleared the ditch before sunrise on Saturday morning, the child had entered the city before the morning market opened.

She smoothed Bess's neck. The pony turned her head, nudging Hannah's shoulder with her frosted muzzle, asking for her oats.

"We have sixteen miles to Salisbury, Bess," Hannah said softly. "And the child was breathing when they crossed this combe."

She climbed back into the side-saddle, settling her skirts over Bess's flank.

Bess snorted, tossing her white mane, and scrambled out of the combe back onto the high ridge.

The wind met them on the crest like an open hand. It blew straight from the east, carrying fine, needle-like grains of frozen sleet that stung the skin of Hannah's cheeks until her lips were numb. But she did not turn her face away.

Across the great white plateau of the Plain, the horizon stretched empty and vast for miles. There were no trees, no cottages, no church towers; only the long, swelling lines of the chalk downs, rising and falling like the waves of a frozen sea under the darkening winter sky.

In the far distance, twelve miles to the south, the thin gray spire of Salisbury Cathedral rose above the winter haze, sharp as a needle against the clouds.

Hannah gathered the leather reins into her numb fingers and urged the mare into a steady, reaching trot.

# Chapter 25: The Parish Tavern

The George Inn at Bishopstrow stood at the junction where the downland track joined the Salisbury turnpike.

It was an ancient, low-built house of flint and timber, its thatched eaves hanging low over leaded casements that glowed with the red light of a sea-coal fire. Outside the door, six carrier wagons were drawn up beneath the dripping horse-chestnut trees, their horses munching chopped clover from wicker nosebags while the drivers warmed their shins within.

Hannah tied Bess to the mounting post beneath the inn sign and pushed open the heavy oak door.

The taproom was packed with men. The air was thick with the reek of stale ale, shag tobacco, wet frieze coats, and the greasy sheep-salve that clung to every drover's boots. At the long trestle table near the hearth sat a dozen tenant farmers and smallholders from the Wylye parishes, their pewter pots thumping on the oak boards as they argued over the news from Nether Bourne.

Hannah stood in the shadow of the porch, shaking the snow from her hood.

"I tell you the girl is guilty under the statute," shouted Martin, the tanner from Heytesbury, slamming his fist onto the table. "The 1624 Act is plain as print! If an unmarried maid conceals the birth of her bastard, and the child cannot be produced alive, the law presumes the child was murdered! It is capital felony, without benefit of clergy!"

"Ay, that is the lawyer's talk," retorted Giles, the village smith, his leather apron still tied around his waist. "The law is an iron collar for the poor, but a silk ribbon for the rich! Who was the father, Martin? Answer me that! Why does the constable keep Edward Callow's name out of the parish warrant?"

A murmur ran around the taproom.

"Edward was at the grammar school," Martin sneered. "He is an educated young gentleman. You think an educated young gentleman would meddle with a common dairy maid who sleeps on a straw pallet in the out-house?"

"He meddled with her right enough," called out an old carter from the corner. "I saw him behind the hay-rick at Michaelmas, giving her a ribbon from Warminster fair. And Goodman Callow knows it! Why did Callow send forty sheep to the magistrate's bailiff on Monday morning? To buy a hanging for the girl, that's why! To keep the parish rate off High Lea Farm!"

"Silence your clapper, Giles!" shouted a tenant farmer in a broadcloth coat. "Goodman Callow is churchwarden of Nether Bourne! He pays fifty pounds a year to the poor-rate! If every bastard dropped in a ditch was laid to the tenant farmers, the whole valley would be bankrupt before the spring shearing!"

The argument raged back and forth across the table, spoons banging against pewter mugs.

The poor cottagers and day-laborers knew the girl was innocent of murder; they knew the Callow family had driven her into the barn to hide their shame. But the tenant farmers who held leases under the Earl feared the parish rate. A single bastard child maintained by the parish from infancy to seven years cost twenty-five pounds in nursling fees, clothing, and bread; and that money came straight from the pockets of the rate-payers at the Easter vestry.

Hannah stepped forward into the firelight.

The iron pattens on her shoes clicked sharply against the flagstones.

The shouting in the taproom ceased as if a knife had cut the air. The tanner froze with his pewter tankard halfway to his mouth; the carter stared; the tenant farmer turned his back to the hearth, his face flushing dark red.

Hannah looked around the circle of men.

"Good day, neighbors," she said.

No man answered for three heartbeats. Then old Giles pulled off his woolen cap, his soot-streaked forehead bowing toward the midwife.

"Mistress Thorne," the smith said, his voice dropping into rough respect. "We did not look to see you on the turnpike in this weather."

"I am on the King's business," Hannah said.

She walked to the tap-bench. The landlord, a fat man with an ale-stained apron, wiped the wooden counter with a dirty cloth.

"A cup of hot cider, Mrs. Thorne?" he asked nervously. "Or a bowl of mutton broth?"

"A cup of boiled water for my stone flask," Hannah answered. "And two quarts of crushed oats for my mare at the post."

She laid a silver threepenny piece upon the timber counter.

The tenant farmer in the broadcloth coat cleared his throat, leaning his shoulder against the chimney breast.

"The magistrates meet tomorrow at Bourne Hall, Mrs. Thorne," he said, trying to make his voice sound casual. "Goodman Callow has retained Counselor Garrow from Salisbury to represent the parish. The counselor says the deposition of the midwife is all that is required to send the baggage to Fisherton."

Hannah took the warm flask from the landlord's hand. She corked it with a birch plug and slid it into her basket.

Then she turned and looked the farmer full in the face.

"Counselor Garrow may say what pleases his fee, Master Barnes," Hannah said, her voice clear and hard as bell-metal. "But Counselor Garrow was not in the lambing barn when the child was born. I was."

She picked up her basket, walked past the silent table, and stepped out into the freezing snow.

# Chapter 26: The Threat of the Fire

Two miles beyond Bishopstrow, where the road climbed the shoulder of Codford Hill, a horseman sat waiting beneath the dripping branches of an ancient ash tree.

The horse was a heavy black cob, seventeen hands high, with thick fetlocks, a docked tail, and broad quarters that smelled of the stallion-shed. The rider wore a triple-caped greatcoat of drab broadcloth, high riding boots of oiled calfskin, and a broad-brimmed beaver hat pulled low against the freezing sleet.

It was Thomas Callow.

He sat motionless in his saddle, his hands resting upon the brass pommel of his holsters, watching the turnpike gate through the falling snow. When he saw Hannah Thorne riding her gray pony up the incline, he touched his beast with the spur and moved out into the center of the road, blocking the track completely.

Hannah did not pull Bess to a halt until the pony's nose was within two yards of the black cob's shoulder.

"Good day, Goodman Callow," Hannah said.

Callow did not touch his hat. His square, fleshy face was mottled red with the cold, his heavy jaw set like a slab of building stone. His small, dark eyes peered at her from beneath bushy gray eyebrows crusted with rime.

"Where are you riding, Hannah?" he asked. His voice was thick, grating like a gravel-shovel.

"To Salisbury," Hannah answered.

"The roads are impassable across the Plain," Callow said. "The carrier from Fisherton turned back at dawn. A woman of your years has no call to be wandering the turnpike in a blizzard."

"My business will not wait for the thaw," Hannah said.

Callow leaned forward in his stirrups, his greatcoat parting to reveal the gold seals dangling from his fob. He rested his right hand upon the leather pommel, his heavy horsewhip dangling from his wrist by a braided thong.

"Let us speak plain, Hannah," the farmer said. "We have known each other thirty years. When your husband John had the timber-shed by the bridge, it was my custom that kept his saws moving. When John died of the pleurisy, who spoke for you at the parish vestry? Who gave you the lease of the flint cottage at sixpence a year?"

"The parish gave it, Master Callow," Hannah replied. "In payment for thirty years of obstetrical duty to the poor."

Callow's jaw hardened beneath his wool cravat. He shifted in the saddle, his heavy iron stirrups clinking against his boot spurs.

"And who pays the parish rate, woman?" Callow demanded, his voice rising above the whistle of the gale. "Who pays four-and-twenty pounds a year to maintain the church and the roads and the bastards of every trull in the valley? Thomas Callow pays it! Sir William Harford sits in his parlor with his flannel bandages and signs what I put before him because he knows who rents the arable. If you think the parish vestry will protect you against the lord of the manor, you are a foolish old crone."

He leaned over his horse's neck, bringing his florid, weather-beaten face within two feet of hers.

"Turn that pony round, Hannah," he urged, his tone changing from anger to a menacing intimacy. "Go home to your warm chimney. I have five gold guineas in my waistcoat pocket. They are yours the moment you ride back to Nether Bourne. You tell the vicar that the girl miscarried on the road, and by tomorrow night Mary Miller will be fifty miles away in Gloucestershire where her brat can work the woolen looms."

Hannah looked directly into the tenant's bloodshot eyes without yielding an inch of ground.

"Keep your guineas for the Salisbury turnpike, Goodman Callow," she said coldly. "They will buy you neither my silence nor the child's soul."

"The parish gave it because I moved the motion," Callow snapped, his voice hardening. "The vestry does what I command, as every man in Nether Bourne knows well. And every winter since John was buried, whose carts brought two loads of seasoned oak clefts to your wood-stack?"

"Your carts brought them," Hannah said. "And I have thanked you for them."

"Then remember the wood, woman," Callow hissed, leaning down toward her. "Remember the fire on your hearth! Tomorrow at two o'clock, Sir William Harford holds the Petty Sessions. Counselor Garrow has drawn up the parish warrant. All that is needed is for you to swear that when you left the lambing barn, the child was stillborn."

Hannah looked straight into his dark, furious eyes.

"The child was born alive, Thomas," she said calmly. "You saw him breathe. You saw him kick upon the straw. You gave me two golden sovereigns to hold my tongue while you carried him away."

"The sovereigns were your lying-in fee!" Callow shouted, his black cob dancing sideways as his spur touched its flank. "Two guineas for your attendance on a sick vagrant! That is no crime in law! But if you swear before Sir William that the babe was living when it vanished, the court will demand the body! They will send the girl to the gallows, and they will drag High Lea into the county gazette!"

"The court will demand the body," Hannah agreed. "And what will you say when they ask where John Pike carried that body?"

Callow went gray around the nostrils. His hand tightened on his whip-stock until the knuckles showed white through his leather gloves.

"Edward has been talking to you," he whispered. "The cowardly whelp. He has the heart of a sheep."

"He has the conscience of a father," Hannah said. "Which is more than his own father carries."

Callow jerked the black cob's head up, bringing the heavy beast closer until its hot breath blew into Hannah's face.

"Listen to me, Hannah Thorne," the farmer snarled, his voice dropping to a vicious, venomous whisper. "You are an old woman living alone in a stone cottage on the waste. Your roof is held by the vestry's grace. Your license to practice is held by the Bishop's favor. If you speak one word against High Lea tomorrow before the bench, I will have your cottage pulled down by the parish bailiffs before Candlemas!"

Hannah did not flinch. She sat steady in her side-saddle, her hands quiet upon the reins.

"You will pull down my cottage?" she asked.

Callow spat into the snow, his breath puffing like smoke from a lime-kiln.

"The cottage stands on the lord's waste," Callow snarled. "The vestry granted the encroachment in 1774 for the parish midwife to dwell in during her good behavior. I have the parish minute book in my parlor cupboard. If the churchwardens declare you a public nuisance who brings false charges against the rate-payers, the vestry will vote your ejectment before Candlemas. The bailiffs will carry your bedding into the turnpike ditch, and Giles will put a padlock on the gate."

Hannah looked at the farmer's heavy, mottled face.

The man's breath was sour with stale ale and tobacco. Beneath his broadcloth coat, his thick chest rose and fell with a heavy, deliberate rhythm, like the stroke of a blacksmith's bellows.

"You speak of your broadcloth, Thomas," Hannah said, her voice dropping into cold, cutting precision. "You speak of your four hundred acres and your team of black oxen. But I remember your father, old Simon Callow. In the hard winter of 1762, Simon Callow was carried to Fisherton debtor's gaol for forty pounds of hay-money he owed to the Earl of Pembroke. Who went to the rector to plead for him? My husband John went. Who brought a sack of potatoes and a flitch of bacon to your mother's hearth while you were a barefoot carter's boy running behind the plow? John Thorne brought them."

Callow flinched as if a cold ash had blown into his eye. His jaw worked, his knuckles tightening on his horsewhip.

"That was thirty years ago," he growled. "The debt was paid with interest."

"The debt of money was paid," Hannah answered. "The debt of Christian charity was never paid. You built your stone dairy; you tiled your six chimneys; you sent your son Edward to learn Latin and gentility. And now, to keep that gentility from the parish rate-book, you would see your grandson starve in a Salisbury cellar and the girl who curdled your milk hanged at Fisherton."

"You have pulled down four cottages in Nether Bourne already, Thomas," she said calmly. "When the common was enclosed in 1780, you pulled down the widow Barnes's dwelling by the sheep-fold; you turned out old Martin when his ague prevented him from ditching your five-acre. You think because the Earl's steward drinks your port wine at Michaelmas that you own the sky above this valley."

"I own four hundred acres of it," Callow shouted. "And I pay sixty pounds a year to keep the parish paupers in bread! I will not have my son saddled with a ditch-born bastard to please an obstinate old woman!"

"I will have the thatch stripped from the rafters," Callow swore. "I will have the vestry cancel your sixpenny lease. And I will ride to Salisbury myself to see the Chancellor of the Diocese. I will swear you are a drunken, disorderly crone who takes bribes from vagrants! You will never deliver another child in this county while you draw breath!"

A cold wind swept over the crest of Codford Hill, driving a swirl of white flakes between them.

Hannah thought of her husband John.

Twelve years ago, when the Enclosure Commissioners had come to Nether Bourne to divide the common downland, John Thorne had stood up before the vestry in the parish church. John had pointed his rough carpenter's finger at Thomas Callow and said: *You take the cottagers' grazing, Master Callow, and you leave them only the parish road to starve on. God will require the bread of the poor at your hands.*

Callow had laughed at John then. Callow had taken away his timber custom; he had raised the rates upon John's workshop; he had watched John cough his lungs out with the pleurisy while the winter wind blew through the unheated shed.

Now Callow stood before John's widow, threatening the same ruin.

Hannah reached into her basket.

She did not draw out her bone-handled shears, nor her phial of squill vinegar. She reached deep into the side pocket and drew out the small tin cylinder that held her episcopal license. She unscrewed the brass cap, pulled out the rolled sheepskin parchment, and unrolled it before Callow's eyes.

The great red wax seal of the Bishop of Salisbury swung from its silk ribbon in the freezing wind, stamped with the figures of the Virgin and Child beneath a Gothic canopy.

"Read the seal, Thomas Callow," Hannah said, her voice rising with an authority that made the farmer's black cob step back into the ditch. "This parchment was signed by the Lord Bishop of Sarum twenty-two years ago. It commands me before Almighty God: *You shall not conceal the birth of any infant, nor suffer it to be put away, nor yield to threat, favor, nor fear of worldly power.*"

She rolled the sheepskin tight and thrust it back into the tin case.

"The Bishop holds his court from the King, Master Callow," she continued, her eyes boring into his face. "Not from the tenant of High Lea Farm. You may take my firewood, and you may pull down my roof. But while I have breath in my body, I will speak the truth before the King's Justice."

She touched Bess with her whip.

The gray pony stepped forward, brushing past the flank of Callow's black cob.

Callow did not raise his whip. He sat in his saddle, his face dark as a thundercloud, his breath coming in short, furious gasps as he watched the midwife ride down the turnpike toward the Salisbury road.

# Chapter 27: The Bishop's Parchment

The turnpike gate at Steeple Langford was barred with an ash pole hung with icicles.

Hannah pulled up before the toll-house, her breath curling into the freezing air. An old man with a wool comforter tied twice around his chin hobbled out of the stone porch, carrying an iron key and a horn lantern.

"Toll for a single horse, mistress," the man croaked. "One penny."

Hannah took a copper penny from her pocket and dropped it into his woolen mitten.

"How is the road toward Salisbury, master?" she asked.

"Heavy snow past Wilton, Mrs. Thorne," the gatekeeper replied, pushing the ash bar back with his shoulder. "The carrier from Fisherton said the drifts are three feet deep in the cutting near the racecourse. If you are heading for the city, you had best keep to the river meadows where the willow hedges break the wind."

"I thank you, neighbor," Hannah said, and rode through the gate.

A half-mile past the toll-bar, where the lane ran beneath the chalk cliff of Grovely Wood, Hannah halted Bess beside a stone cattle-trough fed by a spring that never froze. The clear water bubbled up from the chalk, steaming gently in the twenty-degree frost.

She let Bess drink three swallows of the sweet spring water, then dismounted to loosen the pony's girth and ease her own aching knees.

Beneath the shelter of the hazel coppice, Hannah sat upon a fallen beech trunk. Her feet were numb in her leather boots; her fingers were stiff with cold, the joints swollen with the chronic rheumatism of thirty winters in the saddle.

She took the tin cylinder from her basket, unscrewed the brass cap, and pulled out the rolled parchment.

The sheepskin was worn at the creases, yellowed by smoke and age, but the black Latin ink and the Bishop's great seal were as firm as the day they were written:

*John, by Divine Permission, Lord Bishop of Sarum, to our beloved in Christ, Hannah Thorne, of the parish of Nether Bourne in our county of Wilts, Health, Grace, and Benediction.*

Twenty years ago, on a bitter Michaelmas morning, she had ridden into the cathedral close at Salisbury to receive this commission.

She had not gone alone. The canon law of the Church of England required strict proof before a woman was admitted to the obstetrical office. She had carried letters testimonial signed by six respectable matrons of Nether Bourne whom she had safely delivered, swearing to her skill, her sobriety, and her Christian conversation. She had carried a certificate from the Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte testifying that she was a regular communicant at the parish altar, free from all taint of Anabaptist heresy or Roman superstition.

In the Diocesan Chancellor's stone chamber, beside the great north transept of the cathedral, she had been examined for two hours.

The Chancellor, a grave doctor of civil law in a black silk gown, had questioned her closely upon the craft:

*What do you do when the infant presents by the shoulder or the arm?*

*I grease my right hand with sweet oil or hog-lard,* she had answered, *and gently pushing back the presenting part into the womb, I search for the feet, bringing them down into the passage.*

*What do you do if the mother floods after the delivery of the placenta?*

*I apply linen cloths dipped in cold vinegar and water to the belly,* she had replied, *and give her a draught of warm elderberry wine with burnt hartshorn, keeping her hips raised above her shoulders.*

*Will you use any incantation, circle, or magical word over the child?*

*God forbid,* she had sworn, laying her hand upon the Gospels. *I use no craft but the skill God has given to human hands.*

And then the Chancellor had read the solemn oath:

*Imprimis: You shall not permit or suffer any woman to name any other to be the father of her child than he only who is the true and lawful father thereof, to the utmost of your power and skill.*

*Item: You shall not use any kind of witchcraft, charm, sorcery, invocation, or other unlawful means in the time of labor or delivery, but shall use yourself in all things according to the rules of honest practice and the ancient canon of the Church.*

*Item: You shall faithfully examine every mother in the extremity of her labor, while the child is yet unborn, to declare the true and lawful name of the father thereof, without deceit, corruption, or concealment.*

*Item: You shall not suffer any child to be put away, murdered, or privily conveyed from the parish to the burden of the rate-payers, but shall report the birth thereof to the churchwardens and the civil magistrate.*

*Item: You shall not yield to threat, favor, nor fear of worldly power, but shall execute your office with an upright conscience before God and the King's bench.*

Hannah pressed her cold lips to the Bishop's sheepskin.

When she took that oath, she was thirty years of age, her heart still aching from the loss of her own newborn daughter who lay beneath the yew tree at Nether Bourne. In those days, she had thought the oath was a religious duty, a pious safeguard against the ignorance of village bone-setters and wise women.

Now, after twenty years of rural practice, she understood its true purpose.

The oath was an iron instrument forged by the law of the realm to govern the brutal economic war between the parish rate-payers and the destitute poor.

Under the Poor Laws of Elizabeth and the Settlement Act of King Charles, every human soul belonged to a parish. A poor man who fell sick outside his own parish was whipped back across the county boundary by the beadle. An unmarried pregnant maid was hounded from village to village like a stray sheep, because if she dropped her infant within the parish ditch, that child became a permanent charge upon the tenant farmers' rates.

The tenant farmers of Nether Bourne paid thirty shillings on every hundred pounds of rent to support the parish poor.

To save forty shillings a year on the rate, Thomas Callow would have seen Mary Miller whipped at the cart's tail in Warminster market place. To save the credit of High Lea Farm, he would have let the child freeze in an oilskin sack upon the Salisbury Plain.

Hannah looked up through the bare hazel boughs.

A kestrel hung motionless in the freezing sky above the downland combe, its wings trembling as it watched for mice in the frozen grass.

"Let him take the cottage," Hannah said aloud, her voice clear in the quiet wood. "Let him burn the wood-stack."

She had delivered two thousand souls into the world. She had caught them in her hands as they emerged from the dark passage of the womb; she had blown into their blue lips to wake their breath; she had tied their navel-strings and laid them upon their mothers' breasts. She had closed the eyes of thirty mothers who died of the childbed fever, holding their cold hands while the church bell tolled the passing knell.

A woman who has looked death in the face forty times in a single winter does not tremble before a tenant farmer in a drab broadcloth coat.

She rolled the parchment carefully, sliding it back into its tin case, and screwed the brass cap down tight.

She led Bess to the stone trough, tightened the girth until the buckle bit into the third hole, and mounted the side-saddle.

She touched the pony's flank with her heel, riding down the lane toward the cathedral spire.

# Chapter 28: The Journey to Salisbury

The final six miles from Wilton into Salisbury were a test of bone, sinew, and horseflesh.

The afternoon light was failing fast. Across the water meadows of the Nadder, the sky turned the color of bruised iron, with a low purple bank of snow-clouds rolling up from the south-west. The cathedral spire, four hundred feet of pale Chilmark freestone, stood out sharp against the storm like an iron lance planted in the heart of the valley.

Hannah urged Bess along the gravel margin of the turnpike.

The road was churned into thick yellow slush where three heavy dray-carts from the city breweries had broken through the snow-crust. Bess's gray coat was dark with frozen sweat; her breath came in long, whistling snorts, white plumes streaming back past Hannah's stirrup.

"Steady, girl," Hannah murmured, leaning low over the pony's neck. "Only two miles to Fisherton bridge."

They passed beneath the high stone walls of Wilton House, where the great Earl of Pembroke kept his deer parks, his Italian statues, and his pheasant coverts. The great wrought-iron gates were shut against the storm, guarded by two stone lions with paws resting upon gilded shields.

Hannah glanced at the heraldic lions without stopping.

The Earl owned twenty parishes in the valley, including the roof over Thomas Callow's head and the flint cottage where Hannah lived beside the bourne. But the Earl was in London, attending the King's ministers in Westminster, while forty miles of freezing chalk downs lay between his land-steward and the truth.

To the Earl, High Lea Farm was an entry in an estate rent-roll: *Thomas Callow, gent., four hundred acres, three hundred pounds per annum.* To the Earl, Mary Miller was an invisible unit of labor that curdled twenty cheeses a week in summer and vanished when the winter frost set in.

Past the Wilton boundary, the road ran through the river meadows where the water-meadow drowners had cut their winter ditches.

The sluice gates were frozen fast in the chalk banks, long curtains of green ice hanging from the oak paddles. A company of soldiers from the Wiltshire militia, returning from guard duty at Portsmouth, marched along the verge in ragged scarlet coats, their muskets slung muzzle-down against the sleet. Their corporal, a man with a wooden leg, shouted a rough greeting as the midwife rode past.

Hannah touched her hood with her whip-hand, keeping Bess to a brisk trot.

At four o'clock, the church bells of Saint Thomas began to chime the quarter-hour as Hannah crossed Fisherton bridge into the western suburb of the city.

The river Avon ran black and swift beneath the stone arches, carrying chunks of gray slush and dead reeds toward the sea. Beyond the bridge rose the grim stone walls of Fisherton county gaol, its iron-barred casements staring down upon the riverbank like blind eyes.

Inside those walls, in a damp cell below the water-line, Mary Miller was sitting upon wet straw, weeping for the child she believed was dead.

Hannah halted Bess upon the crown of the stone bridge.

The prison was built of dark Chilmark stone, green with river-slime where the winter flood lapped against the foundation blocks. High up, behind thick iron bars crusted with rust, small square openings looked out over the frozen water meadows. Through the nearest grating, Hannah could see the dim flicker of a rushlight and the ragged sleeve of a debtor who sat spinning hemp yarn to earn his daily pennyworth of bread.

In the lower dungeon beneath the water-level, where the female felons were lodged awaiting the Assizes, there were no rushlights.

A woman imprisoned there lay in total darkness upon damp oat chaff, her ankles secured to an iron ring in the flagstones, her food a pound of brown barley loaf and a pitcher of brackish water drawn from the river. If Mary took the childbed fever in that damp vault, she would be dead before the King's judges reached Salisbury on the spring circuit.

"Two hours," Hannah whispered, her fingers tightening upon Bess's reins. "You have two hours of life left to you, Mary, before the magistrates sign the warrant."

Hannah turned her head away from the gaol. She guided Bess through the narrow archway of the Castle Gate into the crowded streets of the city.

Salisbury on a winter marketing afternoon was a bedlam of mud, noise, and squalor.

The city was built upon water-channels that ran through the center of every street, carrying the offal of fifty slaughterhouses and the sour refuse of twenty breweries toward the river. Today the channels were choked with slush and manure. Draymen in sheepskin waistcoats shouted curses at their steaming dray-horses; fishwives from Christchurch gutted frozen herrings upon wooden boards at the Poultry Cross; pig-drovers with long ash switches beat their squealing herds through the gutters.

Hannah did not stop at the market place.

She guided Bess past the ancient stone octagon of the Poultry Cross, where the country women sat upon wooden blocks with their butter tubs and poultry crates. The market was emptying as the winter twilight fell. Butcher boys in blood-stained aprons were clearing sheep offal from the chopping blocks; fish-carters from Poole were packing unsold whiting into salted barrels; and two city constables with painted staves stood warming their hands over a brazier of burning coal-tar.

The streets smelled of horse-dung, stale brine, wet sheepskins, and the sour yeast of forty city alehouses.

Every few yards, an open gutter crossed the cobblestones, carrying black liquid filth toward the river channels. The city was a web of canals and stagnant ditches, spanned by arched bridges of brick and limestone. Beneath the bridges, the water lay thick and sluggish, coated with grease and coal-dust.

To her left, the great north wall of the cathedral close rose high and serene above the city roofs, its smooth limestone ashlar untouched by the smoke and squalor of the marketplace. Inside those quiet green lawns lived the Bishop, the Dean, and the prebends in their grand brick houses with gravel gardens and library fires. But outside the close wall lay the real city: the five thousand weavers, wool-combers, dyers, and day-laborers who lived on sixpence a day and buried their infants in unhallowed ditches.

Hannah touched Bess with her whip.

She turned down into the low, cramped quarter behind Saint Thomas's churchyard, where the broadcloth weavers lived in timber tenements crowded along the open city ditch.

This was Sheep Street.

The houses here were three stories high, their upper timber floors jutting out over the narrow lane until the eaves nearly touched across the street, shutting out the winter sky. The air was thick and foul, reeking of wet wool, size, stale urine used for scouring fleece, and the sour smoke of cheap pit-coal burning in open iron braziers.

Here lived the army of domestic weavers who made the famous Salisbury flannels and broadcloths for the London merchants.

They were a starving race in this winter of 1792. The mechanization of the northern mills in Yorkshire had begun to undercut the West Country trade; the cloth-factors had cut the piece-rate from eight shillings to five shillings for a forty-yard warp; and two hundred looms in Saint Thomas's parish stood idle while the weavers' wives begged crusts at the cathedral gate.

Hannah dismounted before an old timber house whose corner-post was carved with the faded figure of an angel holding a wool-comb.

Her legs were so stiff that when her boots touched the cobblestones, her knees buckled under her; she had to grip the saddle-bow with both hands until the blood returned to her feet. She tied Bess to an iron ring in the doorpost, threw her woolen horse-rug over the pony's steaming loins, and took her basket from the saddle.

A ragged boy with bare, dirt-caked feet and a running nose stopped to stare at her.

"Where is the cellar of Giles Pike, the weaver?" Hannah asked, holding out a copper halfpenny between her thumb and finger.

The boy bit the halfpenny with sharp yellow teeth, satisfying himself that the copper was honest King's coin and not a Birmingham counterfeit.

"Giles Pike is at his loom, mistress," the lad said, rubbing his sleeve across his running nose. "He's been throwing the shuttle since four in the morning to finish a blue broadcloth piece for the London wagon. But his wife Martha is in a foul humor. The baby that John Pike brought from the valley has been screaming for the teat, and Martha's goat died of the murrain on Sunday. She's been feeding the brat on boiled groats and sour whey, and the child's belly is swollen like an unblown bladder."

Hannah's heart tightened.

"Is the child living, boy?"

"He was crying when I came up the steps ten minutes ago," the boy replied, pocketing the coin in his ragged breeches. "A sharp, angry wail, like a trapped hare in a wire snare. You'll hear him yourself once you clear the coal-hole."

The boy's eyes widened at the coin. He snatched it into his dirty palm and pointed with his chin toward a flight of broken stone steps that led down beneath the timber archway into the earth.

"Down there, mistress," the boy said, wiping his nose with his forearm. "Second door past the cistern. But you had best watch your skirts. The sewer backed up into the court at high tide, and the slush is knee-deep in the gangway."

Hannah gathered her frieze cloak around her hips, gripped her osier basket firmly beneath her arm, and started down the slippery steps into the dark.

# Chapter 29: The Weaver's Cradle

The cellar door was made of rough elm boards, swollen with damp and fastened with an iron latch held by a loop of tarred twine.

Hannah pushed the door open.

A wave of hot, choking air rolled out into the freezing alley: the smell of boiled cabbage, rancid mutton fat, wet wool yarn, and the sour, ammonia tang of stale urine from the scour-tubs.

The cellar was low-ceiled, barely six feet from the damp flagstones to the blackened oak beams above. In the center of the room, occupying two-thirds of the floor, stood a massive broadcloth loom of heavy oak timbers. The ash treadles clattered with a rapid, mechanical stroke; the flying shuttle clicked back and forth through the wool warp with a sharp, wooden snap: *clack-thump, clack-thump, clack-thump*.

At the loom sat an elderly man with hollow, soot-stained cheeks and a dirty wool cap pulled down over his ears. His bare arms were corded with muscle, gray with wool dust.

In the corner near a smoking charcoal brazier sat a woman on a low wooden stool, her bodice unlaced, holding a bundle of rags against her chest. On a straw pallet against the back wall, three small children with rickety legs lay huddled beneath an old horse-cloth, watching the door with wide, hollow eyes.

The clattering loom came to an abrupt halt.

The weaver rested his hand upon the batten, peering through the wool warp at the strange woman standing in the doorway.

"Who are you?" the man demanded. His voice was hoarse, raspy from thirty years of breathing wool fluff in underground rooms. "We have no rent for the bailiff until Saturday. The cloth-factor hasn't weighed the piece yet."

"I am not the bailiff," Hannah said.

She pushed back her wet hood, stepping into the dim light of the tallow candle that hung by an iron wire from the loom beam.

"I am Hannah Thorne, the licensed midwife of Nether Bourne."

The woman by the brazier started, pulling the bundle closer against her chest. She had a gaunt, yellow face, her front teeth missing, her eyes inflamed with chronic coal smoke.

"Hannah Thorne," the woman repeated, her voice sharp with city suspicion. "We know no midwives from Nether Bourne. What do you want in our cellar?"

"I want the child," Hannah said.

She set her basket upon a grain tub and walked straight to the woman's stool.

"The child that John Pike brought in his carrier's cart from High Lea Farm on Friday night."

The weaver let go of the loom batten. He slid off the oak bench, his wooden clogs clattering against the damp flagstones. He stood five feet high, his spine curved from forty years over the cloth-beam, but his shoulders were thick and his jaw was set hard.

"Pike paid us five shillings," the weaver said in a flat, defensive tone. "He came down those steps at four o'clock on Saturday morning, with his boots caked in downland chalk and his sheep-whip in his hand. He had the bundle wrapped in an old oily saddle-cloth. He told us his master was a great gentleman in the valley who had got his dairy girl with child and wanted the matter kept quiet from the parish vestry."

Giles Pike stepped closer to the hearth, his bare arms folded across his chest.

"We have three little ones of our own lying on that straw, mistress," the weaver continued, nodding toward the dark corner. "My girl Jenny died of the croup at Michaelmas; my boy Dick has the rickets so bad his knees bend like ash hoops. When the trade is brisk, I can earn seven shillings a week on a fine broadcloth piece; but the factors in London have cut the rate, and the wool-stapler won't give out the yarn on credit. Five shillings is bread for two weeks in this cellar. You cannot blame a starving man for taking an honest nurse-child into his house." "He said the mother was a country wench who died of the flooding after her labor. He said the gentleman in the valley would pay two shillings a week for the boy's nursing until he was old enough for the parish workhouse."

"The mother is not dead, Giles Pike," Hannah said, her voice dropping into solemn gravity. "The mother is Mary Miller. She lies in Fisherton gaol, three hundred yards from this cellar, with iron fetters on her ankles. She is charged with murdering the babe you hold in that corner."

The woman gave a short, frightened gasp. She looked down at the bundle in her arms, then up at her husband.

"Giles," she whispered. "I told you there was blood on this business! I told you no gentleman sends a healthy child to a weaver's cellar unless there is murder behind it!"

"Hold your tongue, Martha!" the man barked, but his own face had gone the color of tallow. He looked at Hannah's gray frieze cloak, at the dignity of her bearing, and at the brass bodkin pinned at her throat.

"Is this the truth, mistress?" he asked. "Upon your soul before God?"

"Upon my oath before the Bishop and the King's bench," Hannah answered. "The magistrates sit tomorrow at Bourne Hall at two o'clock. If the child is not produced before Sir William Harford, Mary Miller will hang at the next Assizes."

The weaver stood silent for five seconds, the only sound the soft whimpering of the rickety children beneath the horse-cloth.

In this cellar, as in every laborer's dwelling in Wiltshire, the law was an enemy that came with the bailiff's staff, the constable's warrant, or the parish settlement order. The poor had learned to hide their bread, their children, and their grief from the eyes of the parish officers. But the mention of the gallows at Fisherton shook the weaver's soul.

"Give the boy to the midwife, Martha," the man said quietly.

"We have had him four days, Giles," the woman protested, tears gathering in her red-rimmed eyes. "I gave him the goat's milk we bought for little Tommy. I boiled the groats for him twice a day."

"She will pay you for the milk, woman," the weaver said. "We want no gallows work on our heads. The bailiffs are hard enough on weavers without the hangman knocking on the cellar door."

Hannah took two silver half-crowns from her purse and laid them upon the woman's knee.

"You have kept him alive, Martha Pike," Hannah said gently. "God will remember that when your own children are hungry."

The woman sniffled, wiped her eyes with the corner of her apron, and carefully handed the bundle into Hannah's outstretched arms.

Hannah took the baby to the deal table beneath the tallow candle.

She pulled back the dirty wool rags. The child was pale, his lips dry from the cellar heat, but his little chest rose and fell with steady, rhythmic breathing. Hannah dipped a clean linen rag into her flask of warm water, moistened his lips, and gently washed the coal-soot from his tiny forehead.

The infant stirred, opening his dark eyes to gaze up at the woman whose hands had first received him into the world.

Hannah looked at the weaver.

"Where does Simon Clark stable his horses?" she asked.

"At the Black Boy near the Castle Gate," Giles Pike replied. "He has four trotting mares and a light gig. He is an honest man; he carries broadcloth for my brother."

"Go to Simon Clark now," Hannah commanded. "Tell him Hannah Thorne needs a fresh horse and gig put to at daylight. Tell him a mother's life hangs upon his wheels."

The weaver nodded, snatched his woolen cap, and ran up the steps into the freezing alley.

# Chapter 30: The Identity of the Child

In the private back parlor of the Black Boy inn, a coal fire burned bright in the grate.

Hannah had paid the innkeeper's wife sixpence for the room, three quarts of boiled spring water, and a jug of fresh cow's milk from the cathedral dairy.

The baby lay upon the horsehair sofa, wrapped in a clean woolen blanket that Hannah had warmed before the hearth.

She took off her frieze cloak and rolled up the sleeves of her linsey-woolsey gown. Before she dared present this child to Sir William Harford and the sharp-tongued Counselor Garrow, she had to verify every physical mark with obstetrical precision.

In a court of Petty Sessions, the word of a country midwife would be weighed against the oath of Thomas Callow, a churchwarden worth ten thousand pounds. A single flaw in her testimony would send Mary Miller to the condemned cell at Fisherton.

She knelt beside the sofa, bringing the brass candle-branch close to the child's body.

First, she inspected the head.

An infant delivered in the second stage of hard labor carries the physical mold of the birth canal. In Mary's labor, the child had been engaged for four hours in the pelvic brim before crowning. The parietal bones of the cranium had overlapped slightly at the sagittal suture, creating a distinct elongation of the occiput—what village midwives call the *sugar-loaf crown*.

Hannah ran her sensitive fingers over the baby's skull.

The molding was still palpable beneath the dark hair. The anterior fontanelle was soft, pulsating gently with thirty-six beats to the quarter-minute, neither sunken with famine nor bulging with dropsy.

Second, she examined the umbilical cord.

Every practitioner in the West Country has her own manner of tying the navel-string. An unlicenced bone-setter uses rough hempen string; a parish nurse uses a strand of coarse wool; an apothecary's man uses silk thread with a surgeon's knot.

Hannah Thorne used three-ply bleached linen thread, waxed with mutton tallow, tied with a double clove-hitch three finger-breadths from the belly-wall. The ends were cut square with her bone-handled shears, exactly one half-inch beyond the knot.

The clove-hitch was there.

The waxed white thread was unmistakable, holding the shriveled, dark stump of the cord clean and dry against the infant's belly. There was no redness, no swelling, no foul discharge of infant mortification.

Hannah breathed a silent prayer of thanksgiving.

Third, she turned the child gently onto his right side, pulling back the flannel from his left shoulder.

Beneath the collar-bone, two inches above the heart, lay a raised, purple-red birthmark the size of a hazelnut, shaped like a wild strawberry with three distinct lobes.

Hannah had seen that mark within five minutes of the birth, while Mary Miller lay fainting on the wet straw of the lambing shed. She had pointed it out to Edward Callow while he stood trembling with the lantern; she had said to him: *Look at his shoulder, Edward. That mark will know him before the parish vestry if he lives to be eighty years of age.*

"No lawyer in Salisbury can untie that knot," Hannah whispered. "And no tenant farmer can wash that strawberry from your skin."

She lifted the infant into her arms.

The child opened his dark eyes, blinked at the candle flame, and let out a sudden, lusty wail that echoed through the quiet room.

It was a strong, vigorous cry, the sound of sound lungs and a healthy heart.

Hannah reached into her wicker basket. She took her small stone bottle of boiled milk, tested the heat against the inside of her wrist, and let three drops fall onto her little finger.

She slipped her finger into the baby's mouth.

The child sucked greedily, his tiny hands clenching against the folds of the clean flannel, his cries subsiding into soft, contented murmurs of satisfaction.

A heavy knock sounded on the parlor door.

Simon Clark, the carrier, entered the room, his frieze greatcoat crusted with snow, his whip tucked under his arm.

"The gig is ready in the stable yard, Mrs. Thorne," Clark said, touching his forehead. "I have put to the chestnut trotting mare. She can cover the eighteen miles to Bourne Hall in two hours and a quarter if the drifts aren't banked across the turnpike."

"We start at daylight, Simon," Hannah said.

"The clock in the cathedral tower has just struck six," Clark replied, pulling a heavy brass watch from his fob. "If we clear the Castle Gate before seven, we will be in the courtyard of Bourne Hall before the magistrates have taken their morning chocolate."

Hannah wrapped the baby in three layers of boiled flannel, tucked the warm stone bottle beneath his feet, and laid him into the wicker basket.

She buttoned her frieze cloak to her throat, picked up the basket, and followed the carrier out into the freezing courtyard.

# Chapter 31: The Return to Bourne Hall

The cathedral bells of Salisbury were striking seven through the morning mist when the two-wheeled gig rolled out through the Castle Gate.

Simon Clark, the carrier, drove with his ash whip between his knees, his woolen mittens gripping the leather reins. Beside him on the horse-hair cushion sat Hannah Thorne, wrapped in two heavy frieze cloaks. In her lap rested the wicker basket, lined with sheep's fleece and warmed by two stone bottles filled with boiling spring water. Deep inside the wool folds, the infant slept, his face flushed with the dry heat of the blankets.

Behind the gig, tied to the rear axle by a hempen halter, the gray pony Bess trotted with steady, rhythmic strides.

The snow had ceased during the night. A thin, watery sunlight broke through the winter clouds over Old Sarum, turning the frosted downland turf into a sheet of diamond-sparkle. But the air was biting cold; icicles two feet long hung from the thatched eaves of the wayside cottages along the Wilton turnpike.

"We have eighteen miles to Bourne Hall, Mrs. Thorne," Simon Clark remarked, flicking the mare's shoulder with the whip-lash. "If the cutting at Barford is choked with drifts, we'll lose half an hour on the lower meadow road."

"We cannot lose half an hour, Simon," Hannah answered quietly. "Sir William takes the bench at two o'clock. If Counselor Garrow moves the committal before we reach the hall, Mary Miller will be remanded to the Assizes without a hearing."

Clark nodded. He shifted his whip to his left hand and pulled a horn snuff-box from his waistcoat pocket.

"Old Sir William is a hard man on the bench, Mrs. Thorne," the carrier said, tapping the lid with his thumbnail. "He knows the statutes backward, from the Magna Charta down to the latest game act. When a poacher is brought before him, Sir William gives him twelve months in the house of correction without blinking an eye. But he has no love for tenant pride. He remembers when Thomas Callow's father was an unlettered carter on the Pembroke estate; and he knows that Callow has been buying up the small copyholds in the valley to make himself a great man at the county elections."

Clark took a pinch of brown rapee snuff, sneezing loudly into his red handkerchief.

"If you can produce that child alive before Sir William's eyes," the carrier chuckled, "Callow will find that forty sheep and a broadcloth coat won't save him from the King's bench. The old knight loves the law better than he loves any tenant in Wiltshire."

Clark nodded. He leaned forward over the dashboard, whistling a sharp country tune between his teeth, and urged the chestnut mare into a brisk, rolling canter.

The road wound along the floor of the Wylye valley.

To their left, the river ran swift and gray between black willow stumps; to their right, the steep chalk ramparts of Grovely Wood rose like frozen cliffs against the pale sky. They passed through Wishford and Steeple Langford without stopping. At each toll-bar, Clark shouted his name to the gatekeeper, threw a penny into the road, and drove straight through without checking his speed.

The infant stirred beneath the blankets as the iron tires rumbled across the stone bridges.

Hannah slipped her hand into the basket. Her fingers found the child's cheek. It was warm, soft as carded wool, his small mouth turning instinctively toward her palm to suck. She drew out her stone bottle, dipped a linen rag into the warm milk, and let him take the moisture without lifting him into the freezing wind.

"Hold fast, little soul," she whispered against the wicker rim. "You have eighteen miles to travel before you look upon your mother's face."

The journey was a continuous struggle against the winter road.

Between Wishford and Steeple Langford, the snow had drifted four feet deep in the sunken lane where the limestone walls trapped the wind. Twice the chestnut mare stumbled into a hidden ditch, the carriage shafts groaning as the near-wheel sank to the hub in freezing slush.

Simon Clark leaped down into the snow, his cowhide boots splashing through the yellow mud. He gripped the near-side spoke with both hands, shouting encouragement to the mare:

"Hup, Duchess! Hup, my hearty girl! Pull, you old rogue!"

Hannah held the wicker basket tight against her breast, shielding the wicker lid with the thick fold of her frieze cloak. Inside, the infant gave a sharp, fretful whimper as the gig tilted sideways; she slipped her warm finger into his mouth, murmuring quiet words until the wheel crested the rut and righted itself upon the gravel.

At noon they halted for ten minutes at the blacksmith's forge in Wylye.

While the smith cooled the chestnut mare's steaming bit with a bucket of snow, Hannah carried her basket into the warm smithy. By the roaring hearth of pit-coal, she unwrapped the baby. The child was awake, his dark eyes blinking at the shower of orange sparks that flew from the anvil. Hannah dried his limbs, changed the inner linen cloth, and warmed his feet before the coals.

"A fine boy, mistress," the blacksmith said, leaning upon his sledgehammer. "Whose lad is he?"

"He is the King's witness, master," Hannah said.

"He'll need to be stout of heart if he stands before Sir William," the smith remarked with a rough grin. "The old knight has the gout in his left foot today. When the gout takes him, he commits men to Fisherton for sneezing in the churchyard."

Hannah smiled grimly. She wrapped the infant tight, tucked the freshly heated stone bottle beneath his feet, and climbed back into the gig.

At twenty minutes before two, the carriage turned off the turnpike into the gravel avenue of Bourne Hall.

The long driveway was choked with vehicles: light gigs belonging to tenant farmers, heavy broad-wheeled farm wagons, and twenty saddle-horses tied to the iron railings of the deer park. Laborers from Nether Bourne, Bishopstrow, and Upton Lovell were gathered in small, excited clusters beneath the frosted beech trees, talking in low, urgent voices.

When they saw Simon Clark's gig rattling up the avenue with the midwife beside him, an electric hush fell across the yard.

"There she is!" whispered young Tom Gane, stepping aside to let the wheels pass. "The midwife has come!"

"She's too late," muttered an old carter from High Lea. "Callow's counselor has been inside with the magistrates for half an hour. The warrant is drawn."

"Warrant or no warrant," shouted young Giles the blacksmith's prentice, stepping up to the gig, "the midwife is here! Make way for Mrs. Thorne! Stand aside, you High Lea men!"

A dozen cotters and water-meadow laborers formed a rough lane through the press of horses and carts, their heavy ash sticks clearing a path to the portico. They knew the woman who walked between them. There was not a cottage in Nether Bourne where Hannah Thorne had not watched through the small hours of the night beside a sick woman's bed, holding the cold hands, brewing the chamomile decoctions, and receiving the new life into the light of the valley.

"God speed you, Hannah!" called out old goody Povey from the mounting block. "Speak for the poor girl!"

Hannah did not answer with words. She touched the brim of her hood, holding the basket closer against her ribs.

Hannah stepped down from the gig before the wheels had fully stopped.

She unfastened the halter of her pony Bess, handing the rope to Simon Clark.

"Rub her down with a dry sack, Simon," she said. "And give her a warm mash at the stables."

She drew her dark frieze cloak around her shoulders, tucking the wicker basket firmly beneath her left arm so that only the thick woolen fringe of the outer blanket showed above her elbow.

She walked up the broad stone steps of the hall.

At the carved oak doors stood Constable Barnes, his tricorn hat cocked over one ear, his five-foot iron-tipped staff barred across the entrance. When he saw Hannah Thorne mounting the steps, his jaw dropped, his small ferret eyes bulging in alarm.

"Mrs. Thorne!" Barnes stammered, his voice cracking. "You cannot enter now! Sir William has already taken his seat on the bench! Callow's attorney is speaking!"

Hannah did not check her stride. She walked straight at the constable's staff.

"Take down that timber, Barnes," she said, her voice dropping to a low, commanding rasp. "The King's witness does not wait upon a parish attorney."

She pushed the iron-tipped staff aside with her right hand and stepped across the threshold into the great hall.

# Chapter 32: The Magistrate's Bench

The great hall of Bourne Hall was packed with eighty men, smelling of wet frieze coats, stale beer, horse-sweat, and coal smoke.

At the upper end of the room, upon a raised dais beneath the carved royal arms of King George, sat the bench of magistrates.

Sir William Harford presided in the center, seated in a high-backed chair of dark oak lined with crimson velvet. He wore a dark blue coat with silver-gilt buttons, a powdered bag-wig that sat slightly crooked upon his forehead, and steel-rimmed spectacles perched on his hawk-like nose. His left foot, wrapped in bandages of flannel to ease his gout, rested upon a low stool covered with sheepskin.

Beside him sat two junior justices: the Reverend Arthur Thistlethwayte, the aged vicar of Nether Bourne, whose thin white hands rested upon an open Latin testament; and Squire Lambert of Heytesbury, a red-faced gentleman in hunting boots who was paring an apple with a horn-handled pocket knife.

Below the dais, at a long table covered with green baize, sat Mr. Tarrant, the magistrate's clerk, his quills scratching furiously across ruled sheets of legal foolscap.

In the front row of the spectators' benches sat Thomas Callow.

The tenant farmer wore his finest Sunday suit of superfine broadcloth, a scarlet waistcoat with brass buttons, and a clean white neckcloth tied with a lawyer's knot. His silver-headed cane rested between his knees; his heavy face was calm, settled into an expression of patient, righteous civic duty. Beside him sat Counselor Garrow, a sharp-featured barrister from Salisbury with a powdered tie-wig and a sheaf of parchment briefs tied with red tape.

At the bar of the court, guarded by two parish constables with brass-headed staves, stood Mary Miller.

The girl looked shrunk to half her former size. Her linsey-woolsey bodice hung loose upon her shoulders; her wrists were bruised from the iron handcuffs she had worn in the Warminster lock-up; her dark hair fell in tangles around her white, sunken cheeks. She kept her eyes fixed upon the floorboards, her lips moving in a soundless, continuous prayer.

Counselor Garrow was standing at the baize table, holding a sheepskin warrant in his left hand.

"May it please your worships," Garrow began, bowing low to the three justices. "The matter before the bench this afternoon touches the peace of our Sovereign Lord the King and the good order of the parish of Nether Bourne. Under the statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First, chapter twenty-seven—an act entitled *An Act to prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children*—the law of England speaks with unmistakable severity."

He opened a calfskin volume of Dalton's *Country Justice* and read with relish:

"'If any woman be delivered of any issue of her body, male or female, which being born alive, should by the laws of this realm be a bastard, and that she endeavour privately, either by drowning or secret burying thereof, or any other way, either by herself or the procuring of others, so to conceal the death thereof, that it may not come to light, whether it were born alive or not, but be concealed, she shall suffer death as in case of murder.'"

Garrow closed the volume with a heavy thud that echoed through the silent hall.

"The statute, your worships, creates a presumption of capital murder from the single fact of concealment. The mother who hides her delivery must produce at least one witness to prove that the child was dead at the birth; or the law presumes she destroyed the fruit of her body to avoid the shame and the parish burden."

He turned, gesturing toward Thomas Callow.

"On the night of Friday, the seventeenth of November, Goodman Callow, the principal tenant of this manor and a churchwarden whose family has tilled the soil of Nether Bourne for three generations, was aroused by cries from his lambing outbuilding. Upon repairing thither with a lantern, he found the prisoner lying in the straw, having privily delivered herself of an infant. When questioned by Master Callow and the parish officers, she refused to declare the father's name, and she could produce no living child."

Sir William Harford shifted his bandaged foot upon the sheepskin stool, wincing with pain.

"Was the body of the infant discovered upon the premises, Mr. Garrow?" the magistrate asked.

"It was not, Sir William," Garrow replied smoothly. "The parish constables have dragged the sheep-wash, searched the dung-pits, and probed the hay-stacks for three miles around High Lea Farm. No living child has been found; no dead body has been recovered. The conclusion in law is inescapable: the infant was destroyed and secretly conveyed away."

The attorney spread his black silk gown with a practiced sweep of his forearms, turning to face the full assembly of magistrates upon the elevated bench.

"Consider the statutory mischief, gentlemen," Garrow argued, his sharp, nasal voice echoing into the high timbered rafters of the courtroom. "If every casual pauper who drops a child in a field byway is permitted to fix that offspring upon the wealthiest township in the hundred merely by naming a reputable yeoman, no tenant farmer between Salisbury and the Bristol Channel could safely hire a maid-servant. The Act of King William was framed for the explicit protection of property. The statute requires an examination taken before two justices of the peace, under oath, prior to the quarter sessions. Where is that examination? The churchwardens of Nether Bourne have produced neither an affidavit of bastardy nor an order of filiation signed by Sir William Harford."

Harford shifted painfully in his cushioned chair, his bandaged right foot elevated upon the velvet hassock. He struck the table with the flat of his palm, rattling the leaden inkstand.

"The girl was detained in the Warminster cellar without warrant, Mr. Garrow," Harford said coldly. "She was denied access to her friends, denied the attendance of a licensed midwife, and locked up within ten days of her delivery. Do you call that the King's justice, or the private persecution of a parish ratepayer?"

He turned a sharp, disdainful eye upon Mary Miller.

"The prisoner has attempted to cast the blame of this unnatural crime upon the respectable son of our churchwarden, asserting that Edward Callow carried the child away to a carrier's wagon. But where is the proof of this monstrous accusation? Edward Callow is a dutiful son, educated at Warminster grammar school, who denies upon his honor that he ever had unlawful commerce with this hired servant."

Squire Lambert cut off a slice of his apple with his pocket knife, chewing it slowly as he looked down at Garrow.

"A young man's honor is easily asserted in a petty session, Counselor," the squire remarked dryly. "I have heard fifty yeomen's sons swear upon their honor that they never touched a dairy maid, until the affiliation order was sealed and the weekly four shillings demanded from their fathers' pockets."

Garrow smiled with oily condescension.

"Squire Lambert's worldly experience is well known," Garrow countered. "Yet the law requires evidence, not conjecture. Who was present in the outbuilding? The parish constable has taken the deposition of Thomas Callow. Master Callow swears that when he entered the barn at half-past one, he saw blood upon the straw, but no living infant. He questioned the girl; she was sullen, mute, and desperate."

Counselor Garrow smoothed the lace ruffles at his wrists, looking around the crowded room with theatrical assurance.

"The law of England, your worships," Garrow continued, his voice ringing off the oak paneling, "does not depend upon sentiment or country gossip. In the third volume of Sir Edward Coke's *Institutes of the Laws of England*, at the hundredth and seventy-fifth page, the great oracle of our jurisprudence lays down the maxim: *Concealment of the death is an evidence of the murder.* When an unmarried female retires into a private outbuilding, away from the eyes of matrons and neighbors, and there brings forth an infant which is never again seen by human eye, the common law presumes the destruction of the babe."

Reverend Thistlethwayte shifted his spectacles on his nose, looking troubled.

"Yet Coke speaks of concealment of the *death*, Counselor," the old clergyman observed gently. "If the child were born dead, or if it were conveyed away alive by other hands without the mother's consent, where is the malice prepense that constitutes murder?"

"The statute of King James supplies the malice, Reverend Sir!" Garrow replied sharply. "The statute was passed expressly to cure the defect of the common law. Under King James's act, the burden of proof is shifted to the prisoner. She must prove by one witness that the babe was born dead; otherwise, the law adjudges her guilty of capital felony without benefit of clergy."

The barrister turned to Mr. Tarrant.

"Mr. Tarrant, read the sworn information of the churchwarden."

The clerk took up a sheet of foolscap and read in a clear, nasal tone:

"'The information of Thomas Callow of High Lea Farm, yeoman, taken upon oath this twentieth day of November, 1792, before Sir William Harford, Knight. Who saith: That on Friday night last, about the hour of one in the morning, hearing a disturbance among his sheep in the lower fold, he proceeded to the outbuilding called the lambing barn. That he there found Mary Miller, single woman, recently delivered of an infant. That upon demanding where the infant was, she answered not a word. That he saw neither living child nor dead corpse. That he verily believes the said child was destroyed or privily buried in the river meadows by the said Mary Miller or some person acting by her procurement.'"

Mary Miller lifted her face from her hands, her eyes wide with horror.

"It is false!" she cried, her voice echoing through the timbered roof. "Master Callow took the boy with his own hands! He wrapped him in an oilskin sack! He swore if I spoke a word, he would have me whipped at the cart's tail from Nether Bourne to Warminster!"

Sir William Harford struck the table with his wooden gavel.

"Silence, prisoner," Harford said sternly, though his hawk-like eyes lingered upon Callow's impassive countenance. "You will have your turn to answer when the information is closed."

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte leaned forward, his spectacles resting upon his open Bible.

"Was no midwife called to the outbuilding, Counselor?" the old clergyman asked. "In sixty years of pastoral care in this valley, I have never known a mother deliver alone in a winter gale without seeking the aid of her neighbors. Did no woman attend her?"

"The prisoner claims she was attended by Hannah Thorne, the licensed midwife of this parish," Garrow answered with a thin, mocking smile. "A summons was duly issued by Constable Barnes for Mrs. Thorne to attend this bench at one of the clock. It is now half-past two. The midwife has failed to appear."

Garrow spread his white hands with theatrical gravity.

"The midwife is absent. The child is absent. There is no evidence of a living birth known to the law of England. On behalf of the churchwardens and overseers of Nether Bourne, I move that Mary Miller be committed to the county gaol at Fisherton, there to remain until the next general Assizes for the county of Wilts, upon the capital charge of wilful murder."

Sir William Harford took his quill from the lead inkstand. He laid the tip of the feather to the sheepskin warrant lying before him on the baize cloth.

"Mary Miller," Harford said, looking down at the weeping girl. "Have you any witness to call before this warrant is signed?"

Mary lifted her hands, the iron handcuffs clinking against her waist.

"I have no witness, your honor," she sobbed. "Only the Lord God in heaven, who saw my baby breathing upon the straw."

Sir William dipped the quill in the black ink.

A clear, steady voice rang out from the rear of the hall:

"The midwife is not absent, Sir William."

# Chapter 33: The Swearing of the Witness

The eighty men in the hall turned as one body.

Hannah Thorne walked down the center aisle between the wooden benches. Her frieze cloak was dark with melted sleet; her pattens clicked with a sharp, measured cadence upon the stone flagstones. She held her osier basket firmly beneath her left arm, draped in its outer fold of coarse gray wool, keeping her right hand free to balance herself against the bench-ends.

Thomas Callow's heavy jaw twitched. His fingers tightened on the silver head of his cane until the metal bit into his palm. Beside him, Counselor Garrow sprang to his feet, adjusting his powdered tie-wig with a sharp, irritable jerk of his chin.

"Let the woman come forward," Sir William Harford commanded, laying his goose quill back upon the green baize cloth.

Hannah reached the green table. She set her basket upon the oak bench beside Mr. Tarrant, threw back her wet hood, and made a low, respectful curtsy to the three magistrates.

"Hannah Thorne," Sir William said, peering at her over his steel spectacles. "You were summoned to attend this bench at one of the clock. It is now twenty minutes past two. Why have you delayed the King's business?"

"I have ridden forty miles since yesterday noon on the King's business, your honor," Hannah replied, her voice calm and firm. "The roads across the Plain are three feet deep in snow between here and the cathedral close. Bess has done her work, and Simon Clark's trotting mare has done the rest."

Mr. Tarrant, the clerk, held out the black calfskin Bible, its brass corners gleaming in the candlelight.

"Take the book, Mrs. Thorne," said the clerk.

Hannah laid her bare right hand upon the worn leather.

"You shall true answer make to all such questions as shall be demanded of you by the court," the clerk intoned rapidly. "You shall speak the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, so help you God."

"I will," Hannah said. She lifted the Bible to her lips, kissed the calfskin, and turned to face the magistrates.

Counselor Garrow stepped around the table, placing himself between Hannah and the bench. He held a copy of the parish warrant in his left hand, tapping the sheepskin with the gold rim of his quizzing-glass.

"Now, Mrs. Thorne," Garrow began in a smooth, insinuating tone. "You are the licensed midwife of this parish?"

"I am," Hannah said. "Twenty-two years under the seal of the Bishop of Sarum."

"During those twenty-two years, Mrs. Thorne, you have attended many women in their deliveries?"

"Two thousand infants, counselor. Rich and poor alike."

"Quite so," Garrow murmured. "A large number. A very large number. And doubtless, in the course of so vast an experience, an experienced practitioner acquires many convenient receipts? Many decoctions of tansy, pennyroyal, and ergot of rye to relieve young women of their untimely burdens?"

A murmur ran through the hall.

Hannah did not blink. She looked Garrow straight in the eye.

"A woman who has handled four hundred births knows the marks of maturity, Mr. Garrow," Hannah spoke, her voice clear and resonant throughout the quiet hall. "A child born at seven months is thin-skinned, red as a boiled crab, with soft purple nails that do not reach the tips of the fingers. His lungs are weak, his cry is like the mewing of a kitten in a basket, and his skull bones move against each other under the pressure of the midwife's thumb. Mary Miller's boy was born with firm white skin, plump thighs, and dark hair an inch long. His nails were hard and curved over his fingertips, and his cry could be heard across the barnyard. That boy was conceived during the sheep-shearing at High Lea in the first week of June, exactly nine calendar months before his birth."

Squire Lambert leaned forward across the mahogany bench, his bushy white eyebrows knit in concentration. He glanced at the Warminster surgeon who sat in the second row of the bar.

"Mr. Davis," Lambert called out to the medical practitioner. "You have examined the infant in the parish house this morning. Does your observation confirm the midwife's testimony?"

The surgeon stood up, adjusting his silver spectacles.

"It does, your worship," Davis answered respectfully. "The infant is completely mature in every physical sign. The assertion that this child was conceived at Michaelmas is an anatomical impossibility."

"I have taken the bishop's licensing oath, counselor," she said, her voice cutting through the room like a cold draft. "I have sworn before God and the Chancellor never to use any herb, craft, or violence to destroy the child within the womb. If any man in Nether Bourne says otherwise, let him step to this table and swear it upon the book."

Garrow waved his hand dismissively, covering his irritation.

"We will pass that by. On the night of Friday last, the seventeenth of November, you were called to the lambing outbuilding at High Lea Farm?"

"I was called at midnight by Edward Callow," Hannah answered clearly.

Callow's counsel frowned. "Never mind who called you, woman. Attend to the facts. When you reached the outbuilding, you found the prisoner, Mary Miller, in the extremity of labor?"

"I found her on wet oat straw, shivering with the cold," Hannah testified. "There was no fire in the outbuilding; the sleet was blowing through the broken weather-boards. Her waters had broken three hours before. The head was crowning in the second stage of hard labor."

"And what did you do?" Sir William Harford asked, leaning forward over his baize desk.

"I knelt upon the straw, your honor," Hannah said, turning her face to the magistrate. "I anointed my fingers with warm sheep-tallow. I examined the presenting part. The head was engaged in the first position, occiput to the left groin. The contractions were rapid, three in ten minutes, each contraction lasting the space of fifty heartbeats."

"Was the mother conscious?" Reverend Thistlethwayte asked softly.

"She was conscious, sir, but spent with twelve hours of secret agony," Hannah answered. "She cried out for water. I gave her three swallows of warm elderberry cordial from my stone bottle. At twenty minutes past one, under the bearing-down pains, the head emerged into my hands. The face was turned toward the sacrum. I wiped the mouth and nostrils of mucus with a boiled linen cloth. With the next pain, the shoulders cleared the passage, and the child slipped into my apron."

"And the presentation?" Squire Lambert asked, leaning his heavy elbows on the baize. "Was there any unnatural obstruction? Any turning of the child required?"

"None, your worship," Hannah answered clearly. "The pelvis was spacious, the labor natural. When the head was born, the cord was loose, not twisted around the neck. The infant was covered with healthy vernix; his limbs were plump, well-formed, with ten fingers and ten toes complete. I severed the cord three finger-breadths from the navel, tying it with three-ply bleached linen thread waxed with clean tallow."

"Did you clear the airway?" Reverend Thistlethwayte asked.

"I cleared the throat with my little finger wrapped in clean boiled linen, sir," she said. "The babe had swallowed a mouthful of mucus in the passage. When the mucus was removed, he drew his breath."

"And the breath of life?" Sir William demanded. "Did the infant breathe?"

"The child cried out before the navel-string was severed, your honor," Hannah said, her voice rising so that every man in the hall heard every syllable. "He drew a great gasp of the cold air and let out a lusty wail. His chest rose and fell with regular motion. The skin, which was bluish at the crowning, turned bright pink with the circulation of the blood. He kicked with both legs, and his mouth rooted against my thumb for the breast."

A collective gasp broke from the spectators.

Garrow stepped closer, shaking his parchment brief at her face.

"Take care, woman!" Garrow barked. "You are upon your solemn oath before the King's bench! If the child was born living, why did you leave the lambing barn without reporting the birth to the parish overseer or the churchwardens?"

"Because Thomas Callow took the child from my hands," Hannah said.

The great hall went cold and still as an open sepulchre.

Counselor Garrow took a backward step, his powdered tie-wig brushing against the clerk's high candle-bracket. His legal briefs fluttered in his hands; his thin lips parted, but no sound issued from his mouth.

On the dais, Sir William Harford pushed his steel spectacles down his nose, staring directly at the tenant churchwarden.

"You swear that upon your oath, Hannah Thorne?" Sir William asked, his voice dropping to a low, terrible rumble. "You swear that Thomas Callow, a churchwarden of this parish and a commissioner of land tax, took the newborn infant from your custody?"

"I swear it before Almighty God and this bench," Hannah answered with absolute, unyielding calm. "He stood by the outbuilding door with his stable lantern. He had an oilskin sack in his left hand and his heavy horsewhip in his right. He commanded me to leave the mother on the straw and return to my cottage across the water meadows. He laid two golden sovereigns upon the meal-bin and swore that if I spoke a word of what I had seen, he would have my roof pulled down by the parish officers before the spring."

Thomas Callow leaped to his feet with a roar of fury.

"She lies!" the farmer shouted, his heavy walking cane shaking in his fist. "She is a perjured crone! She took two guineas from my pocket as a bribe, and now she comes here with a pack of malicious slanders against my house! I command this bench to commit her for perjury!"

"Silence, Master Callow!" Sir William Harford shouted, his wooden gavel coming down with a crash that made the inkstands jump. "Sit down in your place, sir! You will not interrupt the examination of a sworn witness in this court! Constable Barnes, if that man opens his mouth again without permission, remove him to the lobby!"

Callow sank back into his oak chair, his chest heaving, his face mottled with purple rage.

Sir William leaned over the bench, looking down at Hannah Thorne with stern, judicial gravity.

"Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate said. "You swear upon your oath that Goodman Callow took the living infant from the outbuilding?"

"I do, your honor," Hannah replied. "He wrapped the babe in an oilskin sack and carried him into the yard. He told me to return to my cottage and keep my clapper shut."

"And where is the child now?" Harford asked. "Under the statute of King James, unless the living child can be produced before the court, the presumption of concealment remains against the mother."

"The child is here, your honor," Hannah said.

# Chapter 34: The Living Evidence

Hannah turned to the oak bench beside the clerk's table.

She lifted the wicker basket with both hands, set it upon the green baize cloth directly beneath Sir William Harford's spectacles, and untied the hempen strings that secured the osier lid.

The entire courtroom stood up on the benches. Men in the back row climbed upon the window-sills to see; Constable Barnes forgot his staff and leaned over the table, his mouth open; Counselor Garrow backed away two paces, his gold quizzing-glass dangling from his fingers.

Hannah folded back the outer gray frieze cloak. Beneath it lay the clean white woolen blanket she had boiled in lavender water. She drew back the blanket fold by fold.

In the center of the basket, lying upon a pillow of clean carded fleece, lay the baby.

The sudden glare of the eighty candles and rushlights struck the infant's face. The child stirred, screwed up his small features, sneezed twice, and then let out a piercing, indignant yell that rang through every corner of the great hall.

*Waaah! Waaah! Waaah!*

The cry was strong, lusty, and unmistakable. It was the full-throated cry of a five-day-old infant whose lungs were clean, whose heart was vigorous, and whose belly demanded milk.

At the bar of the court, Mary Miller gave a wild, choking scream.

She broke past the constables, throwing herself across the baize table, her handcuffed arms reaching blindly into the wicker basket. She caught the child to her bosom, burying her face against the warm flannel wrap, her tears falling in a hot rain upon the baby's dark curls.

"My boy!" she cried, rocking the infant against her shoulder. "My little boy! Oh, God be praised! God be praised in heaven! You are alive! You are alive!"

Constable Barnes stepped forward to lay hands upon the girl's shoulder, but Sir William Harford struck the table with his open palm.

"Stand back, Barnes!" the magistrate commanded, his voice shaking with sudden, fierce emotion. "Leave the mother alone! Stand back, all of you!"

Sir William stood up from his velvet chair, his gout forgotten for a moment. He leaned over the green baize table, staring down into the basket at the crying child. Beside him, the Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte leaned his white head forward, his aged eyes filling with tears as he looked upon the newborn infant.

"A living soul," the clergyman whispered, crossing himself. "Born into the parish of Christ, and preserved by the hand of the Almighty."

Squire Lambert rose from his chair, his apple forgotten upon the table. He leaned his heavy shoulders over the bench, peering down at the child with keen, rustic curiosity.

"By the living God, Harford," Lambert muttered, "that is a stout lad! Look at the breadth of his chest! That is no seven-month weakling!"

Sir William turned his hawk-like gaze upon Hannah Thorne.

"Where did you recover this infant, Mrs. Thorne?"

"In a broadcloth weaver's cellar in Sheep Street, behind Saint Thomas's church in Salisbury," Hannah answered clearly, her voice reaching the furthest corners of the gallery. "He was carried thither on Friday night by John Pike, the drover, who was paid twenty shillings by Thomas Callow to take him from the parish."

"How do you identify this child?" Counselor Garrow cried out, his voice shrill with desperation as he saw his case collapsing. "Any beggar's brat from the Salisbury gutters might be brought into this hall to deceive the court! The law requires strict, incontrovertible proof of identity!"

Hannah looked at Garrow with cool contempt.

She stepped to Mary Miller's side, laid her warm hand upon the girl's trembling shoulder, and gently took the crying child from her arms. She laid the baby upon the green baize cloth between the clerk's inkstands.

"I have delivered two thousand children into this county, Counselor Garrow," Hannah said. "A midwife knows the child she has brought through the crowning."

She unbuttoned the little shift of bleached linen at the infant's left shoulder, pulling the cloth back to expose the collar-bone.

"Examine his left shoulder, your worships," Hannah commanded.

Sir William Harford adjusted his spectacles and bent low over the table. Squire Lambert put down his pocket knife and leaned his red face forward.

There, two inches above the beating heart, was the raised, purple-red birthmark the size of a hazelnut, shaped like a wild strawberry with three distinct lobes.

"I noted that mark within five minutes of the birth," Hannah testified. "I showed it to Edward Callow while he held the stable lantern in the lambing shed; I bade him remember the mark for the rest of his life. And examine the navel-string, Sir William."

She turned back the linen swaddling at the infant's belly.

"The umbilical cord is tied with three-ply bleached linen thread, waxed with mutton tallow, and knotted with a double clove-hitch three finger-breadths from the flesh. The ends are cut square at half an inch with bone-handled shears. No surgeon in Salisbury ties that knot; no parish nurse in Wiltshire waxes that thread. That is the seal of my craft."

Squire Lambert touched the waxed linen thread with the tip of his finger.

"The woman speaks the truth, Harford," the squire declared, looking up at his fellow magistrate. "I know Hannah Thorne's work. She delivered three of my own grandchildren at Heytesbury Manor. She ties that identical clove-hitch on every child she brings into the world. That is no city foundling."

Reverend Thistlethwayte adjusted his spectacles and peered closely at the infant's shoulder.

"The strawberry mark is identical to the mark carried by the late old Goodman Callow, Thomas Callow's father," the vicar remarked in a quiet, thoughtful voice. "Old John Callow had that self-same red mark upon his collar-bone when I ministered to him on his deathbed twenty years ago. It is a family token well known in this parish."

A murmur of assent ran through the elderly tenant farmers on the front benches.

Thomas Callow sat frozen in his oak chair, his broadcloth coat buttoned to his chin, his fleshy face drained of all color. His hands gripped the arms of the chair until the walnut wood creaked beneath his weight.

Counselor Garrow gathered his parchment briefs with trembling hands, trying to maintain his professional bluster.

"Even if the identity be admitted, your worships," Garrow stammered, "the legal charge under the statute remains to be considered! The child was concealed from the parish officers! The mother made no declaration to the churchwardens! The parish rate is threatened with the burden of maintenance!"

Sir William Harford straightened his back. He looked down from the bench, his eyes resting upon Counselor Garrow with icy, judicial disdain.

"Counselor Garrow," Sir William said, his voice ringing through the hall like an iron bell. "The statute of the twenty-first year of King James the First was enacted to punish the murder of infants, not to serve as an instrument of extortion for wealthy rate-payers. The statute applies only where the child is destroyed or concealed so that it cannot be known whether it was born alive. The child is here before our eyes. He breathes; he kicks; he cries in the presence of the King's commission. The accusation of capital murder against Mary Miller is utterly groundless, vexatious, and dismissed."

The magistrate turned to Constable Barnes.

"Barnes, take the irons from this girl's wrists instantly."

The constable drew his iron key from his leather pouch, inserted it into the brass padlock, and struck off the handcuffs. The heavy iron bands clattered upon the stone floor with a sharp, ringing crash.

Mary Miller sank to her knees, gathering her crying child back into her arms, pressing her lips to his soft forehead, weeping tears of pure, unburdened deliverance.

She buried her face in the child's soft linen wrap, kissing his cheeks, his chin, and his tiny curled fingers. The infant ceased his crying, his dark eyes gazing up into his mother's weeping face with a calm, untroubled wonder.

Hannah knelt beside the girl on the cold stone flags.

She adjusted the woolen swaddling around the baby's feet, tucking the warm corners beneath Mary's trembling arms.

"He is safe, Mary," Hannah whispered, her weathered hand resting upon the young mother's shoulder. "The King's bench has heard the truth. No man can take him from you now."

Mary lifted her face, her tears wetting Hannah's wool sleeve.

"You rode across the Plain for us, Mrs. Thorne," the girl wept. "You rode through the blizzard to save my boy."

"I took the bishop's oath, Mary," Hannah replied softly. "A midwife does not leave her children in city cellars."

Women in the gallery leaned over the railings, wiping their eyes with their aprons; day-laborers in the back row stamped their heavy boots upon the floorboards in noisy approval.

Old Martha Gane, the blacksmith's wife, leaned from the gallery front, shouting down at the dock:

"God bless you, Mary! God bless your boy! The poor of Nether Bourne will stand by you!"

Constable Barnes struck the railing with his iron-tipped staff to restore order, but his own face was red with excitement. At the table, Mary sat upon the low oak bench, unlacing her linsey-woolsey bodice with trembling fingers. Her milk, which had been dried and checked by the cold of the Warminster cell, began to flow; she guided the infant's rooting mouth to her breast. The child took the nipple with a deep, hungry gasp, his tiny hands kneading against her skin, his cries turning to soft, contented murmurs of nourishment.

Reverend Thistlethwayte looked upon mother and child, his lips moving in silent thanksgiving.

"The natural law of God," the vicar whispered to Squire Lambert. "Stronger than all the penal statutes of Parliament."

Squire Lambert nodded his heavy head, wiping his eyes with his silk pocket handkerchief.

"By God, Thistlethwayte, you are right," the squire muttered. "I'd rather have delivered that babe from the gallows than won fifty guineas on the Salisbury races."

Women in the gallery leaned over the railings, wiping their eyes with their aprons; day-laborers in the back row stamped their heavy boots upon the floorboards in noisy approval.

Sir William Harford raised his hand to restore order. He looked across the crowded hall toward the deep shadows beneath the gallery stairs.

"Constable Barnes," Harford commanded. "Bring Edward Callow to the bar."

# Chapter 35: The Examination of Edward

Edward Callow did not wait for the constable's staff to touch his shoulder.

He had been standing in the deep shadow beneath the gallery stairs, his frieze coat collar turned up to his ears, his hat clutched in both hands against his chest. When the magistrate spoke his name, he stumbled out into the center aisle as if an invisible rope were dragging him toward the green baize table.

His face was the color of sour curd. His knees knocked together beneath his corduroy breeches; he stopped two paces from the bench, staring at the floorboards between his muddy boots.

"Edward Callow," Sir William Harford said, leaning his elbows upon the baize cloth. "You have heard the testimony of the sworn midwife of this parish."

Edward gave a ragged gasp, but no words came from his throat.

Thomas Callow sprang to his feet once more from the front bench, his walking cane brandished in the air.

"Do not speak, Edward!" the father shouted, his voice cracking with fury. "You are not bound to criminate yourself before this bench! Garrow, advise him! Tell him to hold his tongue!"

"Sit down, Master Callow!" Harford thundered, his wooden gavel coming down with a tremendous crack upon the table. "If you utter one more word in this court, I will commit you to Fisherton gaol for contempt of the King's commission! Constable Barnes, take that cane from his hand!"

Barnes stepped forward, wresting the silver-headed cane from Callow's grasp. The farmer sank back into his oak chair, his teeth grinding together in helpless rage.

Sir William turned back to the young man.

"Edward Callow," the magistrate said, his voice dropping into quiet, measured gravity. "You are upon the threshold of your manhood. You have seen this young woman stand at this bar, loaded with felon's irons, facing the gallows at Salisbury for the murder of your child. Look at her."

Edward lifted his head.

He looked at Mary Miller. The girl was standing three feet away, holding her baby against her chest, her dark eyes looking at him not with hatred, but with a terrible, sorrowful pity.

The child in her arms let out a soft, contented whimper, his little hand reaching out to touch the brass buttons of Edward's waistcoat.

That small sound broke the last remnant of the young man's defiance.

Edward dropped to his knees upon the stone floor. He buried his face in his hands, his whole body shaking with long, convulsive sobs that echoed through the quiet hall.

"It is my child, your honor!" he cried, the words tearing from his throat. "I am the father! I lay with Mary in the dairy loft after the sheep-washing! I swore to her by the Holy Gospel that I would take her to the church and make her an honest wife!"

A great sigh, like the wind passing through an autumn wheat field, rose from the laborers in the gallery.

Squire Lambert leaned over the bench, his face flushed with rustic indignation.

"You promised her marriage, young man?" Lambert barked. "And why did you not carry her to the church like an honest Englishman, instead of leaving her to drop your child in a sheep-shed?"

"Father swore he would turn me out, your worship," Edward cried, his voice breaking into a shrill wail. "He said Mary was a common vagrant whose family had taken parish relief in Bishopstrow. He said if I took her to church, he would burn the lease and leave High Lea to our cousins in Devizes. He kept me locked in the parlor on Friday morning; he watched every road with his horsewhip; he swore if I went near the lambing barn, he would strike me down like a dog!"

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte shook his white head in profound sorrow.

"And you valued forty acres of pasture above a human soul, Edward?" the clergyman asked. "You valued your father's broadcloth above the life of your own flesh?"

"I was afraid, Mr. Thistlethwayte," Edward sobbed, beating his breast with his fists. "God help me, I was a coward! I thought Father would find a good nurse in Salisbury; he told me the child would be reared by honest folk and that Mary would take service in another valley where no one knew her shame!"

"And what of Friday night?" Harford demanded. "Who carried the child to John Pike's cart?"

"Father did!" Edward sobbed, lifting his wet face to the bench. "I wanted to fetch Hannah Thorne at dusk, but Father locked me in the stable. He said if the parish heard of the child, the Earl's steward would give the farm lease to our cousins in Devizes. At midnight, when Mary was screaming in the barn, he let me go for Hannah; but he swore if I spoke a word of paternity, he would turn me out onto the turnpike without a shilling!"

He turned and pointed a trembling finger at Thomas Callow.

"He paid John Pike twenty shillings to take the boy to Salisbury! Pike was to leave him with his cousin in Sheep Street! Father said the babe would die of the thrush before the week was out, and no one would ever know the difference!"

Edward reached into the breast pocket of his coat. He pulled out a worn leather pocketbook tied with a whipcord thong and laid it upon the table before the clerk.

His hands shook so violently that the whipcord knot took two minutes to untie.

"Look at the third leaf, your worship," Edward cried, pointing with a trembling finger. "There is the memorandum of the twenty pounds. Father made me write the entry myself in the parlor on Michaelmas night: *Received of Edward Callow, twenty pounds of shearing money, to be held against his marriage or good behavior.* And underneath, in Father's own hand: *Cancelled upon the agreement with J.P. for the removal of the child from Nether Bourne.*"

Squire Lambert leaned over the table, taking the pocketbook from Tarrant's fingers. He examined the coarse paper beneath his reading glass, comparing the brown ink with the signature on Callow's lease.

"The signature is Thomas Callow's beyond all doubt," Lambert declared, his voice heavy with contempt. "I have fifty receipts in my rent-chest signed with that identical flourish. The father took the boy's savings to buy the carriage of his own grandchild."

"There is the proof, Sir William," Edward wept. "That is my account book. On the flyleaf is written the agreement with John Pike, witnessed in my father's own hand: *To John Pike, carter, twenty shillings to convey the child to Salisbury, and two shillings a week for the keeping.* Father kept the book in his iron chest, but I took it this morning before I rode to court."

Mr. Tarrant took the pocketbook, opened the pages, and handed it to Sir William Harford.

Sir William read the entry through his spectacles. His face grew dark as a thundercloud.

"The entry is dated the sixteenth of November," Harford said, reading aloud. "'Agreed with J.P. to take the child from the parish for twenty shillings down, and two shillings weekly till Michaelmas.' It is signed with the initials *T.C.* in the well-known hand of Thomas Callow."

The magistrate looked down at Edward.

"Why did you not speak when Mary was arrested at Warminster, Edward?"

"Father threatened to disinherit me, your worship," the young man answered, tears streaming down his face. "He said he had thirty pounds lodged with Counselor Garrow to defend the parish, and that the girl would be transported to Botany Bay where she could do no harm to High Lea Farm. But when I saw Hannah Thorne in the stable yesterday, my heart failed me. I could not sleep for thinking of Mary in the cold cell."

He turned to Mary Miller, sinking lower on his knees until his forehead touched the hem of her russet skirt.

"Forgive me, Mary," he sobbed. "I was a coward. I listened to my father's pride instead of my own heart."

Mary looked down at him. She did not draw her skirt away, but her hand remained tight upon her sleeping son.

"I forgive you, Edward," she said in a low, trembling whisper. "For the sake of the child."

Sir William Harford laid the pocketbook upon the baize cloth beside the Bible.

"Rise, Edward Callow," the magistrate said. "You have played the part of a coward and a villain; yet by speaking the truth today, you have saved an innocent woman from the gallows and preserved your soul from the guilt of blood. You will remain in court to answer the affiliation order under the statute."

Sir William Harford turned to his clerk.

"Mr. Tarrant, enter this confession upon the minutes: Edward Callow, upon his examination before this bench, doth voluntarily confess and acknowledge that he is the true and reputed father of the male bastard child born of the body of Mary Miller on the seventeenth of November last past."

The magistrate looked at Edward, who remained kneeling on the flagstones.

"Under the act of the sixth year of His present Majesty, chapter thirty-one, you are hereby ordered to stand committed until you shall give security to indemnify the parish of Nether Bourne, or enter into a recognizance with two sufficient sureties to appear at the next quarter sessions."

Edward lifted his face, wiping his wet cheeks.

"I will give the security, Sir William," the young man said. "I have thirty pounds from my sheep-shearing. I will pay every penny that the court orders for Mary and the boy."

He turned his eyes upon Thomas Callow.

The courtroom fell into an ominous, breathless silence as Sir William reached for his gavel.

# Chapter 36: The Collapse of Thomas Callow

Sir William Harford struck the table three times with his heavy oak gavel before the uproar in the great hall subsided into a tense, hostile silence.

The magistrate pushed his spectacles up onto his forehead. His face was set in lines of stern, unyielding judicial severity. He looked at Thomas Callow as an officer looks at a traitor who has disgraced his uniform.

"Thomas Callow," Sir William said, his voice cutting through the stillness like a cold chisel through slate. "Stand up."

Callow did not move at first. He sat slumped in his oak chair, his broadcloth coat bunched around his thick neck, his heavy hands resting open upon his knees. Counselor Garrow had already gathered his briefs, tied them with his red tape, and backed away down the side gangway, leaving his client without an advocate.

"Stand up, sir!" the magistrate commanded.

Constable Barnes laid his rough hand upon the farmer's collar. Callow rose slowly, his knees shaking, his breath coming in short, wheezing gasps.

"You have been a churchwarden of this parish for twelve years," Harford said. "You have sat in the vestry; you have levied the poor-rates upon your poorer neighbors; you have driven vagrants from your gate with the parish beadle. And to save your tenant pride and the lease of your four hundred acres, you have entered into a wicked conspiracy to abandon a newborn infant in a city cellar, and to send an innocent servant maid to the gallows for a murder you knew was never committed."

Callow opened his mouth, his tongue licking his dry, cracked lips.

"Sir William," he croaked, his voice hollow as an empty barrel. "I was thinking of the family name. The boy was a bastard. The girl was a hired servant—"

"Silence, sir!" Harford thundered. "Do not profane this court with your miserable excuses! The law of England does not distinguish between the child of a beggar and the child of a gentleman! They are both subjects of the King, and the life of each is sacred before the law!"

Squire Lambert leaned forward, his face flushed with indignation.

"You have disgraced the yeomanry of Wiltshire, Callow," the squire said bluntly. "I have bought bullocks from you for twenty years, but I would not buy a sheep from your fold after this day's work. You have used the King's justice as a tool to cover your own family's wickedness."

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte closed his Latin testament, looking across at his senior churchwarden with sorrowful condemnation.

"You sat in the front pew on Sunday, Thomas," the old clergyman said softly. "You put two half-crowns into the poor-box; you recited the commandments of God; and all the while, your grandson was starving in a city cellar and the mother of your son's child was weeping in felon's irons. May God grant you repentance before your own hour comes."

Sir William turned to his clerk, Mr. Tarrant.

"Mr. Tarrant, enter this upon the minutes of the Petty Sessions: Thomas Callow of High Lea Farm is hereby ordered to be bound over in his own recognizance of two hundred pounds, with two sufficient sureties in one hundred pounds each, to appear at the next general Assizes for the county of Wilts, to answer an indictment for misdemeanor and conspiracy to commit child abduction and perjury."

Callow flinched as if a whip had struck his cheek.

"Two hundred pounds!" he gasped. "Sir William, that is half the value of my dairy herd! Where am I to find two sureties of a hundred pounds each in this valley?"

"If you cannot find sureties among your neighbors, Master Callow," Harford replied with icy disdain, "the constables will convey you to Fisherton gaol before nightfall, where you may keep company with the common felons until the judges arrive from London."

He looked toward the front benches where the tenant farmers sat.

"Is there any rate-payer in Nether Bourne who will stand surety for Thomas Callow?"

A dead, heavy silence answered the magistrate's call.

Not a hand was raised; not a voice spoke. Master Barnes of Codford looked down at his boots; the miller from Wylye turned his head away; the tenant farmers who had drunk Callow's cider and voted his motions in the vestry for twenty years sat motionless, avoiding his pleading gaze.

Callow's jaw worked silently. He looked from face to face, but he met only the cold, unyielding stare of men who saw that his power was broken.

"Take him into the side parlor, Barnes," Harford commanded. "Keep him in safe custody until his bonds be settled or his warrant of committal made out. And if any man attempts to convey his cattle or sheep from High Lea Farm before the bailiffs have executed the bond, let him be committed without bail."

Barnes took Callow by the elbow.

The great tenant patriarch of Nether Bourne, whose word had ruled forty cottages for a quarter of a century, turned and walked down the side aisle with bowed head, his boots shuffling through the sawdust like an old man of eighty.

As he passed the laborers from his own farm, not a single cap was touched.

Young Tom Gane, who had plowed the five-acre field for six shillings a week, stepped out into the gangway, staring directly into his master's face.

"Where is your four-horse carriage now, Goodman Callow?" the young carter called out, his voice ringing through the hall. "Where is your broadcloth coat and your gold seals? You'll wear the county frieze at Fisherton before the spring plowing!"

Callow flinched, his head sinking lower between his shoulders. He did not dare lift his eyes. Constable Barnes gripped the farmer's elbow tighter, hurrying him past the hostile crowd toward the magistrate's private room.

The spectators began to pour out through the great arched doorway into the winter afternoon, their voices buzzing like a disturbed hive of bees.

Old Giles the smith spat deliberately onto the flagstones at Callow's feet; the carters turned their backs; and a low, mocking hiss ran from man to man until the heavy door of the magistrate's parlor closed behind him with a final, hollow thud.

The courtroom remained hushed for twenty heartbeats after the latch fell.

The tenant farmers who had sat behind Callow on the front benches rose quietly, their hats clutched in their hands, their eyes avoiding the bench. Master Barnes of Codford buttoned his coat to his throat and slipped out through the side doorway without speaking to a neighbor; the miller from Wylye gathered his riding whip and followed him.

Sir William Harford rose from his velvet chair, leaning upon his silver-mounted cane. He stood six feet high, his aristocratic countenance dark with cold fury as he looked down at the disgraced tenant.

"Thomas Callow," Harford said, his voice echoing through the silent hall like thunder over the downs. "You have sat in the churchwarden's seat for twelve years; you have worn broadcloth while your laborers wore rags; you have prided yourself upon the favor of the Earl and the size of your dairy herd. And to save forty shillings on your parish rate, you would have sacrificed an infant's life and an innocent girl's neck upon the scaffold. You are a disgrace to the yeomanry of England."

Callow made no answer. His thick head hung forward upon his chest, his breath coming in hoarse, rattling gasps as Barnes led him toward the side door.

The reign of Thomas Callow over Nether Bourne had ended.

For twenty years, his word had decided who received parish coal in December and who went hungry; his vote had fixed the poor-rates, apprenticed parish orphans to northern cotton mills, and driven destitute widows into the workhouse at Warminster. Now his name was entered upon the magistrate's roll as a common conspirator, bound over to the King's Assizes like a sheep-stealer or a turnpike footpad.

# Chapter 37: The Settlement Order

The clerk Tarrant dipped his goose quill into the heavy lead inkstand, sprinkling a fresh sheet of ruled foolscap with fine black blotting-sand.

Sir William Harford leaned over the baize table, his spectacles pushed down to the bridge of his hawk-like nose. He held a copy of the eighteenth statute of Queen Elizabeth in his left hand, turning the yellowed sheepskin leaves with deliberate care.

"Under the statute of the eighteenth of Elizabeth, chapter three, and the act of the sixth of His present Majesty, chapter thirty-one," Sir William announced to the crowded hall, "two Justices of the Peace, whereof one to be of the quorum, are empowered to make an order of affiliation and maintenance for the relief of the parish and the sustenance of any bastard child."

The magistrate looked at his fellow justice, the Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte.

"Brother Thistlethwayte, do you concur in the adjudication?"

"I concur with all my heart, Sir William," the aged vicar answered, his voice trembling with solemn satisfaction. "The mother has sworn her oath; the midwife has testified to the crowning and the live birth; and the young man has confessed his paternity before God and this bench."

Sir William turned to the clerk.

"Take down the order, Mr. Tarrant. In the name of our Sovereign Lord King George the Third, King of Great Britain, France, and Ireland, Defender of the Faith:

"Whereas complaint hath been made unto us, two of His Majesty's Justices of the Peace for the county of Wilts, by the churchwardens and overseers of the poor of the parish of Nether Bourne, that Mary Miller, single woman, was on the seventeenth day of November last past delivered of a male bastard child in the said parish, which child is likely to become chargeable to the said parish:

"And whereas we have examined the said Mary Miller upon her oath, and have heard the sworn evidence of Hannah Thorne, licensed midwife, and the voluntary confession of Edward Callow of High Lea Farm, yeoman:

"We do therefore adjudge the said Edward Callow to be the true, lawful, and reputed father of the said bastard child."

The scratch of Tarrant's quill was the only sound in the great hall. Eighty men listened in complete silence, leaning forward from their oak benches to catch every phrase of the legal instrument.

"And we do hereby order," Harford continued, his voice rising in clear, authoritative cadence, "that the said Edward Callow shall forthwith pay or cause to be paid unto the churchwardens and overseers of the poor of the parish of Nether Bourne the sum of four pounds ten shillings of lawful British money, for the charges of the lying-in and the midwife's attendance:

"And the further sum of four shillings weekly and every week, to be paid into the hands of the said Mary Miller, for the nursing, clothing, and maintenance of the said child, until the said child shall attain the full age of seven years, or so long as he shall remain chargeable to the said parish."

Edward Callow stepped forward to the table, his eyes red with weeping.

"I accept the order, your honor," the young man said, his voice choking. "I will pay the four pounds ten shillings tomorrow at noon. And the four shillings a week shall be paid every Saturday morning into the overseer's hands."

"See that it is done, young man," Harford warned sternly. "If you fail in a single payment, a distress warrant will issue against your goods and chattels; and if there be no goods, you will be committed to the house of correction at Devizes to be kept to hard labor."

Sir William took the quill from his clerk. He signed his name with a heavy, flourishing stroke: *Wm. Harford, J.P.*

The Reverend Mr. Thistlethwayte took the pen and signed beneath him: *Arthur Thistlethwayte, Vicar.*

Mr. Tarrant sprinkled fine blotting-sand across the wet ink of the order, blowing the black grains away with a soft puff of his cheeks.

"The order is complete, your worships," the clerk announced, presenting the parchment.

Tarrant melted a stick of vermilion sealing wax in the flame of a tallow candle, dropped two broad crimson tears upon the sheepskin margin, and handed the seal to Sir William. The magistrate pressed his heavy gold signet ring into the soft wax, stamping the Harford family crest—a falcon with wings displayed—deep into the seal.

"Let two copies of this order be drawn and engrossed upon parchment," Harford ordered. "One copy to be served upon Edward Callow, and one copy to be delivered to the churchwardens of Nether Bourne to be kept in the parish chest."

The magistrate turned his gaze upon Mary Miller, who stood before the dais holding her sleeping baby in her arms.

"Mary Miller," Sir William said, his dry judicial tone softening into fatherly kindness. "You are discharged from all custody, charges, and bonds. The churchwardens will pay your weekly allowance without deduction. You are free to return to your mother's cottage at Bishopstrow, and no parish officer shall molest you."

Mary dipped a low, trembling curtsy, pressing the baby tight against her bosom.

"God bless your worship," she whispered through her tears. "God bless your worship and the good vicar."

Sir William adjusted his spectacles and looked at Hannah Thorne, who stood quietly beside the baize table with her wicker basket under her arm.

He reached into his embroidered waistcoat pocket, drew out a heavy calfskin purse with silver strings, and counted five gold guineas onto the green cloth. The heavy coins clinked with a clear, musical ring upon the timber.

"And to you, Mrs. Thorne," the magistrate said, leaning forward. "The bench orders this reward of five guineas from the county fine fund, in compensation for your extraordinary labor, your charges in hiring horses and carriage, and your courage in riding across Salisbury Plain in a blizzard to preserve the life of a subject of the King."

Hannah looked at the five golden guineas shining in the candlelight.

It was more money than a day-laborer earned in four months of harvesting. It would buy two loads of seasoned oak firewood, a stone of mutton tallow, and a new winter cloak of fine broadcloth.

She did not reach out her hand.

"I thank your honor with all my heart," Hannah said, dipping a slow, dignified curtsy. "Sir William is ever just to the poor. But I cannot take gold for doing that which my bishop's oath commanded me to perform. When I knelt in the cathedral close twenty years ago, I swore before God never to sell the life of an infant nor take a bribe to alter my testimony."

She pointed to the gold coins.

"Let the five guineas be paid to Mr. Thistlethwayte," she said. "Let them be entered in the churchwardens' book for the relief of the bedridden widows in Nether Bourne almshouse, who have no firewood to keep out the downland frost."

Reverend Thistlethwayte lifted his hands, his eyes shining with tears.

"The Lord will reward you, Hannah," the old clergyman said softly. "The prayers of the poor will keep your cottage warm."

Hannah looked upon Mary Miller and her child.

The girl stood in the center of the hall, surrounded by four village women who were tying a warm woolen shawl around her shoulders and tucking a hot brick beneath the baby's blanket. The parish constable had handed Mary her straw bonnet and her small bundle of linen; and Edward Callow was standing by the door, waiting to lead her safely past the horse-teams to the carrier's gig.

Hannah gathered her frieze cloak around her shoulders. She took her osier basket under her arm, the empty wicker light upon her forearm.

She bowed to the magistrates, turned on her heel, and walked with firm, unhurried steps down the center aisle into the winter afternoon.

# Chapter 38: The Price of Truth

The sun had sunk beneath the long chalk rampart of Upton Lovell when Hannah reached the outskirts of Nether Bourne.

The wind had dropped completely. The bitter gale that had scourged the Salisbury Plain for three days had died away into a freezing, breathless hush. Above the dark downland ridges, the sky faded from pale violet to deep indigo, pierced by the brilliant diamond spark of the evening star hanging over the ancient barrows. Below in the valley, the chalk bourne ran dark and clear between the frosted watercress beds, reflecting the twilight.

Hannah rode Bess across the narrow stone packhorse bridge.

Her body was one continuous ache. Thirty miles in Simon Clark's rattling gig and forty miles in the side-saddle had ground the marrow in her bones; her knees were stiff as seasoned ash poles; the knuckles of her hands were swollen red and throbbing from the freezing reins.

She dismounted at her garden gate, holding the timber post until the blood returned to her frozen feet.

She led Bess into the low stone shed beside the garden wall.

The gray pony was trembling with weariness, her winter coat stiff with white salt-sweat from the turnpike road. Hannah did not think of her own cold hearth until the beast was tended. She took a wisp of dry wheat-straw and rubbed the mare's loins, neck, and fetlocks until the hair was dry and warm. She threw two armfuls of sweet meadow hay into the rack, mixed a warm bran mash with a handful of coarse salt and a cup of black treacle in the wooden tub, and broke the skin of ice on the water-bucket with the heel of her boot.

"Eat, brave girl," Hannah murmured, resting her forehead against the mare's warm neck. "You carried the truth through the snow. There is no better pony in the county of Wilts."

She closed the stable half-door, thrust the ash peg through the iron staple, and walked up the gravel path to her cottage porch.

The stone porch was piled high with gifts.

Thomas Callow's two wagon-loads of seasoned oak firewood would never arrive; the farmer sat under guard in the private parlor of the George Inn, and his team of black oxen was impounded by the bailiffs until his two hundred pounds bail was lodged with the clerk of the peace.

Yet the porch was packed from flagstones to eaves.

Against the flint wall stood thirty bundles of dry hazel faggots, bound with twisted willow withies, cut from the parish coppice by Giles the blacksmith and his two apprentices. Beneath the thatched eaves sat three sacks of stone-ground barley flour from the water-mill; a broad wicker hamper of dried russet apples from Goodwife Povey; two earthenware jars of wild heather honey from the high downland hives; and a fat side of cured bacon, salted and smoked over birch chips, laid across the wooden bench by the carters from the water meadows.

Beside the bacon sat two brown earthenware crocks of pickled cabbage, a stone bottle of elderberry wine, and a wooden trencher filled with fresh brown eggs packed in dry oat husks.

Pinned to the bacon rind with an iron bodkin was a scrap of coarse brown wrapping paper, written in the rough, crooked hand of the village wheelwright:

*For Mrs. Thorne, who remembered the poor when the great men forgot the King.*

Hannah touched the rough paper with her thumb.

A tear slipped from her eye, hot against her frost-bitten cheek, but she brushed it away with the back of her woolen mitten.

She unlocked the heavy oak door and entered her kitchen.

The room was dark and freezing cold. The embers on the open hearth had died to gray powder during her absence; the water in the copper boiler had frozen into a solid sheet of ice an inch thick.

Hannah did not light a candle.

She knelt upon the hearthstones, swept away the white ash with an iron shovel, and laid four dry hazel splits from the blacksmith's bundle across the iron firedogs. She took her iron flint-box from the mantelpiece, struck the curved steel against the black flint until a yellow spark caught the charred linen tinder, and blew gently upon the glow until a tiny curl of birch bark took the flame.

A bright, crackling fire leaped up the wide stone chimney, throwing warm orange light across the whitewashed walls, the rows of pewter spoons, and the dried bunches of tansy and chamomile hanging from the oak rafters.

Hannah filled her iron kettle from the stone pitcher, set it upon the iron crane over the blaze, and sat down in her high-backed oak settle.

She reached into the inner pocket of her stays.

She drew out the stained linen square she had taken from the granary loft at High Lea Farm.

The linen was stiff, dry, and cold to her touch. Near the hem lay the brown, crusted stain of dried vernix and infant blood—the physical evidence that had threatened to send an innocent servant maid to the gallows at Salisbury.

Hannah held the cloth in both hands.

In an 18th-century parish, the life of a poor working maid was fragile as a bird's egg. A single lie from a churchwarden, a single nod from a magistrate, and the hempen rope at Fisherton gaol would have done its work. The law of England was written on heavy parchment by men of property; but the life of the newborn child was written by God upon human flesh.

The child was safe in his mother's arms; Edward Callow was bound to his duty; and the truth had been vindicated before the King's bench.

She looked at the corner of her kitchen where her dead husband's ash tool-chest stood beneath the window.

John Thorne had been an honest craftsman. He had shaped felloes of seasoned ash and spokes of heart-of-oak that carried the county wagons across forty miles of downland chalk without splintering. He had never taken a bribe from a landlord; he had never turned a hungry carter from his door.

"The work is done, John," she whispered into the quiet room. "The child is alive, and the truth holds."

Hannah leaned forward and dropped the linen square into the center of the roaring hazel fire.

The dry flax curled, caught the blaze in a sudden flash of yellow flame, and vanished into black, gossamer ash that rose with the sparks up the chimney toward the winter stars.

She poured the boiling water into her brown earthenware teapot, adding a spoonful of dried chamomile blossom and wild mint from her summer gathering. The fragrant herbal steam rose into the warm kitchen, sweet with the scent of downland hills in July.

She drank the tea slowly from a pewter cup, feeling the comforting warmth spread through her tired limbs.

Outside, the bourne rushed swift and clear through the watercress beds, carrying the thawed winter snows toward the sea.

Hannah sat back upon her dark settle, closed her eyes, and listened to the cheerful singing of the kettle over the hearth.

# Chapter 39: The Parish Baptism

On Christmas morning, the four bells of Saint Mary's Nether Bourne rang out across the valley.

The great winter freeze that had locked the county of Wilts in iron ruts for five weeks had broken three days before. A soft, warm wind had blown up from the Bristol Channel, carrying heavy rains that melted the snowdrifts from the high downland combes. The chalk bourne was running bank-high, a rushing torrent of milky, clear water that brimmed over the willow roots and swirled around the stone piers of the village bridge. Along the churchyard ditch, the green spears of wild snowdrops were thrusting through the wet, thawed turf.

The ancient Norman nave was packed to the doors with two hundred parishioners in their holiday clothes.

Sprigs of glossy dark holly with scarlet berries were stuck into the oak pew-ends; trailing ivy was twined around the massive stone pillars; and boughs of sweet box decorated the carved corbels of the roof. In the west gallery, the village choir was assembled, accompanied by the great wooden serpent, two fiddles, and the bassoon, playing the old Wiltshire Christmas psalm: *Rejoice, ye tenants of the earth, for Christ the Lord is born.*

At the ancient stone font near the south porch stood Mary Miller.

She wore a clean gown of dark russet wool and a white lawn neckerchief pinned with a small silver bodkin. Her cheeks had regained their youthful dairy bloom; her dark hair was neatly braided beneath a pleated mob-cap of bleached linen. In her arms she held her seven-week-old son, swaddled in a christening robe of fine white downland wool, knitted with open-work lace by the women of Bishopstrow.

Beside her stood Hannah Thorne, wearing her best black wool gown, her white apron starched stiff as cardboard, her gray hair covered by a black silk bonnet.

The Reverend Arthur Thistlethwayte stood at the font in his flowing white linen surplice. The silver baptismal basin rested upon the carved Norman rim, filled with clear water drawn that morning from the parish holy well in the vicarage garden.

"Name this child," the aged vicar said, looking down over his steel-rimmed spectacles with fatherly kindness.

Mary Miller looked at Hannah Thorne. The midwife laid her weathered right hand upon the baby's christening shawl and spoke the name in a clear, ringing voice that reached the furthest benches of the nave:

"John Callow Thorne Miller."

The vicar dipped his hand into the holy water. He poured the stream three times across the infant's dark, curling hair:

"John Callow Thorne Miller, I baptize thee in the Name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. Amen."

The water sparkled in the candlelight as it trickled over the baby's smooth forehead. The child did not weep; he opened his dark eyes, blinked up at the carved timber angels on the church ceiling, and reached his small fist toward the shining silver bowl.

"We receive this child into the congregation of Christ's flock," the clergyman intoned, making the sign of the cross upon the infant's forehead with his thumb. "And do sign him with the sign of the Cross, in token that hereafter he shall not be ashamed to confess the faith of Christ crucified, and manfully to fight under His banner against sin, the world, and the devil."

From the fourth pew, Edward Callow watched with bowed head.

He was dressed in plain brown frieze, without his silver buckles or his brass-mounted whip. High Lea Farm had been sequestered by the Earl's bailiff pending the Assizes at Salisbury, but Edward had paid the four pounds ten shillings lying-in charge from his own fleece money, and his four shillings were entered weekly in the churchwardens' book. When the holy water touched his son's forehead, he buried his face in his hands, weeping silently behind his fingers.

When the service ended, the congregation moved out into the pale winter sunlight of the churchyard.

Edward Callow stepped out of the porch and walked to Mary Miller's side. He reached into his coat pocket and drew out a small linen bag containing thirty gold guineas—his entire savings from the autumn sheep-shearing.

"Take it, Mary," the young man whispered, his voice trembling. "It is for the boy's schooling. I have sworn before the altar that I will never see him want for bread while I have two hands to plow."

Mary looked at him, then down at the sleeping child in her arms.

"I thank you, Edward," she said gently. "The boy will know his father was an honest man at the last."

The parishioners gathered on the grass beside the Norman porch, their voices carrying over the rushing water of the bourne.

Old Giles the blacksmith touched his cap to the midwife, holding out a sprig of glossy holly with crimson berries.

"A good Christmas, Mistress Thorne," the smith said, his voice deep and warm. "The valley is at peace today. The church bells ring with a clearer tone when there is no blood upon the parish books."

"A good Christmas, Giles," Hannah answered, taking the holly and pinning it to the lapel of her wool cloak.

She looked toward the church gate where Mary and Edward were walking side by side through the melting snow. The young father was carrying his son against his shoulder, holding the christening robe securely against the south wind.

Hannah stood upon the churchyard path beside the ancient yew tree, watching them walk together down the lane toward Bishopstrow.

# Chapter 40: The Hands That Deliver

On the second Tuesday of January, the knock came upon Hannah's shutter an hour before sunrise.

It was not the urgent, guilty tapping of Edward Callow, nor the heavy iron summons of the parish constable. It was the sharp, clear rap of an ash staff against the oak timber.

Hannah swung her feet out of bed onto the cold braided rug. She pulled on her flannel petticoat, slipped her arms into her dark wool bodice, and drew back the window bolt.

In the lane below stood a young shepherd boy from Upton Lovell, his cheeks red as winter rose-hips from the frost, his sheepskin coat buttoned to his ears.

"Mistress Thorne!" the boy called up, his breath puffing white against the dark flint wall. "My mother has taken her hard labor in the cottage by the middle sheep-fold! Father says the pains are coming two in the hour, and the water has broken!"

"Go to the stable and throw the saddle on Bess, lad," Hannah answered through the casement. "I will be down before you have buckled the girth."

She closed the shutter.

She knelt by her stone hearth, raked away the gray embers, and threw on a handful of dry heather roots to start the kettle. She washed her hands and forearms in cold rainwater from her stone pitcher, scouring her nails with a stiff hog-bristle brush and yellow lye soap until the skin was red and clean.

She went to her deal table.

Her wicker basket stood ready by the window.

She opened the lid, inspecting the contents with practiced, deliberate care:

Six squares of boiled linen, bleached in the summer sun and wrapped in clean oiled silk;

Her pewter bodkin, sharp and polished, lying in its box of dried elder-pith;

Her bone-handled shears, oiled with sweet almond oil and wrapped in chamois leather;

Two stone phials of squill vinegar and dried tansy blossom;

A skein of three-ply bleached linen thread, waxed with mutton tallow for the navel-string;

And her tin license cylinder, bearing the red wax seal of the Bishop of Sarum, tucked safely into the side pocket beneath the leather handle.

She closed the basket lid and buckled the brass strap.

Hannah paused by the threshold, looking back into her kitchen.

The peat fire was burning quietly on the stone hearth, warming the small room with its sweet, earthy scent. On the deal table sat the blue earthenware jar of dried chamomile and the loaf of barley bread given to her by Goodwife Povey. The room was peaceful, clean, and secure.

She looked at her hands.

Her knuckles were rough, scarred by thirty years of hot water, sheep-grease, and winter wind. The thumb of her right hand was broad and calloused from holding the leather reins against the Wiltshire gales; the fingers were long, sensitive, and steady as an ash beam.

Those hands had caught two thousand newborn souls as they emerged into the light; they had severed two thousand navel-strings; they had closed the eyes of the dying and held the truth before the King's Justice when the powerful sought to destroy the weak.

She took her basket under her left arm, lifted her frieze cloak, and stepped through the doorway into the morning air.

Outside in the lane, Bess's iron shoes clicked sharply against the frozen flints as the boy led the pony to the mounting stone.

Hannah stepped out into the winter dawn.

The air was keen, smelling of wet downland turf, crushed juniper, and the sweet wood-smoke of twenty cottage chimneys waking along the bourne. To the east, over the high rampart of the plain, the sky was flushing pale amber and rose, touching the ancient chalk barrows with morning light.

Hannah took the reins from the shepherd boy.

Her fingers were rough, calloused by thirty years of wind, reins, and hot water; the skin of her knuckles was weathered like old oak bark by the rains of Wiltshire.

They were the hands that had delivered two thousand souls into the light of the valley.

They were the hands that had cut the cord, bound the belly, washed the newborn limbs, and held the truth before the King's bench when the powerful sought to destroy the weak.

She placed her boot in the iron stirrup, swung herself up into the side-saddle, and settled her frieze cloak over her knees.

"Lead on, lad," Hannah said.

The gray pony stepped out with a willing, rhythmic stride, her iron shoes ringing sharply against the white chalk flints.

Beside the lane, the water of the bourne ran clear as glass over the dark green watercress beds, reflecting the amber light of the new year's sun. Ahead on the crest of the down, the ancient turf barrows rose like dark islands against the morning sky, their summits touched with gold.

A woman was waiting in the shepherd's cottage by the lower fold. The pains were coming fast; the kettle was on the hearth; and a new soul was knocking at the door of the parish.

Hannah gathered the leather reins into her steady, calloused hands.

The boy broke into a jog-trot along the turf, and Hannah touched the pony's flank with her heel, riding up the downland path into the sunrise.

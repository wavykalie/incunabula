# The Border Skeps

### Chapter 1

The smoke from the clay pot smelled of dried cow-parsnip and rotten alder, a thick, greasy reek that clung to the wool of Maren’s sleeves until her own skin tasted of it. She worked the small hand-bellows twice, squeezing the stitched pig-bladder between her palms, and watched the grey plume slide across the flight-board of the seventh skep.

It was mid-morning, though in the Glen of Orve mid-morning looked no different from dusk or dawn. The sun hung behind the stone lip of the high rim, a flat disc the color of lard, casting no shadows against the scree. In Orve there was only the frost-verge, the single long season that neither broke into green thaw nor deepened into black drift. The dwarf willows kept their woolly grey buds tight against the bark; the bitter-vetch along the runnels remained in its perpetual rust-red flower; the water in the sheep-troughs held a skim of needle-ice that melted by noon and froze again by dark. It had been so since the first stone of the boundary dyke was dragged into the gap, and the old folk said it would remain so until the bees forgot the names of the flowers.

The seventh skep was the oldest in the apiary, older than Maren, older than the stone bothy where she slept with the smell of tallow and dried mint. Its dome had been coiled from rye straw thick as a thumb, bound round with split bramble that had turned black and hard as iron wire over sixty turns of the year. Around its skirt ran a thick caul of dried cow-dung and wood-ash, daubed fresh every four weeks to keep the mountain slugs and earwigs from creeping under the bottom ring.

Maren knelt on the wet slate flag she had laid two seasons ago to keep her knees from the peat. She pulled the horsehair veil down from the brim of her felt hat, tucking the coarse skirt of it into the throat of her sheepskin vest. The veil was stiff with beeswax and sweat, smelling faintly of the clover honey she had strained during the last tithe. Through the dark mesh of horsehair, the world was cross-hatched into tiny gray diamonds, every stone of the ridge sharpened and slightly dimmed.

"Quiet now, little mothers," she whispered, the words humming against the wool at her throat. "Quiet for the iron. Only the knife, only the comb."

Usually, the smoke brought a dull, resonant purr from the heart of the dome, the sound of five thousand wings fanning together in sudden alarm as the workers gorged themselves on their capped stores against the threat of fire. Usually, the guards on the apron would rear back on their hind legs, their front tarsals lifted to test the air, mandibles clicking against the lip of the entrance hole before diving back into the warm, dark belly of the straw.

Today there were no guards on the apron.

Maren waited, the smoker resting between her shins, its iron snout leaking a lazy blue ribbon into the cold air. A single worker crawled out from the narrow slit at the base of the skep. Her wings were tattered at the margins, notched like the edge of an old sickle, and her abdomen was dull, stripped of the fine golden fuzz that marked the nurse-bees of the inner nursery. She dragged herself two inches along the slate, stopped, and turned in a slow circle. She did not raise her abdomen to release the lemony tang of the scent-gland; she did not challenge the smell of the smoke. She simply walked to the edge of the flagstone, tumbled over into the scree, and lay still among the lichen-encrusted pebbles.

Maren felt a small, cold twitch in the hollow beneath her breastbone.

She reached into her wicker basket and lifted the goose-wing. It was the left wing of an upland gander, dried with the primaries intact, stiffened with alum to form a broad, flexible sweep. With her right hand she took the bone-handled pry-knife—the one Corin had ground down from an old hay-scythe blade before he walked up the pass and never came down. The iron was narrow, curved like a goat’s horn, its edge polished bright from years of scraping propolis from the oak stands.

She slid the blade under the dung-crust at the skep’s rim. The seal broke with a dry, biscuit-like snap.

She did not lift the dome entirely. The wind off the Notch was too thin, too bitter; a sudden draft of that high mountain air could chill the brood in five minutes, turning the pearly white grubs grey and rubbery in their wax cradles. Instead, she tilted the straw bell back three inches, resting the wicker hoop against her knee, and blew a soft, steady breath through the veil into the dark underside.

A colony smelled of its health. When a hive was strong, when the queen was dropping two thousand eggs between one twilight and the next, the skep breathed like a hot oven full of fresh malt and summer pollen. The heat rose in a visible waver through the straw crown, carrying the sharp, vinegar-bite of royal jelly and the clean, resinous tang of larch-propolis.

The seventh skep smelled of dry hay and dust.

Maren leaned closer, her temple pressed against the rough wicker of the skirt. She tilted the hand-mirror she had borrowed from her aunt’s chest—a disc of speculum metal no larger than a silver crown, tied to a split hazel lath. She angled it into the slit beneath the hanging combs, catching the flat light of the sky and bouncing it up between the curtains of wax.

The combs hung in seven parallel waves, anchored to the hazel skewers that Corin had driven crosswise through the straw when they rebuilt the stand three autumns back. The outer combs, the heavy walls of amber wax that should have been sealed from rim to crest with the dark, greasy honey of the frost-heather, were patchy. The cappings had been torn open, ragged and dry, chewed back by workers desperate for sugar. The cells beneath were empty, dark hollows like the windows of an abandoned stone shieling.

She turned the lath, angling the speculum deeper, toward the central brood-nest where the cluster should have been packed tight as a fist around the nursery.

The cluster was there, but it was no larger than a child’s ball of yarn. It clung to the third comb, a knot of listless, shivering bees whose wings hummed with a thin, piping note—not the deep church-organ rumble of a prosperous house, but the high, dry rattle of dry grass in the wind.

And in the center of the comb, exposed to the cold reflection of the mirror, the brood cells were bare.

There were no eggs. There were no tiny white crescents floating in droplets of royal jelly like rice grains in cream. There were no capped cells of dark, velvety wax hiding the pupae. There was only the yellow, empty comb, the hexagonal mouths open to the cold, dry and polished until the bottom plates caught the mirror’s gleam.

Maren slid the mirror out. Her hand was shaking slightly, the hazel lath tapping against the rim of the skep with a sound like a beetle in the wall.

"Where is she?" she muttered.

She reached in with two fingers, bare despite the cold, their skin scarred with the yellow pits of a hundred old stings. Her fingertips brushed the fringe of the cluster. The bees did not strike. They were too cold, or too tired; they merely stirred, their legs reaching out to cling to the rough wool of her cuff, seeking the heat of her pulse.

She parted the knot of workers gently, her thumbnail easing between their bodies.

There, in the depression where the hazel skewer pierced the wax, walked the queen.

She was smaller than she had been when Maren last examined the combs at the turning of the sun. Her abdomen, once long and heavy as an almond, pointed like an iron bodkin and banded with rings of dark copper, was shrunken, pulled up tight beneath her wings. Her thorax was bare, the velvet down rubbed away by the press of six generations of workers until the dark chitin shone like a river stone. Her left hind leg dragged limp behind her, caught once in some forgotten crevice of the straw and withered to a dry thread.

She was walking over the empty cells, lowering her head into each one, her antennae trembling over the dry bases. She would pause, test the hexagon with her front legs, then turn to press her abdomen down into the cup.

She held the posture for five seconds, ten. Her wings twitched with an effort that set her whole body trembling.

Then she withdrew. The cell remained empty. She moved to the next, dragged her withered leg across the ridge of wax, and lowered her head again.

"Barren," Maren said. The word was swallowed by the wool of her veil, but it felt loud, monstrous, in the silence of the pass. "The seed is spent."

A queen could live five years if the winter was kind and the robbers were kept from the gate. This one had lived seven. Corin had brought her down from the high limestone screes when Maren was nine years old, carrying her in a box of alder-bark lined with damp moss, sworn that she was the daughter of the wild bees that nested in the clefts where the road ended. She had outlived two colonies of workers; she had survived the great smoke of the fifth year when the rot took the third and fourth skeps; she had laid the daughters that built the wall of stings across the Notch of Orve.

Now the vessel was dry.

Maren eased the skep back down onto the slate. She took a handful of damp clay from the bucket beside her knee and smoothed it over the broken seal, working it with her thumb until the seam was tight against the wind. But the clay would not mend what was broken inside the straw. Without eggs, there was no brood; without brood, there was no pheromone to hold the thousand wills of the hive into a single cord.

A queenless hive did not die quietly. It did not simply curl up in the bottom of the straw and turn into dry chaff.

First came the piping—the thin, screaming challenge of a mother who knew her house was falling. Then came the laying workers, the desperate, unfertilized daughters whose bodies tried to bear the weight of the mother, filling the cells with drone eggs that hatched into nothing but useless, hungry males with big eyes and no stings.

And then came the drift.

She stood up, her joints cracking in the cold air. The wind was coming from the south, off the great shoulder of Mount Callow that walled the western flank of the valley. It was a dry wind, smelling of scree and the dead sulfur that seeped from the fissures below the snow-line.

Fifty yards away, where the stone dyke ended and the mountain rose sheer into the grey cloud, stood the Gap.

It was no wider than a hay-wain. The road—the only road that had ever entered the Glen of Orve—ran between two limestone pillars that looked as though they had been split by an axe. Beyond those pillars, the land fell away into the Southern Reach, into the valleys where the old folk said the seasons turned like wheels, where trees lost their leaves and grew them green again, where men died of strange heats and rot that the cold of Orve never permitted.

No one had walked through the Gap since Maren was an infant, except the dead who were carried out on hurdles, and the three men who had been cast out for the blood-fine.

And Corin.

Maren walked the line of the apiary. There were twenty-four skeps in all, set in four rows of six on benches of rough slate supported by dry-stone piers. They faced the Gap like a battery of stone guns. The flight-boards did not face the sweet heather of the valley floor; they faced the narrow neck of the pass. Every bee that flew from this terrace was channeled by the prevailing wind straight into the gorge, a living screen of venom that hung across the path from dawn until dusk.

If a mountain goat wandered up from the southern scrub, smelling the sour grass of the high meadows, the guards took it at thirty paces. A man would not make ten. The venom of the high bees was not the mild prick of the low-country skeps; it was an oil that burned like grease in the wound, swelling the tongue until it choked the throat, blinding the eyes with water that turned to thick yellow crust before nightfall.

The bees were the gate. As long as the skeps were full, the pass was shut. The Bailiff did not need to keep watchmen on the ridge; the Elders did not need an iron gate with oak bars. The bees took no silver, they did not sleep by the fire, and they did not listen to the travelers who came up from the south with salt and iron to trade for wool.

Maren stopped at the sixteenth skep. It was a strong colony, heavy with bees, the entrance crowded with dark, hairy workers who were fanning vigorously, their wings a blur of cold mica.

She watched their flight.

They rose from the board in tight spirals, orienting against the grey sky, then dropped their heads to the north, beating back down into the valley toward the dwarf willow and the late-blooming ling. That was the work: three miles down to the peat-bogs, a load of brown pollen packed onto their corbiculae, and three miles back against the grade, their bodies heavy with water and nectar.

Then she looked back at the seventh skep.

A worker had taken off from the skirt. It did not spiral. It did not turn its head toward the green floor of the valley.

It rose six feet into the dead air, caught the draft that sucked through the notch between the limestone pillars, and turned south.

Maren watched it. Her eyes were sharp, trained since her sixth year to track the flight of an insect against the dull slate of the cliffs. The bee was a dark grain of rye against the cloud, drifting steadily over the stone dyke, over the boundary marker where the three crossed chisels were carved into the rock, over the empty road where the wagon-ruts had long since filled with fallen shingle.

It did not come back.

Two minutes later, another followed it. Then three together, flying low along the scree, their bodies carried by the current of air that poured through the Gap like water through a breach in a mill-dam.

They were not foraging. They were not seeking the bitter-vetch of the valley benches.

They were drifting.

The stone bothy sat fifty paces behind the apiary, tucked into a notch in the cliff where the overhanging rock kept off the worst of the shingle-slides. It had no chimney, only a hole in the sod roof that let out three parts of the smoke and kept the fourth to season the rafters.

Maren stripped off her veil and hung it from a peg by the door. She took the water-piggin from the bench and poured three fingers of cold water into a wooden noggin, drinking it in two swallows. Her mouth was dry from the alder-smoke.

On the table lay the things she had brought back from the lower terrace yesterday: a ball of hemp twine, a lump of sheep-tallow for greasing the skep-hoops, and a small wooden tally-stick notched with the counts for the autumn harvest.

The tally was short.

The valley expected seventy stones of dark comb from the border stands at the turning of the sun. The Bailiff took thirty for the church at Low-Marrow, where the candles had to burn night and day before the salt-cured bones of the first settler; the Elders took twenty for the cellar stores, where it was mixed with crushed rye and salt to keep the beef from greening; the rest went to the household-heads according to the number of hearths they kept.

If the seventh skep failed, there would be no seventy stones. The seventh was the mother-skep; from its swarms the other stands had been replenished for three generations. The other twenty-three were its daughters and granddaughters, their queens bred from its cells, their hum tuned to its note.

She picked up the small bone knife from the table. It was three inches long, the handle wrapped in waxed thread to give a purchase for cold fingers. Near the pommel, two letters had been cut with a hot needle: *C. V.*

Corin Vance.

He had been nineteen when he went. Not driven out; not called by the Elders. One morning the bothy door had been standing open to the fog, his woolen mantle gone from the peg, his skinning knife gone from the beam. He had left his beekeeper’s veil—the one made of brass wire that their grandfather had carried from the east—folded neatly on the hearthstone with a flat river-pebble resting on top to keep it from the wind.

Beneath the pebble had been three hazel-nuts, their shells cracked and empty, and a dead worker-bee with its wings spread out flat.

In the village they said he had gone mad from the altitude. They said the poison from forty stings had settled in his kidneys and curdled his wits until he thought the pass was an open door to a great hall full of gold and meat. The Bailiff had come up the road with three men carrying iron-shod staves, their faces greased with goose-fat and their hats bound with sheepskin against the guards, to ask if she had seen him.

She had told them no.

They had searched the bothy, kicking over her stacks of dry heather, looking for silver he might have stolen from the tithe-chest, but they found only her jars of propolis and her bundles of smoker-punk. Before they left, the Bailiff—a thick-shouldered man named Orde whose family held the water-mill at Low-Marrow—had stood on the flight-terrace, looking through the Gap with his hand shading his eyes from the glare of the ice-fields.

"He won't make three miles," Orde had said, spitting into the scree. "The road beyond the Notch is gone. Slipped into the canyon fifty years back when the high shelf gave way. If the bees didn't take him in the neck before he reached the pillars, the crows had his liver by noon."

"He had the smoke-wax," Maren had said then. She had been thirteen, her voice still thin and piping like a starling's.

Orde had turned on her, his little grey eyes wet in his fat face. "Then he’s a thief of the parish. That wax belongs to the church. If he comes back through that Notch, Maren, he doesn't get the bread and salt. He gets the iron collar and the pit under the gatehouse."

Corin had not come back.

Three years had passed, each one indistinguishable from the last—the same frost-verge, the same grey sky, the same seventy stones of comb cut from the skeps with the goat-horn knife. The valley stayed behind its walls, eating its salt beef and oats, spinning its coarse grey wool, burying its dead in the frozen gravel of the churchyard where the picks rang like anvils against the flint.

And now the seventh skep was drifting.

Maren walked back out onto the terrace. She did not take the veil. The cold wind bit at her ears, turning the lobes numb, but she wanted to feel the air against her skin. She wanted to know which way the current was moving through the Notch.

She climbed the low stone breastwork that marked the outer boundary of the stands. Here the road began its final rise, fifty yards of broken gravel that ended between the two limestone pillars.

At the foot of the west pillar, three bees were crawling over a patch of orange lichen.

She knelt beside them. They were not dead; their abdomens were pumping slowly, three beats to the second, the rhythm of insects trying to keep their thoracic engines above forty degrees so their wings could bite the air.

One of them turned toward her boot. On its yellow legs, packed into the stiff bristles of the pollen baskets, were two pellets of dust.

They were not the brown pollen of the ling. They were not the grey-white dust of the willow.

They were a bright, burning scarlet, the color of fresh blood on snow.

Maren leaned so close her breath clouded the stone around the bee. She extended the tip of her forefinger. The bee, sluggish with the draft that poured between the pillars, crawled onto her nail, its small claws catching in the groove where she had split the horn with an axe-haft last winter.

She lifted the finger to her face.

The pollen had a smell. It was faint, almost lost beneath the stink of the lichen and the sheep-dung on her boots, but it was there: sharp, resinous, sweet as crushed elderberries and hot sun.

There was no scarlet flower in the Glen of Orve. There had never been one. The plants of the valley were small, grey-leaved things that clung to the crevices, blooming in pale lilacs and dirty whites that looked like frost until you knelt to touch them.

The red dust had come from the other side.

The bee shuddered, lifted its wings, and tried to take flight from her fingernail. It rose two inches, stalled in the southern draft, and dropped back into her palm. It lay on its side, its legs kicking weakly against her calloused skin, until the cold of her hand overcame it and it curled into a tight, dark knot.

Maren looked up at the space between the pillars.

Beyond them, the clouds hung lower than usual, a belly of purple vapor that seemed to rest on the very stones of the track. But through a tear in the mist—no wider than a horse’s eye—she saw, for three heartbeats, a long rib of green timber that did not look like dwarf willow. It looked tall, dark as pine-pitch, and far below it, where the valley floor should have been, the light was not grey.

It was yellow, like the edge of a new comb held up to a tallow candle.

Then the mist closed. The wind sucked back toward the south, carrying with it a faint, distant sound that might have been the rumble of an avalanche three ridges away, or the low, continuous murmur of water falling into an abyss that had no bottom.

Behind her, down the long track that led to Low-Marrow, an iron bell began to toll.

It was the harvest-bell from the church tower, three leagues away, its sound carried thin and clear by the frost-air. Three strokes, then a pause, then three strokes more.

The Elders were assembling at the Tithe-Barn. The Bailiff would have his scale out, his iron weights set on the grease-cloth, his ledger bound in sheep-parchment open to the page marked *Vance*.

They were waiting for the seventy stones.

### Chapter 2

The road down from the Notch was four miles of loose shale and frost-riven flagstones that had not been dressed since the road-wardens were disbanded forty years ago. Maren walked it with the pack-frame strapped to her shoulders, the broad leather tump-line resting across the crown of her felt hat to take the weight off her neck.

On the frame were three firkins of clear run-honey, each one sealed with tallow and bound with split ash hoops, and four round loaves of yellow wax she had rendered in her iron pot over the peat fire. It was less than half of what the ridge owed for the first cutting.

The boy, Kester, met her at the Sheep-Dip, a mile above the first stone enclosures. He was fourteen, the son of the valley’s tanner, with hands stained the dark, walnut-brown of oak-gall and a pair of trousers made of two different hides, one sheep and one goat, stitched together with raw sinew.

He was sitting on a dry-stone dyke, tossing pebbles at an old horned ewe that was cropping the moss between the boulders. When he saw her coming, he slid off the wall and fell into step beside her, his wooden clogs clattering against the loose slate.

"The Bailiff’s got his boots on," Kester said, without looking at her face. He kept his eyes on her basket, where the corner of a clean linen straining-cloth showed beneath the lid. "The black ones with the copper buckles. The ones he only wears when someone’s going in the pit or someone’s getting the three-day irons."

"The ground is hard enough for black boots," Maren said, her breath steady under the load.

"He’s got Elder Brant with him," the boy continued, his voice dropping to that half-whisper the village folk used when they spoke of the Council. "And Brant brought the bail-stick. The one with the brass notches for the honey-tithe. My father says the church cellar is three casks down from last winter because the frost got into the north wall and cracked the bungs."

"The bees don't pay for cracked bungs," Maren said.

"They'll pay for something," Kester muttered. He reached into his pouch, took out a handful of dried service-berries, and offered them to her on a dark, leather-calloused palm. They were shriveled and tasted of sour bark, but she took three and rolled them under her tongue for the moisture. "My father says you haven't brought the fourth firkin down since the feast of St. Jude. He says the border’s getting cold."

Maren stopped. The tump-line shifted against her hat, the weight of the wax-loaves pressing her heels into the grit.

"What does your father know about the border?"

"He knows the hides," Kester said, defensive now. He looked down at his mismatched knees. "The goats from the high sheep-runs aren't coming down with the red-scab anymore. He says when the pass was shut tight, the scab stayed on the other side. Now the sheep on the southern commons have got the yellow discharge in their eyes, same as they had in his grandfather’s time, before the skeps were set."

"The wind changes," Maren said, but her hand went unconsciously to her apron pocket, where the dead worker-bee lay wrapped in a scrap of greasy fleece. "The wind brings the scab, not the bees."

"He says the bees are sleeping."

"The bees don't sleep," she snapped. "They sit on the comb and they wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For you to stop talking," she said, and leaned into the tump-line again.

The village of Low-Marrow sat in the crook of the valley where the two runnels joined to make the Marrow-Water, a gray, glacial stream that ran no deeper than an ox’s hock over beds of black gravel. The houses were all of a piece: low, long-backed barns of dry limestone, their roofs thatched with water-reed that had turned the color of an old kettle under the perpetual frost. No house had a window that looked toward the south. Every wall facing the Notch was blank, built of stones three feet thick, banked to the eaves with turf and sheep-dung to keep out the draft that pulled through the Gap.

The Tithe-Barn was the largest building in the parish, twice the height of the church itself. Its rafters were made of whole larch trunks dragged down from the old forests before the cold settled into the stones, blackened by two hundred years of peat-smoke from the hearth in the floor.

When Maren pushed through the low oak wicket, the smell hit her: old tallow, damp wool, dried mutton, and the sour, vinegar reek of thirty men who had spent five hours in their winter cloaks without opening a shutter.

The Elders were sitting on the High Bench behind the salt-table.

There were seven of them, all old men with beards like lichens, their eyes watering from the smoke of the brazier that stood between their knees. Elder Brant sat in the middle, his cloak of undyed wool pinned at the shoulder with an iron thorn. He was eighty, perhaps older; his flesh had shrunk against his skull until the veins on his temples looked like the hazel skewers Maren drove through the skeps.

Beside him stood Bailiff Orde. He had not taken off his felt cap, and his heavy, pig-like jaws were working on a plug of dried angelica-root.

"Sixteen," Orde said as Maren came into the circle of the brazier’s light. He did not look at her face; he looked at the three firkins as she swung them off the pack-frame and set them on the flagstones. "We counted sixteen days to the first delivery. You're four days behind the bell, girl."

"The weather on the ridge was high," Maren said. She kept her hat on, as beekeepers were permitted to do before the bench, but she unhooked the tump-line and let the frame lean against her knee. "The frost-ling didn't open until the moon turned. The nectar was water; if I’d taken it then, the honey would have soured in the firkin before the salt-meat was packed."

Orde grunted. He stepped forward, his black boots squeaking on the greasy floor, and kicked the smallest of the firkins with his toe.

"It sounds light."

"It's seventy pounds to the vessel," she said. "The weight is the same as the spring cut. You have the stone on your beam."

The Bailiff took a triangular iron auger from the table and drove it through the bung of the first firkin with a single downward heave of his shoulder. He pulled the tool out; the grooved iron was coated in a thick, dark syrup that did not run. It was the color of pitch-pine, smelling intensely of heather-rot and musk.

He brought the auger to his nose, inhaled with a loud, snorting hiss, then touched his tongue to the groove.

"It has the bitter," he said, turning his head to spit into the brazier. The coals hissed where his saliva struck. "The true border-bitter. The priest won't like it for the altar-bread, but it’ll keep the lard from turning grey." He looked down into her basket. "Where is the fourth?"

Maren did not drop her eyes. "There is no fourth."

A silence fell over the bench. Elder Brant leaned forward, his hands, yellow as tallow, gripping the knobby head of his ash-stick.

"The border stand is twenty-four skeps, daughter," the old man said. His voice was like two slates rubbing together in a brook. "Twenty-four skeps have given four firkins at the frost-turning since the covenant was sworn with your grandfather. Not three. Not three and a piece. Four."

"The seventh is queen-dead," Maren said.

The words seemed to drop into the brazier like cold stones. Two of the elders on the left end of the bench—men who held the upper sheep-runs along the western screes—pulled their cloaks tighter across their chests and looked at each other under their grey brows.

Bailiff Orde took the plug of angelica from his cheek and held it between his fingers.

"Queen-dead?" he said. His voice had lost its flat, official boredom; it had a sharp, raspy edge. "The old hive? The one on the stone pier?"

"The queen is there," Maren said, "but her seed is spent. She drops no grubs. The combs are empty from the crown to the hazel-pins. The cluster has shrunk to the size of a goose-egg."

"Then she must be replaced," Brant said mildly, though his knuckles were white against his stick. "A brood-comb from the fifth skep. Cut a queen-cell from the third. You know the craft, girl. Your father had it; your brother had it before he went light-headed."

"There are no queen-cells," Maren said. She spoke slowly, wanting every word to strike the oak bench before them. "It is not the season for swarming. You know that, Elder. The sun has not climbed two fingers above the Notch since the mid-winter fast. The bees will not draw a royal cradle in the cold. If I put a cell from the third skep into the seventh, they would chew the wax to grease and drag the larva to the flight-board."

"Then let it go down," Orde said. He waved his hand toward the door, as though dismissing a stray dog. "Let the straw rot. You have twenty-three stands left. Divide the fourth firkin among them for the spring tithe."

"You don't understand," she said.

"We understand the tithe, girl," the Bailiff said, his face mottling with dark red beneath his grease. "We understand that the church needs thirty stones of wax to burn before the martyrs while the snow sits on the passes, and the parish doesn't pay a beekeeper to come down to the barn with empty buckets and fairy-tales about old queens."

"The bees are drifting," Maren said.

The silence that followed was different from the first. It was the silence that came over the valley when the sheep began to run in circles on the common before a mountain-slide—not a pause in speech, but a cessation of breath.

Elder Brant stood up. He was so thin his woolen robes hung from his collarbones like blankets from a fence, but his eyes were wide, clear, and cold.

"Drifting?" he whispered. "Where are they drifting, Maren?"

Maren reached into her pocket. She took out the scrap of fleece, unwrapped it on the palm of her hand, and stepped up to the salt-table. She laid the dead worker-bee on the bare oak, right beside the Bailiff’s iron auger.

The two red pellets of pollen on its thighs caught the red glow of the charcoal brazier. They looked like drops of sealing-wax against the grey grain of the timber.

"They are crossing the Notch," she said.

One of the younger elders—a man named Garrow who owned the lime-kilns at the foot of the cliffs—reached out a hand to touch the bee, then jerked his fingers back as if he had seen an adder in the thatch.

"That's not valley-dust," Garrow said. His voice was thin. "That's not ling."

"It's thistle," Maren said. "Great thistle, such as grows in the bottoms where the rivers run broad and the mud doesn't freeze until the moon of the dead. It's from the South."

"She brought it back?" Brant asked. His eyes were fixed on the tiny red specks. "The insect went beyond the three chisels? Past the bone-cairn?"

"I found three on the west pillar this morning," Maren said. "Two were dead of the cold draft. This one lived until I took it in my hand. And more went after it. I watched six go over the scree while the harvest-bell was ringing."

"Then the gate is down," Garrow whispered.

"The gate is not down!" Orde barked, though he did not look at the bee. He looked at the rafters, at the iron hooks where the bacon hung in smoke-bags. "The other twenty-three are on their stands. The flight-boards are crowded. I was on the sheep-track three days ago; the noise from the ridge was like a mill-wheel."

"The seventh is the mother-skep," Maren repeated, her patience thinning to a sharp thread. "The others are its seed. If the mother goes queenless, her hum goes sour. The bees on the other stands don't know the border by the stone markers; they know it by her. If she stops calling them back to the bench, the lines break. In ten days, the twenty-three will be drifting after her."

Elder Brant sat down again. His old knees gave a dry crack beneath his robes. He looked down at his boots, where the tallow was crusted thick around the welts.

"We must burn it," he said.

Maren felt her throat tighten until she could scarcely draw the peat-smoke into her lungs. "Burn it?"

"Burn the seventh," Brant said. His voice was steady now, heavy with the weight of forty years of village councils. "Take the sulfur-pot up the road before the sun goes down behind Callow. Set the sulfur under the skirt, seal the entrance with turf, and let them sleep. Then take the straw and the comb and the bench-pins down to the scree and put the fire to them."

"You can't burn the mother-skep," she said, her voice rising. "If you burn the old combs, the smell of the sulfur will lie on the ridge for two moons. The other hives won't work the Notch with the reek of brimstone in the rock. They'll pull back to the valley benches. They'll leave the road wide."

"The road is better empty than broken," Orde said. He had picked up his auger again, turning it between his thick fingers. "If the bees are going south, something is pulling them. If they go south, something can come north."

"Nothing comes north," Maren said.

"Your brother went south," the Bailiff said.

The words were spoken without heat, but they hit her like an iron hammer.

Maren did not move. Her hand remained resting on the edge of the salt-table, three inches from the dead bee.

"Corin is dead," she said.

"Is he?" Orde leaned across the table until she could smell the sour angelica on his tongue. "Then who’s tending the fire on the other side? Who’s planting the red thistles in the gap? Three years we haven't seen a crow on the Notch road, and now the border hive goes barren and the bees fly straight to the old sheep-gate like they were called by name."

"He had no bees with him," she said. "You searched the bothy. You know what he took."

"He took the craft," Brant said, his head still bowed over his knees. "The Vance blood has always had the craft. Your grandfather could call a swarm out of a limestone cleft without a pot or a veil; he did it when the Great Sickness was in the valley, and the pass stayed shut while five parishes died of the black throat on the other side. If Corin is living in the Southern Reach, he knows what a queenless skep means. He knows that when the seventh drifts, the road is open."

"He wouldn't do that," she said, but the memory of the dead bee on the hearthstone—the one left under the river-pebble with its wings spread out—flashed through her mind with the brilliance of grease on a fire.

"We take no chances with the pass," Brant said. He looked up at the Bailiff. "Orde. Take two men from the malt-house. Take the brimstone cakes from the cellar and the three iron spades. Go up with her."

"Now?" Orde asked.

"Before the dark comes off the mountain," the old man said. "The church bell will ring the passing-knell for the hive. When the smoke is up, let the parish know that the tithe is closed for the season. We will eat the dry beef without the honey-cure this winter. Better to eat lean than to sleep with an unbarred door."

Maren stepped back from the table. She caught the pack-frame with her left hand, her fingers digging into the ash wood until the splinters pricked her skin.

"If you burn her," she said, "you will never have another queen from that blood. The low-valley bees are soft. They die if the frost sits on the flight-board three days together. You'll have no border by mid-winter."

"We will have what the Lord gives us," Brant said, closing his eyes against the smoke. "And we will have what the fire keeps out."

### Chapter 3

The two men Orde brought from the malt-house were brothers named Brand and Lucan. They were big, thick-necked men with the pale, floury skin of maltsters, their shoulders bowed from carrying hundredweight sacks of germinated barley from the steeping-vats to the drying-kilns. They smelled of sour mash and wet dog, and neither of them looked at Maren as they climbed the road toward the Notch.

Brand carried the sulfur-can: a battered iron pot with holes punched in the lid and a long wire bail. Lucan carried the two spades and a bundle of dry ash faggots dipped in pine-pitch to start the blaze.

Bailiff Orde walked between them, his heavy walking-pole striking the slate with a rhythmic, metallic *tink-tink-tink* that sounded like a smith working a small anvil.

The wind had shifted. It was no longer coming out of the south; it had stalled, hanging dead in the throat of the valley like water in a clogged gutter. The cold had sharpened. The puddles in the track were no longer ice-crusted; they were solid blocks of grey flint that did not crack under the weight of Orde’s boots.

Maren walked ten paces ahead of them. She had left her pack-frame at the tanner’s house, carrying only her veil and her smoker.

In her apron pocket, her hand was clenched around the small bone knife.

*They will kill them all,* she thought. *Not just the queen. The sulfur doesn't pick. It takes the nurse-bees, the wax-builders, the water-carriers. It takes the combs that Corin shaped with his own fingers.*

She remembered the summer they had built the seventh skep. She had been nine, Corin twelve. Their father was already two years in the gravel behind the church, his lungs filled with the grey stone-dust from the slate-quarry, and their mother had spent her days by the hearth, spinning flax until her fingers bled and her mind wandered back to the eastern valleys where she was born.

Corin had taken her up to the high screes, above the line where even the dwarf willow could find a root. They had spent three days sleeping under a tilted limestone slab, hunting for the wild nests in the deep fissures.

He had found the mother-cleft by the flight-line. He had caught three bees in an elderberry horn, dusted them with flour from their mother’s sack, and let them go one by one, timing their return with his pulse.

"See that, Maren?" he had whispered, his hair wild and full of dried moss. "They don't fly to the valley. The valley’s too sweet for them; the clover makes them fat and lazy. They fly to the slate. They like the cold, because the cold keeps the honey from running out of the cells."

He had reached into the cleft with bare arms, his skin white against the dark rock, and had drawn out the wild comb piece by piece, ignoring the stings that covered his forearms until they were swollen like loaves of dough. And in the very back of the dark cleft, where the limestone stayed dry as bone through the long frost, he had found her: the great, dark queen, moving slowly across a disc of virgin wax no larger than a child’s palm.

He had not put her in a cage. He had held her in his cupped hands against his chest, under his shirt, letting her walk against the skin over his ribs while they climbed down the scree to the apiary.

"She’s our wall, Maren," he had said, his eyes bright with the fever of the stings. "As long as she’s on the ridge, the Elders can't sell the sheep-runs to the Low-Marrow men. They have to come to us for the wax. They have to give us the salt-meat and the meal, because if we don't hold the pass, the valley isn't a valley anymore. It's just a pen."

Now the maltsters were behind her, their clogs heavy on the shale, carrying the sulfur that would turn that dark, wild blood into yellow froth.

They reached the Sheep-Dip. The horned ewe was still there, huddled against the base of the wall, its fleece grey with road-dust.

Orde stopped to catch his wind. His face was purple, his breath coming in whistling gasps through his thick nostrils.

"How much further to the stand, girl?"

"A mile," Maren said, not stopping. "Less if you don't drag your staves."

"Wait," Orde barked.

She turned. The Bailiff had rested his hand on Brand’s shoulder, his small eyes squinting up at the ridge.

The mist had not lifted from the Notch, but it had turned a strange, greasy yellow, the color of an old sheep’s tooth. The two limestone pillars stood out against it like black iron teeth.

And around the top of the west pillar, something was moving.

It was not a bird. It was not a cloud of midges; the cold was four degrees below freezing, too bitter for any fly of the valley to lift its wings.

It was a column, thin as a chimney-rope, that twisted and bent in the dead air. It rose thirty feet above the rock, looped back on itself like a cast line, then dropped into the shadow of the cleft where the road disappeared.

Lucan, the younger maltster, stopped and crossed himself with his spade-handle.

"What's that?" he said. "That's not smoke."

"It's bees," Maren said. Her heart gave a great, sickening leap against her ribs.

"A swarm?" Orde’s voice was hoarse. "You said they don't swarm in the frost."

"They aren't swarming," she said.

A swarm was a festival. A swarm was the excess of life, the old mother taking half her children to find a new house in the hollow oaks while the young queen sang in her cradle. A swarm moved with a deep, heavy roar that shook the air, smelling of honey and beeswax, hanging in golden clusters from the boughs like bunches of grapes.

This column was silent. It was thin, ragged, moving with the frantic, jerky flight of beasts that had been driven from their beds by water or wolves.

"They're clearing," Maren whispered.

"Clearing?" Orde took two steps toward her, his stick raised as if he would strike her. "What does that mean, clearing?"

"The seventh," she said, her voice dead. "She’s left the comb."

A queen never left the comb. Not while she lived, not while there were three workers to feed her the drop of water and the crumb of bee-bread. If she was dying, she crawled down into the dung-crust at the bottom ring and lay with her face to the wood until the cold took her.

Unless the hive was broken from within.

Maren did not wait for the Bailiff. She caught the skirt of her sheepskin vest in her hands, hitched her coarse wool skirts above her knees, and ran.

The shale slipped beneath her boots, the sharp edges of the slate cutting through the leather of her toes, but she did not feel it. The tump-line had left her neck stiff, her shoulders aching, but she threw her weight forward against the grade, her breath tearing at her throat like dry straw.

Behind her, she heard Orde shout—a coarse, angry bellow that was swallowed almost instantly by the mountain—and then the heavy, clattering scramble of the two maltsters trying to follow her with the spades and the sulfur-pot.

She passed the third marker-cairn. She passed the spring where the water fell over the limestone shelf into an iron trough that was now a block of white ice.

The smell reached her before she rounded the shoulder of the cliff: not the sweet, greasy reek of the apiary, but a smell she had never known on the ridge.

It was the smell of wood-smoke.

Not alder. Not dried cow-parsnip.

It was the smell of dry oak and sweet resin, the clean, hot smoke of a fire that had been built with seasoned logs from the deep valley bottoms.

She turned the corner of the rock and came out onto the flight-terrace.

The twenty-four skeps stood in their rows, their straw domes grey against the limestone cliff. The twenty-three were quiet, their aprons empty, their bees huddled deep inside against the sharpening frost.

The seventh skep was overturned.

The straw dome lay on its side against the dry-stone dyke, three yards from its slate bench. Its dung-crust had been smashed to grey powder; the hazel skewers were broken, their splintered ends sticking out through the rye straw like the ribs of a dead sheep.

The combs had been cut.

They lay on the flagstones in the dirt: seven great slabs of amber wax, split and trampled. The honey was running from the torn cells, thick as grease, spreading across the cold slate in dark, sluggish pools that were already skinning over with frost.

And the bees—thousands of them, tens of thousands—were gone.

The terrace was empty save for a few hundred crippled workers that were crawling over the broken wax, their wings glued to the stone by their own honey.

Maren dropped to her knees in the middle of the wreckage. Her hand went down into the spilled comb; the wax was still warm. Not with the heat of the fire, but with the heat of the cluster that had been torn from it not ten minutes ago.

"No," she breathed. "No, no, no."

She turned the broken straw bell over with her bare hands. The inside was empty. The queen was not there. The withered mother who could not lay an egg was gone.

Beside the slate pier where the skep had rested for sixty years, something was lying in the dust.

It was not a stone. It was not a piece of broken hazel.

It was a small bundle of dry mountain-heath, bound round with a strip of wool no wider than a finger.

The wool was dyed.

It was not the dirty, unwashed grey of the Low-Marrow fleeces. It was not the yellow-brown of the elderberry dye the village women used for the church cloths.

It was a bright, harsh red—the color of madder-root, such as the weavers in the South used for the cloaks of the merchant-guards.

Maren picked it up. The wool was coarse, stiff with salt and dried grease, but as she brought it to her face, she caught the scent beneath the sheep-stink.

It smelled of birch-tar and old leather.

And on the strip of red cloth, tied into the knot with three turns of waxed thread, was a single feather from the left wing of a gander.

She knew the feather.

She had cut it herself, three autumns ago, from the wing she had used to sweep the snow from her father’s grave. She had given it to Corin the night he sat by the hearth with his skinning knife, whittling the box of alder-bark that he said was for his fishing-lines.

"He was here," she whispered.

She looked toward the Gap.

The thin column of bees had vanished from the top of the west pillar. The yellow mist had rolled down from the Notch, swallowing the boundary marker, swallowing the road, creeping across the broken scree until it reached the edge of the flight-terrace.

Through the mist, fifty paces up the grade where the limestone walls drew together into the throat of the pass, came the sound of a shoe striking stone.

Not the heavy, wooden clatter of a valley clog.

The soft, dull thud of an iron-shod heel, moving fast, moving south, away from the smoke and the salt-barn and the seventy stones of tithe.

### Chapter 4

"He broke the stand," Bailiff Orde said.

He was standing over the ruined skep, his boots planted in the spilled honey. The yellow sulfur-can sat on the slate between his legs, cold and unused, the wire bail swinging slightly in the draft from the Gap.

Brand and Lucan had laid their spades across the stone dyke. They would not touch the broken combs. They stood three paces back, their faces slack with that dull, bovine terror that came over the valley folk whenever the boundary stones were moved or an old grave was turned up by the plow.

"He didn't break it," Maren said. She had not let go of the strip of red wool; it was tucked deep into the palm of her hand, hidden inside the cuff of her sheepskin vest. "A bear could have done this. A rock from the high shelf."

Orde reached down with his walking-pole and hooked the lip of the broken straw dome. He flipped it over with a wet, sucking sound as the honey tore from the slate.

"A bear doesn't use an iron knife, girl," he said. He pointed the brass tip of his pole at the cross-hazels.

The cuts were clean. The wood had not been snapped by a beast’s paw; it had been severed with three smooth, diagonal strokes of a thin blade, the work of a man who knew exactly where the pins were driven through the straw.

"And a rock doesn't carry the queen away in an alder-box," the Bailiff added. He looked at her, his eyes small and wet under the brim of his cap. "Where is she, Maren?"

"Dead in the shale," she lied. "Trod into the dirt when the stand fell."

"Search the road," Orde told the maltsters.

"Bailiff—" Lucan began, his voice shaking.

"Search the road!" Orde roared, turning on him with his pole raised. "He can't have made twenty rods in this fog! He’s on the Notch! Take the spades and bring him down!"

The two brothers looked at the yellow mist that hung between the limestone pillars. Neither of them moved.

"There's no road beyond the chisels, Bailiff," Brand said, his throat working. "You know that yourself. The cliff went down in our fathers' time. It's three hundred cubits of sheer wall into the Black Sunk. If a man goes into that fog, he doesn't come back on his feet."

"He went," Orde said, his face dark as a beet. "He came up from the south and he took the mother-queen, and you stand there like two bullocks in a ditch while the parish gate is carried off in his pocket!"

He shoved Brand aside, caught up one of the iron spades himself, and started toward the Gap.

Maren did not think. She did not look at the maltsters. She sprang from the terrace, her boots catching the gravel of the road, and passed the Bailiff before he had taken five strides.

"Stay back!" she cried.

Orde swung the spade at her, but his footing was bad on the loose shale; the iron blade clattered against a boulder, throwing up a shower of orange sparks.

"Get out of the track, Vance!"

"You can't go through!" she shouted, turning to face him at the boundary cairn.

The three chisels were three feet behind her, carved into the face of the east pillar at the height of a man’s chest. Beyond them lay the three miles of road that no one had walked—the road that the old folk said was guarded not by men, but by the hunger of the stones.

"He has the queen," she said, her chest heaving against her sheepskin vest. "If you go after him with iron, the bees will take you. The other twenty-three are waking. Look at the terrace!"

Orde stopped. His chest was rattling like an old cart, the sweat running down his grey cheeks despite the sharpening cold.

He looked back over his shoulder.

The terrace was no longer quiet.

From the twenty-three skeps, a low, black smoke was rising. It was not smoke from the sulfur-pot; it was bees.

They were pouring from the flight-boards in thousands, tumbling over one another in their haste to reach the cold air. They did not fly toward the valley; they did not seek the heather benches. They were rising in a great, dark cloud that hung over the stone piers, their wings producing a sound like the roaring of a furnace when the dampers are pulled wide.

The smell of the broken honey from the seventh skep had reached them.

Robbing.

It was the terror of every beekeeper: the madness that took an apiary when honey was spilled on the ground. When the stores were breached, the laws of the stands ceased to exist. The guards left the aprons; the nurses left the brood; the foragers forgot their flight-lines. Every insect became a thief, tearing at the combs of its neighbors, fighting until the flight-boards were piled six inches deep with the dead, their bellies swollen with stolen sugar.

And behind the robbing came the search for the mother.

The air on the ridge was full of them now. They struck Maren’s sheepskin with the force of thrown peas, their small bodies bouncing off the wool, their stings digging into the leather of her sleeves.

A worker struck Orde full on the cheek.

The Bailiff let out a high, womanish squeal and dropped the spade. He clapped his hand to his face, but another took him on the knuckle of his thumb, and a third tangled itself in the grey wool of his cap, its abdomen pumping frantically as it drove its barb into his scalp.

"Lucan!" Orde screamed, stumbling backward down the track. "The sulfur! Light the sulfur!"

"It's no use!" Brand shouted from the terrace. He had thrown his coat over his head, crouching against the base of the dyke. "They're on the stands! You can't burn twenty-three skeps!"

The Bailiff did not wait for the maltsters. He turned and fled down the road toward Low-Marrow, his walking-pole abandoned in the shingle, his hands beating at his head as the bees followed him in a furious, whining cloud.

Brand and Lucan followed him, tumbling over the scree, their clogs striking the rocks in a frantic, irregular rhythm that died away down the valley.

Maren was left alone at the Notch.

She did not pull her veil down. She stood by the east pillar, her hands open at her sides, letting the bees strike her.

They knew her. Or rather, they knew the smell of her clothes—the sixty seasons of propolis and birch-smoke that were ground into her sheepskin, the wax that was crusted under her fingernails. They crawled over her cheeks, their cold feet tickling her lips, their antennae testing the salt on her skin. Two stung her—one on the lobe of her ear, one on the soft skin between her thumb and forefinger—but she did not flinch. The pain was an old neighbor, sharp and hot for three breaths, then settling into a dull, familiar throb that made her blood feel thick and slow.

She looked through the space between the pillars.

The mist had not cleared, but the sound was different now.

The bees that had risen from the twenty-three stands were not all fighting over the spilled honey of the seventh.

Hundreds of them—thousands—were peeling away from the cloud over the terrace. They were rising thirty feet into the dead air, catching the current that sucked through the Gap, and turning south.

They were following the trail of the mother-box.

They flew in a long, dark ribbon that stretched into the yellow fog, their bodies carried by the draft, their wings humming that thin, frantic note of daughters who have lost their house and have nothing left to lose.

Maren took the strip of red wool from her cuff.

She looked at the knot, at the gander’s feather that had been preserved in salt and grease for three years.

*He didn't come to break the valley,* she thought. *He came to fetch her.*

Corin had known that the queen was barren. He had known that the seed was spent, that the Elders would bring the sulfur-pot to the ridge the moment the harvest-tithe fell short. He had lived in the bothy long enough to know how the Council thought: if a thing cannot work, burn it; if a pass cannot be held, wall it with stone.

He had taken her because she was the only queen left that knew the road through the rock.

Even barren, she carried the scent. The workers would follow that scent through the fog, over the slipped shale, down into the valleys where the seasons turned, until the last bee fell cold into the grass.

And when they were gone, the twenty-three skeps on the ridge would be nothing but straw and dead grubs. The pass would be open. Not because the bees were dead, but because there would be no bees left in the Glen of Orve.

Maren looked down at her boots.

The honey on the terrace was already freezing, turning into a dull, waxy crust that would be covered with needle-ice before the parish bell rang for the third watch. If she went down to Low-Marrow, the Elders would be waiting in the Tithe-Barn. They would have the iron collar out. They would say she had helped her brother steal the church’s wax; they would say she had opened the gate to the South.

They would put her in the pit under the gatehouse until the spring, and when they brought her out, the valley would be walled with stone and the bees would be a story told to children by the peat-fire.

She turned her back on the Glen of Orve.

She stepped past the boundary cairn. She stepped past the three chisels carved into the limestone rock.

The road beyond the Notch was narrow, no wider than two spans of a man’s arms. On the right, the wall of Mount Callow rose sheer into the yellow cloud, its limestone fluted like the pillars of the church; on the left, the dark fell away into a gulf that had no sound of water, only the cold, dry draft that smelled of moss and strange wood.

The shale was wet with the mist. And there, pressed into a patch of red clay between two frost-riven slabs, was the print of a boot.

The heel was narrow, shod with an iron plate that had three notches filed into the rim.

Her father’s boots. The ones that had been missing from the bothy floor the morning Corin went.

Maren pulled her hat down tight over her brow. She reached into her basket, took out her smoker and her bone pry-knife, and left the basket itself lying by the east pillar, its clean linen cloth fluttering like a white flag in the draft.

She did not look back.

She followed the dark line of bees that hung like a thread of black wool in the yellow air, moving south along the shelf of the mountain into the country where the frost had no name.

### Chapter 5

The road did not end at the three chisels, but it ceased to be a road.

For three hundred paces beyond the Gap, the shelf held its width, a ledge of dark bituminous limestone carved out of the mountain's flank by men who must have had iron tools harder than any the Low-Marrow smith could lay to the coal. The chisel-marks were still visible where the rock had been sheltered from the south-wind: broad, shallow scoops, three inches across, their edges as sharp as if they had been struck yesterday.

Then the ledge broke.

The slide that Elder Brant had spoken of was not a recent ruin. The rock-face had cleaved along a vertical joint, five hundred cubits of stone falling away into the chasm below, leaving only an irregular seam of scree that clung to the cliff like swallows’ nests under the eaves of a barn.

Maren stopped. Her boots were two inches from the lip.

Below her lay nothing she could name. In the Glen of Orve, the world had bottom: the grey gravel of the river-bed, the peat-hags, the stone foundations of the barns that sat four-square on the rock. Here, the mist was not a blanket laid across the fields; it was a sea that filled a gulf three leagues wide. Through its tears she saw neither water nor fields, but the tops of great trees—not the gnarled dwarf willows of the sheep-runs that never grew higher than a man’s hip, but dark, pointed spires that rose out of the grey belly of the cloud like spears stuck in a marsh.

The air was different.

It did not taste of the frost-verge. It was warm—not with the heat of an oven or a peat-brazier, but with a heavy, moist breath that smelled of rotting leaves, wet bark, and something else that made her throat ache with a strange, sweet nausea.

It was the smell of soil.

In the Glen of Orve there was no soil, only peat that had been dried and burned until it was grey ash, and the cold sand that washed down from the glaciers. Here, the rot was living. It rose from the depths like the steam from a brewer’s vat, carrying on its back the high, thin humming of the bees.

She looked along the cliff.

The bees were not flying across the void. They were keeping close to the rock, their line hugging the seam of broken stone that skirted the precipice fifty feet below the original shelf.

And there, wedged into a cleft where a dwarf cedar had found a purchase in the lime-grit, was a marker.

It was not a cairn of dry stones. It was a hazel hurdle, three feet long, woven from five green withes that still had their silvery bark intact.

Hazel did not grow on the ridge. To get hazel of that thickness, a man had to go down to the lower coppices of the Marrow-Water, where the Elders guarded every rod with iron-tipped staves.

Corin had cut these withes before he left the valley. He had carried them on his pack-frame, cured them with sheep-tallow, and driven them into the rock with an iron spike to make a bridge where the shelf had fallen.

Maren knelt on the ledge. The rock was slick with the condensation that was gathering on her sheepskin, turning the wool dark and heavy. She swung her legs over the lip, her heels searching for the first hazel withe.

The hurdle held. It gave an inch under her weight, the green wood groaning against the iron spike, but the split ash ties did not part.

She climbed down, her face six inches from the limestone wall.

The stone here was not the pale, cold marble of the valley; it was veined with red iron-stone that bled rusty water into the fissures. In the cracks, where the wind could not reach, grew tiny clumps of a weed she had never seen before: a fleshy, three-lobed leaf that was covered in fine, silver hair like the down on a newly-hatched gosling.

A bee was resting on one of the leaves.

It was a worker from the seventh skep. Her wings were flat against her back, her tongue extended to drink the red moisture that seeped from the iron-stone. She did not move as Maren’s fingers brushed the rock beside her; she was gorged with the mineral water, her abdomen swollen until the black rings showed the pink, stretched membrane between the plates of chitin.

"Drink, then," Maren whispered. Her voice was lost in the vast, hollow murmur of the gulf. "It's a long way to the bottom."

She dropped from the hurdle onto the lower seam of scree.

The track—if it could be called a track—wound south along the contour of the massif, descending by long, diagonal traverses across the limestone ribs. As she went lower, the yellow mist thinned, shredded by a breeze that blew steady and cool from the west.

The light changed.

It was no longer the dead, lard-colored glare of the mountain. It had a golden, amber quality that made the wet rocks look as though they had been smeared with honey. High above her, through the breaks in the vapor, the sky was not grey; it was an intense, piercing blue, a color so bright it made her eyes water after sixteen years of looking at the low clouds of the Callow.

At the fourth traverse, she found the smoker.

It was lying in the middle of the path, where the gravel had been trampled by a heavy tread. It was not her clay pot; it was an older vessel, made of hammered copper with a perforated snout, its leather bellows mended with two patches of green hide.

Her father’s smoker. The one that had hung from the main beam of the bothy since the year the snow did not melt from the churchyard until the midsummer bell.

The pot was still warm.

Maren picked it up. She lifted the conical lid; inside lay a cake of dried punk that was still smoldering at the center, a red eye of fire that breathed a thin, fragrant wisp of white smoke into the amber light.

The fuel was not cow-parsnip.

It was dried fungus—the great, woody brackets that grew on the trunks of old oaks in the deep forests, sliced thin and soaked in saltpeter to make them burn without a flame.

Beside the copper pot lay three dead bees.

They had not been stung to death; they had not died of the frost. They were smeared with a sticky, dark brown paste that smelled of walnut-oil and crushed mint.

*The guiding-paste,* she thought, her fingers touching the oily smear on the copper snout.

Her grandfather’s book—the three leaves of sheep-parchment that were kept in the oak chest beneath her cot—had spoken of it. *If a swarm be wild and will not follow the pipe, take the oil of the green nut and the juice of the mountain-balm, and smear the snout of the pot therewith; for the bees do love the bitterness of the nut above the sweetness of the rose, and will follow the smoke of it through three leagues of wood.*

Corin had not run like a thief. He had walked this path with the copper pot in his hand, puffing the walnut-smoke into the air every fifty paces to draw the line behind him.

He was not fleeing the valley.

He was leading the hive.

She stood on the shelf, holding her father’s smoker in one hand and her bone knife in the other, and looked down into the basin below.

The trees were no longer spears; they were a forest.

It stretched from the foot of the limestone cliffs as far as her eye could reach, a great rolling carpet of green, gold, and russet that broke against the mountain like the surf of a sea she had only heard of in the old songs. Through the center of the woods ran a river—not a thin, glacial runnel like the Marrow-Water, but a broad, brown road of water that looped across the valley floor in great, lazy curves, flashing silver where the sun struck the shallows.

And on the near bank of that river, two miles below the line where the limestone gave way to the black earth, rose a thin, blue column of smoke.

It was not the smoke of a charcoal-burner’s heap. It was not the greasy smudge of a village hearth.

It was the clean, straight smoke of a cedar-wood fire, rising from the clearing where the road from the Gap came down to the water.

And straight toward that smoke, flying five yards above the tops of the dwarf cedars, ran the black thread of the bees.

### Chapter 6

The descent took three hours, every step taking her deeper into an air that felt too thick to breathe. It was heavy with moisture, warm as fresh milk, pressing against her chest until she had to unbutton her sheepskin vest and let the cold wind from the high ridge dry the sweat that ran down between her shoulder-blades.

The mountain did not stop suddenly. It frayed into long, sloping spurs of red shale that were covered in a thick scrub of hazel, dwarf oak, and briars that tore at her woolen skirts with thousands of tiny, curved claws.

Here grew the red thistles.

They stood in the runnels between the spurs, great branched plants six feet high, their stems covered in silver wool and their heads as large as a man’s fist, crowned with a brush of stiff, crimson bristles that burned in the sunlight like coals.

The workers from the seventh skep were all over them.

They were not drifting now. They were working with a frantic, desperate energy, their bodies buried up to their wings in the crimson tubes, their legs already thick with the blood-red dust that had terrified the Elders in the Tithe-Barn. The hum was no longer the thin piping of the pass; it was the deep, resonant roar of an apiary that has found a pasture after three weeks of rain.

Maren did not stop to watch them.

She kept her eyes on the path. The boot-prints were clearer now, stamped deep into the soft, red loam that had replaced the slate. Corin was not running; his strides were long and regular, the marks of his staff set two feet to the left of his heel with the steady cadence of a man who has thirty miles behind him and ten ahead.

At the edge of the timber, where the first great oaks began to cast their shadows across the track, she found the hive-stand.

It was not made of slate flags.

It was a platform of split cedar logs, three feet above the ground, supported by four stout posts that had been driven into the earth and charred at the base to keep the ants from climbing.

On the platform sat a skep.

It was not made of rye straw. It had been coiled from long, flexible ropes of mountain-grass, bound with bark from the wild cherry tree, its dome broader and flatter than the high stands of Orve.

The entrance-slit was crowded.

Hundreds of bees were fanning on the cedar apron, their heads turned toward the dark interior, their abdomens lifted to the amber light. And from the hole came that smell—that lost, forgotten smell that Maren had not known since she was a child of seven.

The smell of a queenright house.

Maren dropped her pack-frame into the briars. She walked toward the stand with her hands empty, her veil hanging down her back, her feet making no sound on the deep carpet of moss.

A figure was sitting on an oak log behind the platform.

He was wearing an old shirt of bleached linen, its sleeves rolled up to his elbows, and a pair of trousers made of dark green wool that had never been spun in the Glen of Orve. His hair was long, tied back with a leather thong, his beard trimmed close to his jaw with a small shear.

He was holding an alder-bark box between his knees, scraping the inner rim with the curved point of a knife.

He did not look up when her boots brushed the grass.

"You're four days behind the bell, Maren," he said.

His voice was lower than she remembered, rougher around the edges, like the sound of an iron shovel in gravel, but the turn of the phrase was their father’s.

Maren stopped five paces from the log.

"The Bailiff brought the black boots," she said.

Corin looked up then.

His eyes were the same: that pale, clear grey that looked like the ice on the sheep-troughs, with the dark ring around the iris that their grandfather had called the bee-mark. But his face had lost the thin, sharp hunger of the high valley. His skin was brown, burned by a sun that did not hide behind the rim of the mountain, and across his left cheek ran a white furrow where an old sting had festered and left its mark.

He laid the knife across the alder-box.

"Orde always had a liking for those boots," he said softly. "Did he bring the maltsters?"

"Brand and Lucan," she said. "With the sulfur-can."

Corin nodded. He did not seem surprised. He reached down, picked up a clean wooden noggin from the grass, dipped it into an earthen pitcher that sat in the shade of the log, and held it out to her.

"Drink," he said. "The water in the valley tastes of iron. This is from the hazel-spring."

Maren took the noggin. Her hand was steady, but her fingers were cold against the clay. She brought it to her mouth; it was sweet, cold, smelling of green leaves and limestone, without a trace of the peat-sourness that clung to every drop of water in Low-Marrow.

She drank it to the dregs, then wiped her mouth with the back of her sleeve.

"The queen is barren, Corin."

"I know," he said.

"She hasn't dropped an egg since the midsummer fast. The cells were dry to the crown. The cluster was no bigger than an apple."

"I know that too," he said. He stood up from the log. He was half a head taller than he had been when he left the bothy, his shoulders broad and square under the linen shirt. He walked to the grass skep, leaned over the apron, and listened for five breaths to the hum that rose through the cherry-bark binding. "She gave her last swarm three years ago, Maren. In the cleft above the bothy. The day I went."

Maren felt her breath catch in her throat.

"You took her seed?"

"I took three queen-cells," he said. He did not look at her; he looked at the great wall of green oaks that stretched away toward the river. "In the alder-box, with twenty workers to keep them warm. I carried them down through the slide before the Bailiff was out of his bed. Two died of the damp in the chasm. One lived."

He tapped the side of the grass skep with his knuckle.

"This is her daughter," he said. "The second generation. She’s been laying three hundred eggs a day since the hazel-catkins opened. She has twelve combs inside, every one drawn down to the wood, full of clover-honey and drone-brood."

"Then why did you come back for the old one?" she asked, her voice shaking with the anger that had been gathering behind her ribs since she found the broken pins on the terrace. "Why break the stand? Why leave the twenty-three to rot on the ridge? They were your father’s hives, Corin! You whittled the skewers with your own hands!"

Corin turned to face her. His grey eyes were very still.

"Because they were a wall, Maren," he said.

"They kept the valley safe!"

"They kept the valley in a pen," he said. He pointed his knife toward the high, grey rim of Mount Callow that showed between the oak boughs, three leagues away and two thousand cubits above them. "Look at it. It's a trough. A ditch with a lid of cloud. For sixty years the Elders have told the people that the world outside was dead—that the Great Sickness took the towns, that the rivers were full of poison, that if a man walked past the three chisels his flesh would rot from his shins before he reached the timber."

He stepped closer to her, the smell of walnut-oil and pine-pitch coming from his shirt.

"The towns are there, Maren. They aren't dead. The river goes down to the Great Reach, where there are twenty parishes larger than Low-Marrow, with stone bridges and market-crosses and orchards that bear apples red as your hair. They have iron that doesn't split on the flint; they have wool that's as soft as down; they have salt that's white as snow."

"They have the sickness," she whispered.

"The sickness was sixty years ago," he said, his voice rising with that old, fierce energy that had made their mother weep by the hearth. "It came and it went, like the rot comes to the potatoes when the autumn is wet. But the Elders liked the wall. They liked having twenty-four skeps that kept the sheep-runs from the bailiff’s taxes. They liked having sixty families who couldn't buy a pound of salt without asking Brant for the silver."

"And the old queen?" she asked. "Why take her, if you have the daughter?"

Corin looked down at the alder-box on the log.

"Because she was the mother," he said softly. "The daughter has the blood, but she doesn't have the call. The bees of the high ridge wouldn't follow a strange queen into the South; they would have stayed on the stands until they starved, or until Orde put the sulfur to the skirts. The only thing that could draw the twenty-three across the Notch was the old one’s scent."

He reached into his pocket and brought out a small cage of split cane, no larger than his thumb.

Inside, resting on a bed of dry moss, was the old queen.

She was still alive. Her withered leg was tucked under her body, her antennae moving slowly over the wicker bars. Beside her were three worker-bees, their heads pressed against her thorax, grooming the bare, shiny chitin where the down had been rubbed away by sixty turns of the season.

"She won't last the night," Corin said. "The cold on the shelf took her in the joints. But her work is done. Look up at the spur."

Maren turned.

High above the timber, where the red shale met the limestone cliffs of the Gap, the sky was not empty.

A great, dark cloud was coming down the mountain.

It was not mist. It was not smoke.

It was the bees of the twenty-three stands.

They were descending in a vast, spiraling funnel that stretched from the Notch to the tops of the oaks, two leagues of living iron that hummed with a sound that shook the leaves of the dwarf cedars. They were not fighting now; they were not robbing. They had caught the scent of the mother-cage from the river-breeze, and they were dropping into the valley like the black hail of an autumn storm.

"They're leaving the ridge," she whispered.

"They're coming to the pasture," Corin said. He laid his hand on her shoulder, his fingers warm through the thick wool of her vest. "The pass is open, Maren. The road is clear from the Gap to the water. In three days the sheep will be on the red thistles; in a week the boys will be down with the pack-frames."

Maren looked at the old queen in the cane cage.

The insect had turned its head toward the roar that was filling the clearing. Its small, notched wings gave a single, feeble flutter against the cane bars, then settled flat against its back.

"They'll have no wall," she said.

"They'll have the road," Corin replied.

He took the cane cage, walked to the grass skep on the cedar platform, and laid it gently on the apron, right in the middle of the flight-hole.

The workers from the new colony did not attack her. They did not raise their stings against the barren stranger.

They gathered around the cane bars in their hundreds, their tongues reaching through the wicker to offer the drop of clover-honey, their wings rising together into that deep, organ-roar of a house that has found its mother and has no more need to fear the cold.

Maren stood in the shadow of the great oaks.

She reached up, untied the horsehair veil from the brim of her hat, and let it drop into the red dirt of the path.

The wind from the river was warm. It smelled of earth, of water, of things that grew and died and were born again without asking the leave of the stone markers; and as the first of the high bees began to drop like dark fruit into the grass around her boots, she felt the needle-ice in her own blood begin to run like honey before the fire.

### Chapter 7

The cluster did not settle all at once.

For three days the woods below the red spurs were full of the roaring. It was not a single swarm, but twenty-three separate houses that had to be parted, coaxed, and housed before the night-damp came up from the river to chill the queens.

Corin had prepared twenty benches of split cedar along the river-terrace, each one sheltered from the south-wind by a hurdle of woven hazel and thatched with broad river-reeds. He had worked through two summers to coil the skeps—not of straw, which rotted in the humid bottoms, but of the tough, wiry mountain-grass that the river-people called *spear-sedge*, bound with strips of boiled ash-bark that held the coils tight as an iron hoop.

Maren worked beside him.

She had stripped off her sheepskin vest on the second morning, leaving it hanging from an oak bough where the tithe-grease and the old alder-smoke could air out in the sun. She wore only her coarse linen shift and her woolen kirtle, her arms bare to the shoulder, red with the sun and cross-hatched with the white welts of thirty stings.

The stings in the South were different.

On the ridge, the venom had burned with a dry, mineral heat that lingered in the bone for three days, making the joints click like dry sticks. Here, in the damp warmth of the valley, the flesh swelled soft and hot, itching with a fierce, prickling life that faded by dusk, leaving her skin smelling of crushed mint and river-mud.

"This is the fifth," she said, lifting a heavy grass bell by its top-knot.

The bees had covered the underside of the cedar bench in a black beard three feet long, their bodies linked leg to leg in that miraculous, living net that holds the weight of forty thousand insects without a strand parting. In the center of the beard, safe in the warm dark between two cedar slabs, hung the queen of the fifth stand—a young, yellow-banded mother that Corin’s wild line had thrown two seasons back.

She took the goose-wing.

It was her old wing, the one she had carried down from the ridge, its grey feathers stiff with alum. With three long, sweeping strokes she brushed the beard into the mouth of the grass skep.

The bees fell with a soft, heavy *thump* like a sack of wet grain, then rose in a cloud around her head. But they did not strike. They smelled the sweet cedar-pitch that Corin had rubbed into the crown of the dome; they smelled the drop of anise-water he had sprinkled on the flight-hole; and within five breaths, the fanning began.

Two hundred workers lined the rim of the skep, their heads down, their abdomens lifted high into the amber light, their wings a grey blur of motion as they pumped the scent of their new house into the air.

"They take to it," Corin said. He was kneeling on the next bench, driving an ash pin through the skirt of the sixth skep to pin it against the wind. "The grass holds the warmth better than the rye straw. In the winter here, the frost only sits on the grass for three weeks, between the moon of the wolf and the moon of the thaw. The rest is rain."

"Rain," she said, tasting the word.

In the Glen of Orve, there was no rain. There was only the dry, sifted snow that blew across the scree like flour from a hopper, and the grey mist that sat on the sheep-runs without wetting the wool.

"The mud is deep," he said, looking at her with a faint smile under his beard. "You'll have to learn to wear the leather pattens, Maren. The clogs get sucked off your feet in the bottom-pastures."

She looked out across the clearing.

The river was low, its black stones exposed to the sun like the backs of diving ducks. Along the margins, where the gravel met the loam, grew beds of wild mint three rods wide, their purple flowers so thick that the air smelled like a medicine-chest. Beyond them lay the meadows—not the mossy, stone-choked runs of Low-Marrow, but broad, flat plains of red clover and vetch that stretched away to the blue line of the southern woods.

And on the path that came down from the red spurs, three figures were standing.

They had come out of the timber an hour before noon, their shadows long and thin behind them on the shale. They had not come with iron staves; they had not brought the sulfur-can.

They were boys.

The first was Kester, the tanner’s son, his mismatched leather trousers greased with fat against the briars, his pack-frame loaded with three bundles of dried sheepskins. Behind him were the two sons of Garrow the lime-burner, carrying a sack of barley-seed between them on an ash pole, their faces pale and wide-eyed under their felt caps.

They had stopped at the edge of the scrub, thirty paces from the hive-stands, looking at the great, dark funnels of bees that were still circling the cedar benches.

Kester took two steps forward, his wooden clogs clattering against a boulder. He held his cap in both hands against his stomach, the way he did when he stood before the Bailiff in the Tithe-Barn.

"Maren?" he called.

His voice was thin, cracked by the three leagues of climbing and the terror of the chasm-shelf.

Maren laid down the goose-wing. She walked across the clearing, her bare feet sinking into the warm, damp turf, until she stood at the edge of the hazel-scrub.

"You're past the chisels, Kester," she said.

The boy looked down at his clogs, then up at the great, rolling forest that had no wall of cloud above its boughs.

"The Bailiff’s in his bed," Kester said, his voice dropping to that frightened whisper. "His face is swollen up like an ox-bladder from the stings. He can't see the light through his left eye, and the priest has been sitting by him with the oil-cruet since yesterday’s compline."

"And the Elders?"

"They went to the Notch," Kester said. He looked over his shoulder at the dark cleft in the mountain, two thousand feet above them, where the two limestone pillars looked like specks of charcoal against the blue sky. "They took the mason and the three stone-sledges from the quarry. They were going to build the wall across the road."

"Did they build it?" Corin asked, coming up behind her, the bone knife in his hand.

Kester looked at Corin’s bare arms, at the green trousers and the clean linen shirt, and his eyes grew wider.

"They couldn't get the sledges past the Sheep-Dip," the boy said. "The sheep were in the way."

"The sheep?"

"The wethers from the south commons," Kester said, a sudden, nervous grin breaking through the dirt on his cheeks. "Three hundred of them. They smelled the green scrub from the Gap, and they broke the hurdles. Old Brant was shouting at them with his staff, but they ran right over the sledges and through the Notch. They're on the red spurs now. My father told me to bring the skins down before the Bailiff got his boots back on."

He held out the bundle of hides.

"He wants the salt," Kester said. "The white salt from the river-towns. He says if the skins rot this winter because there's no salt-cure, he'll burn the tannery himself."

Corin laughed. It was a loud, clear sound that startled a pair of grey herons from the river-gravel, sending them flapping away across the red clover with their long legs trailing behind them.

"The salt is at the ferry," Corin said. "Two miles down, where the road crosses the silt. Tell your father he can have six bags for the three bundles, if the leather is sound and the grease is worked out of the flanks."

He turned to the lime-burner’s sons.

"And the barley?"

"For the malt," the older boy said, his voice trembling. "Our father says the parish can't brew with the cold water anymore. He says the malt won't work without the heat."

"Bring it to the clearing," Corin told them. "We have three empty sheds by the cedar-stand. You can sleep on the dry sedge tonight, and tomorrow we'll take the boat down to the market."

The boys did not move for three breaths. They stood looking past him at the broad, sunny water of the river, at the endless meadows of red clover that had no boundaries, at the bees that were dropping like golden sparks onto the mint-blossoms.

Then Kester took off his clogs.

He unbuckled the leather straps, pulled his feet out of the heavy, sodden wood, and stood with his bare toes dug deep into the warm, red earth of the South.

"It doesn't freeze," he said, his face full of a strange, childish wonder. "The dirt... it's soft."

"It's earth, boy," Maren said, her hand resting on the boy’s bony shoulder. "Go down to the river and wash the road-dust from your shins. The bees don't like the smell of old sheep-tallow."

That evening, when the sun had dropped behind the shoulder of Mount Callow and the great shadow of the mountain was creeping across the river-meadows, Maren walked back to the first cedar bench.

The clearing was quiet now.

The twenty-three skeps were settled, their flight-holes marked by a few lingering guards that were fanning the warm river-draft into the combs. In the clearing by the oaks, a small fire of cedar-wood was burning, its smoke rising straight and clear into a sky that was purple as an elderberry, speckled with the first large, white stars she had ever seen clearly.

From the shed came the sound of the boys’ voices, low and wondering, and the soft, rhythmic scrape of Corin’s knife as he mended an ash hoop for the morning’s work.

On the apron of the first skep lay the cane cage.

Maren knelt by the cedar log.

The cage was cold.

The three worker-bees that had been imprisoned with the queen were lying on their backs in the dry moss, their legs curled tight to their bellies, their wings stiff and grey.

And between them lay the old mother.

She had not died of the stings; she had not died of the hunger. Her abdomen was flat, shrunken to a dry thread of copper chitin; her eyes, once bright as black glass, were dull and sunken beneath the brow-plates. Her withered leg was stretched out behind her, pointing back along the path she had traveled—over the red spurs, through the chasm, past the three chisels and the stone pillars to the cold slate terrace where she had reigned for sixty turns of the sun.

She had held the pass until the last daughter was through the gate.

Maren did not take the knife to her. She did not reach into the cage with her fingers.

She lifted the cane box gently, carrying it in both hands down to the margin of the river, where the water ran quiet over a bed of fine, golden sand between the roots of an ancient willow.

She took her goose-wing from her belt.

With the bone quill, she dug a small, round hole in the damp sand, three inches deep, below the line where the winter-floods would reach. She laid the cane cage in the hollow, resting it on a bed of fresh mint-leaves that she crushed between her palms to give them their scent.

Beside the cage she laid the scrap of red wool—the piece Corin had tied to the mountain-heath—and the single feather from the gander’s wing.

"Sleep now, mother," she whispered, the old words of the ridge coming back to her tongue without her thinking. "The stone is set. The flight is done. The comb is full of honey and the gate is wide."

She covered the cage with the golden sand, smoothing it flat with the palm of her hand, then rolled a heavy, water-worn river-pebble over the place to keep the crows from scratching the earth.

The pebble was not slate. It was not flint.

It was a piece of red quartz, rounded by a thousand years of warm water, that caught the last light of the western sky and held it like an ember in the grass.

She stood up.

Across the river, the first night-moths were rising from the vetch, their great, pale wings fluttering like flakes of white paper against the dark trees. From the woods came the call of a bird she did not know—a long, liquid whistle that rose and fell three times before dying away into the murmur of the shallows.

Behind her, on the bench of split cedar, the young queen was piping.

It was not the thin, screaming challenge of a mother whose house was falling; it was the clear, trumpet-note of a daughter whose house was full, calling the foragers to rest before the sun came up over the clover.

Maren turned her face to the South, drew the warm, sweet air deep into her lungs, and walked back toward the light of the fire.

### Chapter 8

The valley of the river was called the Low-Reach, though Maren learned the name only when the pack-train from the market-town at St. Jude-in-the-Fens came up the cart-track five days after the sheep had broken the Gap.

The master of the train was an old man named Hobb, who had two fingers missing from his bridle-hand and wore an oilskin cap that had been greased with linseed until it looked like boiled horn. He had six mules, their pack-saddles loaded with iron pots, sacks of salt from the salt-pans at the estuary, and barrels of green wine that smelled of sour grapes and elder-bark.

He had stopped his lead mule at the edge of the cedar clearing, his mouth open so wide that his three yellow teeth showed like pegs in a rake.

"Vance," he had shouted toward the shed where Corin was splitting ash-laths. "What in the name of the seven bishops have you got here? The whole forest is buzzing like a brewer’s copper."

Corin had come out with his draw-knife in his hand, wiping his brow with his linen sleeve.

"The border hives," he said.

Hobb looked at the rows of grass skeps along the river-terrace, then up at the great, blue wall of Mount Callow that stood like an anvil against the northern sky.

"The border?" The old muleteer spat into the vetch. "You mean the pass is open? The Callow road?"

"Open enough for three hundred wethers and six sacks of barley," Corin said. "The boys are down at the ferry with the skins."

Hobb took off his oilskin cap and scratched his bald scalp with a split fingernail.

"The Bailiff didn't drop the iron portcullis?"

"The Bailiff was in his bed with three bee-stings in his neck," Maren said, coming around the corner of the shed with a crock of fresh-pressed mint-water. She did not wear the veil; her hair was tied back with a strip of willow-bark, and her face was burned the dark, reddish-brown of an acorn. "The portcullis was rotted out forty years back, master. There was only the sheep-hurdle and the three chisels."

The muleteer looked at her, his little, rheumy eyes taking in the bone pry-knife at her girdle and the yellow wax-stains on her kirtle.

"You'll be the sister," he said.

"I'm the beekeeper," she told him.

"Then you've brought a fortune down the rocks, mistress," Hobb said, climbing down from his mule with a groan that set his leather breeches creaking. "The wax in the towns has been three groats a pound since the Spanish war shut the sea-reaches. The monasteries at the fen-foot are burning tallow for the morning mass, and the prior at St. Jude says the saints are turning their faces to the wall for the stink of the sheep-fat."

He walked to the first bench, keeping three paces back from the flight-hole, his eyes wide as he watched the workers tumbling over the cherry-bark rim.

"Look at them," he muttered. "They aren't the yellow Italians from the coast. They're the great black ones from the high lime. They can work the thistle in the drizzle; they don't curl up when the autumn mist comes off the marshes."

"They don't like the horses," Maren warned him. "Keep your mules twenty yards down-wind, or you'll have three dead beasts before the bungs are out of your wine."

"They can have the whole river-bank," Hobb said, backing away with his hands raised. "I've seen the mountain bees work a dog once in the high pass. They had him blind in forty breaths."

He turned back to Corin, his face sharp with the trader’s squint.

"What do the Elders say to the wool?"

"The Elders haven't spoken," Corin said. "They're still on the sheep-runs, trying to count the wethers by the church-bell."

"They'll speak when the winter comes," Maren said, setting her crock on the cedar log. "When the frost sits on the slate at Low-Marrow and there's no forty stones of comb in the cellar to sweeten the salt-beef, they'll have to send the pack-sledges down the Notch or eat their oats with water."

"Let them send the sledges," Corin said. "We have twenty-four stands here that will give sixty stones before the clover turns. The road is six feet wide where the shelf was broken; Kester and the boys spent yesterday filling the gap with limestone rubble from the scree."

He looked at Maren, his grey eyes bright in the shadow of the oaks.

"It's not a wall anymore, sister," he said. "It's a market."

Maren did not answer.

She walked away from the men, down to the edge of the river where the gravel bar ran out into the shallows.

She stood on the water-worn stones, looking up at the high ridge.

From here, two leagues away and half a world below, the Notch looked small, no more than a notch in a woodman’s axe-haft. The great limestone pillars that had seemed like the gates of a prison when she was sixteen were only two teeth of grey rock sticking out of the mountain's side, half-swallowed by the white clouds that were already gathering for the winter frost.

Above the Notch, the sky was grey, flat, and cold, the color of an old iron kettle.

Below the Notch, the sun was shining on three leagues of green timber, on the red clover that reached to the horses’ bellies, and on the broad, brown water that was carrying the first cedar-raft down to the fen-towns with its load of mountain wax and sweet clover-honey.

A worker-bee flew past her ear.

It did not turn toward the high cliffs. It dropped down to the river-pebbles, hovered for three heartbeats over the red quartz stone that marked the old queen’s grave, then rose in a long, golden spiral into the amber light, its head pointed south toward the great orchards where the apples were turning red in the sun.

Maren took the small bone pry-knife from her belt.

She looked at the initials cut into the handle: *C. V.*

Then she took her stone from her pocket—the hard, black river-flint she had carried from the Marrow-Water—and began to scratch a third letter into the bone, right beside her brother’s name:

*M.*

The bone was hard, but her hand was steady, and as the shavings fell into the running water of the shallows, the sound of the twenty-four skeps behind her was like the singing of an army that has crossed the river and set its watchfires in the plain.

### Chapter 9

The first cart from Low-Marrow came through the Gap three weeks after the feast of St. Jude.

It was not a church-wain with ash-spokes and iron-shod wheels; it was an old timber-sledge that had been fitted with two birch axles and wheels cut from solid rounds of mountain larch, their rims bound with raw goat-hide that had shrunk tight in the frost. It was drawn by two horned oxen—small, black, shaggy beasts with eyes like wet sloes, their nostrils steaming in the thin air of the pass.

Elder Garrow walked at the near horn, his ash staff tipped with an iron point that he used to test the shale before every stride.

Behind him came his two sons, each carrying an iron crowbar on his shoulder, their boots wrapped in strips of sheepskin to keep the shale from cutting the welts.

Maren met them at the third traverse, where the road widened between the dwarf cedars. She was sitting on a dry-stone boulder, her smoker resting between her knees, a willow basket full of ripe medlars on the grass beside her.

Garrow stopped the oxen with a low, clicking sound in his throat.

He did not look at her face; he looked at the medlars. In the Glen of Orve, fruit was a thing found only in the old books that the priest kept in the aumbry—tales of green gardens where trees bore sweet meat that had no rind.

"They're soft," Garrow said, his voice dry as an old sack.

"They're ripe," Maren said. "They have to frost once on the bough before the pulp turns sweet. Take two for the boys."

The old man hesitated, his fingers gripping the ash staff. His woolen cloak was pinned with the same iron thorn he had worn in the Tithe-Barn, but the hem was tattered, crusted with the white salt of the high sheep-runs.

"The Bailiff didn't come," he said.

"We saw the black boots weren't on the road," she said.

"He can't walk the grade," Garrow said, taking the two medlars and pressing one into the hand of each of his sons. The boys took them like church-wafers, their grey eyes wide as they smelled the sweet, wine-like rot of the skin. "The third sting went into the nerve of his jaw. His mouth is pulled round to his ear; he speaks like an ox with a turnip in its throat."

"And Brant?"

"Brant is eighty-four," the elder said, looking down at the red mud that was already caking the larch wheels of the sledge. "He sits by the brazier in the barn and he says the world has fallen into the pit. He says the church candles are burning low and the martyrs will have no light by the new year."

"Tell him the wax is at the river," Maren said.

She stood up, swinging the basket onto her hip.

"How much did you bring?" Garrow asked.

"Ten stones of the first cut," she said. "The clear clover-wax from the river-benches. It's white as sheep’s milk; it burns without the alder-smell, and it won't grease the altar-cloths."

The elder looked down the spur toward the great forest. The timber was changing now: the oaks had turned the color of an old copper kettle, their leaves dropping in golden showers whenever the west wind blew from the river, while the wild cherries along the runnels were like torches of scarlet wool stuck in the grey rock.

"Is it true?" he whispered. "What the boys said about the salt?"

"Two sacks for every sledge of timber," she said. "White salt from the pans. The kind that doesn't turn the mutton grey."

Garrow let out a long, shuddering breath that seemed to come from the very bottom of his lean ribs. He reached up, touched the iron thorn at his shoulder, then took it off and dropped it into the willow basket beside the medlars.

"The church can have the white wax," he said. "And the parish will have the salt. The road is six feet wide, Maren. We spent four days on the shelf with the crowbars; a wain can pass now if the driver has an eye for the ditch."

"The bees are on the lower spurs," she told him. "Don't bring the oxen past the cedar-post until the sun goes down. They're working the ivy-bloom on the cliffs, and they don't like the smell of mountain-dung."

"We'll wait for the dusk," the old man said.

He leaned against the sledge-timber, his hand resting on the hairy flank of the near ox, and watched a worker-bee that had settled on the wooden hub of the wheel.

The bee was black, heavy-shouldered, its legs loaded with the pale yellow pollen of the wild ivy. It crawled three inches along the larch grain, tested a crack with its antennae, then rose into the warm, golden air, turning its head toward the high ridge where the clouds were already turning purple with the frost.

It did not go back to the Callow.

It turned in a broad, lazy circle over the sledge, caught the current of air that rolled up from the river-meadows, and drifted down toward the cedar clearing where the smoke of the twenty-four stands rose straight and true into a sky that had no end.

Maren walked beside the oxen, her hand on the leading-halter, her clogs clicking against the stones of the new road as the mountain-shadow fell across the valley, cold and clean, like the falling of a great curtain after a long play was done.
### Chapter 10

The sledge groaned as it settled into the black mud of the river-bank, its solid larch wheels sinking to the axle-trees before the oxen could heave it onto the dry grass of the terrace.

Kester and the two sons of Garrow unhitched the beasts, leading them down to the gravel spit where the Marrow-Water mingled with the brown river. The animals did not plunge their muzzles into the current as lowland cattle did; they stood knee-deep in the shallows, their shaggy heads lowered, testing the water with their lips as if expecting the needle-ice that had skinned every puddle in their home pastures since they were calved.

"They'll founder if they drink their fill of that warm wash," Elder Garrow said. He stood by the sledge, his hand resting on the rough bark of the birch timbers. His boots were sodden, the wool wrapped around his ankles grey with alluvial clay. "Mountain beasts have cold bellies. If you put river-water into them before the sweat dries, their intestines knot like hemp."

"Let them crop the mint first," Corin said, coming down from the cedar shed with an armload of dry sedge. "The mint warms the stomach. The muleteers from St. Jude give their beasts two handfuls of wild pennyroyal before they water them at the ford."

"You built large, boy," Garrow said.

"We built for thirty stones of wax and forty casks of honey," Corin replied, dropping the bedding beside the oxen. "And for the men who bring the timber down. A man can't sleep in his wet wool under an open sky when the autumn rains break."

"It's not a word here," Corin said. "It's three moons of rain and green buds that break until the woods are like an oven. He dipped a wooden spoon into the crock and handed it to Garrow.

The old elder took it with trembling fingers. He brought it to his nose, his nostrils quivering, then put the wood into his mouth.

### Chapter 11

The winter in the Low-Reach did not come with the iron hammer of the mountain frost; it came with water.

By the second week of the new moon, the clouds had rolled up from the southern estuary, great grey bellies of mist that hung in the oak tops and dripped day and night until the earth was a sponge of red mud. The river swelled, its clear brown shallows turning to a yellow torrent that carried whole trunks of mountain cedar down from the gorges, their roots clawing at the gravel bars like drowning beasts.

Maren had to learn a new season.

In the Glen of Orve, a beekeeper’s winter was stone: you sealed the skirts with cow-dung and ash, drove the wooden pegs deep through the hazel hoops, and left the skeps to the silence of the frost. The bees clustered so tight they were like a single wooden ball, burning their honey drop by drop to keep the core at forty degrees, their breath freezing into white needles on the inner straw until the whole dome was lined with rime.

Here, the cold was soft, but the damp was a rot that worked in the dark.

On the fifth day of the rain, Maren lifted the rear skirt of the tenth skep to inspect the floorboards.

The smell that came out was not the clean, resinous tang of larch-propolis; it was the sour, mushroomy reek of mold.

Along the edges of the cedar platform, where the drip from the reed-thatch had driven under the wood, a white, furry skin had spread across the combs. Two of the lower combs—empty store-cells that the workers had not yet filled—were green with fungus, their wax soft and cheesy under her thumb.

And on the bottom board lay twenty dead workers, their bodies covered in grey down, their heads swollen with water.

"Corin!" she shouted through the drizzle.

He was at the shed, shaving an ash plank with the adze to make a new hive-bench. He came across the mud in his bare feet, his linen trousers rolled to his thighs, the wood-shavings clinging to his wet arms.

"The mold," she said, pointing the bone knife at the green wax.

Corin knelt in the mud, his face grim. He reached in with two fingers, pinched off a corner of the infected comb, and brought it to his nose.

"The ground-mist," he said. "The air doesn't move under the oaks. In the mountain, the wind off the Notch cleared the skirts every three hours; here the wet sits in the grass like broth."

"We have to lift them," she said.

"Lift them?"

"Two feet higher," she told him, her mind working through the craft as her father had taught it. "The platforms are too low. The moisture comes off the loam at night and condenses on the underside of the cedar. If we don't put them on stilts, the mold will have half the colonies before the hazel-catkins open."

They worked for three days in the pouring rain, assisted by Kester and the three boys who had stayed behind when Garrow took the sledge back up the mountain.

They cut four-inch oak posts from the scrub-woods, charred their ends in the hearth-fire to keep the wood from rotting in the wet earth, and drove them three feet into the gravel terrace with a heavy beetle of green beech. On these they laid cross-bearers of split ash, raising the hive-platforms to the height of a man’s breast.

Then came the lifting of the colonies.

It was work that could not be done with clumsy hands. A single sudden jolt, a single dropped pin, could shatter the heavy combs inside the domes—combs that were soft with honey and heavy with thousands of sleeping bees.

Maren took the head of each skep herself.

She would slip her bare hands under the grass skirt, feeling for the cross-skewers through the thick sedge-coils, her wrists resting against the warm, humming belly of the colony. Corin and Kester would take the cedar bottom-board, sliding it inch by inch off the old posts and onto the new ash frame, while Maren held the dome steady against the draft.

The bees were restless.

They did not fly into the rain—the cold drops would have beaten them to the mud in ten yards—but they clustered on the flight-aprons in dark, agitated knots, their wings fanning against the wet air, their stings ready for the horsehair veil if she had worn it.

She did not wear it.

Her skin was so accustomed to the venom now that a sting on her forearm produced only a tiny white button that vanished by nightfall. The bees knew her sweat; they knew the smell of the birch-tar soap she used to wash her hair, and they knew the slow, steady rhythm of her breath.

By the fourth evening, all twenty-four stands were mounted on their high stilts.

Beneath the platforms, the wind from the river could move freely, sweeping the ground-mist away through the reeds before it could rise into the skirts. Maren had cut fresh ventilators in the crowns of the domes—small holes no wider than a hazel-nut, plugged with loose bunches of dried horse-mint that let the damp breath of the cluster escape while keeping out the mice and the great mountain-hornets that hunted the river-margins.

"Look at that," Kester said, pointing down toward the ford.

He was standing on the cedar bench, wiping the rain from his chin with the sleeve of his hide trousers.

A boat was coming up the river against the current.

It was not a mountain-raft of logs; it was a broad, flat-bottomed punt built of oak planks, its blunt bow pushed through the yellow water by four men with long ash poles. In the stern sat a man in a black woolen cloak with a hood that fell over his brow like a monk’s cowl, his hands tucked deep into a muff of grey hare-skin.

The boat bumped against the gravel spit, the pole-men driving their irons into the silt to hold her against the wash.

The man in the black cloak stood up. He was sixty, perhaps older, with a thin, sharp face the color of skimmed milk and a pair of spectacles with round brass rims that sat on his long nose, held by a cord of green silk looped behind his ears.

He looked at the twenty-four skeps on their high oak stilts, his spectacles flashing in the grey light.

"Who holds the apiary?" he called across the water.

His voice was thin, high-pitched, like the voice of a church-clerk, with that flat, nasal drawl that marked the people of the fen-towns.

Corin stepped to the edge of the terrace, his adze resting on his shoulder.

"The Vance freehold," he said.

The man in the cloak reached into his breast, brought out an ink-horn and a small roll of white parchment, and unrolled it with his thumbs.

"Corin Vance?"

"The Bailiff of Low-Marrow has filed a protest with the Court of the Marches," he said, reading from the parchment with a rhythmic, sing-song cadence. Corin spat into the river.

"The Bailiff can file his teeth on an iron file," he said. "The bees are of the high screes. Our grandfather brought them from the limestone fissures before the church was roofed with slate. There's no mark of the cross on their wings."

The clerk lowered the parchment. A faint, dry smile appeared at the corners of his thin lips.

"The Prior of St. Jude-in-the-Fens does not concern himself with the Bailiff’s teeth," he said mildly. "Nor does he believe that insects can be bound by an oath to a parish bench. But he does concern himself with the altar-candles."

He stepped from the punt onto the gravel, his leather shoes sinking three inches into the mud before he reached the firm sod of the terrace.

"I am Brother Paul," he said, bowing slightly to Maren. "Master of the Wax-House for the abbey of St. Jude. The muleteer Hobb brought two loaves of your clover-wax to our kitchen three days ago."

He reached into his sleeve, took out a small iron bodkin, and walked to the nearest skep. He did not ask permission; he did not look at the guards that were crawling over the cedar apron. He knelt, took a scrap of cold wax that had fallen from Maren’s pruning-knife, and drove the bodkin through it.

He held the point to his tongue.

"No tallow," he murmured. "No birch-resin. No bean-flour."

"We don't stretch the comb with flour," Maren said, her voice sharp. "That's a town-trick."

The monk looked up at her through his brass rims.

"The chandlers in the market-town have been stretching the church-wax with mutton-fat since the border was closed," he said. "The candles smoke like pitch-torches; they blacken the face of the Virgin until she looks like an old washerwoman, and the smell of the grease makes the novices faint during the midnight vigils."

He stood up, brushing the dirt from the hem of his black gown.

"The abbey will take all you can render," he said. "Seventy stones between now and the feast of the Purification. We will give you four silver pennies a stone, or two bushels of clean wheat and a barrel of salt-herring for every three."

Corin looked at Maren.

Four silver pennies was more money than their father had seen in ten years of the mountain tithe. In Low-Marrow, silver was a thing that belonged to the church chest; a common man traded in fleeces, cheese-cakes, and days of labor at the hay-harvest.

"We don't want the silver," Maren said.

The monk frowned, his spectacles slipping down his nose. "No silver? What does a beekeeper want, if not the King’s coin?"

"We want the iron," she said.

She stepped between him and the hive-stand, her hands on her hips, her linen sleeves rolled high above her scarred arms.

"We want three hundredweight of Swedish bar-iron from the town forge," she told him. "And twenty spade-irons with the sockets turned, and two dozen picks of tempered steel that won't curl their points on the limestone flint."

Brother Paul looked from her face to the high, cloud-wrapped Notch of Mount Callow that showed between the dripping oak branches.

"The mountain," he said softly.

"The road," she corrected him. "The parish has three hundred wethers on the red spurs, Master Clerk. If the winter comes hard, the sheep will have to go up or down. If we have the iron, the boys can clear the shelf so the wains can pass without dropping their axles into the chasm. If we have the iron, the valley isn't a ditch anymore."

The monk was silent for two breaths, his fingers playing with the brass wire of his glasses.

Then he nodded.

"Two hundredweight of iron," he bargained, the merchant’s blood showing through his habit. "And ten picks. And the rest in white salt from the pans."

"Two hundredweight and a half," Maren said. "And twelve picks. And you bring the boat to the gravel-spit twice a moon to fetch the loaves."

Brother Paul looked at the skep beside him.

A worker-bee, driven from the dome by the heat of the cluster, crawled onto the brass rim of his spectacles. It paused there, two inches from his grey eye, its wings fanning slowly, its antennae tasting the ink-horn that hung from his collar.

The monk did not flinch. He smiled, lifted a thin finger, and let the bee crawl onto his knuckle.

"She’s a mountain queen’s child, isn't she?" he whispered. "Large in the thorax. Long in the leg. She can carry thirty grains of pollen without dropping her grade."

"She’s from the seventh," Maren said.

"Then the bargain is struck," the monk said, and shook the mud from his gown as he turned back to the boat.

### Chapter 12

The town of St. Jude-in-the-Fens was three leagues down the river, built on a long ridge of gravel that rose above the grey reed-marshes like the back of a sleeping pike.

Maren made the journey on the abbey’s punt, sitting in the bow with six loaves of white clover-wax wrapped in clean linen between her knees. Corin stayed at the river-camp with Kester to tend the platforms; with the winter rains falling every third day, the ventilators had to be cleared of dead bees every morning lest the damp rot take the brood-nests.

It was the first time she had been more than four miles from the limestone dyke of the Notch.

The world seemed to open out like an unrolled hide. The cliffs that had hemmed her life since her cradle shrank to a low, blue wall on the northern horizon, until they looked no more terrifying than the stone walls of the sheep-pens at Low-Marrow. The river broadened, joining two other streams that came out of the southern forests, until the water was forty yards wide, brown and deep, dotted with the flat rafts of the timber-cutters and the small, wicker coracles of the fen-fishers.

And then came the bells.

In Orve there was only one bell—the church-bell of Low-Marrow, that cracked iron pot that rang for deaths, fires, and the harvest-tithe, its sound thin and dry as an old sheep-cough.

Here, the air was full of bells.

They rang from three different towers along the gravel ridge: deep, bronze roars that shook the water beneath the boat’s ribs, light, silver chimes that scattered like water-drops from the parish of the fishers, and the slow, heavy thud of the great abbey-bell that called the monks to the noon-meal.

The town smelled of things she had no names for.

It smelled of sea-coal, burning with a thick, yellow smoke that made her eyes water; it smelled of tanning-pits five times larger than Kester’s father’s yard, reeking of pigeon-dung and dog-grease; it smelled of fried fat, stale ale, and the sharp, vinegar-scent of ten thousand people living together in houses of brick and lath that leaned toward each other across narrow, filthy lanes.

Brother Paul led her through the Water-Gate, two of the abbey’s lay-brothers carrying the wax-loaves behind them on a wooden barrow.

The people in the lanes stopped to stare at her.

She knew she looked strange to them. She wore her mountain kirtle of undyed, grey wool, stiff with old sheep-grease and beeswax, her feet in wooden clogs that clattered like horses’ hooves on the round river-cobbles of the street. Her hair, red-gold and thick as a pony’s mane, was tied back with a strip of cherry-bark, and her skin was that dark, walnut-brown that only the wind of the high screes could burn.

"A border-girl," a woman with a basket of salt-eels muttered from a doorway, pulling her child back behind her apron. "From the cold ditch. They say they eat dried mutton and don't know the Lord’s prayer."

Maren did not look at her. She kept her eyes on the barrow of wax.

The Abbey Wax-House was a great vaulted cellar behind the south transept of the church, its walls built of yellow freestone that had been brought up the river in barges. Inside, the heat was like the inside of a baker’s oven.

Three great iron cauldrons hung over peat-fires in the stone floor, their contents bubbling with a slow, greasy *glop-glop-glop*. The air was thick with the scent of melted wax, tallow, and the bitter herb-dyes the monks used to color the funerary candles.

An old monk with a wooden ladle in his hand came out of the steam. He had no teeth, and his bare feet were calloused like the pads of a bear.

"From the mountain?" he asked Brother Paul.

"From the high hives," the clerk said.

The old monk took one of Maren’s loaves from the barrow. He laid it on a clean scrubbed board, drew a wire through it to split it in halves, and held the cut surface to the oil-lamp that hung from the vault.

The wax was pale, uniform, without a single streak of dark grease or speck of grit. It had the clean, velvety break of pure clover-comb, smelling so strongly of the river-meadows that the two novices who were skimming the scum from the tallow-pot stopped their work to inhale.

"It's virgin," the old man said, his voice cracked with age. "Not rendered from old brood-combs. This is the first drawing of a young queen."

He looked at Maren, his watery blue eyes full of respect.

"How do you keep the pollen-dust from the melt, daughter?"

"Three strainings," she said. "Through linen that has been boiled in rain-water with ash-lye. And we don't boil the comb; we float it on warm water and let the wax rise to the top while the dross settles into the peat."

"The old way," the monk murmured, nodding his bald head. "The way the Cistercians did it before the wars broke the valley-pacts. Now the chandlers boil the whole skep—straw, bee-bread, dead grubs and all—and skim it with horsehair sieves. It leaves the wax grey as an old clout."

He turned to Brother Paul.

"Take her to the cellar-master. She can have the Swedish iron. And give her two measures of the brown sugar from the London ship."

"Sugar?" Maren asked.

"For the early brood," the old monk said, tapping her hand with his wooden ladle. "A young queen in the South starts laying before the clover opens. If the rain sits on the woods for two weeks in the month of the thaw, the nurse-bees have no nectar to turn into milk for the grubs. A spoonful of the brown sugar boiled with water and mountain-mint will save ten thousand grubs from the stone-brood."

Maren took the piece of sugar he broke from a loaf on the shelf.

It was not honey. It was hard, brown, crumbling like dry clay between her fingers, but when she touched her tongue to it, the sweetness was clean, sharp, without the bitter wild-heather tang of Orve.

*Sugar,* she thought. *They buy sweetness from across the sea, and we lived on the salt-beef and the ling for sixty years.*

They went to the Abbey Store-House by the quay, where the iron from the north-country barges was stacked in heavy, black bars five inches wide and three fingers thick.

Brother Paul had two of the bars loaded onto the barrow, along with eight steel picks and a keg of the grey, coarse salt from the maritime pans.

While the lay-brothers were lashing the load with hemp rope, a man came down the stone stairs from the Prior’s lodging.

He was not a monk.

He wore a doublet of dark blue velvet, its sleeves slashed with white satin, and a gold chain that hung across his chest in three heavy loops. His beard was pointed, trimmed with sweet-smelling oil, and his boots were of soft Spanish leather that did not squeak on the flags.

Behind him walked two men with short hunting-bows of yew and iron caps, their sleeves bearing the badge of a silver falcon with a broken chain in its talons.

"Master Paul," the gentleman said, his voice smooth as cold oil. "The Prior told me I would find the border-carrier here."

Brother Paul bowed low, his spectacles bobbing on his nose.

"My Lord Malmes," he said. "This is Maren Vance, the daughter of the high apiary."

The gentleman stopped two paces from Maren. He did not look at her wooden clogs or her sheepskin-greased kirtle; he looked at her eyes, his own cold, dark, and amused.

"So," he said. "The Vance blood is down from the rocks."

"We hold the freehold," Maren said, her hand resting on the iron bar on the barrow.

"The freehold of what, mistress?" Malmes asked, smiling faintly. "A mile of loose shale and twenty straw domes? The Council of the Marches has looked at the rolls. The Glen of Orve was granted to the third Earl of Malmes in the reign of the second Henry, to hold by the service of one hawk and forty stones of white wax at the feast of St. Michael."

"The wax was paid," she said. "Seventy stones to the church at Low-Marrow."

"The church at Low-Marrow is an out-parish," the gentleman said, tapping his velvet hip with an ivory-handled riding-whip. "The tithe belongs to the see, and the see belongs to the Crown. For sixty years the Vance family has kept the pass shut with their black bees, keeping the King’s collectors from the sheep-runs and the lime-quarries."

"The pass was shut by the sickness," Brother Paul put in, his voice nervous.

"The sickness was dead before my father was breeched, Master Clerk," Malmes snapped, his smile vanishing. "The Vance family kept the pass shut because they were kings in a ditch. They had no taxes; they had no levies; they gave no men for the Scottish wars."

He stepped closer to Maren, the scent of lavender and horse-sweat coming from his clothes.

"Your brother broke the gate, girl."

"The bees broke the gate," she said, meeting his dark eyes without flinching. "The queen went barren. A barren hive doesn't hold a border."

"Then the road belongs to the Marches," Malmes said. "Tell your brother that the Warden’s men will ride the Notch before the spring seed is sown. We will have the sheep-tally from Low-Marrow, and we will have the tax on the lime-kilns. And if there are bees on the road when the horses come up, we have brimstone enough in the town stores to smoke forty leagues of mountain."

He turned on his heel and walked back up the stone stairs, his silver chain clicking against his velvet chest.

Brother Paul looked at Maren, his white face pale as curd.

"He means it, daughter," the monk whispered. "The Earl has three hundred riders at the castle in the marsh. He has been waiting for the pass to open since the old Duke died."

Maren looked down at the heavy iron bars on the barrow.

"He can bring his riders," she said, her voice flat and cold as the mountain-slate. "The Notch is six feet wide, Master Paul. And three hundred riders on the scree are only three hundred targets for the workers when the clover opens."

### Chapter 13

The road back to the river-camp was slow, the punt loaded to the gunwales with the iron bars, the salt-kegs, and three bushels of seed-wheat that Brother Paul had given them from the abbey’s private stores.

When Maren stepped onto the gravel-spit at the cedar-stand, she found the clearing crowded.

It was no longer three boys and an old elder.

There were twenty people from Low-Marrow sitting on the cedar benches under the oaks: women in grey woolen hoods with infants bundled in greasy sheepskins, old men with their hands wrapped in rags against the damp, and four of the quarry-men from the upper screes, their leather aprons stained with the white dust of the lime-pits.

They had brought five timber-sledges down through the Gap, each one loaded with the gear of a household: iron pots, spinning-wheels of split birch, bundles of salted dried mutton, and the small wooden chests where the family tallies were kept.

Corin was standing by the hearth, a wooden bowl of porridge in his hand, talking to a tall, raw-boned woman whose face was so like their mother’s that Maren’s heart stopped for three beats as she came up from the boat.

Her aunt, Judith Vance.

The woman had not changed since the morning Corin went: the same long, horse-like jaw, the same pale grey eyes that looked as though they had never seen a fire that was warm enough to thaw the marrow. She wore three woolen shawls pinned across her chest with an iron bodkin, and her wooden clogs were bound with strips of raw cow-hide to keep the soles from splitting.

"She’s back from the town," Judith said, not looking at Maren, but speaking to Corin as if Maren were still six years old and had come in late from the sheep-run. "Did she bring the meal?"

"She brought the iron," Corin said. "And the seed-wheat."

"Wheat," Judith spat, her breath grey in the damp air. "You can't eat seed-wheat in the month of the frost. The Bailiff has shut the church-cellar, Corin. He has two men from the malt-house sitting on the meal-chest with loaded crossbows. He says the village is under the interdict because the border-hives were stolen."

"The hives weren't stolen," Maren said, dropping her pack-frame by the hearth. "They came down on their own wings."

Judith turned to face her.

"The Bailiff says the priest won't say the mass for the dead until the seventh skep is back on its pier," the woman said, her voice shaking with that old, bitter fury of the high valley. "Old Mother Sula died three nights ago of the chest-rot. She’s lying in the turf-house on two boards, unburied, because the priest says the churchyard is unhallowed ground until the parish gate is shut."

"Then bury her in the loam," Maren said.

The woman stared at her as if she had spoken in the tongue of the Welsh cattle-thieves.

"Bury her in the South?"

"Bury her by the hazel-spring," Maren told her, pointing toward the woods where the wild mint grew thick over the gravel. "The earth is three feet deep. You don't have to break your picks on the flint, and the frost won't heave the stones out of the grave before the year turns."

Judith looked at the great wall of oaks, then down at the river that was carrying the yellow leaves away toward the fens.

"It's strange earth," she muttered, crossing her breast with a dirty thumb. "The dead won't know the bells."

"The dead don't hear the bells in the churchyard either," Corin said gently. "They only hear the sheep-clogs on the slate. Come to the shed, Aunt. We have hot porridge with the brown sugar, and a dry bed of sedge for the children."

The quarry-men came down to the boat to help unload the iron.

They did not speak like the town-people; their words were short, grated out between their teeth like stones in a mill. But when they laid their hands on the Swedish iron bars, their fingers lingered on the metal with a hunger that was worse than the hunger for bread.

"This is sea-iron," one of them—a man named Hal who had lost his left eye to a flint-shard—said, lifting an end of the bar. "It has no sulfur in the grain. You can draw this out into three crowbars that will take the edge of an anvil without splitting."

"We'll need them," Maren said.

Hal looked at her with his single, grey eye. "For the shelf?"

"For the shelf," she said. "And for the Notch. The Lord of the Marches has three hundred horsemen at the castle, Hal. He says he will have the sheep-tally before the spring."

The quarry-man spat into the boat.

"Let him bring his horses," he said. "A horse doesn't like the scree, mistress. A horse’s hoof breaks on the slate like an egg-shell. If we have the iron for the picks, three men can hold the Notch against forty riders until the mountain falls."

"We won't hold it with picks," Corin said, coming down the bank with a coil of rope. "We'll hold it with the hives."

He pointed to the twenty-four stands on their oak stilts.

The rain had stopped for an hour, and the sun had broken through the purple clouds, a low, red shaft of light that struck the fronts of the grass skeps until they looked like domes of gold.

The bees were coming out.

They were not foraging—there was nothing to gather save the last, bitter ivy-pollen on the cliffs—but they were taking their cleansing-flights, thousands of them circling the cedar platforms in wide, golden loops, their bodies dropping the dark, yellow spots of waste that cleared their intestines after three days of confinement.

The air was full of that sharp, clean, vinegary smell of propolis and healthy brood.

"They're strong," Hal muttered, stepping back from the flight-line.

"They're stronger than they were on the ridge," Maren said. "In the mountain, they spent half their honey just keeping the frost from the cluster. Here, they have five combs of brood in every dome. By the time the hazel opens, each skep will throw two swarms."

"Forty-eight stands," Corin said. "Twenty-four for the river, and twenty-four for the road."

"The road?" Judith Vance had come down to the bank, holding a wooden bowl of porridge in her hands. "You're taking them back to the wind?"

"Not to the ridge," Maren said. "To the traverses. Six at the Sheep-Dip, six at the chasm-shelf, and twelve at the two pillars."

She took the bone knife from her girdle and held it up against the mountain.

"If the horsemen come, they must come in single file," she said. "The road doesn't allow two saddles abreast. And when the first horse passes the pillars, the guards will be on the apron."

"And if they bring the sulfur?" Judith asked.

"A man can't carry a sulfur-pot on a galloping horse," Maren said. "And the wind off the Notch blows south, Aunt. If they light brimstone at the Gap, the smoke will blow right back into their own bridles."

The quarry-men looked at each other. A slow, dark grin spread across Hal’s scarred face.

"Your father had that craft," he said. "He told the Bailiff once that if the parish tried to take the fourth firkin without the salt-silver, he’d set twelve skeps in the church porch and let the saints have the stings."

"He didn't do it," Judith said.

"Because the Bailiff paid the silver," Maren replied. "And the Lord of the Marches will pay the iron."

### Chapter 14

The deep winter broke in the fourth week of the month of the wolf.

It was not a thaw such as the town-people knew, with green grass springing from the ditches and birds singing in the hedges; it was a great, roaring gale that came out of the south-west, driving the clouds against the limestone wall of Mount Callow until the mountain was lost in a white fury of sleet and rain.

The river rose four cubits in two nights.

Maren was out of her bed at the third watch, her bare feet splashing through three inches of water that had already crept over the grass terrace.

"Corin!" she screamed into the wind.

The oak hearth was already submerged, the black embers hissing as the yellow flood-water swirled through the stones.

Corin came out of the shed with a lantern of horn, its tallow wick sputtering in the draft. Behind him were the quarry-men, their woolen shirts soaked through, their eyes wild with the sudden roar of the water.

"The platforms!" Corin shouted, his voice nearly drowned by the crash of the river.

The yellow torrent was already lapping at the charred bases of the oak posts. The cross-bearers were shuddering with the force of the current; a great cedar-log, swept down from the gorges, had jammed against the legs of the third bench, tilting the platform four inches to the west.

The bees were awake.

Inside the twenty-four skeps, forty thousand insects in each dome were roaring like the draught of a furnace. They could feel the vibration of the river through the wood; they could smell the cold, yellow mud that was rising toward their aprons.

Maren did not hesitate.

"The ropes!" she cried to Hal. "Lash the domes to the bearers! If a skep tilts, the combs will tear from the hazel-pins!"

She plunged into the flood-water, the cold yellow current hitting her thighs with the force of a kicked horse. The mud was slick beneath her feet, the gravel rolling under her clogs, but she caught the upright of the third bench and held fast.

The bees were pouring from the flight-hole.

They did not fly into the gale—the rain would have smashed them to paste—but they clustered on the wet grass skirt, three thousand of them forming a living shield over the entrance to keep the water from the nursery.

They covered Maren’s arms as she reached up with the hemp rope.

They stung her: six, ten, twenty times, their barbs driving deep into her wrists, her neck, the soft skin beneath her jaw. The venom was like boiling water poured into her veins, but she did not let go of the ash bearer.

"Hold the post!" she screamed to Kester, who had waded out behind her with an iron crowbar.

The boy drove the point of the bar into the gravel between the roots of an oak, throwing his whole weight against the shaft to brace the shaking timber.

Corin was at the next bench, his bare shoulders gleaming with mud and rain as he and Hal lifted the fifth skep—sixty pounds of grass, wax, honey, and bees—and swung it up onto their shoulders to carry it toward the higher ground behind the shed.

It took four hours of fighting in the dark.

One by one, the twenty-four stands were unlashed, lifted from their high stilts, and carried through the rising water to the stone shieling that the quarry-men had built against the limestone cliff fifty paces above the flood-line.

When the last skep—the sixth, the one that held the daughter of the old queen—was set on the dry rock floor of the shieling, the grey light of dawn was breaking through the rain.

Maren sat down on the threshold, her legs shaking so violently she could not stand.

Her face was swollen, her right eye closed by three stings on the brow, her lips thick and numb. Her woolen kirtle was torn, plastered to her skin with red mud and dead bees, and her hands were so cramped around the hemp rope that Corin had to pry her fingers open one by one with the handle of his knife.

He held a wooden cup of hot mint-water to her mouth.

"Drink," he said, his own face grey with exhaustion, a deep gash bleeding on his shoulder where the cedar log had struck him.

She swallowed the hot, bitter brew. It tasted of smoke and mint, but it cleared the salt-fever that was burning in her throat.

"Did we lose any?" she whispered, her voice cracked.

"Not a comb," Corin said. He sat down beside her on the stone slab, letting his head rest against the rough wall. "Two platforms went down after we stripped them. The current took the posts like straw. But the straw held the wax, Maren. Every queen is singing."

She looked into the dark interior of the shieling.

The twenty-four grass skeps sat in two rows along the dry rock, their domes smelling of warm wax, cedar-pitch, and the sweet brown sugar they had fed to the nurses.

And from the center of the second row came that sound.

It was not the piping of alarm.

The young queen of the sixth stand was walking the front apron, her yellow abdomen heavy with the eggs of the new season, her wings beating a slow, steady rhythm that was answered by forty thousand workers throughout the shed.

The flood-water was roaring thirty paces away, tearing at the willows and drowning the red-clover meadows under two feet of yellow silt, but in the shieling the air was warm, dry, and safe.

The boy, Kester, came to the door.

He had lost his cap in the river, his hair plastered to his forehead with mud, but he held something in his hand.

It was an ash branch, three feet long, that he had cut from the scrub-woods behind the cliff.

On the tips of the twigs, the buds had broken.

They were not the grey, woolly buttons of the mountain willows; they were long, green fingers of leaf, soft as silk, their tips crowned with tiny, yellow catkins that were already dusting the boy’s knuckles with the first pollen of the spring.

"The hazel is out," Kester said, his face shining through the mud.

Maren reached out her swollen hand and took the branch.

She brought the catkins to her nose. The pollen was dry, sweet, smelling of green wood and warm earth.

She turned to the sixth skep.

A single worker-bee crawled out from the entrance-slit. It had lost half its fuzz in the night’s battle with the rain, its wings notched by the wind, but it lifted its head to the grey light of the doorway.

It tested the air with its antennae.

It found the hazel branch in Maren’s fingers.

It crawled onto the green leaf, lowered its head to the yellow dust of the catkin, and began to pack the first grains into the baskets of its hind legs.

Maren watched it, the tears cutting clean tracks through the red mud on her cheeks.

"The gate is wide, Corin," she said.

"The gate is gone, Maren," her brother replied, his hand resting on the hilt of his father’s knife. "There's only the road now."

### Chapter 15

The hazel-month brought forty wains through the Notch.

They did not come with the haste of thieves; they came with the slow, measured tread of a people who have learned that the mountain is no longer an iron wall, but a stair that can be climbed if the oxen are sound and the drivers have patience with the grade.

The quarry-men had widened the chasm-shelf to eight feet, setting a breastwork of dry limestone along the outer lip so the wheels would not slip into the abyss. At the two pillars where the three chisels had marked the boundary for sixty years, they had built a small stone bothy with a chimney of river-slate, where two men from Low-Marrow sat night and day to grease the axles and collect the road-tithe.

The tithe was not taken in coin.

It was taken in meal: a peck of clean oats for every cart that went down, and two pecks of barley for every sledge that went up with salt and iron.

By the time the wild cherries along the river-margins were white with bloom, sixty families had come out of the high valley to build their shielings along the red spurs.

The village of Low-Marrow was not empty, but it was no longer a prison.

The old people still lived by the Marrow-Water, spinning their coarse wool and tending the few wethers that remained on the high commons; but the young men were on the river-rafts, learning to work the great steering-oars that took the mountain timber down to the abbey shipyards at the estuary.

And on the ridge where the twenty-four stone piers had stood for three generations, Maren Vance set the new apiary.

She did not use the rye-straw skeps.

She had coiled twelve new domes from the spear-sedge of the river, binding them with ash-bark and mounting them on slate benches that had been dressed with the new Swedish chisels. They did not face the Gap; they faced the southern meadows, where the red thistles were already rising three feet high along the runnels.

She sat on the boundary-cairn by the east pillar, her bone knife in her hand, watching the flight-lines.

The bees were everywhere.

They were working the dwarf willows on the high screes; they were working the wild cherries in the gorges; they were crossing the Notch in thousands, their bodies loaded with the yellow hazel-dust and the crimson pollen of the lower spurs.

The hum was no longer a wall of stings.

It was a bridge that stretched from the grey clouds of Mount Callow down to the broad, sunny waters of the Low-Reach, carrying the breath of the two valleys back and forth until a man could not tell where the mountain ended and the forest began.

Corin came up the road from the bothy, carrying a pack-frame loaded with two loaves of the new spring wax.

He was twenty-two now, his shoulders as broad as their father’s had been when he built the stone dyke, his face brown and quiet under his felt hat. He stopped by the cairn, set down his load, and looked out across the shelf.

A company of horsemen was coming up the track from the South.

There were twenty of them, their steel caps flashing in the morning sun, their yew bows cased in waxed leather against the mountain mist. At their head rode Lord Malmes, his blue velvet cloak pinned with a brooch of silver, his soft Spanish boots grey with the road-dust.

He did not ride with his sword drawn.

He rode with his hands resting on his saddle-bow, his dark eyes looking up at the two limestone pillars where the bees were circling in the amber light.

He reined his horse twenty paces from the cairn, his beasts snorting at the smell of the smoke that rose from Maren’s pot.

"Vance," the gentleman said, touching his hat with his riding-whip.

"My Lord," Corin replied, not moving from the path.

Malmes looked at the twelve grass skeps on their slate benches, then at the stone bothy where Hal the quarry-man stood in the doorway with an iron crowbar resting against his thigh.

"The Council of the Marches has received the forty stones of white wax," the gentleman said, his voice smooth and cold as it had been at the abbey quay. "The Prior of St. Jude says the candles burn without smoke, and the church has no fault to find with the weight."

"The weight was seventy pounds to the stone," Maren said from the cairn. "The Vance measure has always been three pounds heavier than the town-scale."

The gentleman turned his horse to look down into the Glen of Orve.

The valley lay below them like an open hand, its grey stone houses tiny as pebbles along the black runnel of the Marrow-Water, its sheep-runs greening with the first pale shoots of the spring moss.

"It's a poor country," Malmes said softly.

"It's an old country," Maren told him.

He looked at her, his eyes taking in the bone knife, the horsehair veil that hung unused from her girdle, and the yellow wax that was crusted under her nails.

"The Warden has signed the patent," he said, drawing a small parchment from his saddle-bag and tossing it onto the cairn at her feet. "The Vance freehold is confirmed, from the river-ford to the high snow-line. You hold the road, Maren Vance. By the service of forty stones of wax and the keeping of the peace between the valley and the towns."

Maren did not pick up the parchment. She let it lie on the slate, where a worker-bee crawled across the red wax of the Earl’s seal, its antennae testing the scent of the resin.

"We hold the road," she said.

The gentleman sat his horse for a moment longer, looking at the bees that were circling his head. One settled on the gold chain at his neck, its wings fanning the air, but he did not brush it away. He watched it with a strange, wondering curiosity, then touched his spurs to his horse and turned back down the mountain, his riders following him in single file along the edge of the chasm.

Corin watched them go until the steel caps were lost in the hazel-scrub below the red spurs.

Then he reached down, took the parchment from the stone, and handed it to Maren.

She did not read it. She could not read the Latin characters that the clerk had drawn with his iron pen.

She walked to the edge of the terrace, where the old queen was buried beneath the red quartz pebble by the river-shallows two leagues below.

She took the small bone pry-knife from her belt.

On the handle, beneath the letters *C. V.* and *M.*, she cut a fourth mark: a single, straight line that crossed the bone from rim to rim, like the road that ran through the Gap.

Then she took her smoker, blew a soft, white puff of cedar-smoke across the flight-board of the first skep, and listened to the bees begin their work.

The mountain was silent behind her, but the valley was full of the sound of water, of wings, and of the great carts that were going down to the sea.
### Chapter 16

The swarming began on the third day of the apple-bloom, when the wind from the estuary died away and left the river-meadows steaming under a white, humid heat that smelled of clover and wet gravel.

Maren was at the rendering-trough behind the shieling, using a curved scraper of boxwood to clean the scum of propolis from the cedar floorboards, when the note changed.

It was not the steady, organ-deep rumble of the foragers returning from the timber. It was a high, thin, whistling scream that rose from the ninth skep—a sound that cut through the humid air like a scythe-blade through green oats.

*Piping.*

She dropped the scraper into the bucket and ran to the terrace.

The ninth skep was one of the spear-sedge domes they had coiled during the winter rains, housing a daughter-colony of the high mountain stock. The entrance-apron was empty of guards. Inside, the combs were so packed with bees that the cluster bulged from the bottom skirt, a black, quivering apron of bodies three inches thick that hung down between the cedar stilts.

From within the straw came the challenge: *peep-peep-peep*, sharp and metallic, like a brass bodkin struck against a slate.

A breath later, from the dark recesses of the fifth comb, came the answer: a muffled, lower *quahk-quahk-quahk*, the cry of a rival virgin queen still trapped inside her capped cell, her mouth pressed against the thin wax dome as the nurse-bees fed her honey through a hair-fine slit.

"Corin!" Maren shouted toward the scrub-woods. "Bring the swarm-nets! The prime is breaking!"

Corin came out of the hazel-copse, carrying three empty grass skeps strung on an ash pole across his shoulders, his forehead dripping with sweat. Kester was behind him with the smoker, puffing white clouds of elder-pith into the air to keep the horseflies from his bare shoulders.

"Which one?" Corin called, dumping the skeps into the grass.

"The ninth," she said, her fingers already working at the hazel ties that secured the skirt. "The old queen is leaving. The virgin broke her cap an hour ago; I hear her tearing at the rival cells."

Even as she spoke, the ninth skep seemed to boil over.

It did not happen slowly. A black torrent of bees erupted from the flight-slit, pouring out across the cedar apron in a living wave that spilled over the edges into the mud. They took flight not in ones or twos, but in thousands, rising into the humid air until the sun was dimmed by a swirling, roaring vortex forty yards wide.

The noise was deafening—a physical pressure that vibrated in Maren’s jaw and rattled the iron hoops of the water-piggin by the shed.

The air smelled intensely of the swarm: a hot, sharp, lemon-scented reek of geraniol, the aggregation pheromone released by ten thousand workers whose Nasonov glands were fully exposed, the white bands on their abdomens raised to the sky like tiny beacons.

"Where's the mother?" Kester yelled, shielding his eyes with his arm. The boy had learned the craft, but the sheer fury of a mountain swarm still made his voice jump into his throat.

"She won't fly far," Maren said, her eyes tracking the dark funnel against the green wall of the oaks. "Her abdomen is heavy with unlaid eggs. She’ll take the first branch that breaks the wind."

The cloud drifted fifty yards across the clearing, undulating like a great sheepskin shaken in the wind. Then it checked.

At the edge of the timber stood an ancient crab-apple tree, its boughs knotted and grey with lichen, white with the bitter mountain-blossoms that had just begun to shed their petals. The vortex narrowed, swirling around a low horizontal limb six feet above the turf.

The roar began to settle into a deeper, rhythmic hum.

On the underside of the crab-apple branch, a knot of bees formed, no larger than a fist. Within three minutes, workers were piling onto it from every quarter of the sky, tumbling over one another, locking their legs together until a great, black pear of living bodies hung from the bark, three feet long and sixteen inches through the middle.

Silence fell over the clearing, save for the heavy, contented purr of the cluster and the slow dripping of the river against the gravel.

Maren walked to the tree, carrying a grass skep in her left hand and the goose-wing in her right.

She did not hurry. A swarm that had clustered was in its gentlest temper; each bee had gorged herself on honey before leaving the parent dome, carrying three days of stores in her honey-sac to feed the wax-glands while they drew the first combs of their new home. A gorged bee could scarcely bend her abdomen to strike with her sting.

Corin knelt beneath the branch, holding a clean square of linen cloth spread across the grass.

"Ready?" he asked.

"Hold the cloth wide," she said.

She positioned the mouth of the empty skep directly beneath the cluster, lifting it until the bottom twigs of the branch were inside the rim. With her right hand, she took the limb above the cluster and gave it a single, sharp, downward jerk.

The pear dropped.

It fell into the skep with a soft, heavy *thud*, like a sheep’s fleece dropped from a shearing-bench. A few thousand bees spilled onto the linen cloth, running in frantic circles over the bleached flax, but the great mass remained inside the grass dome.

Maren inverted the skep onto the linen, propping the front rim up on a flat river-pebble no thicker than her thumb.

Within thirty seconds, the workers on the cloth had oriented themselves. They turned toward the pebble-slit, lifted their abdomens, and began to fan, beating their wings with that furious, triumphant rhythm that told every straggler in the air that the mother was housed, the dome was dark, and the new colony had found its hearth.

"That's forty pounds of bees," Corin said, wiping his brow with his arm. "They'll draw six combs before the moon turns."

"Look at the ninth," Kester said from the terrace.

Maren turned.

At the flight-hole of the parent skep, a small, dark insect had emerged.

It was not a worker. It was longer, leaner, its abdomen pointed like a thorn, moving with a quick, nervous, darting gait that was entirely different from the heavy, dignified march of a laying queen.

The virgin.

She ran to the edge of the apron, raised herself on her front legs, and turned her head toward the sun. She rubbed her wings together with a dry, rustling sound, then took flight, climbing straight up into the blue sky until she was lost in the glare.

"Her wedding-flight," Corin murmured.

"She’ll meet the drones above the river-spit," Maren said. "The big males from the third and seventh stands have been circling the high oaks since noon."

She looked up into the clear air, where the distant speck of the young queen had vanished.

In the old days on the ridge, a virgin queen was a terror. The high wind off the Notch would often beat them against the limestone cliffs or carry them over the precipice into the Black Sunk, leaving the parent colony queenless and doomed to the sulfur-can.

Here, the air was warm, buoyant, sweet with the scent of five leagues of clover and wild apple. The drones would find her by the hundreds, tumbling over each other in their aerial pursuit, and she would return to the sedge-skep within the hour, carrying the white thread of the mating-sign that proved the lineage was renewed for another seven years.

"Maren," Kester called, his voice dropping.

He was looking down the track that ran along the river-margin toward the lower ferry.

Two men were coming through the willows.

They did not walk like the fen-dwellers or the boatmen from the abbey quays. They had the long, loose, rolling stride of mountain men who are accustomed to setting their heels against shale; but they wore the grey, unbleached wool of Low-Marrow, their coats bound with cords of raw hemp.

The foremost was a man named Donald, the nephew of Elder Brant. He had a face like an axe-blade, pinched and pale from the winter fast, and his right shoulder was higher than his left from thirty years of dragging the lime-sledges.

Behind him came a boy of twelve, his cousin, carrying an iron-shod staff that was tipped with a bunch of dried mountain-sage—the traditional sign of a messenger from the Parish Bench.

Corin reached down and picked up his adze, leaning his weight against the haft as the two men came up the gravel bank.

"Donald," Corin said. "The snow must be off the high sheep-runs if you're down to the timber."

The messenger stopped ten paces from the cedar platform. He did not look at the twenty-four skeps, nor at the great cluster under the crab-apple tree; he looked at his own boots, where the wet mud of the lowlands was already soaking through the split cow-hide of his welts.

"The snow is gone from the runs," Donald said, his voice flat, grated thin by the mountain-wind. "The grass is coming up black on the commons. But the parish has no salt."

"We sent three casks up with Garrow's wain," Maren said, stepping forward with the goose-wing in her hand.

"The Bailiff took the casks," the messenger said.

"Orde?" Corin’s brow furrowed. "Garrow said his jaw was locked with the stings."

"The lock came off when the pain went down," Donald said. "His face is pulled round like an old shoe, and he can only chew on the left side; but he can talk enough to swear the church-guards. He has four men from the lower maltings with him, and two fellows from the town across the western gap who carry long knives in their belts."

"Speculators," Corin spat.

"They brought thirty head of black cattle through the lower ditch," Donald continued, his eyes shifting nervously to the great cloud of bees that still hung above the ninth skep. "Lowland beasts, with big horns and ribs like wicker crates. They turned them into the church-meadow where the ewes were dropping their lambs."

"And the salt?" Maren demanded.

"The Bailiff traded the three casks to the cattle-men for six barrels of sour beer and an iron kettle," Donald said. "He told the village that the salt from the South was cursed—that it was made from the sweat of the dead men in the fens, and that whoever ate it would have the black rot in his liver before the winter came again."

He looked at Maren then, his pale eyes watering in the warm sunlight.

"The ewes are dying, Maren."

"Dying of what?"

"The murrain," the man said, crossing himself with his thumb. "The cattle brought it up from the marshes. The sheep get the black water in their urine; their fleeces slip from their hides in sheets, and by the third day their tongues swell until they choke in the pens. Twenty have died in the Upper Fold since the new moon."

Maren felt a cold, familiar knot tighten beneath her ribs.

The murrain. It was the plague that the old folk spoke of in the winter nights—the sickness that had swept through the southern valleys sixty years ago, the very thing that had frightened the first settlers into dragging the limestone boulders across the Notch and planting the twenty-four skeps as an iron wall against the world.

"Does it touch the cattle?" Corin asked.

"The black cattle are fat," Donald said with a bitter twist of his thin lips. "They crop the ling and they trample the runnels into red soup. The men with the long knives sit in the church porch, drinking the Bailiff’s beer and shooting at the crows with their steel bows. If a tenant comes to the gate to ask for his lamb-tithe, they set the dogs on him."

"And Elder Brant?" Maren asked.

"Brant is shut in the vestry," Donald said. "He hasn't come out since the cattle crossed the ditch. He sits on the iron chest with the parish rolls in his lap, and he sings the penitential psalms until his throat is like an old shoe. He won't speak to the Bailiff, and he won't speak to the village. He says the wall is broken and the abomination of desolation is standing in the holy place."

The boy with the staff looked up at Corin.

"My mother said to tell you that the meal-chest in the parsonage is empty," the child whispered. "She has three bannocks left for the children, and then there's only the boiled nettles."

Corin looked at Maren.

His face had gone very still, that same hard, cold look coming into his jaw that she had seen the morning he brought the wild queen down from the limestone screes.

"We have thirty bushels of the seed-wheat in the shed," he said.

"The wheat won't cure the sheep," Maren said.

She turned to the cedar bench where the sixth skep sat on its stilts.

A worker had landed on her bare wrist, its small, velvet body heavy with the dark, greenish-brown pollen of the wild boxwood that grew on the river-cliffs.

"What cures the murrain?" Corin asked.

"Propolis," she said.

The men stared at her.

"Propolis?" Donald asked, his brow creasing. "The bee-glue? The black stuff you scrape from the rafters to patch the water-piggins?"

"It's not glue," Maren said. "It's the resin of the birch and the larch, gathered by the bees when the buds break, mixed with the wax from their own glands and the grease of their stomachs. It keeps the rot from the combs. If a mouse creeps into a skep in the winter and the bees sting it to death, they can't drag the carcass through the narrow flight-hole. It would rot in the straw and poison the colony."

She looked at her brother.

"What do they do with the mouse, Corin?"

"They coat it," Corin said softly. "They cover the whole beast in propolis, three coats thick, until it's sealed in an iron shell. It stays there three years without stinking, dry as a bone, sweet as dried mint."

"If it seals the mouse against the rot," Maren said, "it seals the sheep’s throat against the black water. Our grandfather did it when the red-scab was on the commons thirty years ago. He boiled the propolis in sheep-butter with crushed garlic and elder-bark, and he drenched every ewe with two hornfuls before the sun went down."

"Do we have enough?" Corin asked.

Maren looked at the twenty-four stands.

"We have sixty pounds of the scraped gum from the winter boards," she said. "And forty jars of the run-honey to carry the bitter. But it has to be boiled on the mountain, Corin. It won't keep its strength if it travels three leagues in the sun."

She reached down and took her bone pry-knife from her girdle.

"Kester," she said to the tanner’s son. "Go to the shed. Get the iron pot—the great one with the three legs. And tell Hal to bring six of the quarry-picks and the two Swedish crowbars."

"We're going up?" the boy asked, his eyes wide.

"We're going up," Maren said. "The Bailiff wants the border back. We'll give him a border he won't forget until his teeth rot from his other jaw."

### Chapter 17

The climb through the Gap was different now that the road was eight feet wide, but the mountain had not lost its malice.

As they passed the chasm-shelf, the warm, sweet air of the Low-Reach fell away behind them like an old coat dropped on the path. The wind off the high shoulders of Mount Callow met them at the second traverse: a thin, grey knife that smelled of old snow and wet slate, drying the sweat on Maren’s neck until her skin felt tight as parchment.

They were eight in all: Maren, Corin, Kester, Donald the messenger, and four of the quarry-men from the river-camp, each carrying an iron bar or a heavy pick on his shoulder.

Behind them came two pack-mules that Corin had hired from the abbey’s carrier, their wicker panniers loaded with the sixty pounds of propolis, three firkins of honey, two sacks of seed-wheat, and forty pounds of clean tallow from the abbey kitchen.

When they rounded the west pillar and came onto the old flight-terrace, Maren stopped.

The twenty-four stone piers were still there, grey and bare against the limestone cliff, their surfaces crusted with the white lichen that had grown over the places where the straw skirts had sat for sixty years.

The seventh pier was shattered.

Someone had taken a heavy stone-sledge to the slate flag, splitting it into four jagged pieces that lay in the shingle like the bones of an ox. The ground around it was black, charred by a fire that had been fed with peat-turf and old heather.

Beside the ruin stood an iron pole, driven deep into a cleft in the rock.

On the top of the pole was spiked the skull of an old horned ram, its eye-sockets stuffed with dried mountain-ling, facing south toward the road like a blind watchman.

"The Bailiff’s work," Donald muttered, spitting into the scree. "He set it there the day after the cattle came through. He said it was the boundary of the holy parish, and that no man should carry unhallowed meat past the horns."

Hal the quarry-man stepped forward without a word.

He swung his five-foot iron bar with both hands, the heavy steel whistling through the cold air. The blow took the iron pole at the base; the metal shrieked as it snapped from the rock, sending the pole and the ram’s skull tumbling over the precipice into the Black Sunk, their clatter echoing against the cliffs until it was swallowed by the void.

"There's your boundary," Hal said, resting the bar across his shoulders.

They did not stop at the bothy. They walked straight down the road toward Low-Marrow, their boots striking the flagstones with that steady, rhythmic crunch that had not been heard in the upper valley since the road-wardens were dismissed.

The valley of Orve was unrecognizable.

It was not the cold that had changed it; the frost-verge was still there, the low, lard-colored clouds hanging from the rims of the cliffs, the needle-ice skinning the margins of the Marrow-Water.

It was the ruin.

The high commons, where the short, sweet mountain-moss had supported sixty generations of sheep without a sod turning, were torn to black rags. The thirty head of lowland cattle had trampled the turf into a mire of grey clay, their heavy, cloven hooves breaking through the fragile crust of peat to expose the wet gravel beneath.

The water in the sheep-runnels was thick and yellow, smelling of cow-dung and the sweet, putrid reek of dead flesh.

A quarter-mile above the Sheep-Dip, they found the first ewe.

She lay on her side in the mud, her legs stretched out stiff, her fleece torn away in great, greasy patches where the crows had been at the ulcers on her ribs. Her head was pulled back, her black tongue protruding three inches from her teeth, swollen and dry as a piece of salted beef.

Kester turned his face away, his hand going to his throat.

"The murrain," Donald said, his voice trembling. "There's three more behind the stone dyke. And the water is full of it."

Maren knelt by the dead sheep.

She did not touch the fleece; she drew her bone knife and slit the skin of the animal’s throat with a single, practiced cut.

The blood that came out was not red. It was a dark, purple tar, thick as treacle, that did not run into the gravel. It had the heavy, sickening stench of gangrene and bitter bile.

"The liver is black," she said, standing up and wiping the blade on the heels of her boots. "The blood has turned to lye. If the cattle stay in the upper meadows for another ten days, there won't be a living hoof in the parish."

They came to the Sheep-Dip.

The stone walls of the enclosure had been breached. Two sections of the dry-stone dyke had been thrown down to make a gate for the cattle, and across the opening sat three men on inverted firkins, their cloaks of greasy frieze pulled tight against the mountain-wind.

They were the strangers Donald had spoken of.

They were not mountain-folk; they had the thick, fleshy noses and wet, pale lips of men who lived on salt-fish and ditch-water in the fen-margins. One of them held a steel crossbow across his knees, its square quarrel tipped with an iron point that had been smeared with grease; the other two had long, straight knives with brass pommels tucked into their belts.

Beside them stood a rough timber hurdle, and on the hurdle lay three slaughtered lambs, their throats cut, their carcasses already blue with the frost.

As Maren and her company came around the shoulder of the wall, the man with the crossbow stood up.

He did not look like a soldier; he looked like a butcher’s carter who had been given an iron weapon and a leather jerkin. He cranked the bow’s lever with a rhythmic, clicking sound, bringing the cord to the notch, then laid the quarrel in the groove.

"Back down the track, mountain-shits," he shouted, his voice coarse and nasal. "The Bailiff has closed the fold. No cattle move without the toll."

Corin did not check his stride. He walked straight toward the hurdle, his adze swinging loose in his right hand.

"The road is the King’s," Corin said.

"The road belongs to Master Vane," the crossbowman retorted, raising the weapon to his shoulder and squinting along the iron sight. "And Master Vane says if any of the Vance bastards come through the Gap, they get two inches of Swedish bolt through the brisket."

He had his finger on the lever.

Maren did not draw a knife. She reached into the wicker basket that hung from her hip—the small basket she had carried from the river-terrace, its lid held down by a leather toggle.

She slipped the toggle.

She did not throw a stone; she did not speak a charm.

She took out a small ball of dry mountain-moss that had been soaked in walnut-oil and the guiding-paste, set it on the iron point of her bone knife, and struck a sulfur-match against the stone of the wall.

The moss flared with a bright, blue flame, giving off a thick, greasy puff of white smoke that smelled intensely of bitter nuts, elder-resin, and female bee-scent.

She blew the smoke straight into the draft that pulled through the breach in the dyke.

"What's that reek?" the crossbowman began, lowering the weapon an inch to peer through the vapor.

He did not finish the word.

From the two wicker panniers on the lead mule came a sound like the tearing of silk.

The mules were not carrying only propolis and seed-wheat.

In the bottom of each basket, tucked into the dark between two straw hurdles, sat a three-comb mating-nuc—a small box of split cedar containing two thousand high-mountain workers and a young, unmated queen from the spring brood.

The bees had been shut in the dark for six hours. They were warm from the mules’ sweat; they were gorged on honey-sugar; and when the smell of the guiding-smoke hit the ventilator-slits, they knew their work.

They came out like shot from a mortar.

It was not a general swarm; it was a targeted strike. The mountain bees did not circle; they followed the blue ribbon of walnut-smoke as if it were an iron wire stretched through the air.

The crossbowman saw them coming—a black, whining fist of insects that flew three feet above the mud, straight for his throat.

He fired the bow, but his hands were shaking; the iron quarrel struck the slate wall three yards to the left of Corin’s head, throwing up a shower of dry sparks.

Then the bees hit him.

They did not strike his jerkin; they went for his eyes, his nostrils, the wet, open red of his mouth as he let out a scream that brought the cattle to their feet half a mile away.

Twenty bees took him on the lips at once, their barbs driving deep into the soft, vascular flesh, pumping that dark, oily venom that had been bred on the high limestone screes to stop wolves from the honey-clefts.

The other two men did not draw their knives.

They dropped their firkins, threw their arms over their caps, and fled down the track toward Low-Marrow, screaming like women whose aprons have caught the fire.

The crossbowman tumbled into the mud of the enclosure, thrashing like a speared pike, his hands clawing at his face until the skin was torn with his own nails.

"Corin," Maren said quietly.

Corin stepped forward, caught the man by the collar of his leather jerkin, and dragged him out of the breach. He took the smoker from Kester’s hand, blew three puffs of cool elder-smoke across the man’s face to drive the remaining guards away, then kicked the fallen crossbow into the ditch.

The stranger’s lips were already swollen to the size of sausages, his eyes puffed shut beneath great, white, watery blisters that looked like lard. He could not speak; he could only make a dry, whistling hiss in his windpipe as his tongue enlarged against the roof of his mouth.

"Take his boots," Hal the quarry-man said, stepping through the wall. "They're sound leather."

"Leave his boots," Maren told him. "Let him walk down to the village in his stocking-feet. He’ll tell the Bailiff what kind of border we brought down from the ridge."

She turned to Donald.

"Where is the church-key?"

"Brant has it," the messenger said, his voice hushed as he watched the stranger crawl away through the mire on his hands and knees. "Inside the vestry."

"Then we'll have it out," Maren said. "We have sixty ewes to drench before the sun goes behind Callow, and the churchyard is the only place with an iron kettle that can boil three stones of butter."

### Chapter 18

The village of Low-Marrow was silent as a tomb.

The low stone barns were all barred from within, their oak shutters fastened with raw-hide thongs, the smoke from the roof-holes rising thin and blue into the frost-air like the breath of dying men. No sheep cropped the commons; no women were at the washing-stones by the Marrow-Water.

Only the pigs were out: six lean, long-snouted mountain swine that were rooting through the turf by the churchyard wall, fighting over the carcass of a half-grown lamb that had been dragged into the ditch by the dogs.

The church of St. Jude-in-the-Gap sat on a knoll of grey limestone in the center of the parish, its low, square tower built of unmortared blocks three feet thick, roofed with slabs of rough slate that were green with moss.

In the porch sat Bailiff Orde.

He was not wearing his black boots; his feet were wrapped in bundles of greasy fleece tied round with hemp twine. His right cheek was still swollen, the flesh pulled down into his throat in a great, wattle-like pouch that was marbled with yellow bruises, and his right eye was fixed in a wide, unblinking stare that gave his face the look of an owl that had been struck with a club.

Beside him on the stone bench sat a man in a red wool doublet—a fat, soft-bellied man with rings on his greasy fingers and a ledger bound in green calf-skin resting on his knees.

Master Vane. The cattle-merchant from the western gap.

When Maren and her company came through the lych-gate, the two men did not move. Orde tried to stand, his hands clutching the stone bench, but his knees gave way beneath his weight and he fell back with a wet, bubbling grunt that was swallowed by his jowls.

Master Vane closed his green ledger with a snap.

"You're the Vance wench," he said. His voice was soft, greasy, like the broth in a tripe-pot. "The one who broke the King’s pass."

"The pass is open," Maren said, stopping at the foot of the church steps. "And the road is clear."

"The road is not clear, mistress," Vane said, tapping the leather book with his silver signet-ring. "The Council of the Marches has leased the pasture of Orve to my company for twenty years, to hold by the rent of sixty pounds of silver and the cleared tithes of the lime-quarries. Your brother has no more right in this valley than a sheep-thief from the borders."

Corin walked up the three steps, his adze resting in the crook of his arm.

He did not look at the merchant; he looked at Orde.

"Where is the butter, Bailiff?"

Orde’s mouth worked, his tongue clicking against his yellow teeth, but no words came. He raised a shaking hand and pointed toward the Tithe-Barn across the road.

"The butter is seized," Master Vane said, his voice hardening. "For the debt of the parish. The village owes ninety groats for the cattle-tolls, and sixty more for the salt-casks that were broken at the Gap."

"The butter belongs to the sheep," Maren said.

She stepped past the merchant without looking at him, her shoulder striking his red doublet with enough force to spin him half-round on the stone bench.

She walked to the great oak door of the church.

It was barred from the inside, the heavy iron bolt driven deep into the stone sockets of the jambs.

Maren did not knock with her fist. She drew her bone pry-knife and tapped the pommel three times against the center of the wood—three sharp, clean strokes, the rhythm her father had used when he brought the honey-firkins to the church door at the frost-turning.

"Elder Brant," she called.

Her voice was not loud, but it had that clear, carrying edge that she had used to call the bees across the scree.

From within the dark nave came no sound of a bolt sliding. There was only a dry, rustling noise, like an old dog turning in its straw, and then the slow, dragging scrape of wooden clogs across the flags.

A wicket-hole, four inches square, opened in the center of the oak plank.

Through the iron grating showed a face that looked as though it had been dug from the gravel of the churchyard. Brant’s skin had turned the grey-white of lard; his beard was matted with dried spittle and porridge, and his eyes were sunken so deep into their sockets that they showed only as two tiny points of yellow fire in the dark.

"Is the smoke up?" the old man whispered.

"The smoke is down, Elder," Maren said.

"Did you burn the seventh?"

"The seventh is in the South," she told him. "Her daughter has forty thousand children in the cedar-glade, and the white wax is at the abbey altar."

The old man was silent for five breaths. She could hear his lungs whistling in his chest, a dry, rattly sound like wind in a chimney.

"Then the world is over," he murmured.

"The world is beginning," she said. "Open the door, Brant. The murrain is in the valley. We have the propolis from the lower stands, and forty pounds of abbey tallow. If we don't boil the drench before the sun goes down, there won't be a ewe left to give a fleece for the winter tithe."

The wicket remained open.

The old man’s fingers, yellow as horn and tipped with black nails, appeared through the iron grating, clutching the bars until the skin turned white.

"Your father came to me," Brant whispered. "Before he went to the quarry. The year the great snow didn't leave the churchyard until the midsummer."

Maren felt her skin prickle. "My father?"

"He told me the bees were getting soft," the old man said, his words coming in short, gasping jerks. "He said the mountain was too cold for them. He said every generation had smaller wings and thinner legs, because there was no new blood from the outside. He said if we didn't open the Notch, the twenty-four stands would be dead in thirty turns of the sun."

"And you wouldn't open it," Corin said from the steps.

Brant turned his hollow eyes toward the young man.

"We couldn't open it," the elder whispered. "You don't know what was outside, boy. You only know the sun and the clover. You didn't see the carts that came through the Gap when we were lads—the carts full of children with their tongues black and their eyes weeping yellow water. You didn't see the press-gangs from the Duke’s castle, dragging every third lad away in an iron collar to die in the Scots mosses."

He leaned his forehead against the iron bars of the wicket.

"We made a covenant with the rock," he said. "The rock gave us the cold, and the cold gave us the wall. We were poor, aye. We ate the salt-beef and the ling, and our children died of the stone-rot in their chests before they were forty. But we died in our own beds, Corin. No king took our wool; no bailiff took our daughters."

"The covenant is broken, Elder," Maren said gently. "The rock didn't break it. The queen broke it. She was spent."

"She was sixty years old," Brant said, and for the first time his voice sounded human, broken by a grief that was older than the church walls. "She was the last thing that remembered the world before the cold."

The wicket remained still for a moment, then the old man withdrew his face.

From inside the church came the heavy, groaning rattle of the iron bolt being drawn from its socket. The wood creaked, the hinges screaming with twenty years of rust, and the great door swung inward three feet.

Brant stood in the opening.

He had no shoes; his feet were wrapped in sheepskin rags that were soaked with grease. He held the great church-key—ten inches of rusted iron with three wards—in his left hand, and in his right he held a bundle of sheep-parchment bound with split hazel.

He looked at Master Vane on the stone bench.

"Get off the church steps, cattle-man," the old man said, his voice rising until it rang against the slate tower like a bronze bell.

Vane blinked, his soft mouth open. "Elder—"

"Get off the steps!" Brant roared, raising his iron staff. "You brought the rot into the parish! You brought the black water into the runnels! The church owes you no silver; the church owes you only the pit under the belfry!"

The merchant stood up, his green ledger clutched to his belly, and backed down the steps toward the lych-gate, looking nervously at Hal and the quarry-men who had closed the road behind him.

Brant turned to Maren.

He held out the bundle of parchment.

"Take them," he said.

"What are they?" she asked.

"The old rolls," he said. "The rolls from the first foundation, before the wall was built. It has the names of the flowers, Maren. The flowers that used to grow in the valley before the cold came down: the Great Clover, the Mountain-Balm, and the sweet White Mustard that the bees loved better than the heather."

Maren took the parchments. They were dry, yellow, smelling of church-smoke and old wax, but the ink was black and clear.

"We have the seed," she said. "Corin brought three sacks of the clover-seed from the abbey."

"Then sow it in the gravel," Brant said. He turned back into the dark church, his wooden clogs clicking slowly on the cold stones of the aisle. "And light the kettle, daughter. The ewes are waiting by the fold."

### Chapter 19

They set the great iron kettle in the middle of the churchyard, between the grave of Maren’s grandfather and the stone preaching-cross that had lost its arms to the frost sixty years ago.

Kester and Donald hauled the dry peat from the tithe-stack, building a fire that burned with a hot, blue heart beneath the iron legs.

Maren worked the drench.

It was craft that had to be watched with every breath. If the tallow got too hot, the propolis would turn to black pitch, losing the volatile oils that gave it its healing power; if it was too cold, the resin would not dissolve, separating into sticky brown clots that would choke the sheep instead of coating their throats.

She shaved the sixty pounds of mountain propolis into the pot with her bone knife, watching the yellow shavings melt into the bubbling sheep-butter until the mixture turned the color of an old bridle, smelling so intensely of birch-resin, garlic, and wild thyme that the crows in the belfry took flight with loud, indignant croaks.

They did not come with their staves; they came with their sheep.

Maren took the drenching-horn—a cow’s horn with the tip sawed off and smoothed with beeswax to keep it from tearing the gums.

She would straddle the neck of each ewe, her knees gripping the woolly ribs, her left hand forcing the jaw open while her thumb held the black, swollen tongue down against the lower teeth.

With her right hand, she poured four ounces of the warm, amber brew down the throat, holding the animal’s muzzle high until she felt the convulsive swallow in the gullet.

The sheep coughed, their breath coming in whistling gasps as the bitter, resinous grease hit the ulcers in their throats; but within ten breaths, the coughing checked. The propolis did its work: the swelling in the tongue began to soften, the foul, gangrenous reek of the breath was replaced by the clean scent of birch-pitch, and the animal could draw the air without the rattle of death in its windpipe.

They drenched ninety ewes before the dusk came off Mount Callow.

Only three died—three old wethers whose lungs had already filled with the black water before the kettle was hot. The rest were turned into the church-glebe, where the grass had been sheltered from the cattle by the limestone walls.

As the sun went down behind the western rim, turning the snow-patches on Callow to the color of dried blood, Bailiff Orde was still sitting in the church porch.

He had not moved. His bundle-wrapped feet were tucked under the bench, his one good eye fixed on the empty kettle that was cooling in the peat-ash.

Maren walked up the steps, carrying a wooden noggin of the drench-broth that she had mixed with hot water and honey.

She set it on the stone beside his hand.

The Bailiff did not look up. His thick, purple jowls quivered slightly in the mountain-draft, but he reached out with his left hand—the one that had not been stiffened by the stings—and touched the wooden cup.

"Is the road open, girl?" he asked.

His voice was no longer the arrogant bark of the Tithe-Barn; it was a low, cracked whisper that sounded like the water under the ice.

"The road is open, Bailiff," she said.

"And the town?"

"The town is there," she told him. "With three market-crosses and fifty ships at the quay. They have white salt for every sheep-pen in the parish, and Swedish iron enough to shoe all the wains in the north."

Orde brought the noggin to his lips. He drank the bitter, greasy brew in three slow, painful swallows, his throat clicking like a dry pump.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his greasy sleeve.

"The church owes you forty stones of comb," he said.

"The church owes nothing," Maren said, leaning against the stone jamb of the porch. "The debt was paid when the old queen died."

"Where is she?"

"In the sand by the river," she said. "Under a red quartz stone."

The Bailiff looked down at his fleece-wrapped feet.

"Your father was a hard man, Maren."

"He was a beekeeper," she said.

"He told me once... he said the mountain was only a box with the lid shut." The old man gave a faint, rattling sigh that shook his heavy shoulders. "He said if you leave the lid shut too long, the bees eat their own wax and turn to cannibals. I thought he was light-headed. I thought the altitude had got into his brain-pan."

He looked up at her then, his one eye wet and clear in the twilight.

"He wasn't light-headed, was he?"

"No," she said. "He just knew which way the wind was blowing through the Gap."

### Chapter 21

The heat came into the Low-Reach in the middle of the hay-month, not with the dry, thin glare of the mountain peaks, but with a heavy, yellow stagnation that sat upon the river like sheep-dip in a vat.

The water shrank between its banks, leaving broad shelves of grey silt that cracked into hexagonal tiles under the sun, smelling of rotting weed and freshwater mussels. "The moth," she whispered.

"Two nights on the cold slate will kill the eggs," she said, her father’s craft rising clear and sharp in her mind. ### Chapter 22

### Chapter 23

The journey up to the Notch with the eight crates of moth-combs took four hours, the mules climbing the new eight-foot shelf with the slow, rhythmic cadence of beasts that knew the mountain.

Kester led the first mule; Maren walked at the head of the second, her bone knife in her hand, her eyes searching the limestone screes for the wild thyme that was just beginning to bloom in the crevices.

When they reached the stone bothy behind the old flight-terrace, the world seemed to drop into that great, cold silence that Maren had forgotten in the humid clamor of the river-valleys.

The wind through the Gap was blowing steady and thin from the north-west, carrying the scent of the high ice-fields of Mount Callow. The sun was bright, but it gave no heat to the grey slate; the stones remained cold as iron, their edges sharp enough to cut the welts of the mules’ shoes.

They unloaded the eight crates into the dark interior of the bothy.

The floor was made of great slabs of blue limestone that had been split from the quarry sixty years ago, dry as bone, chilled by the air that sucked down from the snow-line through the sod roof.

Maren unpacked the combs one by one.

She laid them out on the flags, resting their edges on laths of dry hazel so the air could move beneath them.

The difference in temperature was immediate.

In the river-glade, the wax was soft, greasy, smelling of the hot clover and the lime-blossom. Here, within twenty minutes, the wax had stiffened until it was hard and brittle as glass.

She knelt beside the comb from the fourteenth skep, bringing her horn-glass to her eye to examine the silken gallery she had slit that morning.

A moth-worm was there—a small one, no thicker than a horsehair, that had hatched from an egg in the cell-corner an hour before they left the bottoms.

It was not crawling.

The cold of the slate had taken it in the joints. Its white body had curled into a tight, crescent-shaped loop; its eight claws had lost their grip on the wax midrib, and as Maren touched it with the point of her bodkin, it rolled over into the cell-dust, grey, stiff, and dead.

"It works," Kester whispered, kneeling beside her on the cold stone, his breath showing white in the draft.

"It always worked," she said. "The cold is a knife, Kester. A knife doesn't ask who holds it; it just cuts whatever has no wool."

She stood up and walked to the doorway of the bothy.

Looking down the road toward the Glen of Orve, she saw a single figure coming up the track from the Sheep-Dip.

It was not an elder; it was not a quarry-man.

It was a girl of fourteen, her cousin Elspeth, the daughter of Judith Vance. She was running, her wooden clogs clattering against the shale with a frantic, irregular rhythm that sent the mountain goats bounding up the rocks of the west pillar.

She was waving a strip of white linen above her head—the straining-cloth from the lower dairy.

"Maren!" the girl screamed, her voice cracking in the thin air. "Maren! Come down!"

Maren ran down the path to meet her, catching the girl by her woolen shoulders as she stumbled over a frost-crack in the road.

"What is it, Elspeth? The ewes?"

"The shed!" the child gasped, her chest heaving against her sheepskin vest, her face purple with the climb. "The honey-shed at the river! Men with iron caps... they came through the willow-copses with pitch-torches!"

"Not the fat man," Elspeth cried, the tears cutting through the road-dust on her cheeks. "Men with blue coats! They threw the torches into the reed-thatch behind the presses! Corin is fighting them with the adze, but they have iron swords, and the wind is blowing from the water!"

She turned to Kester, who had run out of the bothy with his iron crowbar.

"The mules," she said, her voice dropping into that flat, terrible quiet that their father had used when the drift-ice took the quarry-works.

"We can't get the mules down in time," Kester said, his eyes wide with terror. "It's two leagues, Maren! The fire will have the thatch in twenty minutes!"

"We don't take the road," she said.

Between the east pillar and the wall of Mount Callow ran the Shingle-Slide: a great shoot of loose, grey scree that fell three thousand feet from the Notch straight down to the red spurs below the timber-line.

In the old days, no man walked the Shingle-Slide. A stone set rolling at the top would reach the bottom in ninety seconds, leaping thirty cubits into the air at every bounce, smashing itself to powder against the limestone ribs.

Only the goats used it when the wolves were behind them.

And Corin, the day he went.

"Maren, no," Kester whispered, catching her sleeve. "You'll break your neck. The shale won't hold a foot."

She tore her sleeve from his fingers.

She reached into the bothy, caught up her sheepskin vest, and took her bone knife in her teeth, holding the horn handle between her jaws.

"Stay with the combs, Kester," she said around the bone. "If the frost takes them, they're saved. If the fire takes the river, we have nothing left to house."

She ran past the east pillar, past the three chisels carved into the limestone rock, and leaped out onto the lip of the Shingle-Slide.

The shale gave way beneath her boots with a dry, roaring hiss like a falling wave of water.

The mountain dropped away beneath her.

It was not the white, sweet-scented smoke of cedar-billets.

It was the greasy, yellow smudge of burning reeds and pitch.

### Chapter 24

The honey-shed was half-gone.

The western roof-slope, where the reed-thatch had been dried to tinder by twelve days of the lime-sun, was a roaring wall of orange flame that reached thirty feet into the oak boughs, throwing showers of burning sparks across the grass toward the twenty-four skeps on their ash stilts.

At the edge of the terrace stood four men in the blue coats of the Chandler's Guild.

Two of them were working a pair of long iron pruning-hooks, trying to drag down the cedar corner-posts of the shed so the burning roof would collapse onto the wax-presses inside.

The other two stood guard with short hunting-swords drawn, their backs to the fire, their eyes on the hazel-copse.

In the middle of the grass lay Corin.

He was on his hands and knees, his head bleeding from a long, ragged cut that had laid open the skin of his temple from the brow to the hair-line. His adze lay five paces away in the mud, its ash handle broken in two.

One of the blue-coated swordsmen—a tall, scarred carter named Gault, whom Maren remembered from the proctor’s barge—stepped toward her brother, his heavy boot raised to kick the young man in the ribs.

"Where is the white wax, mountain-bastard?" Gault shouted over the roar of the fire. "Tell us where the barrels are hid, or we'll throw you onto the thatch to roast with your own mutton."

Maren came out of the bracken like a wolf from the rocks.

She did not scream. She did not shout.

She had the bone knife in her right hand, but she did not strike with the blade. She ran straight at Gault’s back, her head lowered, and drove the point of her heavy wooden clog full into the hollow behind the man’s knee.

The joint snapped like a green ash-stick.

Gault let out a high, bubbling scream and went down in the mud, his sword flying from his hand into the burning reeds.

Before the other swordsman could turn, Maren had caught up the broken half of Corin’s adze-haft from the grass. It was two feet of seasoned mountain-ash, four inches through, heavy as iron.

She swung it with both hands, using the full weight of her shoulders and the momentum of her slide down the mountain.

The blow took the second man on the iron rim of his skull-cap. The steel rang like an anvil; the man’s knees buckled beneath him, and he fell face-forward into the ditch, his sword sticking upright in the loam.

The two men with the pruning-hooks dropped their irons and turned to look at her.

She stood between them and her brother, her woolen kirtle torn to grey rags, her face covered in limestone-dust and blood, her eyes wide, white, and furious under her red hair. In her right hand she held the adze-haft; in her left she held the bone knife, its edge gleaming in the fire-light.

"Touch the posts," she whispered.

Her voice was not loud, but it had that terrible, whistling edge of a queen whose nursery has been breached.

The two carters looked at the man with the broken knee, who was thrashing in the mud with his hands clawing at his leg; they looked at the swordsman lying motionless in the ditch; and then they looked at the woods behind her.

"Corin," she said, her voice shaking. "Corin, wake."

"The Abbot has spoken, daughter," the clerk said softly.

Maren looked at him through her hair. "The Abbot?"

He looked at the twenty-four skeps on their stilts.

The bees had come out to meet the smoke.

They were not attacking; they were clustering on the front aprons in great, solid shields of bodies, their wings fanning against the heat of the fire, their breath driving the sweet scent of lime-honey and propolis into the air to overcome the stink of the burning pitch.

Not a single colony had broken. Not a single queen had fled.

"The Prior has confirmed the Freehold," the monk said, his fingers steady on Corin’s wound. "Under the Great Seal of the Abbey. You hold the mountain, Maren Vance. From the river-gravel to the high snow-line. No guild-proctor may set his foot upon the road without your leave; and the wax belongs to the altar and to the poor of the parish forever."

Maren looked down at her brother’s face.

The bleeding had checked under the monk’s oil; his eyes were clear now, looking up through the green oak boughs to where the great grey ridge of Mount Callow showed between the drifting pillars of smoke.

She took the small bone pry-knife from her belt.

She looked at the marks cut into the horn: *C. V.*, *M.*, and the straight line that was the road through the Gap.

Beneath them, with the point of the blade, she cut a fifth mark: a tiny, five-pointed star, the mark that the old beekeepers used to set upon a hive that had survived the fire, the frost, and the rot, and had lived to see the clover turn to seed.

The fire in the shed was dying now, falling into a heap of red embers that hissed as the foresters threw river-water over the cedar posts.

The sun was going down behind the mountain, and from the six leagues of forest between the river and the sea came the evening draft: cool, sweet, smelling of the white lime-bloom, of the wild mint, and of the great road that was open to all the people of the earth.

Maren helped her brother to his feet.

They stood together on the river-terrace, their hands joined, their shoulders touching, while sixty thousand bees circled above their heads in the golden dusk, their wings singing that great, endless song of the hive that has no wall, no winter, and no end.
### Chapter 25

The rebuilding of the river-camp took three weeks of the dry month that followed the hay-harvest, but they did not rebuild with cedar shakes and reed-thatch.

Hal and the quarry-men from the upper screes brought twelve sledges of grey limestone down through the Gap—great, squared blocks that had been quarried from the dry beds behind the bothy, their faces dressed with the Swedish chisels until they fit together without lime or mortar.

They built the new honey-shed four-square on the gravel terrace, thirty paces from the water, with walls three feet thick and a roof of split mountain-slate that could laugh at a thrown torch. The rafters were whole trunks of water-seasoned oak dragged from the river-shallows, their bark stripped and charred black in the hearth to keep the boring-beetles from the timber.

"No carter's hook will drag that down," Hal said, striking the stone corner-post with the flat of his hand. "A man could light a bonfire against the door-cheeks and sleep inside without coughing."

He had shaped twenty new cedar bottom-boards with his adze, cutting them two inches wider than the old pattern to give the colonies a deeper entrance-well that could be barred against mice with a perforated slip of brass.

It did not come out of the estuary; it shifted to the north-east, blowing steady and cool off the high limestone crest of Mount Callow.

The bees were not flying to the clover.

They rose from the ash aprons in tight spirals, climbing sixty feet into the clear air, their heads turned toward the mountain. They were testing the northern draft, their antennae trembling, their wings buzzing with a sharp, impatient note that set the whole terrace vibrating.

A worker had returned from the north. She had not come from the river-margins; her hair was grey with the dry stone-dust of the high cliffs, and her corbiculae were packed not with the crimson dust of the thistles or the golden flour of the lime, but with that dark, brownish-purple pollen that grew only on the screes of Orve.

The bitter-ling.

The honey it gave was not the water-clear syrup of the white clover; it was a dark, reddish-amber grease that set in the comb like jelly, smelling of peat-smoke, iron-stone, and wild thyme—the honey that had kept the valley alive through sixty turns of the cold season.

### Chapter 26

The transhumance began on the night of the full moon.

Eight timber-wains, their birch axles well-greased with sheep-tallow, stood in a line along the river-road, their teams of horned oxen standing quiet in the mist that rose from the shallows.

Maren had sealed the thirty skeps an hour after dusk.

She did not use cow-dung and ash; she used perforated slips of thin copper that Corin had beaten from the proctor’s dented pomander, their holes no larger than a grain of wheat. They allowed the night-air to move through the bottom-boards while keeping the thirty thousand inmates of each dome secure from the draft.

Over each skep she had slipped a jacket of coarse linen, bound round the skirt with a hemp cord that held the grass coils firm against the vibration of the road.

They loaded four to a wain, resting the cedar boards on thick mattresses of river-reeds, lashing the frames to the side-stakes with double turns of raw-hide that would stretch without snapping if a wheel dropped into a rut.

Kester walked at the horn of the leading ox, carrying an iron lantern with plates of shaved horn that cast a soft, yellow puddle of light across the gravel of the track.

Corin and Hal walked on the outer flank, their heavy ash levers ready to throw under an axle if a stone gave way on the ditch-side.

Maren walked alone at the rear of the train, her clay smoker in her hand, her ears tuned to the sound of the wagons.

It was a strange, marvelous music.

Beneath the creak of the birch timbers, the clatter of the iron-shod ox-shoes on the flint, and the slow, heavy breathing of the beasts, rose the murmur of the hives.

One hundred and twenty thousand bees were traveling up the mountain in the dark.

Inside the thirty domes, the clusters were not asleep. They hung from their hazel-skewers like living fruit, their bodies linked together to steady the heavy wax walls against the sway of the road. When a wheel dropped off a stone, the hum would rise in a sudden, sharp hiss of alarm—a sound like dry leaves stirred by a sudden gust—and then subside into that deep, rhythmic purr that told her the combs were sound, the honey was whole, and the queen was safe in the core.

They reached the red spurs as the first grey light was washing the black peaks of Mount Callow.

The valley below them was hidden beneath a sea of white cloud that stretched away to the fens, but here, three thousand feet above the bottoms, the air was clear, cold, and thin as spring-water.

The road wound along the eight-foot shelf, past the Shingle-Slide where Maren’s boots had ridden the shale, past the cedar-post where the old boundary had stood.

The oxen did not falter.

Their mountain lungs drank the thin air with a deep, whistling satisfaction, their cloven hooves finding the dressed flagstones of the shelf with the sure-footed confidence of beasts that were returning to their native pasture.

At the second traverse, they met the village children.

There were twenty of them, led by Elspeth and the sons of Garrow, their arms full of branches of the wild mountain-balm that they had cut from the crevices to sweep the road before the wagons.

They did not speak; they fell into step beside the wains, their bare feet making no sound on the slate, their grey eyes wide as they watched the linen-wrapped domes that held the honey of the South.

They reached the Notch an hour before noon.

The two limestone pillars stood out against the pale sky like the gateposts of a great hall whose roof had been lifted to the sun. The three chisels, carved into the stone sixty years ago, were almost hidden beneath a thick cushion of orange lichen that had spread across the rock since the sulfur-smoke had ceased to drift through the gap.

They did not set the thirty stands on the old flight-terrace.

The quarry-men had leveled a new bench fifty yards higher, on a natural shelf of limestone that hung over the Glen of Orve like a balcony, sheltered from the north gales by the great overhang of Callow’s western buttress.

Here the sun struck the stone at the third hour of dawn and stayed until the red twilight faded into the ice-peaks.

One by one, the thirty skeps were lifted from their beds of sedge and carried to the slate piers.

Maren did not draw the copper entrance-slips immediately.

She let them sit for two hours in the cold mountain air, until the bees had calmed from the motion of the wains and the cluster had reorganized itself around the brood.

Then, with the bone knife in her hand and her horsehair veil tucked into her collar, she walked the line.

She pulled the first copper slip from the seventh skep.

A single bee stepped out onto the cold slate of the apron.

She was large, jet-black, her velvet thorax shining with health, her wings whole and strong. She had traveled three leagues in the dark; she had crossed the chasm-shelf and the Notch; she had left the sweet lime-groves of the fens behind her.

She turned her head to the north.

Before her lay the Glen of Orve—three leagues of grey limestone, green runnels, and the endless, rolling purple carpet of the frost-ling that filled the cirque from the church-glebe to the screes.

The bee did not circle to orient herself.

Her ancestors had worked these rocks for three hundred years. The scent of the peat-hags, the cold mineral breath of the Marrow-Water, and the sharp, resinous bite of the ling were in her blood.

She lifted her wings, gave three short, testing beats against the cold air, and shot straight out from the slate pier, diving into the purple sea of heather like a hawk into the fern.

Within five minutes, the terrace was alive with thirty thousand workers, their flight-lines stretching across the valley like the spokes of a great wheel, their hum shaking the thin mountain air until the sheep on the commons stopped their grazing to listen to the song.

Maren stood on the stone lip of the shelf, her hand resting on the ash hoop of the mother-skep.

"They're home, Corin," she said.

"They have two homes now, Maren," her brother replied, leaning on the tail-board of the wain. "The river for the children, and the mountain for the comb."

### Chapter 27

Elder Brant died on the night the first snow touched the summit of Mount Callow.

He did not die in the dark vestry where he had barred himself with the parish rolls; he died in his own bed in the parsonage, with the south-facing shutter thrown wide so he could hear the sound of the wains climbing the Notch.

Maren sat beside his cot on an oak stool, her bone knife lying across her knees, her woolen kirtle smelling of the bitter-heather honey she had cut that morning from the high stands.

The old man’s face had lost its grey, lard-like pallor; his skin was thin, translucent as a scrap of scraped parchment, through which the blue veins showed like the rivers on the abbey map. His eyes were wide, clear, and quiet, looking out into the autumn dusk where the first large, dry flakes of snow were drifting through the gap like white bees.

"Maren," he whispered.

His voice was so low she had to bend her head until her ear was two inches from his white beard.

"I am here, Elder."

"The books," he said.

He reached out a withered hand, his fingers knotty and cold as roots, and touched the oak chest that sat beneath the window.

"The three leaves... behind the iron lock. Take them."

Maren stood up. She took the small iron church-key that hung from the bed-post, fitted it to the rusted ward of the chest, and turned the iron. The lock gave a dry, metallic groan that sounded like a sheep-shear on an anvil.

Inside lay the church silver: three small chalices of beaten tin, a pewter pyx, and a bundle of sheepskin rolls tied round with split willow.

Beneath the rolls lay a single sheet of heavy, stiff parchment, folded twice, its margins sealed with yellow beeswax that had been stamped with an eagle’s foot.

She brought it to the cot.

"Open it," Brant whispered.

She broke the wax seal with her thumbnail.

The parchment was old—older than the church itself. The ink was not the black iron-gall that the modern clerks used; it was a dark, purple vegetable dye that had sunk deep into the grain of the skin, and at the head of the leaf was drawn a picture: a bee-skep of straw, set between two mountains, with an open road running beneath the floorboards.

Beneath the drawing were four lines of writing, not in Latin, but in the old northern tongue that the shearers used for the counting of the fleeces:

*When the pass is shut, the parish dies;*  
*When the hive is burnt, the valley cries;*  
*Hold the mother on the shelf,*  
*And the mountain guards itself.*

Maren read the words twice, her lips moving without sound.

"Your great-grandfather wrote them," Brant said, his breath coming in slow, measured intervals like the ticking of an abbey clock. "He was the first of the Vance blood that came through the gap. He had the craft from the monks of the North-Riding, before the King pulled the abbeys down."

"He built the wall?"

"He built the wall to keep the soldiers out," the old man said, his eyes turning to the window where the snow was thickening against the slate sill. He reached out and caught her sleeve with sudden, fierce strength.

"We didn't shut the pass because we hated the South, Maren. We shut it because the South was an iron mill that ate our flesh and gave us back only the tax-roll and the broken bones. The bees were our shield. As long as the road was a wall of venom, no bailiff would bring his horse up the shale."

"And the sickness?" she asked softly.

"The sickness was real," Brant said, his eyes clouding for a moment. "It came to the fens forty years after the wall was set. The sickness died, and the soldiers died, and the King died... and we stayed in the ditch."

He let his hand fall back onto the sheepskin coverlet.

"We forgot," he whispered. "We forgot that a wall is only good while the wolf is in the scrub. When the wolf is dead, the wall is only a prison that keeps the sunlight from the corn."

He looked at her face, his old fingers groping across the wool until they touched the bone handle of the knife at her belt.

"You're the Warden, daughter," he said.

"The Bailiff—"

"Orde is a carter with an iron shoe," Brant spat, with a faint flare of his ancient fire. "He knows how to weigh a firkin and kick a tenant in the ditch; he doesn't know the road. "Her daughter is on the pier, Elder," Maren said, leaning over him. "And sixty thousand of her seed are in the heather."

"Good," the old man said.

### Chapter 28

The winter that followed was the first winter of the two valleys.

It was neither the endless, stony frost-verge of the high cwm nor the soggy, rotting rain-damp of the lower fens; it was a season of balance, where the two halves of the Vance freehold sustained each other like two legs under a bench.

When the great snows of the wolf-month choked the Notch, piling twelve feet deep in the gorge between the limestone pillars, the people of Low-Marrow did not sit in their dark barns eating bitter oats and tallow.

They had twenty barrels of lowland wheat stored in the Tithe-Barn, and three hogsheads of salted herrings from the abbey boats, and two hundredweight of white sea-salt in the bins that kept the mutton sweet to the bone.

And when the river-bottoms were flooded by the spring freshets, drowning the clover-flats under three feet of yellow wash, the timber-wains were already on the high road, carrying forty barrels of the dark, thick heather-honey down to the market-cross at St. Jude, where the merchants from the city of London were waiting with their leather pouches full of silver groats.

Maren lived between the two.

She spent the frost-months in the stone bothy by the Gap, where the thirty mountain-skeps slept beneath their blankets of rime, their clusters tight as boxwood balls, their breathing slow and steady in the clean, dry cold.

Every five days she walked the shelf to inspect the entrances, clearing the ice from the copper slips with her goose-wing, listening through the straw to the deep, organ-hum of the queens.

And when the hazel-catkins began to break along the river-margins in the month of the thaw, she went down to the cedar clearing, where Kester and the boys were splitting the ash-laths for fifty new spear-sedge domes.

The boy had grown to a man in the eighteen months since he carried the hides down the mountain.

He was sixteen now, his hands brown and deft with the knife, his hair bleached by the lime-sun, his mismatched leather trousers replaced by a sound pair of wool breeches dyed the dark, mossy green of the abbey foresters.

He could hive a prime swarm from a crab-apple bough without a veil, his fingers moving among the living bees with that calm, gentle rhythm that had belonged to Maren’s father.

"Look at the third stand, Maren," Kester said, pointing with his pry-knife as she came into the clearing on the morning of the equinox.

The third stand was one of the hybrid colonies—a daughter of the high mountain queen that had been mated to a drone from the yellow lowland stock of the river-orchards.

The apron was crowded.

The workers were unlike any bees that had ever flown in the north.

They had the great, broad thorax and long, sturdy legs of the mountain bees, with their thick coat of black fur that could face the mist off the glacier; but their abdomens were banded with three broad rings of bright, burnished gold, and their wings were two millimeters longer than the wild stock of the screes.

They were flying while the rime was still white on the sedge-thatches.

They rose from the ash board in hundreds, their heads turned to the east where the wild willow-copses were bursting into green leaf, their flight-lines swift, low, and direct, indifferent to the cold river-breeze that kept the native lowland bees huddled in their combs.

"The new blood," Corin said, coming down from the timber-shed with an oak bucket of warm sugar-syrup to feed the nursery.

"The bridge-bees," Maren said, her hand resting on the boy’s shoulder. "They have the mountain heart and the river wing. They can work the thistle in the frost, and they can work the lime in the heat."

She looked up the road toward the Notch.

The two limestone pillars stood out sharp against the blue sky, two leagues away and three thousand feet above them, looking no larger than two pegs in a shoe-board.

A wain was coming through the gap—a long timber-wagon drawn by four red oxen from the fens, its bed loaded with bar-iron from the northern ships, climbing the new road with the slow, steady certainty of a people who have found their way through the rock.

Beside the wain walked Donald, the new Bailiff of Low-Marrow, his ash staff tipped with a copper ferrule that flashed like a star in the morning sun.

He was not carrying an iron collar. He was carrying a small wicker cage, wrapped in damp linen, that held three young queen-mothers of the Vance line to replenish the apiaries of the outer valleys.

Maren drew her bone pry-knife from her belt.

She looked at the marks cut into the horn: the initials of her father, the name of her brother, the road through the Gap, and the five-pointed star of the hive that had survived the fire.

The bone was polished smooth, yellow as virgin comb, smelling of propolis, cedar-pitch, and sixty seasons of sweet labor.

She slipped the knife into its leather sheath at her girdle, picked up her goose-wing, and walked out onto the flight-terrace where the sixty thousand daughters of the seventh skep were rising into the golden air, their wings singing the great, open charter of the world that had no borders, no prisons, and no end.
### Chapter 29

The second year of the open road brought the great gathering of the combs, a harvest unlike any the high cwm had known since the first stones of the boundary dyke were hewn from the living limestone.

In the old days, the harvest had been a grim, furtive labor carried out under the grey shroud of the frost-verge, when the sheep-bells were silenced by the thickening rime and the people of Low-Marrow huddled over their peat-braziers waiting for the Bailiff to announce the short-weight of the winter tithe. It had been measured in ounces of bitter, resinous scrapings, torn from starved colonies with iron hooks, smelling of sulfur and the despair of a parish that had eaten its seed-barley before the midwinter moon.

Now, the harvest began at the river-terrace three weeks before the ling opened on the mountain screes.

Maren stood on the stone threshold of the new honey-house, her apron of heavy sail-cloth white with chalk-dust and beeswax. Through the broad oak doors, thrown wide to the morning breeze that rolled off the fens, came the rhythmic, heavy clatter of the two screw-presses that Hal and the quarry-men had mounted on foundations of ash-timber three feet into the gravel.

They were presses of a new pattern, such as the cider-makers of the southern shires used, their screws carved from whole trunks of wild service-tree by the turners of Saint-Denis, their heavy iron plates pierced with hundreds of hair-fine holes that let the pure, golden nectar run free while holding back the wax and the crushed cocoons.

Kester worked the eastern press, his bare torso glistening with sweat, his leather-bound hands throwing his weight against the four-foot ash lever at every turn.

"Down, boy," Corin commanded from the vat-side, his hand resting on the copper rim of the settling-pan. "Ease the screw when the resistance hardens. If you crush the comb to paste, the pollen-milk clouds the run, and Brother Paul will dock three pence a stone for the clearing."

The boy eased his pressure, the great wooden screw creaking with a dull, oily groan as the plate settled against the linen cheese-cloths.

From the bottom of the press, through a spout of tinned iron, poured a solid, shining rope of honey three inches thick. It did not run like the thin mountain-drainage; it fell with a heavy, folded ribboning, piling upon itself in the vat like molten amber before smoothing out into a mirror that caught the yellow morning sun from the doorway.

It was the honey of the lime-bloom and the wild river-clover, clear as water drawn from a chalk-spring, carrying on its surface that delicate, pale foam that the old beekeepers called the *bee-cream*.

Maren dipped a clean hazel lath into the stream, lifted it, and held it horizontally before her face.

The honey did not drop from the wood in round beads; it hung in a broad, glassy sheet that held its edge for six heartbeats before tearing into three slow, globular tears that smelled of mint, vanilla, and the sweet breath of twenty leagues of flowering forest.

"Nineteen degrees of sugar," she said, touching the edge of the sheet to her tongue.

"Will it keep the winter without souring?" Corin asked, looking up from the copper pan.

"It will keep until your grandchildren have white beards," she told him. "The water is less than a fifth part of the weight. You could drop an iron bodkin into that vat and it would take ten breaths to touch the bottom-boards."

She walked to the settling-tanks—six great tuns of seasoned oak that had been washed with boiling water and scrubbed with crushed elder-leaves to remove the tannic bite of the wood.

In these the honey rested for three days in the cool dark of the stone walls, while the microscopic bubbles of air and the stray grains of golden pollen floated to the top, forming a dense, velvety crust that was skimmed off with broad horn spatulas before the clear liquor was drawn off through the bottom-plugs into the firkins.

Outside, in the shade of the great linden-trees, the carts were waiting.

They were not the rough, two-wheeled sledges of Low-Marrow; they were four-wheeled road-wains from the market-towns, their high ash-spoked wheels bound with hoops of Swedish iron, their canvas tilts bleached white as sea-foam in the sun.

At the head of the foremost team sat Hobb the muleteer, smoking an old clay pipe filled with dried coltsfoot, his three yellow teeth gripping the reed stem with stubborn contentment. Beside him sat two carters from the cathedral city of York, their blue wool jerkins marked on the breast with the keys of Saint Peter, their teams of heavy roan horses snorting at the smell of the bees that drifted from the terrace.

"Thirty firkins of the white run, mistress," Hobb called out, touching his greasy oilskin cap with the knuckle of his bridle-hand. "And twelve loaves of the sun-bleached wax for the Minster choir. The Dean’s proctor sent the gold by the York post; four nobles and eight silver groats, counted into the Prior’s iron box at St. Jude."

"The wax is on the drying-hurdles," Maren said, walking to the low hurdles that stood in the center of the clover-pasture.

Here, sixty loaves of pure wax, each weighing twenty pounds, were laid out under the open sky to catch the summer sun. They were not yellow; they had been shaved into thin ribbons with a double-handled draw-knife, melted three times over rain-water, and exposed to the dew and the sun until the last trace of the heather-bitter and the iron-grease had been bleached away.

They were white as lard, hard as horn, their edges ringing like bell-metal when struck with the bone knife, smelling only of clean wax-scales and the cold mountain air that had chilled them on the high shelf.

Hobb climbed down from his seat, his leather gaiters creaking, and picked up one of the loaves.

He held it up against the morning sun; the light passed through the edges in a clear, translucent glow, without a single streak of dark dross or speck of charcoal.

"The chandlers in the city are crying like whipped hounds," the old carrier said, a greasy chuckle shaking his belly. "Varner has three warehouses full of tallow-grease that he can't sell to a stable-boy for axle-wax. The bishops won't look at his dips; the priors send his carters away from the gates with a curse, and the King’s customs-officer told him yesterday that if he caught him mixing bone-grease with the altar-wax again, he’d have his ears nailed to the pillory at the castle-ditch."

"Varner is welcome to his tallow," Corin said, coming out with an ash cooper’s adze in his hand. "Let him grease the wain-axles with it. The Church has the bee-wax, and the people have the light."

He turned to the river-bank, where forty young men and girls from the high valley were loading the empty firkins onto the rafts.

They were no longer the starved, hollow-eyed serfs who had cowered behind the sheep-hurdles while the Bailiff weighed their oats. They were brown-armed, broad-shouldered, their linen shirts clean and white, their voices rising across the water in the old mountain songs that had once been whispered only in the dark corners of the turf-houses.

And as the noon-bell from the distant abbey tower came floating up the river on the summer breeze, the great rafts pushed off from the gravel spit, their long steering-oars bending against the current as they began their voyage down to the broad estuaries and the open sea.

### Chapter 30

By the middle of the harvest-month, the trade of the four shires had found its way to the foot of Mount Callow.

It was not merely the honey and the wax that drew the wains through the southern woods; it was the stone.

The blue limestone of the high cirque—the dense, fine-grained marble that had chilled the bees through sixty years of the frost-verge—was discovered to have a virtue that no lowland freestone possessed. When burned in the great kilns that Garrow and his sons had built along the red spurs, it produced a lime so pure, so white, and so fiercely hydraulic that it would set under running water, hard as iron, within three tides.

The builders of the new cathedral at Lincoln sent their master-mason with three carts to purchase forty tons of the high lime; the harbor-masters of the south-coast ports sent their agents to buy the great squared blocks for the sea-jetties; and the weavers of the West-Riding came up the road with pack-trains of fine wool to trade for the white mountain-tallow that cleaned their looms without spotting the fleece.

A market-cross had been erected at the two limestone pillars of the Notch.

It was not a cross of wood or soft sandstone; Hal the quarry-man had carved it from a single five-ton rib of the blue limestone, nine feet high, its shaft four-square, its arms terminating in carved clusters of hazel-nuts and clover-leaves.

On the base, where the three ancient chisels had marked the boundary of fear, he had cut the new covenant of the pass:

*The road is free to all who bring the honest weight;*  
*The stone belongs to the quarry;*  
*The wax belongs to the light;*  
*And the bee belongs to no man.*

Around the base of the cross, three days a week, the market was held.

The women of Low-Marrow came up the eight-foot shelf with baskets of fresh mountain-butter wrapped in docks, cheeses cured in the dark cellars of the shielings, and hanks of fine yarn dyed with the red thistle and the birch-bark.

From the South came the ironmongers with their nails, their scythe-blades, and their pots of tinned brass; the colliers with their sacks of pit-coal from the western seams; and the book-sellers from the universities, who brought books of white rag-paper printed with the new metal types from Germany, bound in calf-skin and sealed with the clear, aromatic wax of the Vance apiaries.

Maren sat on the stone bench beneath the cross, her bone knife in her lap, watching the people move between the wains.

She was seventeen now.

Her frame had filled out, her shoulders broad and straight as an ash-pole, her face quiet with that deep, inward stillness that comes to those who have spent their youth listening to the voices of the hives. She wore her sheepskin vest only when the night-wind blew off the snow-line; her kirtle was of dark blue wool spun in the village and dyed with the lowland woad, but she still wore her wooden clogs, their soles shod with the iron plates that her father had ground on his stone.

Beside her sat a young woman from the fens—a girl of fifteen named Cecily, the daughter of a parchment-maker from the Weald, whom Brother Paul had sent up the mountain to learn the craft of the wax-clearing.

Cecily held a shallow wooden dish of warm honey-wash on her knees, watching with round, fascinated eyes as three worker-bees drank from the rim, their tongues pumping the amber fluid into their honey-sacs with rhythmic, eager strokes.

"They don't sting, Maren," the girl whispered, her breath held tight.

"Why should they sting?" Maren asked, her fingers gently smoothing the bee-down on the thorax of the nearest worker. "You have no smoke on your breath; you have no grease on your hands, and you sit quiet as a stone. A bee does not strike for malice, Cecily. She strikes only when she thinks the house is falling or the mother is in the dark."

"In the Weald," the girl said, looking down the long shelf toward the distant blue line of the fens, "they say the mountain-bees are devils. They say they were bred by the old hermits out of adders’ venom and horse-dung, and that they will follow a thief three leagues into the marsh to put their barbs through his eyeballs."

Maren laughed—a low, clear sound that had in it the music of the hazel-catkins in the spring wind.

"They are not devils, child," she said. "They are the daughters of the limestone. They know the names of the flowers, and they know the road through the clouds; that is all the magic they have. If a thief comes with an honest heart, they will let him drink from the apron; if a king comes with an iron hook, they will give him the wall."

She looked down the road toward the Glen of Orve.

The old valley was no longer a grey ditch of despair.

The stone barns of Low-Marrow had been whitewashed with the new lime, their roofs re-thatched with clean water-reed from the fens that shone like gold in the afternoon sun. Along the runnels of the Marrow-Water, where the bitter-ling had once been the only pasture, the red thistles and the mountain-balm were growing three feet high, their blossoms covered in a shimmering haze of workers that hummed with the contentment of a house whose cellars are full to the eaves.

In the middle of the village, where the old tithe-barn had stood like an iron prison, the people had built a new hall of dressed limestone, its floor paved with slate, its windows open to the South to let in the light and the warm mountain-air.

And through the Gap, in an endless, peaceful procession, the wains went down and the wains came up, carrying the breath of the two worlds back and forth across the stone that had once been an iron door.

### Chapter 31

In the third week of the month of the harvest, when the high screes were purple with the last of the ling and the nights were sharp with the promise of the mountain frost, Maren and Corin walked up to the High Cirque above Mount Callow.

It was an expedition they had planned since the morning the old queen died, but the work of the road, the building of the sheds, and the defense of the freehold had kept them tethered to the lower benches.

They took no mules.

They carried only their light ash pack-frames, loaded with three loaves of hard rye-bread, a piece of salt-mutton, their leather water-bottles filled with the hazel-spring water, and the two copper smokers mended with the abbey-calfskin.

At her girdle, Maren wore her bone pry-knife; at his, Corin carried his adze and a sixty-foot coil of light hemp rope that had been boiled in beeswax to make it supple against the frost.

They left the Notch at the second watch of dawn, while the stars were still bright over the limestone buttresses.

The track—if it could be called a track—wound upward behind the stone bothy, following a natural fault in the rock that climbed by long, dizzying shelves across the sheer northern face of Callow.

Here the dwarf willows could find no purchase. The rock was bare, polished smooth by ten thousand years of glacial ice, veined with white quartz and blue fluor-spar that glittered in the starlight like frost.

The air was thin, cold, and fierce.

At every twenty strides they had to stop, their chests heaving, their mouths open to draw the thin, icy vapor that smelled of nothing—no peat, no loam, no leaves—only the vast, ancient mineral silence of the mountain that had never known the foot of a horse or the sound of an iron bell.

By noon they reached the lip of the Cirque.

It was a great, round bowl of stone, a mile across, carved out of the heart of the mountain three thousand feet above the Gap. Its floor was a flat sheet of blue ice, snow-crusted at the margins, fed by three small hanging glaciers that clung to the upper cliffs like frozen waterfalls.

And around the southern rim of the bowl, where the limestone wall faced the midday sun and was sheltered from the polar gales by a projecting rib of granite, the cliff was broken into thousands of horizontal clefts.

Maren stopped. Her hand went to her brother’s arm.

"Listen," she whispered.

Corin stood motionless, his head tilted back, the pack-frame creaking against his shoulders.

At first there was only the wind—a low, hollow sigh that moved across the ice-sheet like the breath of a sleeping beast.

Then, faint at first, like the sound of a distant bell heard across three leagues of marsh, it came.

A hum.

Not the thin, frantic piping of a queenless colony; not the heavy, sluggish roar of the river-stands gorged on the linden-nectar.

It was a sound that had the weight of the mountain itself: deep, resonant, ancient as the rock, vibrating through the limestone beneath their boots until the soles of their feet tingled with the rhythm.

"The wild mothers," Corin whispered, his grey eyes wide with awe.

They walked along the southern shelf, their boots crunching on the frozen scree, until they stood beneath the honeycomb cliffs.

Here, in the dry, protected crevices where the snow never drifted, were the nests.

They were not made of rye-straw or spear-sedge; they were built of the pure, dark stone-wax that the bees had drawn against the blue limestone itself.

Great curtains of comb, five feet long and three feet through the middle, hung from the roofs of the clefts like the flutings of a church organ, their lower edges rounded and smooth, black with the propolis of a thousand seasons.

The bees were everywhere.

They were not yellow; they were not banded with gold.

They were the pure, primeval race of the high peaks: great, black, hairy insects with wings that had an oily, blue sheen like the surface of tempered steel. They moved across the rock with a slow, majestic deliberation, indifferent to the freezing draft that blew off the glacier, their bodies packed tight around the deep fissures where the winter clusters were already forming.

Maren knelt on a slab of fallen stone beneath the central cleft.

She did not draw her smoker; she did not reach for her veil.

She extended her bare arm, resting her palm flat upon the cold limestone three inches from the entrance of the largest nest.

A guard-bee stepped out from the dark.

She was half again the size of the workers on the river-terrace. Her thorax was covered in a dense ruff of silver-grey down that looked like the pelt of an otter, her antennae long, thick, and black, testing the air with slow, sweeping arcs.

She walked onto Maren’s wrist.

The bee’s feet were cold, like tiny points of ice, but as she reached the hollow over Maren’s pulse, she stopped.

She did not raise her abdomen to sting; she did not fan her wings in alarm.

She lowered her head, extended her long, dark tongue, and began to drink the sweat that was dried into the salt-lines of Maren’s skin.

A second worker followed her, then three, then ten, until Maren’s forearm was covered in a black, quivering bracelet of living velvet, their wings humming that great, deep note that was the foundation of all the hives in the world.

Corin knelt beside her, his breath showing white against the dark rock.

"This is where he found her," he said softly.

"Our father?"

"Our grandfather," Corin said. "And his father before him. They came up to the Cirque every seven years, Maren. Not to take the honey; you couldn't take ten pounds of comb from these cliffs without twenty men and five hundred cubits of rope. They came to fetch the virgin queens."

He pointed to the base of the cleft, where three small, rounded cups of wax, no larger than acorns, hung from the lower edge of the comb.

"They took one cell," he said. "Only one. They carried it down in the alder-box, wrapped in their own wool, and they gave it to a colony that had grown soft in the valley. That was the covenant, Maren. To keep the wild iron in the blood, so the bees would never forget how to face the winter."

Maren looked up at the great limestone wall that rose five hundred feet above their heads into the turquoise sky.

She felt small—no larger than a grain of rye dropped into the shingle—and yet she felt anchored, held by a chain of living bodies that stretched back through the centuries to the time before the mountains had names.

She knew now that the seventh skep had not been an accident.

It had not been a wall built by fear; it had been an outpost of this ancient, unconquerable life, set down at the gate of the valley to remind the people that the world was larger than their barns, harder than their laws, and sweeter than their salt.

She waited until the guard-bees had finished their drink and had crawled back into the warm dark of the fissure.

Then she took from her pack-frame a small piece of the bleached river-wax from the linden-harvest—white, pure, and sweet with the scent of the southern clover.

She laid it on the stone shelf at the mouth of the cleft: a gift from the river to the mountain, an earnest that the road between them would never again be barred by fire, by fear, or by the forgetfulness of men.

The great guard-bee turned on the rock, touched the white wax with its antennae, tested the sweetness with its tongue, and then, with three slow strokes of its great blue wings, dragged the treasure into the dark of the cleft to feed the mothers of the snow.

### Chapter 32

The third spring came to the Low-Reach not with the roaring flood of the first year, but with a gentle, soaking rain that broke the bud-sheaths of the oaks and turned the clover-meadows to a sea of emerald green within ten days of the equinox.

At the river-camp, the training of the new beekeepers was in full swing.

There were twelve apprentices now: six lads from the high shielings of Orve, and six from the abbey manors of the fens, living together in the long stone dormitory that Hal had built behind the honey-shed.

They wore the same clothes: coarse linen shifts, breeches of moss-green wool, and aprons of split calf-skin that had been rubbed with birch-tar and propolis to keep the bees from taking offense at the smell of human sweat.

Maren stood in the center of the terrace, surrounded by the lads, her smoker cold in her hand.

On the bench before them sat an empty grass skep, five hazel skewers, and a bundle of split ash-ties that had been soaked in water to make them flexible.

"Show me the crown-turn, Kester," she said.

The young man stepped forward. His hands, once clumsy with the tanner’s shears, moved with the swift, effortless grace of an artist.

He took the thick rope of spear-sedge, bent the first three inches into a tight, circular knot no larger than a walnut, and bound it round with a strip of ash-bark, driving the split withe through the center with a polished bone bodkin.

"Tight, lads," Kester explained, turning the coil for the boys to see. "If the crown has a crevice no wider than a barley-corn, the rain will find it in the winter, and the damp will curdle the nursery. The first three coils are the foundation of the house; if they are loose, the whole dome will twist when the honey gets heavy on the pins."

He added a second coil, feeding the sedge from his left hand while his right worked the bodkin, the ash-binding clicking against the grass with a rhythmic, shoemaker’s snap.

Maren watched him, her heart quiet with that deep satisfaction that comes to a master who sees his craft pass whole and sound into the hands of the young.

She turned to a lad of fourteen—little Robin, the youngest son of Garrow the lime-burner, who had lost two fingers to a rock-fall in the quarry three summers ago.

"Robin," she said. "What is the three-day rule?"

The boy stood straight, his face reddening beneath his freckles, his single good hand tucked into his apron-string.

"The three-day rule, mistress," the lad recited, his voice clear and proud. "If a queen be lost or trodden in the comb, the nurse-bees must have a grub that has not seen three suns from the egg. If the grub be four suns old, she has eaten the worker-bread, and her body will not take the royal jelly; she will grow to be half-mother and half-worker, with small wings and dry vessels, and she will die in her cell without a pipe."

"And if you have no three-day grub?"

"Then you take a comb from the mother-stand," Robin said instantly. "A comb with the fresh eggs standing upright in the cell-bottoms like little candles. You cut a three-cornered window in the wax, you shave the lower walls so the grubs have room to hang their cradles down, and you give it to the cluster in the dark."

"Good," Maren said, her hand resting on the boy’s curly red head. "You have the ear, Robin. In two seasons you shall have four stands of your own on the red spurs, to hold by the service of six pounds of wax to the school at Low-Marrow."

Corin came down the path from the timber-copse, leading two pack-mules that were loaded with crates of fresh cedar bottom-boards.

He had laid aside his adze; he wore a coat of fine grey cloth with buttons of turned horn, and at his belt hung the ink-horn and the parchment-roll of the Bailiff’s deputy—an office that the Council of the Marches had confirmed upon him by the unanimous voice of the two valleys.

He stopped by the bench, looking at the boys with a warm smile that crinkled the silver scar on his temple.

"The Bishop’s proctor is at the ferry, Maren," he said.

Maren looked up from the skep. "A new proctor?"

"Not a chandler," Corin said, laughing softly. "A scholar from the King’s college at Oxford. Master Linacre. He has three mules loaded with books, and he says he has come to see the girl who keeps the bees without a brimstone-pot."

"Tell him the bees are working the willow," she said. "If he wants to see them, he must come to the terrace on his own feet and leave his Latin in his saddle-bags."

The scholar came an hour later.

He was a little, dried-up man of fifty, with eyes as bright and inquisitive as a robin’s, wearing a scholar’s gown of rusty black cloth that had been mended with blue thread at the elbows. He did not bring an armed beadle; he brought an ink-horn, a bundle of goose-quills, and a great blank book bound in pig-skin that smelled of cedar-oil and old rag-paper.

He sat on an oak billet at the edge of the terrace for three hours, without speaking, his spectacles pushed high on his wrinkled brow, watching Maren and the boys work the seventy-two stands.

He watched her lift a skep from its stilts with bare hands; he watched her part the combs with her bone pry-knife to inspect the brood; and he watched forty thousand bees circle her head without a single worker lowering its abdomen to strike.

When the sun began to drop behind Mount Callow, casting the great purple shadow of the mountain across the river, the scholar stood up and walked to the bench.

He dipped his quill into the ink-horn and held the book out to her.

"Mistress Vance," he said, his voice soft, courteous, and full of that scholarly wonder that looks at the world as if it were a newly-opened letter. "I have read Aristotle’s book of the insects; I have read Pliny’s chapters on the commonwealth of the comb; and I have read the treatises of the Arabic masters of Cordova."

He turned the parchment leaf toward her.

On it he had drawn, with exquisite, hair-fine strokes of his pen, the flight-apron of the seventh skep, with the young queen walking among her daughters, and the two limestone pillars of the Notch showing between the oak boughs in the distance.

"None of them knew," the scholar whispered.

"Knew what, master?" Maren asked, wiping her fingers on her apron.

"They thought the hive was an empire," he said. "They thought there was a King who gave the orders, who sat upon an iron throne in the center of the comb and sent the workers out with whips to gather the tithe. They thought the bees were soldiers who fought for glory and obeyed the laws of a prince."

He pointed his quill at the great cloud of workers that was dropping into the grass domes in the twilight.

"They did not know that the hive is a mother," he said. "That she does not rule; she only loves. That she does not command; she only bears. And that the law of the comb is not the law of the sword, but the law of the bread and the cradle."

He closed the book with a soft, reverent snap.

"Will you write your name upon my page, Maren Vance?"

Maren looked at the white parchment, then at her own hands—dark, scarred, calloused by the knife and the ash-ties, stained with the indelible yellow propolis that had sunk so deep into her pores that no lye-soap could ever wash it away.

She took the quill from his fingers.

She did not know the letters that the clerks drew in their Latin books; but she knew the marks that her father had cut into the bone handle of the knife.

With a steady, unhurried hand, she drew upon the corner of the pig-skin page:

*A straight line for the road;*  
*A circle for the dome;*  
*And a five-pointed star for the hive that had broken the gate.*

The scholar looked at the three marks, then bowed to her as low as if she had been the Queen of England sitting upon her throne of gold at Westminster.

"It is enough," he said. "The mark will stand when the cathedral of Saint-Denis is a heap of gravel in the fens."

### Chapter 33

The third autumn brought the great reconciliation to the church of St. Jude-in-the-Gap.

It was held on the feast of Saint Michael, the day when the ancient tithes had been paid to the stone bench since the time of the third Earl.

The church had been cleaned.

The green moss had been scraped from the slate roof; the narrow slit-windows had been glazed with small, diamond-shaped panes of green glass brought up from the Weald by the wains; and the great oak door stood wide to the autumn sun, its rusted iron bolts removed and hung above the lintel as a memorial of the days of the wall.

The churchyard was full of people.

Three hundred folk from the high shielings of Orve, and two hundred from the lower parishes of the river, stood together among the limestone headstones, their cloaks of grey and blue wool mingled like the clouds on the mountain.

In the chancel sat the Prior of St. Jude-in-the-Fens, his white Cistercian habit gleaming in the light of sixty candles of pure mountain-wax that burned before the altar without smoke, without guttering, and without the reek of animal fat.

Beside him sat Bailiff Donald, his copper-tipped staff resting between his knees, and Brother Paul, his brass spectacles shining on his long nose as he turned the leaves of the new parish register.

There was no Bailiff Orde.

The old man had died in the winter, peaceful in his bed, his swollen jaw having shrunk back to its natural shape before the end; he had been buried in the churchyard beside Maren’s father, under a flat slab of blue limestone that bore no name, only the carving of a worker-bee with its wings spread out for flight.

Corin and Maren walked up the center aisle.

They were not dressed in velvet or fine lawn; they wore their clean mountain wool, their wooden clogs clicking with that familiar, steady cadence on the flags.

Between them they carried the great harvest-offering: not four small firkins of bitter heather-grease, but a chest of seasoned cedar-wood, three feet long, bound with straps of polished Swedish iron.

Inside lay eighty loaves of the white river-wax, and six stone jars of the clear linden-honey, sealed with the abbey’s mark.

They set the chest on the altar-step, at the Prior’s feet.

The Prior stood up. He was an old man, eighty years of age, his face furrowed like an old plow-land, his grey eyes quiet and merciful beneath his white cowl.

He did not touch the silver chalice; he reached out his hands and laid them upon the heads of the young man and the girl as they knelt before the step.

"In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost," the Prior said, his voice filling the low stone vault like the rumble of the river. "We declare that the parish of St. Jude-in-the-Gap is no longer an out-parish of the desert, but a mother-church of the Marches forever."

He turned to the people who stood crowded in the nave.

"For sixty years," the old priest said, "you lived in the dark of your own fear. You built a wall of rock, and you thought the rock was your friend; you built a wall of stings, and you thought the poison was your peace. You ate the dry oats and you drank the bitter water, and you turned your faces from your brethren because you believed that the world outside was full of death."

He reached down and lifted from the chest one of the loaves of white wax, holding it high in both hands so the light from the south window struck through it like an amber sun.

"Behold the light of the rock," he cried. "It was not born of hatred; it was born of the flowers that grow upon the precipice. It was not gathered by the sword; it was gathered by the smallest of God’s creatures, who know no boundaries, who pay no toll to the prince, and who seek only the sweetness that is hidden in the stone."

He set the wax back upon the cedar wood.

"The wall is broken," he said. "The gate is wide. Go back to your houses, people of the two valleys; eat your white bread with the honey of the lime, and let your children learn the song of the road that has no end."

The people did not shout.

They fell upon their knees on the cold flagstones, their heads bowed, while from the belfry above them the great iron bell—re-cast at the town forge with three hundredweight of new copper—began to ring.

It was no longer the cracked, dry cough of a frightened parish.

It was a deep, bronze roar that rolled out through the church door, across the churchyard, over the red spurs, and up the eight-foot shelf of the mountain, echoing against the great cliffs of Mount Callow until the high glaciers themselves seemed to answer with the music of the bells.

### Chapter 34

The sun was an hour from setting when Maren walked down to the river-bank alone.

The market-wains had all departed; the apprentices were in the stone dormitory, their voices rising in the evening hymn as they cleaned their knives by the light of the wax-dips; and Corin was in the honey-house, writing the tallies of the winter stores in the great ledger that the Oxford scholar had given him.

The river was low, clear as glass, flowing with a soft, liquid murmur over the beds of golden sand between the roots of the ancient willows.

She walked to the gravel bar where the red quartz pebble lay in the grass.

The stone had not moved in three years.

The winter floods had washed around it, polishing its rounded flanks until the red quartz shone like an ember in the twilight; the wild mint had grown around its base, forming a thick, aromatic cushion of purple flowers that were covered in the evening dew.

Maren knelt in the grass.

She did not draw her knife; she laid her hands flat upon the cold red stone, feeling the pulse of the river vibrating through the quartz.

"The seventy stands are full, mother," she whispered.

A worker-bee, late from the ivy-bloom on the limestone cliffs, landed on the edge of the stone.

She was one of the hybrid daughters—large, broad-thoraxed, her abdomen banded with gold, her legs heavy with the pale, green pollen of the wild ivy.

She did not take wing into the dusk.

She crawled down from the quartz, found the skin of Maren’s thumb, and settled herself in the hollow of her hand, tucking her wings close to her back as the night-draft began to stir the leaves of the willows.

She was cold. Her six legs moved slowly, her antennae drooping over her eyes as the mountain-chill overtook the river-valley.

Maren did not shake her off.

She brought her other hand over the bee, cupping her palms together to form a small, dark house of living skin, breathing into the narrow space between her thumbs to give the insect the heat of her own lungs.

Within her hands, the bee stirred.

She felt the tiny claws catch in the callouses of her palms; she felt the wings stretch, the abdomen begin its slow, rhythmic pumping, and then, from the dark between her fingers, came that sound:

The hum.

Not the roar of fifty thousand workers in a cedar dome; not the piping of a virgin on her wedding-flight.

Just the single, quiet, patient murmur of a daughter who knows that her mother is near, that the house is dry, and that the dawn will come again over the heather.

Maren looked up through the willow branches.

High above the river, three thousand feet in the purple sky, the two limestone pillars of the Notch caught the last red beam of the sunken sun.

They did not look like iron teeth. They did not look like the posts of a prison.

They looked like two fingers lifted in blessing over the two valleys, marking the place where a girl of sixteen had followed a grain of red thistle-dust through the clouds, and had found that the world had no borders save those that love had not yet had the courage to cross.

She opened her hands.

The bee climbed to the tip of her forefinger, paused for three breaths to taste the evening air, and then, with a sharp, joyful buzz that was swallowed instantly by the murmur of the water, rose into the twilight, heading straight for the warmth of the seventy-second stand.

Maren stood up.

She wiped the river-sand from her knees, pulled her blue woolen kirtle straight against the autumn wind, and turned back toward the light of the stone house, where the fire was burning sweet and clear on the hearth of her father’s children.
### Chapter 35

The winter that closed the third year did not bring the silence of death; it brought the quiet of a house that has finished its work before the dark.

In the Glen of Orve, the snow fell early, laying two cubits of soft, white wool across the screes before the moon of the dead was full. But it was not the grey, stony frost-verge of the old times.

The smoke that rose from the sixty stone chimneys of Low-Marrow was white, sweet with the scent of seasoned birch and cedar-billets that had been hauled up the eight-foot shelf during the autumn carting. In every cellar sat three barrels of the white pan-salt, two casks of the clear linden-honey, and forty bushels of the lowland wheat that the village girls ground in the water-mill without fear of the Bailiff’s toll-dish.

The sheep in the lower folds were fat.

They had been drenched twice more with the warm propolis-butter before the rime touched their fleeces, and though the wind off Mount Callow blew with its ancient, bitter roar, not a single ewe dropped her lamb in the mire, and not a single wether showed the black discharge in its eyes.

Maren spent the month of the wolf in the stone bothy by the Notch.

She lived alone, as her father had lived, but she was not an exile.

Every three days, the boys from the lower dormitory would come up the road with their snow-shovels, clearing the drifts from the eight-foot shelf, bringing her fresh loaves of the white wheat-bread from the abbey ovens and stone jugs of milk from the village goats.

She had thirty stands on the limestone shelf behind the bothy.

They were buried to their straw crowns in the dry, powdery snow that had blown off the glacier, but inside the spear-sedge domes, the bees were safe.

The snow was not their enemy; it was their blanket.

It kept the bitter drafts from the bottom-boards, maintaining the air within the domes at a steady, silent cold that allowed the clusters to hang from their hazel-skewers without burning their winter stores in frantic shivering.

Maren would walk the terrace at the second watch of dawn, her snow-shoes of bent ash and raw-hide clicking softly upon the crust.

She did not carry a shovel.

She carried only her goose-wing and an ash lath, three feet long, with a small iron scraper bound to the tip with waxed thread.

She would kneel by each skep, clear the drift from the copper entrance-slip with the feathers, and slide the ash lath an inch into the dark interior.

When she drew the lath out, she held it to her nose.

If it smelled of sweet wax, dried mint, and the dry, powdery residue of healthy combs, she nodded and smoothed the snow back around the skirt.

If she found three dead workers on the scraper, she examined their abdomens by the light of the morning stars.

They were dry, hollow, their honey-sacs empty: the old foragers who had spent their strength in the heather-flow and had crawled down to the bottom-board to die in the cold, so that their decaying bodies would not foul the nursery in the spring.

At the seventh skep—the new dome that housed the direct daughter of the ancient mother—she stopped longest.

She did not use the lath.

She leaned her temple against the rough rye-straw of the dome, right beneath the overhanging thatch of mountain-sedge, and pulled her woolen hood back from her ear.

She listened.

At first, through the three inches of straw and the two feet of snow, there was only the vast, rushing murmur of the mountain gale thirty feet above her head.

Then, from the very center of the dome, six inches above the slate flag, it rose:

*Zzz-mmm-zzz.*

A single, continuous, breathing chord.

It was not loud; it was no louder than the sound of a kettle coming to the boil across a quiet room, or the murmur of the river through the reeds on a summer noon.

It was the sound of forty thousand hearts beating together in the dark.

In the center of that cluster, she knew, walked the young queen.

Her abdomen was small now, resting from the long summer’s laying, her wings folded flat along her dark, copper-banded back. Surrounding her were three layers of nurse-bees, their heads turned inward toward her body, their legs linked together, feeding her drops of the thick, dark heather-honey from their own crops, shielding her from the draft with their own flesh.

Every twelve hours, the cluster rotated.

The cold bees from the outer shell, their bodies chilled almost to the point of numbness by the mountain-frost, would crawl slowly inward toward the warm core, while the warm, well-fed workers from the center would move out to take their places on the frozen surface of the ball.

There was no master. There was no overseer.

Each insect knew its hour; each bee gave its heat to the mother and took its turn upon the wall, until the cold passed and the sun returned to the high pass.

Maren straightened her back, her breath rising in a straight, white pillar into the turquoise sky.

"Rest now, little mothers," she whispered, her words lost in the wind that came off the peak. "The ling is sleeping under the drift, and the snow has no teeth for the house that has the comb."

### Chapter 36

The spring of the fourth year broke with a sudden, astonishing magnificence that brought the tears to the eyes of the oldest shearers in Low-Marrow.

It was not a gradual loosening of the frost; it was a warm, south-western gale that came out of the sea-marshes three weeks before the feast of the Annunciation, carrying on its wings the heat of the great southern forests and the smell of twenty leagues of salt-water.

The snow did not melt by inches; it rotted.

The drifts on the high screes collapsed into roaring torrents of clear, turquoise water that plunged over the limestone shelves into the gorges, their spray throwing rainbows across the eight-foot road that stood firm upon its Swedish iron pins.

The river rose, but it did not breach the stone terrace; the great limestone walls of the new honey-house stood like a pier against the yellow wash, directing the current into the deep channel between the willows.

And on the morning of the equinox, the great marriage of the hives began.

Maren stood on the stone lip of the Notch, her horsehair veil laid aside forever, her red-gold hair tied back with a strip of white calf-skin that had been blessed by the Prior at the altar-foot.

Beside her stood Corin, his leather coat open to the warm wind, his grey eyes shining as he looked down the long road toward the fens.

The sixty skeps on the high shelf and the seventy-two stands by the river had broken their clusters together.

It was not a drift; it was not a flight of despair.

It was the great prime-flow of the renewed lineage.

From the valley below rose a cloud of gold—hundreds of thousands of workers from the river-stands, their yellow-banded abdomens flashing in the sun like sparks from a forge, climbing the three thousand feet of mountain-road in a broad, shimmering highway of wings.

And from the high shelf dropped the dark daughters of the stone—the great, black, otter-furred bees of the ancient screes, their blue-sheened wings carrying them down to the wild cherry-copses that were bursting into snow-white bloom along the red spurs.

They met at the two limestone pillars of the Gap.

They did not fight.

The bees of the mountain and the bees of the plain mingled in the throat of the pass in a vast, spiraling dance forty yards wide, circling the carved limestone cross that Hal had raised above the boundary.

The air was so thick with them that the two pillars seemed to tremble, their grey surfaces softened by the living velvet that crawled across the ancient chisel-marks, tasting the orange lichen, testing the new wind that blew through the cleft without a check.

The sound was not an alarm.

It was a hymn that filled the defile from the screes to the snow-line: that great, deep-throated organ-note of a world whose doors have been unhung, whose rivers have found the sea, and whose people have ceased to fear the harvest of the earth.

A party of riders came up the road from the South.

They were not soldiers with iron caps and yew bows; they were merchants from the great wool-staple of Flanders, their mules loaded with bolts of fine blue cloth, barrels of Spanish wine, and rolls of white paper from the Rhine.

At their head rode Lord Malmes, his velvet cloak discarded for a coat of dark hunting-leather, his silver chain gleaming against his chest.

He reined his horse thirty paces from the pillars, taking off his broad-brimmed beaver hat to let the mountain-wind cool his brow.

He looked at the great cloud of bees that hung like a golden arch above the road; he looked at the stone bothy where the apprentices were oiling the cart-harness; and then he looked at Maren Vance, who stood upon the rock with her bone knife in her girdle and her hands open at her sides.

The gentleman smiled—a slow, quiet smile that had lost all its courtier's malice.

"The Warden of the Gap," he called across the shale.

"The Beekeeper of the Freehold, my Lord," Maren answered, her voice clear and strong above the murmur of the wings.

The nobleman rode his horse forward until the beast’s nose was three feet from the carved base of the market-cross.

A worker-bee landed on the silver buckle of his bridle, its wings fanning the air, its antennae tasting the salt of the horse’s foam.

The horse did not rear; it dropped its head, sniffed the warm limestone of the step, and blew a soft, contented snort through its nostrils.

Malmes reached into his saddle-bag and drew out a small wooden box of cedar, carved with the arms of the Marches.

He held it out to Maren.

"From the King’s Chancellor," the gentleman said. "Delivered at the Parliament of Gloucester."

Maren took the box.

She opened the lid; inside lay no parchment roll, no iron seal, no Latin charter with green wax cords.

Inside lay three small, dried bulbs of a flower she had never seen before: round as onions, covered in a skin of golden scales that shone like polished bronze, their tips showing tiny points of pale, emerald-green leaf that were ready to break into growth.

"The Saffron-Crocus," Malmes said softly. "Brought by the pilgrims from the valleys of Greece. The Chancellor sends them to the high mountain, Maren Vance. He says the kings have enough of iron and blood; they would have their altars smell of the gold that grows upon the rocks."

Maren looked at the three bulbs.

She touched the golden scales with the tip of her forefinger, feeling the cool, firm flesh beneath the skin—the living seed that had traveled five hundred leagues across the sea to find a home in the limestone gravel of Orve.

"They will grow at the Sheep-Dip," she said. "Between the runnels and the stone wall, where the snow stays long enough to keep the bulbs from the summer heat."

"And the bees?" Malmes asked, looking up at the golden vortex that circled the cross.

"The bees will take the purple dust," she told him. "And the Church will have the saffron for the Easter mass, and the weavers will have the yellow dye that doesn't fade before the rain."

The nobleman bowed his head, touched his hat to Corin, and led his company through the space between the two pillars, their horses’ shoes ringing against the dressed flags of the new road as they descended into the great green bowl of the Glen of Orve.

### Chapter 37

That evening, when the sun had dropped behind the high buttress of Mount Callow and the market-wains had all found their berths in the lower courtyards, Maren walked up the spur to the limestone shelf alone.

The wind had died with the dusk.

The sky above the mountain was that pale, luminous green that comes only on the clearest evenings of the spring, when the high ice-fields reflect the light of the sunken sun long after the valleys have gathered the purple shadows to their hearths.

She walked the line of the thirty stands.

The copper entrance-slips had been removed for the season, and on the slate aprons sat the guards, their heads turned to the south, their wings humming that soft, low twilight-song that settles a house before the stars appear.

She stopped at the seventh skep.

The spear-sedge dome had darkened with the weathering of three winters, its ash-bindings turned to the color of old horn, its dung-crust hard and solid as a Roman wall.

She knelt upon the cold slate flag.

She reached into her girdle, drew out the small bone pry-knife, and laid it flat upon the apron, right in the center of the flight-hole.

The bone was old.

The letters *C. V.* and *M.* were dark with the grease of her hands; the straight line of the road was clear and deep; and the five-pointed star of the survived hive caught the green light of the western sky like a silver spark.

A bee stepped out from the dark of the dome.

She was one of the true mountain lineage: black-bodied, broad-shouldered, her silver-grey ruff thick around her head, her movements slow, dignified, and fearless.

She walked onto the bone handle.

She followed the groove of the letter *C*; she crossed the straight line of the road; she paused for three breaths upon the five-pointed star, her antennae trembling over the carved horn as if she were reading the history of her house with her feet.

Then she turned to Maren’s thumb.

She climbed onto the dark, calloused skin, walked to the hollow between the knuckles, and lifted her head to the mountain-air.

She did not fly.

She stood there upon Maren’s hand, a tiny, living bridge between the stone of the mountain and the blood of the woman who held it, while from the valley below, two leagues away in the green forest of the Low-Reach, rose the sound of the evening bells.

They rang from three towers now: the abbey of St. Jude-in-the-Fens, the church of the fishers at the marsh-mouth, and the new chapel that the masons had raised at the river-ford.

And behind them, thin and clear through the cold air of the Gap, came the bell of Low-Marrow—the deep, bronze roar of the new bell that had no cracked note, no fear of the tithe, and no memory of the wall.

Maren did not speak.

There was no need for words on the limestone shelf.

The road was there, six feet wide, dressed with the Swedish chisels, running from the salt-pans of the sea to the high gravel of the sheep-runs.

The harvest was there, seventy firkins of the white run and forty loaves of the bleached wax resting in the stone sheds, waiting for the wains that would carry them to the ends of the kingdom.

And her brother was there, down by the cedar-hearth with his adze and his ledgers, his face quiet in the fire-light, his heart at peace with the valley that he had unbarred with his hands.

She lifted her arm toward the Notch.

The bee upon her thumb raised its wings, gave two testing strokes that sent a tiny draft against Maren’s skin, and then, with a sharp, sweet buzz that was like the snapping of an iron thread, rose into the green sky.

It circled once above the market-cross; it caught the current of air that poured through the Gap between the two limestone pillars; and then it flew south, down the long road that had no gate, into the great, open world that was waiting for the morning.

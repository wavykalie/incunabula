# The Good Months

### Chapter 1

The centrifuges at the Dutch Gap Animal Clinic had a wobble in the bearings that Dr. Arthur Mercer refused to pay three hundred dollars to calibrate. Every afternoon at four, when the day’s blood draws were spun down to read the packed cell volume and total solids, the little gray machine on the back counter would begin its slow, rhythmic jitter across the Formica, humming like a fat bumblebee trapped in a zinc bucket.

Tess Avery did not look up from the refractometer. She reached out with her left hand—the hand where the pale, sun-bleached circle on her ring finger had finally begun to fade into the freckled brown of her knuckle—and nudged the machine back against the ceramic tile wall with the heel of her palm.

"One point zero two eight," she said to the room.

Across the stainless-steel prep island, Arthur was paring the dry necrotic edge from a Boer goat’s left hoof with a curved Swiss knife that smelled faintly of old lard and axle grease. At seventy-one, he moved with the unhurried, heavy-booted gait of a man who had delivered calves in every mud-bottomed holler between the Iron Mountain ridge and the Tennessee state line, and who considered modern small-animal medicine to be eighty percent human neurosis and twenty percent thyroid supplements.

"That's Mrs. Gidley’s beagle?" Arthur asked through the gray fringe of his mustache.

"Mrs. Gidley’s beagle. Urine specific gravity is fine. He isn’t diabetic, Arthur. He’s just seven pounds overweight because she feeds him Vienna sausages when the weather turns off damp."

"Tell her to give him frozen green beans," Arthur said, flicking a scrap of horn into the plastic scrap bucket. "Tell her the salt in the sausage water will blow his kidneys out by Thanksgiving. And don't charge her for the dipstick. Her husband’s sister died in Abingdon on Sunday."

"I already charged her for the dipstick," Tess said, setting the blue refractometer into its felt-lined wooden box. "The practice has three thousand dollars in delinquent accounts from the sheep farmers in North Fork. We can't absorb Mrs. Gidley’s sausage habit."

Arthur made a small, wet sound in his teeth that was neither agreement nor dispute. Outside the high, frosted casement windows of the treatment area, the late October wind was coming down off the ridges, carrying the sharp, cold tannic reek of rotting oak leaves and the bitter iron tang of early chimney smoke. Dutch Gap sat in the crook of two high mountain shoulders where the sun dropped behind the hemlocks forty minutes before the valley towns down on the four-lane got dark. By four-fifteen, the mercury vapor light over the clinic’s gravel lot had already buzzed to life, casting a cold, violet-gray glare across the rusted hoods of three farm trucks and Tess’s mud-spattered 2011 Subaru Outback.

The front bell chimed—two brass notes that clinked against each other on an old screen-door spring.

"That'll be the Henderson heifer's banamine," Arthur said without looking up. "I told Clyde he could pick it up before five."

Tess wiped the stage of the refractometer with an alcohol swab, dropped the swab into the biohazard bin, and pushed through the swinging wooden half-doors into the reception room.

It was not Clyde Henderson.

A woman was standing by the corner of the low pine counter, holding a frayed nylon leash with both hands. She was wearing an oversized fleece pullover the color of wet river slate, muddy work boots with the yellow laces double-knotted, and her dark hair was gathered into a rough knot at the nape of her neck with a wooden carpenter’s pencil stuck through it. She was breathing hard, the front of her fleece rising and falling, and her knuckles were white where they gripped the looped handle of the lead.

At the end of the leash, lying flat out on the salt-pitted linoleum with his heavy ribcage heaving against the floor, was Roscoe.

Tess stopped three feet inside the doorway. Her boots seemed to adhere to the wax.

"Tess," the woman said.

Her voice was low, slightly cracked from the wind, carrying that slight, flat-voweled cadence of someone who had grown up east of the Blue Ridge, down in the flat Piedmont clay, rather than in the limestone gaps.

Her name was Clara Lind.

Tess had seen her exactly three times in the six months since the county judge in Marion had stamped the decree with a purple rubber seal: once through the plate-glass window of the hardware store when Clara was buying three rolls of builder’s felt and a bundle of cedar laths; once at the pump at the Gulf station, where Clara had looked up from the nozzle, caught Tess’s eye through the windshield of the Subaru, and stood entirely still until Tess looked away; and once in the passenger seat of Luke’s green Dodge flatbed, her profile silhouetted against the river fog as they crossed the bridge over Kettle Creek.

"He wouldn't get up off the porch," Clara said. Her words were too fast, tumbling over each other with the thin, brittle edge of someone trying very hard not to drop a glass dish. "He had breakfast at seven. He seemed fine. Then around two he started this... this dry coughing. Like he had a piece of briar caught in the back of his throat. And then forty minutes ago he went to stand up to come inside and his back legs just gave out. He slid right down the three porch steps onto the gravel."

Tess did not look at Clara’s face. She dropped straight to her knees on the linoleum, her denim work pants hitting the cold floor with a dull thud.

"Roscoe," Tess whispered.

The dog’s tail gave one single, exhausted thump against the base of the retail display of flea collars. He was an eight-year-old hound-shepherd mix, seventy-two pounds of rough, grizzled brown-and-black coat with an apron of white across his chest that had yellowed around the collar from river water. Luke had pulled him out of a flooded ditch behind the limestone quarry when he was six weeks old, soaking wet and shivering inside a cardboard oil carton, the summer they had turned twenty-two and rented the tenant house on Kettle Creek. Tess had bottle-fed him goat’s milk through an eyedropper on the kitchen counter while Luke rebuilt the carburetor on his first chainsaw.

Tess laid her fingers against the dog’s femoral artery, high inside the thin fur of his inner thigh.

The pulse was rapid—well over a hundred and sixty—and it was thread-thin, jumping under her fingertips like a frayed wire, then dropping out entirely for a beat, then fluttering back with a weak, frantic irregularity.

She peeled back Roscoe’s black upper lip with her thumb.

The gums were not pink. They were the color of skim milk poured into a chipped porcelain saucer.

"Arthur," Tess called. She did not raise her voice, but there was an octave drop in it that she had used twice in six years—once for a heifer with an inverted uterus, and once for a pointer that had taken six rounds of birdshot in the lungs. "Arthur, come here now."

The swinging doors banged open. Arthur Mercer came through with his sleeves still rolled above his knotty forearms, holding a clean surgical towel. He took one look at the dog on the floor, one look at the grayish tinge in the whites of Roscoe's brown eyes, and stepped around the counter without asking who the woman with the pencil in her hair was.

"Gums?" Arthur asked, dropping onto his right knee.

"Porcelain," Tess said. Her fingers hadn't left the femoral groove. "Pulse is thready, irregular, probably one-sixty to one-seventy. Capillary refill is three seconds plus. Tachypneic. Abdomen feels slightly distended, but it’s doughy, not hard."

Arthur laid his flat, calloused palm over Roscoe’s left chest wall. He closed his eyes for five seconds, feeling the muffled, waterlogged thump of the heart against his palm.

"Muffled heart sounds," Arthur murmured. "Get him on the gurney, Tess. Let's get him back to the table."

Clara stepped forward, her hands reaching out reflexively. "Can I—"

"Don't touch his back end," Tess said, not looking up. "Take the head. Support his neck. Keep his airway straight."

Between the three of them, they lifted the heavy, limp weight of the dog onto the wheeled canvas stretcher. Roscoe’s head lolled back against Clara’s fleece, leaving a smear of gray drool across her sleeve. Clara didn't flinch. She held the base of his skull with two steady hands, her face pale, her jaw locked so tightly that the tendon in her throat stood out like a cord.

They wheeled him through the swinging doors into the treatment room under the bright fluorescent tubes. The smell hit immediately: the sharp, cold iodine, the wet copper of dog blood from an earlier laceration repair, the pine-oil disinfectant on the floor.

"Ultrasound," Arthur said, reaching for the bottle of blue coupling gel. "Plug it in, Tess. Let's see if the belly’s full of blood or if it's the sac."

Tess clicked the toggle on the portable Sonosite machine—an old black-and-white unit they had bought used from an outpatient clinic in Johnson City four years ago. Her hands were completely steady. This was the work. You did not think about the fact that this dog had slept across your shins through five mountain winters; you did not think about the fact that the woman standing against the oxygen cage, clutching her own elbows, was living in the timber-frame house Luke had built on the ridge with the money from the state road bond. You set the depth to twelve centimeters, you squirted three ounces of cold blue gel onto the clippers, and you took the hair off the right side of the chest in three clean, downward sweeps.

The clippers buzzed, dropping a mat of grizzled brown hair into the stainless catch-basin.

Arthur pressed the curved probe against the bare, goose-pimpled skin between Roscoe's fourth and fifth ribs.

The monitor flickered, gray static resolving into the dark, pulsing anatomy of a living chest.

Tess stared at the screen. Her breath caught in the back of her throat, an old, cold knot that tasted like iron.

The heart was there, beating inside the ultrasound window, but it was not filling the screen. It was floating, small and frantic, suspended inside a massive, black halo of fluid that filled the pericardial sac. With every beat, the heart struggled against the pressure of its own surrounding lake of trapped blood, unable to expand, unable to draw in the venous return from the exhausted body.

"Cardiac tamponade," Arthur said quietly. "Look at the right auricle, Tess."

Tess leaned in. Her eyes tracked the small, fluttering edge of the right atrium.

There, nestled against the base of the great vessels, was a ragged, cauliflower-like mass the size of a walnut. It had the classic mixed echogenicity of an indolent hemangiosarcoma—a vascular tumor that had grown quietly, without a sound, until one of its fragile, blood-filled caverns had wept three hundred milliliters of dark blood into the fibrous pericardium, strangling the heart from the outside in.

"God damn it," Arthur whispered. It was as close to a prayer as he ever got in the clinic.

Beside the oxygen cage, Clara’s voice came out small and thin. "What is that? What are you looking at?"

Tess didn't turn around. Her eyes stayed locked on the black ring of fluid on the monitor. "His heart is bleeding into the sack around it," she said. Her voice was flat, clinical, stripped of every tremor. "The sack doesn't stretch. The fluid is crushing the heart so it can't fill. If we don't tap it right now, he'll go into arrest in ten minutes."

"Set up for a pericardiocentesis," Arthur told her. "Sixteen-gauge over-the-needle catheter. Three-way stopcock. Extension set. Sixty-cc syringe. And get a bowl."

"I've got it," Tess said.

She moved with the mechanical efficiency that had kept Arthur Mercer from drowning in emergency work for six years. She pulled the sterile tray from the bottom drawer, ripped the paper sheath off the catheter with her teeth, connected the clear plastic tubing to the stopcock, and swabbed Roscoe’s shaved chest with three alternating scrubs of betadine and isopropyl alcohol.

Roscoe lay on his left side on the cold steel table. His eyes were half-open, glazed, tracking nothing. His tongue hung limp from the side of his mouth, dry and purple at the tip.

Clara had moved to the head of the table. She didn't ask permission. She knelt down on the floor so her face was level with Roscoe's nose. She laid one hand on his muzzle, her fingers stroking the soft, velvety skin right above his black leather nose, where the gray hairs were thickest.

"Hey, boy," Clara whispered. Her voice was trembling now, but she kept her hands still. "Hey, sweet boy. You stay here. You hear me? You stay right here with us."

Roscoe’s nostrils twitched once at the sound of her voice.

Tess looked at Clara’s hand on the dog's nose. Clara’s thumb had a blue ink stain near the cuticle—the mark of a draftsperson’s drafting pen—and a small, clean scratch across the knuckle from a blackberry vine.

"Hold his front legs, Clara," Tess said.

Clara looked up, startled.

"Take both forelegs above the carpus," Tess instructed, her voice steady and hard as river slate. "Hold them firm against the table. If he twitches when the needle hits the pericardium, Arthur could lacerate the coronary artery. You understand?"

"Yes," Clara said. She reached down, took Roscoe’s heavy front legs in her hands, and leaned her weight across his shoulders, pressing him down with a calm, deliberate leverage that surprised Tess.

Arthur took the long sixteen-gauge needle. He rested his left palm against Roscoe’s ribs, feeling for the fifth intercostal space at the costochondral junction.

"Ready, Tess?"

"Ready."

Arthur slipped the needle through the skin. It made a tiny, wet *pop* as it pierced the intercostal muscle. He advanced it two millimeters, three millimeters, his old eyes fixed on the hub of the plastic catheter.

Suddenly, a rush of dark, non-clotting, port-wine-colored blood surged into the clear plastic chamber.

"Advance the catheter," Arthur said. "Withdraw the stylet."

Tess slid the flexible Teflon sleeve off the steel needle, deep into the pericardial space. She clicked the three-way stopcock into position, locked the sixty-cc syringe onto the port, and drew back the plunger.

The syringe filled almost instantly with dark, purplish-black blood.

Sixty milliliters.

She turned the stopcock, evacuated the blood into the stainless steel basin—where it splashed with a heavy, metallic sound—turned the valve back, and pulled again.

Another sixty milliliters.

One hundred and twenty.

One hundred and eighty.

Two hundred and forty.

With every syringe of blood Tess drew out of the chest, the pressure inside Roscoe’s thorax dropped. Under Clara’s hands, the dog took a sudden, deep, shuddering gasp of air—the first full breath he had taken in six hours. His front legs relaxed. His eyes lost their milky, dead glaze and focused on the wall.

Tess reached down with her left hand and lifted his lip.

The gums were turning pink. Not the bright, healthy salmon of a young dog, but a pale, dusky rose.

"Pulse is slowing," Tess said, her fingers back on the femoral artery. "One-twenty. One-ten. It’s got volume now. It’s not bouncing."

Arthur took the ultrasound probe, wiped it on his trousers, and touched the shaved skin again.

On the screen, the black halo had shrunk to a sliver. The heart was expanding fully, its thick ventricular walls pumping with rhythmic, muscular power. But there, clinging to the right atrium like an ugly burr, the walnut-sized mass remained, dark and irregular.

Arthur sighed. He reached over, switched off the ultrasound machine, and dropped the probe into its cradle.

"That's all she wrote for the tap," Arthur said. He pulled off his latex gloves with two loud snaps and tossed them into the trash. "We took two hundred and eighty ccs out of him. He’ll feel like a two-year-old for forty-eight hours."

Clara was still leaning over Roscoe’s head. Her forehead was pressed against the dog’s neck fur. Her shoulders hitched once, a silent, convulsive sob that she swallowed immediately, forcing her back straight. When she looked up, her eyes were rimmed with red, but her face was set in a tight, determined mask.

"Is he... is he going to be okay?" she asked.

Arthur looked at Tess. It was the look he always gave her when a dairy farmer’s favorite milker had hardware disease, or when an old woman’s barn cat had reached the end of its kidneys. It was the look that said: *You tell them, or I'll tell them with the bark on.*

Tess picked up a gauze square and wiped the smear of dark blood from Roscoe’s chest wall. She threw the gauze in the basin.

"No," Tess said.

Clara flinched as if Tess had slapped her.

"He isn't okay," Tess continued, looking Clara directly in the face. "What we just did was like bailing water out of a boat with a hole in the bottom. The hole is a hemangiosarcoma. It's a blood vessel tumor on his heart. It’s malignant."

"Can you cut it out?" Clara asked. "Can we take him to the university hospital in Blacksburg? I have some savings. Luke has the money from the state contract—"

"You can't cut it off the right atrium," Tess said. Her voice was level, relentless in its precision. "If you try to resect an atrial hemangiosarcoma in an eight-year-old dog, he dies on the table under bypass. And even if you survived the surgery, it has already seeded microscopically to his lungs and spleen. Chemo doesn't cure it. Chemo buys you three weeks of nausea and anorexia."

Clara stared at the steel table. "How long?"

"Without the tap, he would have been dead by six o'clock," Tess said. "With the tap, and with Yunnan Baiyao to help his blood clot, and low-dose prednisone, and maybe some pimobendan if the muscle weakens... he has months."

"How many months?"

"Six," Arthur said from the sink, where he was scrubbing his forearms with brown carbolic soap. "Maybe nine if the tumor stays quiet and doesn't burst again. A year if you’re lucky enough to find twenty-dollar bills growing in your garden."

"A year," Clara whispered.

Roscoe lifted his head from the table. He gave a low, questioning grunt, sniffed at the edge of the stainless steel, and then turned his muzzle toward Tess’s chest. He pushed his nose into the pocket of her scrub top, right where she kept the dry liver treats for the post-op dogs.

Tess felt her throat tighten, a sudden, brutal ache behind her cheekbones. She reached into her pocket, found a hard brown pellet of liver, and held it out on her flat palm.

Roscoe took it with a gentle, dry click of his teeth, chewed twice, and looked around the room.

"Where is Luke?" Tess asked.

The question fell into the quiet room like a dropped wrench.

Clara looked down at her muddy boots. She wiped her nose with the back of her slate-gray fleece sleeve.

"He's up on the parkway," Clara said. "They're pouring the footings for the bridge at Milepost 214 before the hard freeze next week. He’s working the four-to-four shift. He doesn't have cell service down in the gap between the ridges."

"He has a radio in the truck," Tess said.

"He has the radio off when he’s running the track-hoe," Clara said. There was no defense in her tone, only an exhaustion so familiar that Tess could have charted its contours in her sleep. "He won't be back down until Friday morning."

Tess looked at the dog on the table. Roscoe was trying to sit up, his hind legs sliding on the smooth steel until Clara caught his flank with her forearm to steady him.

"He can't stay at your place alone while you work," Tess said. "If that sac fills up again while you’re out on a job, he’ll suffocate in three hours."

"I work from home," Clara said quickly. "I do historical documentation for the preservation trust. I have my drafting table in the front room. I'm there all day."

"And at night?"

"I'm there at night," Clara said. Her chin came up, a sudden flash of flint in her gray eyes. "I live there, Tess."

Tess felt a heat rise along her collarbones, hot and stupid and useless.

*I know you live there,* she thought. *I planted the rhubarb by the pump house. I painted the trim on the south windows twice because the mountain rot gets into the pine.*

"He needs medication every eight hours," Tess said, her voice dropping into that flat, clinical register again. "Pimobendan on an empty stomach an hour before meals. Yunnan Baiyao twice a day with food. He needs his respiratory rate counted every morning while he’s asleep. If his sleeping breaths go over thirty a minute, it means the fluid is building again and he has to come straight here for another tap."

Clara reached into the pocket of her jeans and pulled out a small, battered black notebook and a mechanical pencil. "Tell me the doses."

Tess looked at her.

Clara was standing by the examination table, her dark hair coming loose from the carpenter’s pencil, her sleeves smeared with dog drool and dried betadine, holding her little notebook open to a blank page. She wasn't crying. She wasn't looking around the clinic as if she were in an enemy camp. She was waiting for the milligrams.

"Pimobendan," Tess said, "five milligrams. One capsule at six in the morning, one at six at night. Yunnan Baiyao, two capsules twice a day. Prednisone, ten milligrams every other day with a full meal."

Clara wrote it all down in small, precise architectural block letters, the pencil making a sharp *skrrt-skrrt* against the paper.

"I can't take him every day," Clara said quietly, not looking up from the page. "I have site visits on Tuesdays and Thursdays. I have to survey an old timber barn in Grayson County tomorrow morning."

Tess looked down at Roscoe. The dog had shifted around so that his graying muzzle was resting on Tess’s knee. His tail made a soft, lazy sweep across the stainless steel.

"He comes to the clinic with me," Tess said.

Clara stopped writing. She looked up. "What?"

"He comes here," Tess said. "He sleeps in the back room behind the autoclave on his old foam bed. He knows the place. Arthur doesn't care. I work Monday through Thursday. You have him Thursday night through Sunday night. We swap on Thursdays at five, and Mondays at seven in the morning."

Clara stared at her. "You want to share him?"

"I don't *want* to share him with you," Tess said, and the words were neither angry nor cruel; they were simply the unvarnished truth of a mountain town where people had to split cords of firewood with neighbors they hadn't spoken to since the timber dispute of 1994. "I want him not to die alone on a porch while Luke is fifty miles away running an excavator and you're in Grayson County measuring a barn. We have nine months. Maybe ten. He doesn't know whose house he’s at; he just knows when his chest hurts."

Clara stood very still. The wind rattled the high frosted windows again, a sudden gust that made the overhead lights sway half an inch on their chains.

"All right," Clara said.

"All right," Tess said.

Arthur came back from the sink, drying his hands on a blue paper towel. He looked at the two of them standing on opposite sides of the gurney, with the dog between them smelling of alcohol and liver treats.

"He needs to stay on IV fluids for an hour to flush his kidneys," Arthur said gruffly. "Tess, drop an eighteen-gauge in his cephalic vein. Miss Lind, you can sit in the waiting room, or you can go down to the diner and get a cup of coffee that tastes like wet socks. Come back at five-thirty."

Clara looked at Roscoe. She leaned down, kissed the soft spot right between his ears—a gesture so natural, so accustomed, that it hit Tess like a dull blow to the solar plexus—and then straightened up.

"Five-thirty," Clara said.

She turned and walked out through the swinging doors, her muddy boots squeaking against the linoleum until the front screen-door bell chimed twice and went silent.

### Chapter 2

The rain began around six, not as a storm, but as that high, drifting mountain mist that clings to the hemlocks until the needles look like gray wool.

Tess sat in the cab of her Subaru in the gravel turnout beside the Old Iron Bridge over Kettle Creek. The engine was idling, the heater blowing a dry, dusty warmth against her knees that smelled of dog hair and old receipts. In the back, spread across the folded-down rear seats on an old patchwork quilt, Roscoe was snoring softly, his front paws twitching occasionally as he chased some half-remembered rabbit through the high fescue of three summers ago.

Across the single-lane wooden deck of the bridge, the headlights of Luke’s old Dodge flatbed appeared, cutting through the yellow fog like two dirty lanterns.

The truck didn't pull across. It stopped on the far apron, its big six-cylinder diesel chugging with that familiar, hollow *clack-clack-clack* that Tess had listened to for seven years every time Luke pulled into the lane after dark.

The driver’s side door opened. A figure stepped down into the gravel, ducking against the drizzle.

It was not Luke.

Clara Lind was wearing a long yellow oilskin coat that had belonged to Luke’s father—the sleeves turned back three times at the cuffs to show the brown corduroy lining—and a wool watch cap pulled down over her ears. She walked across the bridge deck, her boots hollow against the wet white-oak planks, her hands buried deep in the coat pockets.

She came up to the passenger window of the Subaru and tapped once with a knuckle.

Tess rolled the glass down four inches. The cold air rushed in, carrying the smell of wet river slate and the pungent, sulfurous reek of the diesel exhaust drifting across the bridge.

"Luke called from the gap," Clara said without greeting. "He got down to the store at Milepost 190 where there's a landline."

Tess looked straight ahead through the windshield, where the wiper blades were clearing two gray arcs in the mist. "What did he say?"

"He asked if the dog was dead."

Tess closed her eyes for three seconds. She let the breath out through her nose. "And?"

"I told him we tapped his chest," Clara said. "I told him about the tumor. I told him what Arthur said about the year."

"Did he understand?"

"He said dogs don't have tumors," Clara said, and her voice had a flat, bruised quality that Tess recognized with an intimacy that made her stomach turn over. "He said dogs just get old and stiff, and then one day they go down into the laurel by the creek and they don't come back up. He said we were making a project out of it."

Tess laughed once—a short, sharp bark that had no humor in it at all.

"That's Luke," Tess said. "If you can't fix it with a six-foot pry bar and a grease gun, it isn't broken; it’s just stubborn."

Clara rested both forearms on the lowered glass of the door. Up close, in the dim green glow of the dashboard lights, Tess could see the dark hollows under her cheekbones. Clara had small, clear features that looked like they had been carved out of applewood with a very sharp chisel, but her skin had that papery, translucent look of someone who had spent thirty hours worrying without eating.

"He told me I should have let Arthur put him down," Clara whispered.

Tess turned her head sharply.

Clara was looking into the back of the wagon, where Roscoe had lifted his head, his ears pricked at the sound of her voice.

"He told you that?" Tess asked.

"He said it would be cleaner," Clara said. "He said you and I sharing a dog across the creek was going to make the whole county talk. He said he didn't want people in Dutch Gap thinking he was letting his ex-wife pay for his dog's pills."

"I'm not paying for his pills," Tess said coldly. "The Yunnan Baiyao is twenty-two dollars a box. The pimobendan is ninety-six a month. I took it off the clinic shelf at cost, and I wrote it down in the daybook under Luke’s name. If he doesn't want to pay Arthur, Arthur will put a lien on his road trailer. Luke knows that."

Clara reached into the pocket of the yellow oilskin and pulled out a small, white envelope, folded in half. She pushed it through the four-inch opening in the glass.

Tess looked at it. "What's that?"

"Three hundred dollars," Clara said. "Cash. It's from my personal account. It covers the tap and the first month’s medicine."

"I don't want your money, Clara."

"It isn't for you," Clara said, her voice dropping into a hard, stubborn groove that made her sound ten years older. "It's for the clinic. I don't want Arthur Mercer carrying a debt on his book because Luke is five miles up a ridge pretending nothing is dying. If we’re doing this, we’re doing it square. Nobody owes anybody anything."

Tess looked at the envelope sitting on the passenger seat.

The rain was coming harder now, drumming on the steel roof of the Subaru with a steady, hollow rattle. In the back, Roscoe stood up, his nails clicking on the rubber cargo mat, and pushed his nose between the front bucket seats. He licked Clara’s ear through the open window, a slow, warm swipe of his tongue.

Clara reached in, her bare fingers burying themselves in the thick ruff of fur behind his neck. She closed her eyes, her forehead leaning against the wet glass of the window frame, and for five seconds neither of them said a word.

"Did you make a calendar?" Tess asked.

Clara opened her eyes. "What?"

"A calendar," Tess said, reaching down beside the handbrake. She picked up a blue plastic three-ring binder that she had grabbed from the supply closet at the clinic. "If Luke isn't going to be down here, we need a log. Every dose. Every time he has a soft stool. Every time he coughs more than twice in an hour. We can't be texting each other thirty times a day like two people who have something to say to each other."

She pushed the binder through the window.

Clara took it. She held the blue plastic against the yellow oilskin of her chest.

"Thursday nights," Clara said. "Five-thirty here at the bridge."

"Five-thirty," Tess said. "If the creek is up over the low-water bridge on the back road, we meet at the Gulf station under the awning."

"Mondays at seven in the morning?"

"Seven in the morning," Tess said. "Don't feed him his breakfast before you bring him. He gets carsick if he rides on a full belly after the pimobendan."

Clara nodded once. She stepped back from the car into the rain, the yellow coat hanging heavy and stiff around her shins.

"Tess?"

Tess had her hand on the window crank. "What?"

"Thank you," Clara said. "For not listening to him."

Tess didn't answer. She wound the glass up until it thudded into the rubber weatherstripping, shifted the Subaru into reverse, and backed away from the bridge into the dark.

### Chapter 3

The first two weeks were measured in capsules.

Tess developed a routine that had the cold, rhythmic precision of a surgery prep. At five-forty-five every morning, before the mountain frost had begun to steam off her tin roof, she would get out of bed in the small four-room cottage on Mill Creek that she had rented after the divorce. The cottage was eighty years old, built of chestnut logs covered in white asphalt siding that had peeled away in strips near the foundation, and the kitchen floor leaned two inches toward the creek.

She would boil water for instant coffee in a blackened tin pot, then take the small plastic pill-minder from the windowsill.

Roscoe would be waiting by the back door, his chin resting on the bottom rail of the kitchen chair.

"Six o'clock," Tess would say aloud to the empty kitchen.

She would take a single half-inch square of cheap white American cheese, mold it around the brown-and-white capsule of pimobendan, and hold it out. Roscoe would inhale it without chewing, his throat making a single, dry gulp. Then they would wait forty-five minutes for the drug to clear his stomach before she poured his cup of senior kibble, mixed with two tablespoons of warm water and the dark, herbal powder of the Yunnan Baiyao.

He looked good. That was the terrifying thing about hemangiosarcoma: it was a disease of illusions.

Once the pressure on his pericardium had been relieved, Roscoe moved with a loose, easy swinging gait that he hadn't had since late summer. He followed Tess out to the woodpile, sniffing with deep, snorting interest at the dry oak bark where a woodmouse had nested under the plastic tarp. When she opened the tailgate of the Subaru at six-thirty, he jumped in without hesitating, his hocks clearing the bumper by six inches.

At the clinic, he had his place.

Behind the treatment area, between the industrial washer and the shelf where Arthur kept the gallon jugs of mineral oil and povidone scrub, Tess had laid down an old orthopedic mattress covered in two wool horse blankets. Roscoe would curl himself into a tight, graying crescent, his nose tucked under his tail, listening with complete indifference to the daily theater of the Dutch Gap Animal Clinic: the hysterical yapping of miniature schnauzers having their anal glands expressed; the frantic scrabbling of farm collies on the scale; the deep, resonant groans of Guernsey cows being stitched up after a rough calving out in the stocks behind the barn.

Every two hours, Tess would walk back, lay two fingers against his ribs, and count his breaths.

Eighteen. Sixteen. Twenty.

"He's running clear," Arthur said on the second Wednesday, pausing by the shelf with a box of disposable syringes. "Sac hasn't filled a drop."

"It's only been twelve days, Arthur," Tess said, entering the number nineteen into the blue binder with a red ballpoint pen.

"I know how many days it's been," Arthur said. "I'm just saying, don't look at the dog like he’s already in the ground, Tess. He’s eating, isn't he?"

"He's eating."

"He take a leak this morning?"

"Twice."

"Then let him be a dog for twenty minutes," Arthur grunted, turning back toward the exam rooms. "A beast doesn't know what twelve months means. A beast only knows if his feet are dry and if somebody scratched him behind the ears before breakfast."

At four-forty-five on Thursday afternoon, Tess packed the canvas tote bag.

The bag had become a third entity in the transaction: an old olive-drab LL Bean boat-and-tote that Luke had used for duck hunting ten years ago, its canvas stiffened with salt and river mud.

Into the bag went:
1. The pill-minder, filled through Sunday night.
2. The blue three-ring binder, with every breath count recorded in neat columns.
3. A half-roll of paper towels.
4. Two cans of prescription digestive-care food in case his gut turned loose.
5. A plastic bag of dried beef liver.

She loaded Roscoe into the back of the wagon and drove down the gap toward Kettle Creek.

The mountain had changed in twelve days. The last of the red oaks had dropped their leaves, leaving the ridges gray and bristling like the spine of an old hog. The air had that dry, limestone-dust smell that comes before the first real mountain freeze, when the creek drops to three inches of clear, icy water running over yellow cobbles.

When she reached the Old Iron Bridge, Clara’s truck was already there.

It was not Luke’s Dodge flatbed this time. It was a 2004 Toyota Tacoma, dark red with a dented tailgate and a wooden ladder rack built from rough-cut two-by-fours.

Clara was sitting on the lowered tailgate, wearing a heavy brown duck-canvas chore coat and fingerless wool gloves. She was holding a thermos cup between her knees, her eyes fixed on the gray water swirling around the stone abutment of the bridge.

When the Subaru’s tires crunched into the gravel, Clara set the cup down and hopped off the tailgate.

Tess killed the engine. She climbed out, walked to the back of the wagon, and opened the hatch.

"Hey, boy," Clara said.

She didn't rush him. She stood three feet back, letting Roscoe climb down at his own pace. The dog hit the gravel, shook himself until his collar jingled like small bells, and then trotted straight to Clara, his tail swinging in wide, lazy circles. He pushed his head into the space between her knees, leaning all seventy pounds of himself against her shins with a deep, contented sigh.

Clara buried her face in the fur of his neck. Her shoulders dropped three inches, the tension going out of her like air from a punctured tire.

"How was he?" Clara asked, looking up at Tess.

"Good," Tess said. She reached into the wagon and hauled out the olive-drab tote bag. "Breaths are stable. Running between sixteen and twenty-two. No coughing. He had a loose stool on Tuesday morning, but it cleared up after two meals of rice and boiled hen."

Clara took the bag. She unzipped the front pocket, checked the pill-minder with a quick, practiced glance, and then looked at the blue binder.

"You wrote down the times?"

"Every six hours," Tess said. "The Yunnan Baiyao has to be given with food, Clara. Don't give it on an empty stomach or he'll vomit the red bile."

"I know," Clara said. "I read the sheet you printed."

They stood in the cold air, five feet apart, with the dog between them.

The silence between them was not the sharp, bitter silence of an argument; it was the strange, hollow quiet of two strangers who had been handed the same heavy piece of furniture and told to carry it up three flights of stairs without dropping it.

"Luke's crew got rained out yesterday," Clara said suddenly.

Tess looked down at her own boots. "Did they?"

"They're coming down tonight," Clara said. "He’ll be home around eight. He's staying through Sunday."

Tess felt a small, cold twitch in her jaw. "Is he going to take Roscoe out in the truck?"

"No," Clara said. "I told him the dog can't ride in the bed anymore. The wind dries out his throat and starts the coughing."

"What did Luke say?"

Clara looked out over the river. The water was gray-green, sliding smoothly over a shelf of slate before breaking into white ripples around a drowned sycamore root.

"He said I was treating an eight-year-old hound like a crystal vase," Clara said quietly. "He said if the dog is dying anyway, he ought to be allowed to chase a groundhog if he wants to."

"If he chases a groundhog," Tess said, her voice rising half an octave, "his heart rate hits two hundred, the right atrium tears at the margin of the mass, and he drops dead in the briars before Luke can get his boots off."

"I know," Clara said. She looked at Tess then—really looked at her—and Tess saw that her eyes were not just tired; they were steady, clear, and completely without compromise. "That's why I'm keeping him on the lead. I don't care what Luke says about the truck. Roscoe stays with me in the workroom."

Tess let her breath out. It hung between them in a white, ragged plume.

For the first time, Tess noticed that Clara was wearing an old silver ring on her middle finger—not a wedding band, but a wide silver band chased with the pattern of wild hemlock needles. It was handmade, slightly battered, the silver darkened with age in the grooves.

"You do the drafting for the preservation trust?" Tess asked, the question slipping out before she could stop it.

Clara seemed surprised. She looked down at her hands, then tucked them into the pockets of the chore coat.

"Mostly measured drawings," Clara said. "Old timber barns. Grist mills. The state wants CAD records before the roofs collapse and the owners push the logs into a ravine with a dozer. It's... it's quiet work. Just me, a hundred-foot tape, and a laser level."

"Sounds lonely," Tess said.

Clara looked at the river again. A pair of wood ducks shot out from the willow brush, their wings whistling in the cold air as they banked toward the lower pool.

"I like old wood," Clara said. "It doesn't pretend it isn't rotting."

She reached down, took Roscoe’s leash, and clipped it to the brass ring on his collar.

"Monday morning," Clara said. "Seven o'clock?"

"Seven o'clock," Tess said. "Don't be late. Arthur has a three-year-old filly coming in at seven-thirty for an hock injection, and I need Roscoe settled before she gets off the trailer."

"I won't be late," Clara said.

She turned and led Roscoe toward the little red Toyota. The dog hopped into the passenger seat, turned around twice on the sheepskin cover Clara had laid across the vinyl, and pressed his nose against the side glass, watching Tess with his ears cocked.

Clara climbed into the cab, started the engine—it turned over with a clean, high-pitched Japanese whine, so completely different from Luke’s coughing diesel—and backed away from the bridge.

Tess stood by the rear bumper of her Subaru until the red taillights disappeared around the limestone bluff at the mouth of the gap.

She looked down at her own hand. It was red from the cold, the knuckles stiff.

She climbed into the car, pulled the door shut against the wind, and sat there for ten minutes with the engine off, listening to Kettle Creek tear itself to pieces over the stones.

### Chapter 4

November came in with four days of hard, black frost that froze the mud in the ruts of Mill Creek Road until the tires rattled like they were running over railroad ties.

The calendar in the blue binder was filling up.

Clara’s handwriting was different from Tess’s. Tess wrote with the rapid, slanting, impatient hand of a medical tech: *11/4 - 0600 - Pimo 5mg PO - RR 18 - App good - Stool normal.* Clara wrote in those small, perfectly square architectural blocks, using a sharp 0.5-millimeter mechanical pencil that indented the paper without tearing it: *11/06 - 06:15 - MEDS GIVEN W/ CHEDDAR. SLEPT BY WOODSTOVE UNTIL 08:00. WALKED 20 MIN ON FLAT GRADE (OLD LOGGING SPUR). NO COUGH. HE LIKED THE FROZEN POND.*

Under the entry for November eighth, Clara had drawn a tiny, three-quarter-inch sketch in the margin of the log: Roscoe’s head in profile, his ears laid back in the wind, a single blade of fescue grass hanging from the corner of his lips.

Tess had touched the graphite sketch with the tip of her index finger three times before she closed the book on Monday morning.

The rhythm had become an iron track.

Mondays at seven. Thursdays at five-thirty.

They had stopped doing the handoff through the open car windows. Now, they both got out. They stood together on the gravel apron while Roscoe relieved himself in the tall, dead ironweed by the bridge abutment.

The conversations were still short, but they had begun to fray at the edges, small threads of their lives catching on the rough surface of the dog’s illness.

"Luke went back to the highway camp on Sunday night," Clara said on the third Thursday of the month. She was wearing a heavy woolen scarf wrapped twice around her throat, the wool dyed a deep, dark indigo that made her gray eyes look like river stones under water. "They're trying to finish the bridge deck before the first snow."

"He always hates November," Tess said, leaning against the fender of her car with her hands in her pockets. "The cold gets into that shoulder he broke when the timber skidder rolled back in eighteen."

Clara looked at her, a quick, sharp glance. "He never told me he broke his shoulder."

Tess felt a small, sour drop of guilt in her throat. "He wouldn't. He thinks telling somebody you hurt is the same as asking them to carry your tools for you."

Clara stared down at the frozen ground. She kicked a loose lump of limestone with the toe of her boot, watching it skitter into the dead weeds.

"Sometimes," Clara said, her voice so low that Tess had to lean in against the wind to hear it, "I feel like I'm living in a house where all the furniture was bolted to the floor before I got there."

Tess didn't know what to say to that.

The house on the ridge—the timber-frame house Luke had built with the post-and-beam timbers he had salvaged from the old tannery down in Damascus—had been designed on a piece of graph paper that Tess had held on her knees while Luke drove the stakes into the red clay. She knew where every light switch was; she knew that the water heater had to be reset with a butter knife if the well pump kicked off during a lightning storm; she knew that the third step from the bottom of the open staircase had a high, singing squeak that you couldn't get rid of because the timber had dried green.

"The house is hard to heat," Tess said finally.

"It's not the heat," Clara said.

She didn't elaborate. She took the tote bag from Tess’s hands, their fingers brushing against each other for a second—Clara’s wool glove rough against the bare, dry skin of Tess’s wrist—and then she turned to load Roscoe into the Tacoma.

The call came at eleven-twenty on a Tuesday night.

Tess was sitting at her small kitchen table, wearing a flannel nightshirt and heavy wool socks, reading a five-year-old issue of *The Journal of Veterinary Emergency and Critical Care* under a brass gooseneck lamp. The cottage was quiet except for the wind working at the loose tin on the eaves and the rhythmic *thump-clack* of the baseboard heater.

The telephone on the wall—the old landline that she had kept because the cell service down in the hollow was only one bar on the back porch—screeched three times with that shrill, mechanical bell.

Tess was on her feet before the second ring finished.

"Yes?"

"Tess."

It was Clara. Her voice was not tight or controlled this time; it was thin, gasping, shaking with a terror that made the telephone receiver buzz against Tess’s ear.

"It's happening," Clara said. "He’s... he’s on his side. He won't get up. He’s breathing forty-five times a minute and his stomach looks... it looks huge, Tess. It looks like a watermelon."

Tess felt the cold drop through her ribs, straight into her gut.

"Is he coughing?"

"No. Just... just grunting. Every breath is a grunt. His gums are gray. Tess, please. Luke isn't here. I don't know what to do."

"Listen to me," Tess said, her voice dropping into that low, flat command that brooked no argument. "Do not move him. Do not try to put him in your truck. If you lift him into the cab with a belly full of blood, the tumor will rupture wide open and he’ll bleed out before you reach the hard road."

"What do I do?" Clara was crying now, the tears catching in her throat, making her words wet and choked.

"Go to the bathroom," Tess ordered. "Get two large bath towels. Put them under his head to keep his neck straight. Do not give him water. Do not give him food. Leave the porch light on and unlock the front gate. I am leaving right now."

"Tess—"

"Twenty minutes, Clara. Keep him still."

Tess slammed the receiver onto the hook. She didn't change out of the flannel nightshirt; she pulled her heavy Carhartt bib overalls on over it, shoved her bare feet into her mud boots, grabbed her heavy canvas coat from the peg, and ran out the back door into the black frost.

She didn't take the Subaru toward the clinic. She drove straight down Mill Creek Road, the tires screaming on the frozen gravel, then took the turn toward the clinic at sixty miles an hour.

She let herself into the back door of the clinic with her brass key, not bothering with the alarm code. She didn't turn on the overhead fluorescents; she used the small penlight between her teeth.

From the pharmacy cabinet, she grabbed:
- Two sterile sixteen-gauge over-the-needle catheters.
- A sixty-cc syringe.
- A three-way stopcock.
- A bottle of injectable Yunnan Baiyao.
- A hundred-milliliter bottle of aminocaproic acid to promote clotting.
- Two butterfly infusion sets.
- A bottle of lidocaine.

She dumped it all into an empty cardboard antibiotic carton, threw it onto the passenger seat of the Subaru, and slammed the door.

The climb up to Luke’s ridge was four miles of switchbacks on a single-lane road that had been graded with three-inch river stone that had never settled. The car drifted on the corners, the all-wheel drive clicking and whining as the tires fought the frozen mud. The hemlocks crowded the edge of the ditch, their black boughs heavy with frost, whipping against the side mirrors with sharp, cracking slaps.

At the four-mile mark, the wooden gate stood open, the yellow reflector on the post catching her high beams.

Tess swung the car through the gate, her headlights sweeping across the cleared meadow, the stack of seasoning oak logs covered in black plastic, the cedar-shake pump house—

And there was the house.

The timber-frame house stood against the black silhouette of the ridge, its high, peaked roof cutting into the stars. Every light on the ground floor was blazing, casting long, golden rectangles across the frosted clover of the front yard.

Tess killed the engine before the car had completely stopped rolling. She grabbed the cardboard box from the seat and ran up the three stone steps to the front door.

The door was unlocked. She threw it open with her shoulder.

The heat hit her first—the dry, sweet, cherry-wood heat of the big cast-iron woodstove that Luke had set on a hearth of river slate.

In the center of the great room, lying on the braided wool rug that Tess had bought at an estate auction in Rural Retreat seven years ago, was Roscoe.

Clara was on her knees beside him. She was wearing an old gray sweatshirt with the sleeves pushed up, her bare knees pressed into the wool rug, both hands laid flat over Roscoe’s heaving ribs. Her face was pale as tallow, her hair falling wild around her shoulders, her eyes wide and black in the lamplight.

"He's still here," Clara whispered, her voice cracking. "He's still here, Tess."

Tess dropped onto the rug opposite her, her boots skidding on the hardwood.

She didn't speak. She laid her palm over Roscoe’s heart.

The beat was a chaotic, fluttering mess—nearly two hundred a minute—and his abdomen was visibly distended, rounded and taut like a drum.

"It's not the pericardium this time," Tess said, her fingers exploring the dog's flanks with rapid, deep palpations. "The sac is clear. The tumor on the spleen has started weeping into the peritoneal cavity. He’s bleeding into his belly."

Clara grabbed Tess’s forearm. Her grip was astonishingly strong, her fingers digging through the Carhartt canvas into the meat of Tess’s arm.

"Can you stop it?"

"I don't know," Tess said. "If it’s a major vessel, no. If it’s an oozing surface tear, we give him the antifibrinolytics, we wrap his belly with an ace bandage to create counter-pressure, and we pray the clot holds."

"Tell me what to hold," Clara said.

Tess looked at her. Clara wasn't trembling anymore. The panic had burned away, leaving only that hard, desperate mountain clarity that comes when there is nothing left to lose.

"Get the belt off Luke’s work trousers," Tess said. "The leather one behind the door. And two heavy bath towels."

Clara was up and back in five seconds.

Tess drew up the aminocaproic acid with a twenty-gauge needle, tapped the air out of the plastic barrel with her knuckle, and tied off Roscoe’s left cephalic vein with the rubber tourniquet.

The dog didn't move. He lay on his side, his eyes half-closed, his breath coming in short, shallow grunts that rattled the loose skin of his jowls.

"Hold the head, Clara," Tess said. "Talk to him."

Clara dropped down, sliding her face right next to the dog’s ear. "Roscoe," she murmured, her voice steady now, rhythmic, a low chant that had no words, just sound. "Good boy. Good, sweet boy. We've got you. Tess is here. You stay right here."

Tess slipped the needle into the vein.

A dark flash of blood appeared in the hub. She released the tourniquet, slowly pushed the clear liquid into the vein, and flushed it with five milliliters of sterile saline.

"Now the wrap," Tess said.

Together, working with a frantic, silent coordination that felt like they had been doing it for twenty years, they rolled the thick bath towels around the dog's swollen abdomen, pulling them tight to compress the bleeding vessels, and secured them with three wraps of Luke’s heavy leather work belt, buckling it down to the fourth hole.

Roscoe gave a single, deep sigh—a release of air so long that Tess thought for one terrible second his heart had stopped.

Then his head dropped flat against Clara’s thigh.

Tess kept her fingers on his femoral artery.

One-eighty.

One-seventy.

One-sixty.

The pulse wasn't getting stronger, but it wasn't getting thinner. It was holding.

Ten minutes passed. The only sound in the great room was the ticking of the woodstove as the cherry logs burned down to coals, and the slow, wet breathing of the dog between them.

Outside, the mountain wind hit the south wall of the house, making the heavy eight-by-eight hemlock posts groan in their mortises.

Clara was still on her knees, her hands resting on the dog’s neck. She looked up at Tess across the brown fur of Roscoe's shoulder.

Her face was streaked with soot and tears, her gray sweatshirt damp with sweat around the collar.

"Did we do it?" Clara whispered.

"The bleeding has slowed down," Tess said. Her own voice sounded hollow, like it was coming from the bottom of an empty silo. "The pressure from the towels is helping the clot form. If he can make it through until six in the morning without the pressure dropping again, the body will reabsorb the fluid."

Clara let out a ragged, shaking breath. She leaned back against the bottom rail of the timber-frame staircase, her head thumping against the upright post.

She closed her eyes.

Tess sat on the floor, her legs stretched out on the braided rug, her back resting against the stone base of the woodstove. Her overalls were covered in red clay from the driveway and dog hair from Roscoe's flank.

She looked around the room.

It was the first time she had been inside this house since the day in May when she had loaded her grandmother’s cedar chest and three boxes of kitchenware into the back of her father’s cattle trailer.

The walls were still the same pale, unfinished pine, but the kitchen counter had a crock of dried lavender that hadn't been there before. Above the sink, where Tess had always meant to hang a shelf for her blue Mason jars, someone had pinned four large architectural blueprints of an eighteenth-century timber church in Carroll County, the blue paper curled at the corners like dried leaves.

And on the small table by the window, beside Clara’s drafting pens and a box of brass thumb tacks, was a small, round stone from Kettle Creek, polished smooth by fifty years of mountain water, holding down a stack of tracing paper.

"He didn't want to build the loft," Clara said quietly, her eyes still closed.

Tess turned her head.

Clara was still leaning against the post, her hands limp in her lap.

"Luke," Clara said. "He told me he built the loft because you wanted a place to sleep where you could see the sun come up over the gap. He said every time he walked up those stairs, he felt like he was walking into somebody else’s morning."

Tess felt a dull, heavy ache settle behind her breastbone. It wasn't anger; it was just the vast, stupid sadness of two people who had spent seven years trying to turn a stone into a loaf of bread.

"I did want to see the sun," Tess said.

Clara opened her eyes. They were very clear in the firelight, dark gray and steady.

"He isn't coming back down on Friday," Clara said.

Tess looked at her. "Why?"

"He took a winter shift on the pipeline in West Virginia," Clara said. Her voice had no tremor now; it was just flat, plain fact. "He leaves from the gap on Monday. He’ll be gone until March."

Tess stared at the dog on the floor. Roscoe’s breathing had dropped to twenty-four. His ears were warm under her fingers.

"What about you?" Tess asked.

Clara looked around the high, empty room, at the pine walls that had dried green, at the cherry fire dying behind the glass door of the stove.

"I have two more grist mills to measure in Grayson County," Clara said. "And the dog needs his medicine every eight hours."

She reached out across Roscoe’s back, her hand open, her palm facing up.

Tess looked at the hand—the short, clean nails, the drafting ink on the thumb, the silver ring carved with hemlock needles.

Slowly, without thinking about the six months of court papers, or the county gossip at the hardware store, or the green truck crossing the river bridge in the fog, Tess reached out and took Clara’s hand in her own.

Clara’s fingers were cold. Tess’s were warm from the stove.

They sat there on the braided rug in the middle of the night, between the dying fire and the dying dog, holding on to each other while the wind came down off the ridge and shook the house to its stones.

### Chapter 5

December in Dutch Gap was not a season; it was an occupation.

The frost got into the ground six inches deep, turning the red mountain clay to the hardness of pig iron. The logging roads above the gap were closed with orange steel gates that had padlocks the size of saucers, and the county road crew spent their mornings spreading crushed limestone and cinders along the steep hairpins of Route 58.

The calendar in the blue binder was three-quarters full.

The crisis on the ridge had passed, leaving Roscoe four pounds lighter and slightly more gray around the muzzle, but his belly had flattened out as the pooled blood reabsorbed, and his appetite had returned with a vengeance. He had developed a fierce, stubborn attachment to canned pumpkin, which Clara bought by the case at the general store in Whitetop and delivered to the clinic in brown paper sacks with the tops rolled down.

The handoffs at the Old Iron Bridge had shifted to five o'clock because the darkness came down out of the hemlocks like a black curtain by quarter-past four.

On the second Thursday of December, the snow began as they were both pulling into the gravel turnout.

It was not the dry, powdery snow of the northern valleys; it was the heavy, wet mountain snow that sticks to the windshield like lard and pulls down the boughs of the white pines until they touch the ground with a soft, wet *swish*.

Clara had left the Tacoma idling, its yellow fog lights cutting two butter-colored tunnels through the falling flakes. She had the hood of her chore coat pulled up, her hands tucked inside the sleeves.

"Come inside the cab," Clara called across the wind as Tess stepped out of the Subaru. "It’s too wet to stand on the road."

Tess didn't argue. She unclipped Roscoe’s leash, let him jump directly from the rear of the wagon into the passenger side of the Toyota, and then climbed in after him, pulling the heavy steel door shut against the storm.

The cab of the little truck was small, crowded, and smelled powerfully of cedar shavings, damp wool, and the spicy, citrus reek of the tea Clara kept in a stainless thermos on the transmission hump.

Roscoe immediately curled himself into the footwell between their boots, his head resting across the toes of Tess’s left boot, his tail wedged against the gearshift.

Clara reached down, unscrewed the black plastic lid of the thermos, and poured it full of dark, steaming liquid. She handed it to Tess without asking.

"Earl Grey," Clara said. "With two spoonfuls of sourwood honey from the roadside stand at the mouth of the gap."

Tess took the cup. The heat soaked through the thin plastic into her frozen palms. She took a sip; it was sweet, smoky, and scalding hot.

"Arthur said the heifer's leg healed clean," Tess said, looking out through the windshield where the wipers were struggling against the slush.

"Which heifer?"

"The one at the Gidley place. The one you helped hold while Arthur put the cast on three weeks ago."

Clara smiled—a small, quick movement that changed the whole structure of her face, softening the sharp, carved lines of her cheekbones. "I didn't help hold her. I was terrified she was going to kick my teeth down my throat. Arthur just yelled at me to lean on her flank until she stopped grunting."

"That's Arthur's version of formal clinical instruction," Tess said, a smile tugging at the corner of her own mouth. "He did the same thing to me when I was nineteen. Only it was an eight-hundred-pound sow that had eaten half a bushel of fermented windfall apples."

They sat in the warm, narrow cab, watching the snow gather on the iron girders of the bridge. The river below was black, moving with a silent, heavy rush between shelves of white ice that had begun to creep out from the willow banks.

Between them on the bench seat lay the olive-drab tote bag.

"Luke called from Morgantown yesterday," Clara said.

She said it quietly, without looking at Tess, her eyes fixed on the wipers.

Tess held the cup of tea with both hands. "How is the pipeline?"

"Cold," Clara said. "He said the mud is up to the axles on the service trucks. He said they're working ten hours on, six hours off, living in a camp with fifty men who don't wash their coveralls."

She paused. A gust of wind hit the side of the truck, rocking the cab on its leaf springs.

"He asked if I was lonely up on the ridge," Clara said.

Tess looked at the side of Clara’s face. In the dim yellow light from the dashboard, her skin looked smooth, cool, the color of birch bark.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him no," Clara said. "I told him the house was very quiet, and that Roscoe and I had a routine, and that I had the work on the Grayson mills to finish before the trust’s fiscal year ended in January."

"Did he ask about me?"

Clara turned her head. Her gray eyes were wide, dark in the shadows under the hood of the chore coat.

"He asked if you were still angry," Clara said.

Tess looked down at the tea. A tiny circle of steam rose from the dark surface, vanishing against the frosted glass of the windshield.

"I don't think I was ever angry," Tess said, and as she said the words, she realized they were true. "Not the way he thought. When we signed the papers in Marion, he kept waiting for me to throw something. He stood by the counter with his hands in his pockets, looking at the floor, waiting for me to say something that would let him feel like he was the one who had been wronged."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because there wasn't anything to say," Tess whispered. "We had lived together for seven years, Clara, and by the end, we were like two trees that had grown up too close to each other in a thicket. We didn't have any branches on the sides that faced each other. Just dead wood."

Clara didn't answer. She reached out with her right hand—her bare hand, cold from the steering wheel—and laid it over Tess’s knuckles where they gripped the plastic cup.

Her fingers were light, tentative, like a bird settling on a fence wire.

Tess didn't pull away. She felt the warmth of her own blood rising to meet the cool skin of Clara’s palm.

"I didn't know him," Clara said, her voice dropping into a register so low that it seemed to belong to the hum of the engine. "When I met him in August down at the lumber yard, he seemed... he seemed like something that had been built out of mountain stone. Solid. Like he wouldn't blow away when the wind came up. I had just spent two years in Richmond watching my father die of pancreatic cancer in a hospital room that smelled of floor wax and canned ginger ale, and Luke looked like... he looked like nothing could ever break him."

She let out a short, ragged breath that misted the glass.

"I didn't realize until October," Clara continued, "that the stone was hollow. That there was nobody inside it."

Tess turned her hand under Clara’s palm until their fingers laced together.

It was not a sudden movement. It was slow, inevitable, like water finding its way through a crack in the limestone. Clara’s grip tightened instantly, her fingers pressing into the spaces between Tess’s knuckles with a fierce, quiet desperation that made Tess’s throat close up with an ache that had nothing to do with Luke Avery.

Below their knees, Roscoe sighed in his sleep. He stretched his hind legs out, his claws clicking against the steel floorboard, and laid his chin heavier across Tess’s boots.

"He has maybe five months left, Clara," Tess said, her voice shaking slightly. "The splenic bleed was a warning. The next one won't stop with a belt and two bath towels."

Clara looked at their joined hands resting on the olive-drab canvas of the tote bag between them.

"I know," Clara said.

"And when he goes," Tess said, "there won't be any reason for you to meet me at the bridge."

Clara looked up. Her eyes were completely steady, dark and fierce in the small cab, reflecting the yellow light of the snow.

"Don't tell me what reasons I have, Tess Avery," Clara whispered.

She leaned in across the bench seat.

She didn't hesitate. She brought her other hand up, her bare fingers cupping the side of Tess’s jaw, her thumb brushing the small, pale scar near the corner of Tess’s mouth where an angry barn cat had scratched her four years ago.

Her lips were cold from the snow, but her mouth was soft, tasting of honey and Earl Grey tea.

The kiss was slow, deep, carrying the whole weight of the four miles of frozen switchbacks, the three hundred dollars in the white envelope, the blue binder with its rows of numbers, and the sound of the river tearing itself against the stones in the dark.

When Clara pulled back, her forehead rested against Tess’s temple, their breath mingling in a single, ragged cloud.

"I haven't slept in that loft since the night you came up the mountain," Clara whispered against her skin. "I sleep on the cot in the drafting room."

Tess closed her eyes. She could smell the cherry wood on Clara’s coat, the cold wool of the scarf, the iron tang of the river.

"Clara," she said.

"Don't go back to Mill Creek tonight," Clara said. "The road is going to drift two feet deep before midnight. You won't make the turn at the second bridge."

Tess opened her eyes. Through the windshield, the snow was falling so thickly that the iron bridge had vanished, replaced by a swirling, white curtain that had erased the road, the river, and the county line.

She looked down at Roscoe, who had opened one brown eye, watching them with that calm, ancient hound patience that asks for nothing except to be where the pack is.

"All right," Tess said.

### Chapter 6

The snow stayed on the high ridges for sixteen days.

It was not the kind of snow that brought the county to a standstill with plows and salt spreaders; it was the quiet, settled snow that packed down into a blue, glassy crust along the hollows, muffling the sound of the creeks until the whole mountain seemed to be holding its breath under a quilt of frozen wool.

In the house on the ridge, the days developed a new, secret architecture.

Tess’s Subaru stayed parked under the cedar lean-to beside Luke’s stack of seasoning oak. In the mornings, while the light over the gap was still that deep, slate violet that precedes the sun, Tess would walk down to the pump house with a five-gallon bucket of hot water from the stove to thaw the brass petcock that always froze when the temperature dropped below twelve.

Clara would already be at the drafting table in the front room, wearing an old red flannel shirt over her sweatshirt, her drafting lamp bent low over a four-foot sheet of mylar.

The sound of the house was different now.

It had lost that hollow, singing resonance that Tess had hated in the spring. It was filled with the small, domestic noises of two women who had stopped explaining why they were in the same room: the scratch of Clara’s 0.3-millimeter pencil against the plastic film; the clatter of the cast-iron skillet as Tess fried cornmeal mush with butter; the heavy, rhythmic thump of Roscoe’s tail against the hearth rug whenever either of them walked past the stove.

They did not talk about Luke.

His absence was not a presence; it was a blank space on a map, like the unorganized townships in Maine that Clara had shown Tess in an old atlas on the shelf—territory that had been surveyed once with a compass and chain, and then abandoned to the spruce.

On the third night of the freeze, Roscoe had an episode of weakness.

It was not a bleed; it was the slow, inevitable creep of the anemia that accompanies chronic malignancy. He went to follow Clara into the kitchen when she went to fetch a pitcher of water, and his hocks simply buckled beneath him on the smooth pine floor. He sat down hard, his rear legs splayed out like a puppy's, his breath coming in two short, puzzled snorts.

Clara dropped the pitcher. The water splashed across the boards, soaking the hem of her trousers.

"Tess!"

Tess came out of the bathroom with a towel in her hands. She saw the dog on the floor, saw the white rims of his eyes, and dropped to her knees beside him before Clara could reach his head.

"Easy," Tess said, her hands moving over his spine, feeling the heat of his muscles. "Easy, old man. You just got ahead of your legs."

She checked his gums. They were pale—a faint, dusty peach—but they were not porcelain.

"He's tired, Clara," Tess said quietly, looking up. "His bone marrow can't keep up with the red cell turnover. The tumor steals the iron."

Clara stood over them, her knuckles pressed against her lips, her face pale in the lamplight.

"What do we do?"

"We don't do anything," Tess said. She put her arms around Roscoe’s chest, under his front legs, and gathered his seventy pounds against her bib overalls. "We help him onto his bed. We give him his dinner in a bowl on the floor so he doesn't have to stand up to reach it. And we don't make him walk out to the woods to relieve himself if he can't make the gravel."

Together, they slid him onto the thick orthopedic mattress by the woodstove.

Roscoe lay on his side, his ribs expanding and contracting with a slow, heavy rhythm. When Clara brought him a bowl of warm broth with shredded hen and pumpkin, he ate it with small, deliberate licks, never lifting his head from the fleece cushion.

When he was finished, he licked Clara’s wrist twice, left a wet smudge of broth on the cuff of her flannel shirt, and went to sleep.

That night, Tess did not sleep on the cot in the workroom.

She lay beside Clara in the narrow bed in the back room—the room that had been Luke’s tool room, where the walls still smelled faintly of neat’s-foot oil and chainsaw bar lubricant. The bed was small, an iron cot with a horsehair mattress that sagged slightly toward the middle, covered in three heavy wool blankets that had been washed until the nap was thick and dense as felt.

The room was freezing. Their breath rose together in small, white plumes toward the uninsulated rafters.

Clara lay on her side, her face pressed into the hollow of Tess’s throat, her arms wrapped around Tess’s ribcage with a fierce, possessive tightness that felt like she was trying to hold the bones together against the cold.

"I'm afraid," Clara whispered into the skin of Tess’s neck.

Tess laid her hand on the back of Clara’s head, her fingers tangling in the thick, dark hair that had come loose from its pins.

"I know," Tess said.

"Not of him dying," Clara said. "I mean... I am. I think about his heart every time he sighs. But I'm afraid of... after."

Tess stared up at the black hemlock rafters. Through a knot-hole in the siding, a single ray of moonlight fell across the floor, cold and blue as a needle.

"There won't be an after that isn't this," Tess said.

"How do you know?"

"Because you don't go back down the mountain after you've seen the frost from the top," Tess whispered. "Luke thought this ridge was something you owned because you had a deed registered at the courthouse in Marion. He didn't understand that the ridge doesn't care whose name is on the paper. You have to carry the water every morning. If you don't carry the water, the pipes split."

Clara lifted her head. In the dim blue moonlight, her eyes were dark pools, her mouth parted slightly.

She kissed Tess—not the desperate, frozen kiss of the truck cab, but a slow, quiet, aching kiss that tasted of the cold room and the salt of her own skin.

Tess reached down, found the hem of Clara’s flannel shirt, and slid her hand up under the wool.

Clara’s skin was smooth, burning with that fierce, interior mountain heat that defies the frost on the glass. When Tess’s palm touched the curve of her hip, Clara let out a long, shuddering sigh that shook her whole frame, her mouth opening against Tess’s lips, her fingers digging into Tess’s shoulder blades as if she were climbing an iron ladder out of the dark.

They moved together under the heavy wool blankets, slow and deliberate, their bodies finding each other with a quiet, certain knowledge that made the six months of silence seem like nothing more than the time it takes for a field to lie fallow between the wheat and the clover.

Below the window, on his bed by the dying cherry fire, Roscoe gave one long, contented groan in his sleep, and then the house went completely silent under the snow.

### Chapter 7

The letter came on the third of January.

It was not in a legal envelope; it was a single piece of yellow lined legal paper, folded twice and tucked into a brown envelope with Luke’s company logo in the corner: *AVERY EXCAVATION & GRADING - DUTCH GAP, VA.* It had been mailed from Beckley, West Virginia, with an eight-cent surcharge for insufficient postage that the mail carrier had collected from Clara in two nickels at the box by the road.

Clara brought it into the clinic at four on a Tuesday afternoon.

The snow had begun to rot under three days of warm, yellow fog—the January thaw that turns the mountain hollows into rivers of red grease and fills the roadside ditches with foam.

Tess was sitting at the centrifuge table, filling out the state rabies certificates for the month of December.

Clara laid the yellow paper on the Formica beside the refractometer.

Tess looked at it.

The handwriting was Luke’s: large, awkward, slanting heavily to the right, written with a thick carpenter’s pencil that had blunted on the fourth line.

> *Clara,*  
> *The job up here is running through April. They hit a seam of shale on the second cut that has to be hammered out with the pneumatic breaker, so I took the night lead on the compressor crew. It pays twenty-eight an hour with the sub.*  
>  
> *Clyde Henderson called my brother on Sunday. He said he saw Tess’s car up at the house three times last week. He said she was carrying wood in from the lean-to like she lived there.*  
>  
> *I don't know what you two are doing with that dog. I don't know if you're trying to make a fool out of me in the county or if you just think because I'm fifty miles up a hole I don't hear what people say at the feed store.*  
>  
> *If the dog is sick, bring him down to Arthur and let Arthur do what needs doing. I'll pay the bill when I come down for the tax assessment in February.*  
>  
> *Tell Tess her mother’s sewing machine is still in the dry shed behind the shop. If she wants it, she better get it out before the roof lets go on the west corner.*  
>  
> *Luke*

Tess read it twice. She didn't look up until she had folded the paper back along its original creases.

Clara was standing by the examination table, her yellow oilskin coat dripping dirty snow-water onto the rubber mat. Her face was set in that cold, stubborn mask that Tess had come to recognize as her defense against the world east of the Blue Ridge.

"I'm not leaving the house, Tess," Clara said.

"He didn't ask you to leave," Tess said quietly.

"He didn't ask me anything," Clara said, her voice rising slightly. "He wrote to me like I was a tenant who had let the ditch get full of leaves. He doesn't care about the house. He doesn't even care about the dog. He just cares that Clyde Henderson saw your car."

Arthur Mercer came through the swinging doors from the kennel room, carrying a tray of clean glass syringes. He took one look at the yellow paper on the counter, one look at the two women standing three feet apart under the neon lights, and set the tray down on the drying rack with a soft *clink*.

"Clyde Henderson," Arthur said, without looking at either of them, "has three heifers with foot rot and an eighty-acre pasture that hasn't seen a bag of lime since the Carter administration. If Clyde spent half as much time cleaning his milking parlor as he does sitting on the bench at the feed store watching who drives up the ridge, he wouldn't owe me fourteen hundred dollars for oxytetracycline."

He reached into his pocket, pulled out a peppermint lozenge, and unwrapped it with his thick, scarred thumbs.

"How's the dog today, Miss Lind?" Arthur asked, popping the candy into his cheek.

"He's quiet," Clara said, her voice dropping. "He ate his rice. He didn't want to get up for his noon walk."

Arthur nodded. He looked at Tess.

"Let's put the probe on him," Arthur said.

Tess felt the cold drop through her again—that old, familiar dread that never really left her ribs.

They brought Roscoe in from the back room. He walked slowly, his nails clicking with a dull, heavy cadence on the linoleum, his head carried low, level with his shoulders. He didn't look at Arthur. He walked straight to Clara, stood between her boots, and leaned his weight against her knees with that deep, shuddering sigh that had become his way of greeting the room.

Tess knelt down with the clippers, but there was no need to shave him; the hair on his right chest had never grown back fully, leaving a gray, fuzzy patch over the fifth rib.

She squirted the blue gel onto the skin.

Arthur pressed the probe into the space.

The screen flickered.

The black halo was back.

It was not as large as it had been on that first terrible afternoon in October, but it was there—a dark, crescent-shaped pocket of fluid forty millimeters deep, pressing against the right ventricular wall like a heavy thumb.

And the mass on the atrium had changed.

It was no longer the size of a walnut. It had spread along the margin of the great vessels, ragged and gray, its borders ragged as a piece of wet moss clinging to an old stone.

Arthur stared at the screen for thirty seconds. He didn't move the probe. The only sound in the room was the hum of the unbalanced centrifuge and the slow, heavy breathing of the three people around the table.

Finally, Arthur clicked the freeze button on the machine.

He took a paper towel, wiped the blue gel from the dog's chest, and let Roscoe’s lip drop back over his teeth.

"Arthur?" Clara asked.

Arthur didn't look at her. He looked at Tess.

"The wall is thin, Tess," Arthur said softly. "It's weeping through the myocardium. We can tap it again today, and he’ll breathe easy for two weeks. Maybe three. But we're near the end of the rope."

"How near?" Clara whispered.

Arthur turned to her then. His old eyes were very kind, surrounded by deep, yellow wrinkles that looked like they had been carved into his face by fifty years of mountain wind.

"Miss Lind," Arthur said, "an animal doesn't have an appointment with next year. He has today. Right now, he isn't hurting. His chest is tight, and he's tired, but he isn't in agony. When that mass lets go completely—and it will let go, probably between now and the time the redbuds bloom—he won't suffer for two weeks. It'll be over in twenty minutes."

He laid his heavy, scarred hand on Roscoe’s head, right between the ears, his fingers stroking the soft fur.

"The question isn't how long we can keep the heart beating," Arthur said. "The question is whether you want him to spend his last good days riding up and down the mountain in a cardboard box, or if you want him to lie on that rug by the stove until he tells you he's done."

Clara looked at Tess.

Tess was kneeling on the floor, her hand resting on Roscoe’s shoulder. She could feel the faint, muffled flutter of the heart against his ribs—that small, brave engine that had carried Luke across five hollows, that had slept across her own feet through seven years of marriage, and that had brought this woman with the cedar-wood coat into her life like an unexpected thaw in the middle of January.

"We tap him today," Tess said, her voice clear and hard as river glass. "We give him the two weeks. And then we don't bring him back to this table."

Clara let out a small, wet sob. She dropped to her knees beside Tess, her arms going around Roscoe’s neck, her forehead pressed against his grizzled muzzle.

"All right," Clara whispered into his fur. "All right, boy. Two weeks."

Arthur reached for the sixteen-gauge needle.

"Set up the stopcock, Tess," he said.

### Chapter 8

The two weeks stretched into three, and then into the first week of February.

The thaw had passed, replaced by that gray, iron cold that comes to the high Appalachians before the spring breaks—not snow, but a dry, bitter frost that freezes the water buckets in the barn by nine in the evening and leaves the salt licks coated in a rim of white rime that tastes of stone.

Luke came down on a Friday night.

He didn't call from the gap. He didn't leave a message with his brother.

Tess was in the kitchen of the house on the ridge, her sleeves rolled up, kneading a loaf of sourdough bread on the maple counter that Luke had planed out of an old beam five years ago.

Through the high south windows, the headlights appeared at the bottom of the meadow—the long, yellow beams of the Dodge flatbed, bouncing over the frozen ruts of the driveway with that heavy, familiar clatter of loose tire chains.

Clara came out of the drafting room, a ruler still in her hand.

She stopped at the edge of the kitchen linoleum. Her face was very pale, but her eyes were steady.

"He's here," Clara said.

"I hear him," Tess said.

She didn't wipe the flour from her hands. She stood by the counter, her back straight, listening to the truck pull up beside the Subaru under the lean-to.

The engine died with a violent shudder that rattled the tin roof of the shed.

For two minutes, there was no sound except the ticking of the woodstove and Roscoe’s breathing from his bed by the hearth. The dog had not gotten up. He had lifted his head, his ears turned toward the door, his nostrils twitching twice as he caught the scent of diesel and road salt drifting through the threshold.

The heavy oak door swung open.

Luke Avery stood in the doorway.

He looked older. The three months in the West Virginia shale camp had weathered his skin to the color of an old boot, and his right shoulder was hitched slightly higher than his left, his hand buried in the pocket of an oil-stained Carhartt jacket that had the zipper pulled up to his chin with a piece of copper wire.

He carried a canvas duffel bag in his left hand.

He stopped three feet inside the room, his muddy work boots planted on the braided rug.

He looked at Clara.

Then he looked at Tess standing by the maple counter with flour on her forearms.

Then he looked down at the dog.

Roscoe gave a single, low whine—a sound so thin and rusty that it seemed to take all the breath left in his chest. He thumped his tail once against the sheepskin pad, but his hips did not move. He lay there, his brown eyes cloudy with cataract and anemia, watching Luke with that ancient, unasking recognition that dogs have for the men who found them in the rain.

Luke didn't drop his duffel bag. He stood entirely still, his jaw working behind his three-day beard.

"You look like hell, Luke," Tess said.

Her voice was not sharp; it was just the plain, level truth of two people who had known each other since they were nine years old at the consolidated school in Dutch Gap.

Luke let his breath out through his teeth. It smelled of stale coffee and rolling tobacco.

"The compressor blew a head gasket on Tuesday," Luke said, his voice gravel-rough from the night shift. "We were twelve hours in the sleet trying to turn the studs out with a pipe wrench."

He dropped the duffel bag. It hit the floor with a heavy, metallic thud—the sound of three heavy crescent wrenches and an iron pry bar shifting in the bottom.

He walked across the room, his boots leaving gray crusts of dried shale on the pine boards, and stopped beside the woodstove.

He looked down at Roscoe.

He reached out with his right boot—the heavy, steel-toed work boot that had kicked eighty different tractor tires—and nudged the edge of the sheepskin cushion.

"He's thin," Luke said.

"He's eighty pounds of bone and tumor, Luke," Tess said, walking over with a dry towel to wipe her hands. "He hasn't had forty pounds of muscle since Thanksgiving."

Luke knelt down.

It was an awkward, heavy movement—the movement of a thirty-one-year-old man whose joints were already twenty years older than his birth certificate. He laid his bare, grease-blackened hand on Roscoe’s ribs.

Under his palm, the heart was fluttering like a moth trapped inside an envelope.

Luke’s thumb found the notched ear—the old scar from the raccoon fight behind the lumber yard when they were twenty-three and living on seven dollars an hour.

"He don't know me," Luke said, his voice dropping into a register that Tess had heard only once before—the night his father’s tractor had rolled into the pond on Kettle Creek.

"He knows you," Clara said from the kitchen doorway.

Luke looked up.

He looked at Clara’s red flannel shirt—the shirt that had belonged to his younger brother, who had moved to North Carolina five years ago. He looked at the blueprints pinned above the sink. He looked at the loaf of bread rising on the maple counter.

"Clyde said you two were living here together," Luke said.

"We are," Clara said.

Luke didn't curse. He didn't stand up. He stayed on one knee on the braided rug, his hand still resting on the dog's ribcage.

"My brother said people are talking down at the church," Luke said. "He said Mrs. Gidley told the preacher that the two women who took Luke Avery’s dog were sleeping in his bed."

Tess walked over and stood beside Clara. She laid her hand on Clara’s shoulder—not as a defiance, not as a show of possession, but as a plain, unmovable fact, like an oak corner-post that had been tamped four feet into the rocky ground.

"We're sleeping in the back room on the cot, Luke," Tess said. "And Mrs. Gidley hasn't had anything to think about since her husband went into the ground eight years ago. If you want to fight somebody about what people say at the church, you go down to the gap and fight Clyde Henderson. He’s the one who spends four hours a day sitting on a lard bucket at the store."

Luke looked at Tess’s hand on Clara’s shoulder.

He looked at it for a long time.

His face didn't go red; it went pale under the grease and the shale dust, the lines around his mouth deepening into two gray grooves that looked like they had been gouged with a cold chisel.

"You took everything I had, Tess," Luke whispered.

The words had no anger in them. They were just heavy, tired, falling into the room like stones dropped down a dry well.

Tess shook her head.

"I didn't take anything, Luke," she said, her voice dropping into that quiet, terrible intimacy that only belongs to people who have shared an empty kitchen. "You left it. You left the house, you left the dog, and you left me three years before we ever signed that paper in Marion. You left because you couldn't stand being in a room where somebody wanted you to say something that wasn't about a culvert or a chainsaw."

She stepped forward and knelt down on the rug opposite him, with Roscoe’s head between them.

"Look at this dog, Luke," Tess said.

Luke looked down.

"He has three days," Tess said. "Maybe four. His kidneys are shutting down from the pressure on his vena cava. He stopped eating this morning. When he stops drinking, Arthur is coming up the ridge with the blue bottle."

Luke’s throat worked. A single drop of sweat, dark with shale dust, ran down the side of his neck and disappeared into the collar of his Carhartt.

"I don't want to see it," Luke said.

"I know you don't," Tess said softly. "You never want to see it. But you're here. And Clara is here. And I'm here. That's the only three people this dog ever knew."

Luke laid his forehead against the dog’s neck.

His heavy shoulders hitched once, a silent, violent spasm that shook the whole frame of his body. He didn't weep out loud; he just stayed there, his grease-stained forehead pressed into the rough, gray fur of the hound he had pulled out of a ditch seven years ago, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps that smelled of diesel and mountain dirt.

Clara walked across the room.

She didn't look at Tess. She knelt down beside Luke, on his left side, and laid her hand on his bad shoulder—the shoulder that had been pinned together with three stainless-steel screws after the timber skidder rolled.

She didn't speak. She just held him there by the woodstove, between the flour on the counter and the shale dust on the floor, while the mountain night closed in around the house and the hemlocks leaned against the eaves in the cold.

### Chapter 9

On Monday morning, the wind shifted into the southwest.

It was the true break—the warm, wet Atlantic wind that comes up over the Blue Ridge in early February, smelling of wet earth, rotting leaves, and the first faint, green hint of wild ramps pushing their way through the limestone shale along the creek bottoms.

The ice had gone out of the ruts on the driveway.

Arthur Mercer’s white Dodge truck came up the ridge at ten o'clock, moving slowly, its big tires throwing sprays of red mud against the dried laurel on the banks.

Arthur got out of the cab, carrying his black leather bag in one hand and a gallon jug of povidone scrub in the other. He had his heavy wool coat on, but he had left his hat on the seat of the truck, his gray hair blowing wild in the southwest wind.

He walked up the stone steps without knocking and pushed the door open.

The room was clean.

The sourdough bread had been baked and set under a clean linen towel on the counter. The duffel bag was gone from the floor; Luke had carried it down to his flatbed at dawn, but he hadn't left. He was sitting on the wooden bench by the woodstove, his hands between his knees, wearing a clean chambray shirt that smelled of lye soap.

Roscoe was lying on his quilt in the center of the room.

He had not moved since Sunday night. His eyes were half-open, glazed, tracking only the light from the south windows where the sun was pouring across the pine floorboards like warm honey.

Arthur set his black bag on the corner of the hearth.

He didn't say good morning. He looked at Luke, gave a single, short nod of his head, and then dropped onto his knee beside the dog.

He laid his fingers on Roscoe’s femoral groove.

"He's running at forty," Arthur said quietly. "The kidneys are done, Tess."

Tess was sitting on the floor by Roscoe’s head, her knees pulled up to her chest. She had her scrub top on—the faded blue one with the small paw-print pocket that Roscoe had nosed for liver treats through six years of Thursdays.

"I know," Tess said.

Clara was sitting on the other side, her hand resting flat over Roscoe’s ribcage.

Arthur opened the black bag. He took out the small, amber bottle of sodium pentobarbital—the thick, clear liquid that has the color of mountain honey—and an eighteen-gauge syringe.

He laid the syringe on a clean paper towel on the hearth.

"Luke," Arthur said. "You want to hold him?"

Luke looked at the dog.

He stood up from the bench, his boots heavy on the floorboards. He walked over, knelt down at Roscoe’s rear, and gathered the dog’s back legs into his arms, holding them against his chest with that steady, massive leverage that he used when he was setting a six-by-six post into wet concrete.

"I've got him, Doc," Luke said. His voice was steady now, low and clear as river water.

Arthur looked at Tess. "Cephalic or saphenous?"

"Cephalic," Tess said.

She took Roscoe’s right front leg—the leg that had the gray, shaved patch from the November catheter. She rolled the vein with her thumb, feeling the thin, fragile vessel rise under the skin like a wet thread.

Clara leaned down until her cheek was touching Roscoe’s forehead.

"Roscoe," Clara whispered. "Good boy. Good, sweet boy. You go on now. You go on down to the creek."

Arthur slipped the needle into the vein.

He drew back; a small plume of dark, venous blood surged into the barrel of the syringe, mixing with the blue liquid.

"All right, Tess," Arthur said softly.

Tess released the vein.

Arthur pushed the plunger.

It took six seconds.

Under Tess’s fingers, the thin pulse gave three rapid, fluttering beats, then slowed: one... two... three...

And then stopped.

Roscoe took one long, deep shudder of a breath—the breath of a dog who had run ten miles through the high laurel and had finally reached the shade of the big hemlock by the water—and his head settled into Clara’s lap.

The room was completely quiet.

Through the south windows, the southwest wind rattled the glass in its pine frames, carrying the sound of Kettle Creek roaring in full flood down in the gap below.

Arthur laid his stethoscope against the dog's chest for ten seconds. He took the earpieces out, let the tubing hang around his neck, and closed his black bag with a sharp, brass click.

He stood up.

"He was a good dog, Luke," Arthur said.

"Yes, sir," Luke said. He didn't let go of the hind legs. He stayed on his knees, his face turned toward the fire, his hands buried in the coarse brown fur.

Arthur walked to the door. He paused on the threshold, looking back at the three of them sitting around the empty dog on the floor.

"I left the receipt on the counter, Luke," Arthur said gruffly. "You can pay it when you get the check from the pipeline."

He pulled the door shut behind him. His truck started with a low rumble in the yard, backed around the lean-to, and clattered down the ridge toward the hard road.

### Chapter 10

They buried him in the meadow above the house, where the ridge flattened out into a shelf of wild fescue that looked down into the blue notch of Dutch Gap.

Luke dug the hole.

He used his five-foot iron spud bar and an Ames round-point shovel, working with that steady, rhythmic mountain pace that doesn't stop for breath or water. He cut through the three inches of thawed topsoil, then through the layer of yellow clay, down into the gray limestone shale until the hole was four feet deep and six feet long, with the sides shaved straight and square as a timber cellar.

He threw the shovel out onto the grass, climbed up out of the hole, and wiped his brow with his forearm.

Tess and Clara carried Roscoe up the slope on his patchwork quilt, holding the four corners between them.

They laid him in the bottom of the shale.

Luke stood on the pile of yellow dirt, leaning on his shovel handle. He looked down into the hole for two minutes, his eyes shaded by his work cap.

Then he reached into his pocket and pulled out an old, brass-tagged collar—the leather collar with the three brass rivets that Roscoe had worn the first winter on Kettle Creek, before they had ever bought the nylon leads at the clinic.

He tossed it into the hole. It landed on the quilt with a soft, dull *thud*.

"He was always a good dog to find things in the brush," Luke said to the valley.

He took the shovel and began throwing the yellow dirt back into the hole.

Tess and Clara stood together by the edge of the woods, their shoulders touching against the warm wind. Tess held Clara’s hand, their fingers locked together inside the pocket of Clara’s chore coat.

When the mound was rounded and tamped smooth with the flat of the shovel, Luke laid three heavy slabs of gray limestone across the top to keep the foxes from digging in the fresh clay.

He stood up, wiped the dirt from his palms onto his trousers, and looked at them.

The sun was directly overhead now, warm and golden through the bare branches of the chestnuts.

"I'm leaving the flatbed with the boy down at the garage in Meacham," Luke said. "He’s going to put a new clutch in it before I go back to West Virginia."

Tess looked at him. "When are you going back?"

"Tonight," Luke said. "They got a shift starting at midnight on the river cut."

He looked at Clara.

"You staying in the house?" he asked.

"Yes," Clara said.

"The roof on the dry shed is going to go if we get a wet snow in March," Luke said, his voice level, matter-of-fact, stripped of every trace of the bitterness that had lived in the room on Friday night. "You tell Tess’s brother to come up with his tractor and pull those rafters down before they crush the timber. He can have the oak for his woodstove."

Clara nodded. "I'll tell him."

Luke looked at Tess then.

He took off his work cap, ran his dirty hand through his thinning brown hair, and put the cap back on, pulling the bill down low over his eyes.

"You look good, Tess," he said.

It was the first time he had looked her in the eyes without flinching since the day they had divided the Mason jars on the kitchen table in May.

### Chapter 11

The habit of the hand was the hardest thing to break.

### Chapter 12

March in the Blue Ridge did not arrive with lambs; it arrived with mud.

The frost line came out of the ground in three days of warm, sulfurous rain that smelled like an old cellar. The red clay on the secondary roads dissolved into a thick, grease-like paste that pulled the hubcaps off the mail trucks and left two-foot ruts along the ditch lines of Route 58.

Arthur Mercer’s right knee—the one that had been stepped on by a Belgian draft horse during the bicentennial year—had swollen to the circumference of an eight-quart lard pail. He sat on an upturned milk crate in the surgery room with a bag of frozen peas strapped to his patella, his spectacles pushed up into the gray brush of his hair, barking orders through the open door while Tess loaded the back of the clinic’s white four-wheel-drive flatbed.

"Take two bottles of calcium borogluconate," Arthur shouted, his voice hoarse from tobacco and damp air. "And check the expiration on the oxytocin. Clyde Henderson had a heifer down in the bottom pasture this morning that’s been trying to turn herself inside out since four o'clock."

"I don't care if Clyde hasn't paid his taxes to the state of Virginia," Arthur growled. "The calf isn't the one who bought the bad hay. Go down there, Tess. If the head’s turned back in the pelvic canal, you use the chains. Don't you try to muscle a three-hundred-pound calf out of a two-year-old heifer with your bare arms or you'll tear her vagina to ribbons."

"And take the girl," Arthur added.

Arthur was looking at her through the screen door, his old face crossed with the wire mesh.

"What girl?"

"Miss Lind," Arthur said. "She’s been sitting on the bench by the library in the gap for three days with a clipboard, looking like somebody stole her lunch money. Clara took the highlighter out of her mouth. "What's wrong? Did another dog come in?"

"No," Tess said. "Clyde Henderson has an obstetrical emergency in a heifer. Arthur's knee is the size of an anvil. Put your boots on."

### Chapter 13

The dry shed behind the house on the ridge gave up on a Tuesday in the middle of April.

It didn't collapse with a roar; it simply groaned three times during a warm spring thunder shower, the rotten oak rafters on the west corner sliding off the top plate with a dry, grinding *crump* that dropped twenty sheets of rusted tin into the wild blackberries.

Tess’s brother, Caleb, came up the lane at four that afternoon.

He was driving their father’s 1968 John Deere 4020—a high-wheeled, two-cylinder diesel tractor that smelled of raw fuel and burnt hydraulic oil, its green paint faded to the color of pond scum. Caleb was twenty-six, six feet two, with the same gray, deep-set eyes as Tess, and he wore his baseball cap turned backward so the grease on the bill wouldn't get in his hair when he was under a chassis.

He backed the tractor up to the ruins of the shed, killed the throttle, and hopped down into the weeds.

Clara was standing by the pump house, holding a crowbar and an old flathead screwdriver.

Caleb stopped three feet from her. He looked at the crowbar in her hand, then at the silver ring with the hemlock needles, then at her boots.

"You the one that draws the barns?" Caleb asked.

His voice had the same slow, drawling mountain cadence as Luke’s, but it was lighter, without that hard, defensive iron in the middle of it.

"I'm Clara," she said.

"Caleb," he said, touching the back of his cap with two greasy fingers. "Tess said there was an old White rotary sewing machine under the west wall. Said if we didn't get it out today, the rain would rust the treadle until it wouldn't turn."

"It's back behind the cedar laths," Clara said, turning toward the wreckage. "I tried to move the rafters, but the eight-by-eights are pinned with locust trunnels."

Caleb walked over, looked at the tangle of gray oak timbers and rusted tin, and spat into the blackberries.

"Luke always did build things like he was expecting an artillery barrage," Caleb remarked. "A man don't need eight-by-eight hemlock to keep the weather off forty dollars' worth of dry lath."

He unhitched a heavy logging chain from the tractor’s three-point hitch, dragged forty pounds of iron links through the weeds, and wrapped them twice around the main corner post.

"Get back by the pump house, Clara," Caleb said. "If this link snaps, it'll take your shin bone off."

Clara stepped back.

Caleb climbed into the steel pan seat of the tractor, kicked the starter with his heel, and eased the clutch out.

The big John Deere roared, its vertical exhaust pipe throwing two puffs of black soot against the hemlocks. The rear tires dug four inches into the wet clover, the chain came bar-tight with a high, singing *ping*, and then the whole west corner of the shed lifted six inches into the air and came down into the weeds with a splintering crash.

Caleb cut the engine.

"There she is," he said, pointing with a dirty thumb.

Beneath the shattered roof, sitting on a dry pallet of cedar boards, was the sewing machine.

It was an old cast-iron treadle machine from the twenties, its black enamel decorated with faded gold scrollwork that depicted wild grapevines. It was covered in twenty years of sawdust and dried spiderwebs, but the heavy iron wheel turned when Clara touched it with her finger, spinning with a smooth, silent glide that smelled of sewing-machine oil and cedar wood.

Tess came up the path from the house, carrying a gallon jug of spring water and three tin cups.

She stopped by the tractor, looking at the heap of timber.

"Did you break the treadle, Caleb?"

"Treadle's fine," Caleb said, taking a cup of water from her hand. "Your friend here had it staked out before I got the engine warm."

He drank the water in three long, rhythmic swallows that moved the Adam's apple in his throat, then wiped his chin with his sleeve.

He looked at Tess. Then he looked at Clara, who was down on her knees brushing the dry leaves off the black iron base of the machine.

"Luke was at the sale barn in Wytheville on Saturday," Caleb said.

The mountain seemed to go very quiet. The wind in the hemlocks dropped, leaving only the hum of a bumblebee working at the yellow dandelions by the well.

Tess didn't move. "Was he?"

"He bought four Black Angus yearlings," Caleb said. "He had a new gooseneck trailer behind his truck. Green one. Looked like he paid cash for it."

"He's running cattle?" Clara asked, looking up from the sewing machine.

"He leased the thirty acres on Kettle Creek from old man Gidley," Caleb said. "The bottom land by the old lime kiln. He told my father he was done with the pipeline. Said the mud in West Virginia made his shoulder ache every time the rain set in."

"He asked if you two had killed each other yet," Caleb said to Tess.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him you pulled Clyde Henderson's bull calf out of a two-year-old heifer two weeks ago," Caleb said, a slow, wide grin breaking across his sun-browned face. "And I told him your friend Clara was holding the head while you did it."

"He didn't say nothing," Caleb said. "He just chewed on his match-stem for a minute, looked at the steer pens, and then he said, 'Well. At least somebody got seventy-two dollars out of Clyde Henderson. That's more than I ever managed.'"

"Where do you want it, Tess?"

"In the front room," Tess said. "By the drafting table."

The late afternoon sun was coming down through the gap, long and amber, turning the hemlock bark to the color of cinnamon. Above them, in the high meadow, the three slabs of gray limestone were bathed in the golden light, surrounded by a ring of fresh, green wild fescue that was already six inches high.

Clara reached out and took Tess’s hand.

Her fingers were dirty from the cedar sawdust, but her palm was warm, solid, completely real against Tess’s skin.

"He's on Kettle Creek," Clara said quietly.

"Three miles," Tess said.

Tess looked at the three limestone slabs on the hill. She thought of the eight-year-old hound who had died between Luke’s heavy boots and Clara’s lap, whose old brass collar was resting under the clay beneath the fescue.

"No," Tess said, and as she said it, she knew it was the whole truth of her life. "Three miles is just far enough to let a man tend his own steers."

### Chapter 14

The letter from the Virginia Historic Preservation Trust arrived on the first of May.

It was delivered to the small wooden box at the end of the lane, alongside two seed catalogs and the electric bill for the well pump. It was printed on heavy, off-white bond paper with the state seal embossed in gold foil at the top: *COMMONWEALTH OF VIRGINIA - DEPARTMENT OF HISTORIC RESOURCES - RICHMOND.*

Clara opened it on the front porch while Tess was sitting on the top step, oiling the leather on her work boots with a tin of mink fat.

The morning was warm—the first true mountain summer morning, when the dew stays on the clover until ten o'clock and the air smells of sweet shrub and wet slate.

Clara read the two pages twice.

She didn't speak. She let the paper drop into her lap, her hands resting flat on the cedar decking on either side of her thighs.

Tess dipped her rag into the tin of grease. She didn't look up from the tongue of her boot. "Lexington?"

"Staunton," Clara said.

Her voice was very quiet, almost drowned out by the sound of the tractor working two ridges over on the Henderson place.

"The Woodrow Wilson Birthplace and the old Augusta County Courthouse," Clara said. "Full-time project manager for the restoration of the timber roof trusses. Eighteen months. State benefits. Sixty-four thousand a year."

Tess’s rag stopped moving.

The smell of the mink fat was suddenly very sharp in the warm air, animal and heavy.

"That's what you came down here to find, isn't it?" Tess asked.

Her own voice sounded far away, like someone speaking through an iron pipe under a road.

"When I left Richmond," Clara said, staring out over the meadow toward the gap, "I thought I wanted a project that had an end. A courthouse that had a date on the cornerstone. Something you could measure with a twenty-foot rod, draw on mylar, stamp with a seal, and walk away from when the scaffolding came down."

She turned her head.

The sunlight caught the small silver ring on her finger, the hemlock needles gleaming against her brown skin.

"They want me to start on June the first," Clara said.

"Staunton is two hours up the interstate," Tess said, looking down at the greasy leather of her boot. "You can take the four-lane all the way to Lexington."

"Tess."

Tess stood up. She carried the boot by the heel, the tin of mink fat in her other hand. She walked to the edge of the porch, looking up at the high ridge where the oak leaves were the size of squirrel ears, pale green and translucent against the blue sky.

"You don't have to stay here for me, Clara," Tess said. Her throat was tight, a hard, dry knot that felt like she had swallowed a mouthful of river sand. "We didn't sign anything in a court. We don't have a deed together. We had a dog that had six good months, and now the six months are over."

Clara stood up from the decking.

She didn't drop the letter; she crumpled the two pages of off-white bond paper in her right fist, crushing the state seal into a hard, wrinkled ball the size of a walnut.

She walked across the porch and threw the paper straight into the galvanized tub of water Tess used for washing her boots.

The white ball hit the water with a soft *plop*, bobbing among the drowned beetles.

"Do you know what they have in Staunton, Tess?" Clara asked, her voice shaking with an anger that was so sudden, so fierce, that Tess took half a step back.

"No," Tess said.

"They have brick houses with asphalt shingles," Clara said, stepping into the space between them until her chest was two inches from Tess’s scrub top. "They have paved sidewalks with concrete curbs. They have people who go to dinner parties and talk about architectural preservation like it was an antique chest of drawers you bought at a shop in Charlottesville."

She reached out and grabbed the front of Tess’s scrub top with both hands, twisting the cotton in her fists.

"I spent two years in Richmond watching my father die while six different doctors told me what the percentages were," Clara whispered, her face pale, her gray eyes black with fury. "And then I came up this mountain and met a man who didn't have anything to say, and a dog that was drowning in his own chest."

Her knuckles dug into Tess’s collarbone.

"And then I met you," Clara said. "You were kneeling on a floor in a pair of overalls that smelled like sheep dip, drawing blood out of an animal's heart with a plastic syringe, telling me the truth with the bark on. You didn't give me any percentages, Tess. You just gave me six o'clock in the morning and a three-ring binder."

She leaned her forehead against Tess’s chest, her breath coming in ragged, angry hitches that shook her shoulders.

"I wrote to the trust on Monday," Clara said into the cotton of Tess’s shirt. "Before this letter ever came."

"I told them I was opening an office in Dutch Gap," Clara said, her voice muffled against Tess’s throat. Tess closed her eyes.

The sun was hot on the back of her neck. Down in the meadow, the three slabs of limestone were white against the green fescue, and from the direction of Kettle Creek came the faint, distant sound of Luke Avery’s tractor, turning the red clay for the summer corn.

"Arthur's selling the practice," Tess whispered into Clara’s hair.

Clara pulled back her head. "What?"

Clara looked at her.

The fury had gone out of her face, replaced by that wide, clear stillness that Tess had seen for the first time by the river bridge in the snow.

"Forty percent?" Clara asked.

"Forty percent," Tess said. "It takes twelve thousand dollars down to buy into the partnership. Arthur said he’d carry the note for five years at three percent if I promised not to let Clyde Henderson run up a bill over fifty dollars."

Clara smiled—that slow, brilliant smile that transformed her face into something young and wild.

She reached into her pocket and pulled out the small, black-handled penknife she used for sharpening her pencils.

"I have nine thousand dollars from the Grayson survey," Clara said.

"Clara—"

"Shut up, Tess," Clara said softly. "It's an investment in the county infrastructure. Every architectural office needs an emergency clinic next door in case the draftsperson gets bitten by a sheep."

She reached down, took Tess’s hand, and led her off the porch into the warm, tall clover of the yard.

### Chapter 15

The encounter happened at the feed store on the second Tuesday of June.

Dutch Gap Farmers Supply sat beside the railroad tracks at the mouth of the gap—a long, red-painted warehouse with a concrete loading dock that smelled of molasses, mineral blocks, and dried timothy hay.

Tess had backed the clinic’s white truck up to the dock to load twenty bags of milk replacer for the spring orphan calves.

He looked at the twenty bags of milk replacer.

Then he looked at Tess.

"Sarah Mercer back from North Carolina?" Luke asked.

### Chapter 16

"Forty years," Tess said.

### Chapter 17

The lease on the Mill Creek cottage expired on the thirty-first of July.

It took Tess four trips in the Subaru to clear the four rooms. When she stood in the center of the kitchen on the final afternoon, with the broom in her hand and a bucket of pine-oil wash at her feet, the house did not look like somewhere she had lived for six months; it looked like somewhere a hunter had slept during a rough week of deer season before packing his kerosene stove and moving on.

"Wanted to know if you paid your water bill," Holcomb said. "Said he didn't want the county cutting off the meter on the main line while the well on the ridge was low."

"The well on the ridge isn't low," Tess said. "It's running sixteen gallons a minute from the limestone seam."

"That's what I told him," Holcomb grunted, his knife moving again. "Told him you had that draftsperson up there hauling water like an ox. Said she carried two ten-gallon cans up from the spring house every morning before breakfast just to water her geraniums."

### Chapter 18

The old stone chapel on Whitetop Mountain had been built in 1844 by three Scotch-Irish brothers who had quarry-faced every block of gray gneiss with an eight-pound sledge and an iron star-drill.

It sat on a high, wind-scoured shoulder of the mountain, four thousand feet above the valley floor, where the red spruce trees were permanently stunted by the gales, their branches growing only on the eastern side like green flags pointing toward the sea.

Tess drove the Subaru up the gravel forestry road on Friday afternoon.

The road was narrow, bordered by dense thickets of catawba rhododendron whose purple blossoms had dried to brown seed-pods in the summer heat. At the four-mile marker, the timber gave way to the high mountain balds—broad, rolling expanses of wild oat grass and blackberry brambles that stretched away under a sky so huge and pale that it made the heart feel small in the chest.

The red Tacoma was parked in the shadow of the chapel’s dry-stone cemetery wall.

Clara was not inside the building.

She was standing on a four-foot step-ladder set against the west gable, wearing a pair of faded canvas trousers held up by wide red suspenders, her sleeves rolled past her elbows, holding a twenty-five-foot steel tape against the weathered stone of the belfry arch.

The wind on the bald was constant—a cool, steady rush of air that smelled of wild thyme, spruce resin, and the dry, ancient cold of the high rocks. It whipped Clara’s dark hair across her face, pulling the strands loose from their copper pins, her red flannel shirt snapping like a pennant against her ribs.

Tess killed the engine. She sat in the car for a minute, her hands resting on the steering wheel, just watching.

There was a geometric precision to the way Clara moved that Tess never grew tired of seeing. She didn't hurry; she set her boots on the rungs of the ladder with the quiet certainty of someone who trusted her own center of gravity, her fingers steady as she aligned the brass hook of the tape with the mortar line, her lips moving as she memorized the fraction before she reached for the little wooden pencil behind her ear.

Tess climbed out of the car, carrying a tin pail of cold well water and two ripe yellow peaches she had bought from an orchard truck at the bottom of the mountain.

The wind hit her immediately, cold and thin, lifting the hem of her scrub top.

"Thirty-two feet, seven inches to the spring line," Clara called down without looking. "Don't shake the ladder, Tess. The ground under the south leg is mostly mountain shale."

Tess set the bucket down and held the wooden rails of the ladder with both hands. "You've been up here six hours, Clara. Your ears are the color of a boiled beet."

Clara locked the steel tape with her thumb, let it zip back into its chrome housing with a sharp *clack*, and climbed down the rungs with that light, swinging ease that came from six years of clambering over eighteenth-century roof trusses.

She hit the grass, took the pencil from her ear, and wrote three figures into the black notebook she had tucked into her waistband.

Then she took the tin cup from the bucket, dipped it full, and drank the water without stopping, her throat working in three long, clean swallows.

Water dripped from her chin, sparkling in the high mountain sunlight.

"It's ashlar masonry," Clara said, wiping her mouth with the back of her forearm, her eyes bright with that clear, fierce excitement that only came when she had found something that hadn't moved in a hundred and fifty years. "Look at the corner returns, Tess. They didn't use lime mortar. They bedded the stones in crushed river gravel and clay from the creek bottom. The walls are two feet thick. You couldn't move this building with a D-8 dozer."

Tess handed her a yellow peach.

Clara took it with both hands, broke it in half along the seam with her thumbs, and handed the side with the stone back to Tess.

The fruit was cold, sweet, the juice running down their fingers in sticky yellow ribbons that the wind dried almost instantly.

"Sarah said you're putting the tin on the kennel shed," Tess said, chewing the sweet fruit.

Clara looked at her over the stone wall of the cemetery, where fifty gray fieldstone markers were tilted at forty-five-degree angles in the wild fescue, their inscriptions worn down by a hundred years of mountain rain until they looked like river pebbles.

"The rafters are solid white oak," Clara said. "They're just three inches out of square because the dry shed on the west side settled. If we sister two-by-sixes onto the rafter tails, the tin will lay flat as a pond."

She leaned her back against the dry gneiss of the church wall, looking down into the vast, blue bowl of the valley.

You could see the silver thread of Kettle Creek winding through the pastures, and far to the south, the tiny green square that was Luke Avery’s bottom field, where thirty head of black cattle were clustered like ants around the shadow of a lone sycamore.

"Luke's building a new fence," Clara said quietly.

Tess looked down at the valley. "I know. He bought two hundred locust posts from the sawmill in Damascus."

"He came by here on Wednesday," Clara said.

Tess turned her head.

"He asked if the church was going to fall down," Clara said. "I told him it had been standing since before the railroad was built, and that it would probably be standing when the interstate was covered in briars."

"He gave me this," Clara said.

She didn't take the plumb-bob. She reached out with both hands, slid her fingers under the hem of Tess’s scrub top, and rested her warm palms against the bare skin of Tess’s hips.

"I love you, Tess," Clara whispered into the space between their faces.

"I know you do," Tess said.

She brought her own hands up, her fingers burying themselves in the thick ruff of Clara’s dark hair, and kissed her there on the wind-scoured bald, with the ancient stones at their backs and forty miles of Virginia mountains rolling away to the edge of the world.

### Chapter 19

The drought broke on the tenth of August with thirty-six hours of high, violent mountain thunder that shook the rafters of the house on the ridge until the tin roof rattled like dry corn in a hopper.

It was not a rain that soaked the ground; it was that hard, driving Appalachian downpour that sheets off the baked mountain clay in red rivers, filling the gullies with foam and turning the quiet, rock-strewn creeks into brown, roaring torrents that carry drowned fence posts and whole sycamore trees down toward the valley.

The call came into the clinic at quarter-past five on a Wednesday afternoon.

Sarah Mercer had already taken the white Dodge truck toward Whitetop to deliver a foal that had presented with a retained placenta. Arthur was sitting in the front office with his titanium knee propped on a leather hassock, his spectacles perched on the end of his nose, entering drug inventories into a black ledger with a steel-nibbed pen.

Tess answered the phone on the second ring.

"Dutch Gap Clinic."

"Tess."

The voice was Luke’s, but it was ragged, stripped of that slow, heavy mountain cadence, coming through the wire with a wet, gasping edge that brought her instantly to her feet.

"Luke? What's happened?"

"The sinkhole on the Gidley lease," Luke said, his words fighting the sound of water roaring in the background. "The bottom gave way under the limestone shelf with this rain. I got four yearlings down in the mud. Three of 'em are on their feet, but the black heifer... she’s slid down into the throat of the hole. The red clay’s up to her flank, Tess. The water from the ditch is pouring straight into the throat."

Tess’s medical brain engaged with that cold, mechanical efficiency that had saved fifty animals in six years.

"How deep is she?"

"Eight feet," Luke said. "I got a chain around an oak tree on the bank, but every time she lunges, she sinks three inches deeper into the silt. The water’s coming up to her brisket. If she takes the slurry into her lungs, she’ll drown before I can get the track-hoe down the lane."

"Where is your tractor?"

"Axle-deep in the creek bottom," Luke said through his teeth. "The ground went soft under the drive wheels. I'm on foot, Tess. I need a winch and two heavy straps. And I need somebody who knows how to keep a heifer from breaking her neck while she’s being pulled."

Tess looked at Arthur through the glass door.

Arthur had stopped his pen. He had heard the tone of her voice; he was already reaching for his aluminum crutches behind the desk.

"Sarah's on Whitetop," Tess said into the receiver. "She's two hours out."

"I don't have two hours, Tess," Luke said, and for the first time in her life, she heard the break in his voice—the pure, naked panic of a man who was watching eight hundred dollars of his own blood and bone drown in sixty seconds of red mud. "She’s got twenty minutes."

Tess did not look at Arthur. She looked through the front window, where Clara’s red Tacoma was idling by the gas pump, Clara inside waiting to take Tess home for supper.

"Ten minutes, Luke," Tess said. "Get the chains ready."

She slammed the phone down.

"Arthur," Tess shouted through the door. "Get the large-animal resuscitation kit and forty ccs of xylazine from the lockbox. Put it in a dry bucket."

"I gave old man Gidley six hundred dollars in cash every November," Luke said. "I mowed the thistle out of his ditches. I replaced eighty rods of woven-wire fence along the road out of my own pocket."

"Who are the heirs?" Clara asked.

"Two daughters," Luke said. "One of 'em works for a pharmaceutical company in Durham. The other one married an accountant in Cary. They haven't set foot in Dutch Gap since their mother died in 2014. Clara stood up.

She walked out of the shed into the pale autumn sunlight, looking across the creek toward the high stone foundation of the old grist mill that stood half-hidden by wild grapevines three hundred yards above the bridge.

Luke looked at her, his eyes narrowing. "Old man Gidley said his great-grandfather built it after the second war with the British. Said they ground corn for the whole south side of the mountain until the railroad came through in eighty-eight."

Luke stared at her. "What does that mean in English, Clara?"

"It means," Clara said, turning around with her chin high, "that a developer from Charlotte can't run fifty-two septic tanks within five hundred yards of a documented eighteenth-century millrace without a Class-One Archaeological Assessment and a state conservation permit from Richmond."

Luke looked from Clara to Tess, then back to the stone mill in the weeds.

"How long does an assessment take?" Luke asked.

"Eighteen months," Clara said, a slow, wicked grin breaking across her face. "Maybe twenty-four if the state archaeologist has to come down from Charlottesville with a trowel and a screen to look for hand-forged square nails."

Luke looked at his socket wrench lying on the bench.

He picked it up, turned it over twice in his grease-stained palm, and then tossed it into his metal toolbox with a ringing *clank*.

"Clara," Luke said, his mustache twitching with the first real smile she had seen on his face since July. "You got your boots in the truck?"

"I've got my boots," Clara said.

"Then let's go down to the courthouse in Marion," Luke said. "I reckon it's about time somebody showed them girls in Cary what an eighteenth-century millrace looks like on a piece of paper."

### Chapter 24

The deed room in the Smyth County Courthouse smelled of old vinegar, sulfur, and eighty years of damp basement limestone.

It sat in the sub-grade floor beneath the circuit court, behind an iron-barred gate that had been painted with three coats of institutional green enamel that was flaking off the hinges in long, brittle curls.

Tess, Clara, and Luke arrived at eight-thirty on Friday morning.

She looked at the three of them over the rim of her spectacles.

"Luke Avery," Miss Verna said, her voice like two shingles rubbing together. "I thought you were in West Virginia drilling holes in the shale."

"I'm back on Kettle Creek, Miss Verna," Luke said, leaning his forearms on the oak counter with that natural, respectful mountain slouch he used for women over seventy. "We need to look at the Gidley patents. The ones on the lower fork."

Miss Verna took a slow bite of her cracker. She looked at Tess, then her eyes settled on Clara, taking in the red suspenders, the carpenter's pencil, and the silver ring of hemlock needles.

"You the one living in Luke’s house up on the ridge?" Miss Verna asked.

"I'm Clara Lind," Clara said, meeting the old woman’s gaze without blinking. "I work for the Department of Historic Resources."

Miss Verna made a small, wet sound in her teeth.

"Your father was Thomas Lind," she said.

"Thomas Lind was on the State Library Board when they microfilmed the land grants in seventy-six," Miss Verna said, reaching beneath the counter for a heavy brass ring of iron keys. "Come on back," Miss Verna said. "The Gidley books are in the south stack. Don't you drop no grease on the paper, Luke Avery. Your hands look like you just pulled a calf out of a ditch."

"I washed 'em with lye, Miss Verna," Luke muttered.

For four hours, the only sound in the basement was the heavy *thump* of sixteen-pound leather ledgers being laid onto the zinc research tables, the dry rustle of rag paper that had been made during the Madison administration, and the scratch of Clara’s mechanical pencil.

The grant was there.

It was recorded in Deed Book 4, Page 112, dated October 14, 1794: a patent of three hundred and twenty acres granted by the Commonwealth of Virginia to *Archibald Gidley, Gentleman, late a sergeant in the Continental Line.*

Clara leaned over the table, her magnifying loupe pressed to her eye, her breath misting the old vellum.

"Look at the metes and bounds, Tess," Clara whispered.

Tess leaned in. The ink was brown, faded to the color of dried tea, written in that sharp, flowing eighteenth-century hand that used the long *s* that looked like an *f*:

> *...beginning at a white oak marked with four chops on the north bank of Kettle Creek, thence south forty-two degrees west eighty poles to a limestone rock marked with a cross on the hill above the spring, thence down the meanders of the millrace to the great sycamore at the wagon ford, reserving forever unto the inhabitants of the gap the right of passage and the water-turn for the grinding of meal without toll or hindrance...*

Clara straightened up. Her face was flushed, her gray eyes brilliant with that cold architectural triumph.

"It's a public covenant," Clara said.

Luke was standing behind her, his big hands resting on the edge of the table. "A what?"

"A public covenant," Clara repeated, turning to him. "Archibald Gidley didn't just patent the land, Luke. He dedicated the millrace and the water rights to the town of Dutch Gap in perpetuity. Luke looked at the brown writing on the yellow page.

"The town council," Luke said slowly, "is Clyde Henderson, my second cousin Billy, and Arthur Mercer’s sister."

She looked over Clara’s shoulder at Deed Book 4.

"That's Archibald's grant," Miss Verna said, nodding once. "His bones are up in the cemetery on the ridge behind your house, Luke. Under that flat slab of slate where the groundhogs dig."

She laid a dry, wrinkled hand on Clara’s sleeve—a gesture so light, so brief, that it was gone before Clara could turn around.

"Your father would have liked that drawing of the stone chapel, Clara," Miss Verna said quietly. "I saw it in the state bulletin last week. You got his eye for a corner."

"Thank you, Miss Verna," Clara whispered.

Miss Verna turned toward the iron gate.

### Chapter 25

The meeting of the Dutch Gap Town Council was held on the third Monday of November in the basement of the volunteer fire hall.

The room smelled of stale coffee, wet turnout gear, and the sweet, greasy smoke of the woodstove that heated the three engine bays next door.

Forty people had crowded into the space, sitting on folding metal chairs that screeched against the concrete floor: dairy farmers in clean overalls; women from the church auxiliary with their coats on their laps; three timber contractors from Damascus; and in the second row, sitting with his titanium knee stretched straight out into the aisle, Dr. Arthur Mercer, leaning both hands on a hickory cane he had cut from the fence line thirty years ago.

Clara Lind was standing by the door to the engine bays, wearing her clean white linen shirt, her red suspenders, and her dark hair gathered into a neat knot with two polished wooden pegs.

Clara walked down the center aisle, her boots making a clean, hollow sound on the concrete.

The room leaned forward. Even Clyde Henderson pushed his spectacles down his nose to look.

"The millrace," Clara explained, her finger tracing the clean, black lines of the drawing, "was constructed in 1795 as a timber-lined stone conduit that diverts thirty percent of Kettle Creek through a six-foot drop. Mr. Vance-Holloway gave a short, high-pitched laugh that sounded like a dry clutch slipping.

"Madam," he said, stepping forward with his silver pointer, "that mill has been a ruin since the Second World War. The timber is rotted. The race is filled with river gravel. Under the doctrine of laches and public abandonment—"

"The race is not filled with gravel, Mr. Holloway," a voice barked from the second row.

Arthur Mercer stood up.

"I pulled seven head of cattle out of that race during the flood of sixty-nine," Arthur said, his voice roaring through the basement. He turned to the room, his old eyes blazing behind his thick glasses.

"And if you think this town is going to trade Archibald Gidley’s stone for fifty-two cardboard houses full of people from Charlotte who complain about the smell of cow manure every time the wind comes out of the south," Arthur shouted, "then you have got a whole lot more water on the brain than I have on my knee!"

The room erupted.

Three dairy farmers in the back row began to stomp their heavy work boots on the concrete floor, the sound booming through the building like thunder. Caleb Avery gave a piercing mountain whistle through his teeth, and Mrs. Gidley’s sister began to fan herself with a fire-safety brochure.

Mrs. Martha Mercer Vance picked up her black patent-leather handbag.

She brought it down on the table with a crash that made Mr. Vance-Holloway jump six inches off his Italian leather loafers.

"Order," Mrs. Vance said.

The room went dead silent.

Mrs. Vance looked at Clara’s drawing. She looked at the signature on the bottom right corner: *CLARA M. LIND - ARCHITECTURAL SURVEYOR - DUTCH GAP, VIRGINIA.*

"Clyde," Mrs. Vance said, her voice flat and cold as river slate. "Do you have a motion?"

"Second," Billy Avery said before Clyde had even closed his mouth.

"All in favor," Mrs. Vance said.

"Aye," the three council members said together.

Tess smiled.

She touched the brass whistle in her pocket, took Copper’s lead, and waited by the door to walk her home.

Evelyn Lind arrived on a Friday in the first week of December in a dark blue Volvo wagon that had two mountain bikes on the roof rack and an emissions inspection sticker from the city of Richmond on the windshield.

### Chapter 27

### Chapter 28

The winter solstice came in with thirty-six hours of blue mountain freeze that dropped the temperature on the ridge to four below zero before the wind died.

The mountain went entirely still.

It was that deep, acoustic cold where you could hear the timber rafters in the house groan as the moisture in the pine shrank, and the sound of an axe chopping three miles away on the North Fork sounded like somebody tapping on the windowpane with an iron thimble.

"You got a woodstove that runs hot," Luke said. "A woman who bakes bread on a flat stone ought to have something to keep the tin from burning."

"Thank you, Luke," Clara said softly.

Luke cleared his throat. He pulled his work cap down over his eyes, grabbed a ninety-pound round of cherry wood in each arm, and walked toward the lean-to with that heavy, sure-footed mountain roll that had carried his father through fifty winters.

"Help me stack this wood, Clara," Luke shouted over his shoulder. "The sleet's coming out of the north by four o'clock, and I don't want to be hauling cherry logs in the dark."

Tess and Clara carried the smaller splits, their boots packing down the frost on the path, while Luke heaved the massive butt-cuts into the back of the shed, stacking them four feet high against the hemlock posts until the shed smelled like a lumber yard.

When the truck was empty, Luke banged the tailgate shut.

He stood by the front fender, wiping his brow with his mitten, his breath white in the noon sun.

Tess walked over, carrying a two-quart Mason jar of hot apple cider spiced with wild cinnamon and cloves that she had fetched from the kitchen.

She handed it to him.

Luke took the jar with both hands. He blew the steam away from the rim, took three long, scalding swallows, and closed his eyes as the warmth hit his chest.

"Sarah Mercer said you're doing the large-animal books next year," Luke said, opening his eyes to look at Tess.

"Sarah wants to do the medicine," Tess said. "She doesn't like sitting in the office with the state tax forms."

"You always were good with a ledger," Luke said.

He looked at the house—at the high peaked roof, at the green steel on the kennel shed, at the smoke pouring out of the chimney in a straight, blue line toward the gap.

"The place looks good, Tess," Luke said quietly.

"It looks like a home, Luke," Tess said.

Luke smiled—a small, clean, unburdened smile that reached all the way to the corners of his brown eyes.

"Yes," Luke said. "I reckon it does."

He handed the jar back to her, climbed into the cab of the green Dodge, and pulled the choke out half an inch.

The engine caught with a roar that sent three crows flapping out of the sugar maple. He eased the clutch out, backed around the lean-to, and turned down the mountain toward his cattle on Kettle Creek, his red taillights shining like two rubies through the winter trees until the gap swallowed the sound of the diesel.

### Chapter 29

The ice storm struck on the twelfth of January.

It was not a snow; it was that catastrophic mountain glaze that comes when a warm front from the Gulf of Mexico slides over a shallow pool of Arctic air trapped in the high valleys.

The rain fell at twenty-eight degrees, freezing the instant it touched a hemlock needle, a telephone wire, or a fence post.

By midnight, the trees on the ridges were encased in two inches of solid, green-tinted ice.

The sound began around two in the morning: a series of sharp, explosive cracks that sounded like twelve-gauge shotguns firing in the dark as forty-foot white pines and eighty-year-old chestnut oaks snapped under the weight of fifteen tons of ice, crashing through the underbrush with long, tearing roars that shook the ground.

By daylight, the county power grid had ceased to exist.

Nine hundred miles of high-voltage transmission lines lay twisted in the ditches between Marion and Damascus, their aluminum cables torn from the cross-arms by the weight of the ice.

The clinic’s telephone line went dead at five in the morning.

Tess and Clara woke to a house that was entirely silent except for the rhythmic *thump-crack* of falling timber on the mountain above them.

The windows were completely frosted over with a crystal pattern that was half an inch thick, turning the morning light into a weird, milky green glare that made the room look like the bottom of a frozen pond.

Tess was out of bed before Clara had opened her eyes.

She pulled her heavy wool bibs on over two layers of long underwear, shoved her feet into her insulated Pac boots, and walked to the kitchen.

The stove was still warm, the cherry coals holding their heat in the ash.

She stoked the fire, set the iron kettle on the lid, and walked to the front door.

When she threw the latch, the door wouldn't budge.

The ice had sealed the threshold with a three-inch bead of clear glaze that had locked the oak planks to the jamb like cement.

Tess picked up Luke’s heavy iron froe from the kindling box, wedged the steel blade into the seam, and struck the poll with the heel of her boot.

With a loud, splintering *crack* that sounded like glass breaking, the seal gave way.

The door swung open.

The world outside was terrifying and magnificent.

Every blade of fescue in the meadow was encased in an inch of clear ice, standing straight and rigid like a million needles of green glass. The hemlocks along the driveway were bent double, their tops frozen to the roadbed in high, shining arches that looked like an iron cathedral.

Above the gap, the sky was a deep, hard, brilliant cobalt blue—the clear, heartless blue of Arctic air—and the sun was rising over the Iron Mountain crest, turning the whole county into a blinding, flashing furnace of reflected light that burned the eyes.

From down in the gap came the sound of a chainsaw.

A Stihl 044, running wide open, its high-pitched two-stroke scream echoing off the limestone cliffs with a frantic, tearing intensity.

"Luke," Clara said, coming up behind Tess with her chore coat on.

"Luke and Caleb," Tess said, shading her eyes with her mitten. "They’ll be trying to clear the road to the home place before the old people freeze."

Tess turned back to the room.

"We have to get down to the clinic, Clara," Tess said, her voice dropping into that cold emergency register. "The backup generator on the surgery unit has four hours of diesel in the day tank. If the power stays off, the vaccine refrigerator will drop below freezing in six hours, and three thousand dollars' worth of rabies and parvo antigen will be ruined."

"The Subaru won't make the second switchback," Clara said, looking at the road through the frozen hemlock arches. "The blacktop is four inches of solid ice."

"We take the tractor," Tess said.

"What tractor?"

"Arthur's old Massey Ferguson 135," Tess said. "It's in the lower shed behind the pump house. It’s got diamond-pattern tire chains on the drive wheels that'll bite through two inches of pig iron."

Twenty minutes later, the red tractor roared to life with a cloud of blue diesel smoke that smelled of raw fuel and cold oil.

Tess sat in the steel spring seat, her face wrapped in a wool scarf until only her gray eyes showed, her hands in heavy leather mitts on the big steering wheel.

Clara stood on the drawbar behind her, her boots wedged between the axle housing and the fender, her arms wrapped around Tess’s waist, holding Copper inside the front of her yellow oilskin coat like a red parcel.

The three-legged dog had his nose tucked under her chin, his ears pinned back against the roar of the engine, his brown eyes watching the frozen trees slide by with calm, hound indifference.

The descent down the mountain was four miles of pure terror.

The tire chains bit into the ice with a crunching, grinding roar that shook their teeth, the tractor sliding three inches sideways on every steep hairpin before the iron links caught the frozen shale beneath the glaze.

Twice they had to stop.

Clara hopped down onto the ice with the bow-saw from the tractor’s tool box, cutting through three-inch oak branches that blocked the roadbed, dragging the heavy, ice-coated limbs into the ditch with her bare hands until her knuckles were bleeding from the sharp edges of the glaze.

At the second bridge over Kettle Creek, they found the county road crew.

Luke’s green flatbed was parked in the middle of the road, its front winch cable hooked around a forty-foot white pine that had fallen across the bridge deck.

Caleb was on the trunk with his Stihl saw, the chain throwing showers of white ice chips six feet into the air as he cut through the eighteen-inch butt.

Luke was standing on the bridge abutment, wearing his yellow hard hat and a high-visibility vest over his Carhartt, directing the winch operator with sharp, rhythmic chops of his hand.

When the red Massey Ferguson came clattering off the mountain, its chains screaming on the roadbed, Luke turned around.

He pushed his safety glasses up onto his forehead.

He looked at Tess on the tractor seat. He looked at Clara standing on the drawbar with her arms around Tess’s ribs. He looked at the red dog’s head poking out of the yellow coat.

He didn't say, *What are you doing on an open tractor at four below zero?*

He walked over to the fender, reached into his vest pocket, and pulled out an eight-ounce can of starting fluid and a dry rag.

"Sarah's at the clinic," Luke shouted over the idle of the tractor. "She walked three miles from her house on snowshoes. She got the small generator started, but the water line from the town main froze at the meter."

Tess pulled her scarf down. "Did the vaccine stay cold?"

"She put the antigen in two styrofoam coolers with snow," Luke said. "She’s got three dogs and an old woman from the trailer park in the treatment room by the woodstove."

He looked at Clara.

"You got that pry bar in your box?" Luke asked.

"Always," Clara said.

"The oak tree on the bridge deck is bound on the bottom stringer," Luke said. "I need somebody to wedge the tension off the cut while Caleb finishes the undercut so the saw don't pinch."

Clara didn't hesitate.

She handed Copper up to Tess, hopped down from the drawbar onto the ice, and grabbed her four-foot iron bar from the tractor’s three-point frame.

For twenty minutes, the four of them worked on the bridge deck in the blinding Arctic sunlight—Caleb with the screaming saw, Clara bearing her whole weight down on the iron pry bar to keep the kerf open, Luke hauling on the winch cable, and Tess holding the tractor’s throttle at twelve hundred RPM to run the tow chain.

With a massive, groaning crack that echoed off the frozen water below, the white pine trunk parted.

The winch groaned, the cable took the weight, and the forty-foot crown swung off the bridge deck, crashing into the swollen, ice-choked creek with a booming splash that sent chunks of green ice flying forty feet downstream.

The bridge was clear.

Luke stood in the middle of the road, his chest heaving, his face red with sweat that was already freezing into white frost on his mustache.

He looked at Clara, who was leaning on her pry bar with her hands on her hips, her yellow coat covered in white sawdust.

"The clinic's three miles, Tess," Luke shouted, waving his mitten toward the gap. "Go on through. I'll have the road plowed all the way to the hard road before noon."

Tess threw the tractor into fourth gear.

The chains bit the deck, the engine roared, and the red Massey Ferguson clattered across the bridge into the gap, carrying the two women and the three-legged hound toward the town where the smoke was rising from thirty chimneys against the blue, heartless winter sky.

### Chapter 30

The letter from Luke Avery’s lawyer in Marion arrived on the tenth of March.

It was not delivered by the mail carrier; Luke brought it himself on a Sunday afternoon when the mountain had begun to smell of wet dirt and thawing cedar needles.

The ice storm had been gone for three weeks, leaving the ridges bristling with shattered treetops that looked like someone had run a giant bush-hog through the high timber, but the road was dry, the ruts packed down with three loads of crushed river limestone that Luke had hauled from the quarry.

"It's a deed of gift," Luke said.

"Why, Luke?"

Luke looked at the three slabs of limestone between his boots.

He reached down, his calloused fingers touching the cold gray stone where the wild mountain mint was already pushing its first green, square-stemmed shoots through the shale.

"Because the dog is buried here, Tess," Luke said softly.

Copper trotted beside him all the way to the gate, his one upright ear cocked, his red tail swinging, until Luke climbed into the cab, pulled the door shut with that clean, solid click, and drove down the mountain into the spring haze.

### Chapter 31

The clerk’s office in Marion on Tuesday afternoon was very quiet.

Miss Verna Peake had put a pot of wild mountain tea on the kerosene stove behind the counter, and the smell of sweet sassafras and dried peppermint filled the basement room with a warm, spicy fragrance that cut through the old paper and damp limestone.

The deed lay between them on a sheet of stiff, blue-backed legal stock: *DEED OF CONVEYANCE AND PARTITION - COMMONWEALTH OF VIRGINIA - SMYTH COUNTY.*

Miss Verna Peake set her brass embossing die on the table with a heavy, metallic *clunk*.

She pushed her magnifying visor up into her blue curls, reached into her cardigan pocket, and laid a heavy black fountain pen beside the paper.

"Sign on the line with your full Christian names," Miss Verna said. "Use the black ink. The state scanner don't pick up blue ballpoint when they microfilm the records for Richmond."

Tess picked up the pen.

The barrel was heavy, cold brass covered in black lacquer, its gold nib shining in the lamplight.

She looked at Clara.

Clara was wearing her clean white linen shirt, her red suspenders, and the silver ring of hemlock needles on her middle finger. Her dark hair was combed back, her gray eyes wide and clear as the pool below the milldam on Kettle Creek.

Clara smiled—that slow, steady, quiet smile that had carried them through fifteen months of milligrams, ice storms, and court deeds.

She laid her hand over Tess’s left knuckles.

"Sign it, Tess," Clara whispered.

The black ink flowed smoothly from the gold point, drawing the letters in a clear, slanting hand: *Tess Margaret Avery.*

She passed the pen to Clara.

Clara took it with two fingers, held her breath for three seconds, and wrote beneath Tess’s name: *Clara Madeline Lind.*

Miss Verna Peake took the paper.

She picked up the heavy iron die, slid the bottom corner of the blue-backed vellum between the steel jaws, and brought her weight down on the lever.

### Chapter 32

The timbers for the north studio were cut from four white oaks that had blown down on the Gidley lease during the January ice storm.

### Chapter 34

### Chapter 35

Donnie had grown three inches in six months. His shoulders had broadened from hauling ninety-pound white-oak timbers, and he carried his grandfather’s four-foot folding rule in the pocket of his overalls with the same unconscious ease that Clara carried her technical pens.

He stood beside Clara in the shade of an 1830s cantilever barn, holding a twenty-foot telescoping rod against the corner post.

"Look at the corner notch, Donnie," Clara called out, pointing with her pencil. "What kind of notch is that?"

Donnie squinted through the dust at the junction of the twenty-inch chestnut logs.

"Half-dovetail," Donnie said, his voice slow, sure, carrying that quiet mountain timber certainty. "The top cheek is sloped at a six-to-one pitch to turn the rain out of the joint, but the bottom cheek is dead flat so the timber don't creep when the snow piles on the roof."

Clara looked at him, her gray eyes shining with pride.

"And how old is the log?"

Donnie stepped up to the post. He ran his thumb across the broadaxe marks—the rhythmic, diagonal scallops that an old farmer had cut into the green chestnut before the Mexican War.

"Eighteen-forty," Donnie said. "You can tell by the scoring marks. He was using a six-pound broadaxe with a curved hickory helve, and he swung it left-handed."

Clara wrote the figures into her black notebook: *McAllister Barn - Cantilever, 1840. Hand-hewn chestnut. Metes and bounds verified w/ D. Cleary, apprentice.*

When the cattle were finished, old man McAllister brought out a wooden basket of yellow transparent apples and a gallon jug of buttermilk from the spring house.

They sat together on the tail-gate of the green trailer—Sarah with her dairy boots covered in lime, Tess wiping her stethoscope with an alcohol pad, Clara sketching the roofline on tracing paper, and Donnie Cleary sharing half an apple with Copper in the clover.

The sun was sitting high and golden over the crest of Mount Rogers, casting forty miles of blue mountain ridges into a haze that looked like lavender wool.

Sarah took a swallow of the cold buttermilk, wiped her mouth with her sleeve, and looked at Tess.

"Arthur’s coming out to the clinic tomorrow, Tess," Sarah said.

Tess smiled. "Is he bringing his cane?"

"He threw the cane in the ditch behind the church on Sunday," Sarah said, laughing. "Told the preacher that a man with seventy years of mountain dirt in his blood didn't need three legs to tell whether a heifer was in heat."

Copper lifted his red head from Donnie’s knee, looked at Sarah with his one upright ear cocked, and gave a single, indignant snort that made the whole tail-gate break into laughter that echoed off the chestnut logs into the quiet afternoon.

### Chapter 38

The raising of the clinic’s new hay shed took place on the first Saturday of August.

It was Donnie Cleary’s first independent bent.

He had worked for six weeks in Luke’s shop, cutting the mortise-and-tenon joints in eight-by-eight white-oak timbers with a three-pound wooden beetle and an old two-inch framing chisel that had belonged to his own grandfather.

Every joint had been laid out with a five-millimeter pencil and checked with Clara’s brass square; every trunnel hole had been bored with an iron spoon-bit that Luke had turned on his forge until the iron was true to within a thirty-second of an inch.

By eight in the morning, thirty people had gathered in the gravel lot behind the clinic.

Clyde Henderson had brought his tractor to pull the gin-pole; Arthur Mercer sat on an overturned mineral tub with a thermos of black coffee, watching the boy’s hands with those sharp, ancient eyes; and Donnie’s mother, Mrs. Cleary, was standing by the clinic porch in her Sunday cotton dress, clutching her handbag with both hands, her eyes bright with tears she was trying to blink away.

Luke Avery was standing by the gin-pole cable.

He didn't touch the timber. He stood with his hands tucked into the back pockets of his work trousers, his Carhartt cap pulled low, his brown face completely still.

"It's your bent, Donnie," Luke said quietly. "Call the lift."

Donnie stepped to the center of the bottom plate.

His voice was clear, loud, carrying that unshakeable mountain command that had built every ridge settlement since the wilderness road:

"Pike poles to the center post! Take the strain on the winch! Lift together!"

The four men on the poles—Caleb, Clyde Henderson, Sarah Mercer, and Tess—heaved together.

The six-hundred-pound frame of white oak rose into the morning sunlight, the gin-pole cable singing with a high, iron hum as the tenons hovered over the mortise pockets in the locust sills.

"Hold!" Donnie shouted.

He stepped under the swinging timber, his movements sure, calm, without a single wasted motion.

He set his shoulder against the post, sighted down the plumb-line that hung from the top rafter, and guided the three tenons into their mortises with a rhythmic, wooden *thump-thump-thump* that seated the frame dead true on the stone piers.

"Plumb," Donnie said.

He picked up the wooden beetle from the grass.

He took the two-inch locust peg from his pocket, set the point into the master trunnel hole, and swung the beetle with both arms.

The sound was clean, dry, ringing like an iron anvil through the valley.

Three strokes.

The master peg seated flush.

The frame was home.

The crowd in the lot broke into a cheer that brought two farm collies out from under the porch barking with excitement.

Clyde Henderson slapped Donnie on the shoulder until the boy’s cap fell into the clover; Mrs. Cleary was weeping openly into her handkerchief; and Arthur Mercer stood up from his mineral tub, walked across the gravel without his cane, and laid his heavy, scarred palm over the boy’s knuckles where they held the beetle.

"That's a square joint, Donnie," Arthur said gruffly. "Your grandfather would have been proud to put his broadaxe on that timber."

Luke walked over.

He didn't make a speech. He reached into his pocket, pulled out his grandfather’s six-inch brass drawing compass—the one in the worn calfskin case—and laid it in Donnie’s hand.

"A man who can cut a bent like that," Luke said, his voice low, steady, carrying the whole weight of fifty years of mountain work, "has got a right to carry his own brass."

Donnie looked down at the old compass.

His throat worked once, his jaw tightening as he nodded his head, unable to speak, his fingers closing around the dark, polished metal.

Tess and Clara stood together by the clinic door.

Clara had her arm hooked through Tess’s, her head resting against Tess’s shoulder, watching Luke and the boy standing by the fresh white-oak frame.

"He's a good man, Tess," Clara whispered.

Tess looked at Luke’s broad back, at the gray thread that had begun to show in his hair above the ears, at the easy, unburdened way he stood in the morning sun.

"He is," Tess said softly. "It just took him a long time to find out what he was building."

### Chapter 39

The summer rain came down on Kettle Creek on a Tuesday evening in late August.

It was not a storm; it was that warm, fragrant mountain rain that falls without wind, drumming softly on the sycamore leaves and turning the limestone cobbles in the creek bed to the color of wet jade.

Tess and Clara walked down from the house at six o'clock, their rain slickers unbuttoned, their boots splashing through the shallow puddles in the lane.

Copper trotted ahead of them, his three legs bounding through the tall fescue, his red coat glistening with raindrops, stopping every thirty yards to sniff at a wild mint stalk or chase a leopard frog into the ditch.

They met Luke at the Old Iron Bridge.

The green Dodge flatbed was parked on the far apron, its yellow parking lights glowing like lanterns through the warm, drifting mist.

Luke was leaning his forearms on the weathered oak railing of the bridge, watching the clear water slide over the limestone ledge below. He had his Carhartt coat on, his cap pushed back, a tin cup of black coffee in his hand.

He turned his head as their boots crunched onto the bridge timbers.

"Creek's running six inches above the ford," Luke said, his voice blending with the rush of the water.

Tess leaned against the railing beside him.

She looked down at the river bottom.

The Archibald Gidley mill stood three hundred yards above the bridge, its stone walls dark and majestic in the rain, the water rushing through the cleared race in a clean, powerful cascade that threw white foam against the roots of the great sycamore.

Across the pasture, Luke’s black steers were grazing peacefully in the sweet grass, their hides shining like black glass under the evening sky.

"Sarah said the state conservation certificate came on Monday," Tess said.

Luke smiled—that slow, quiet, unasking smile that belonged to the river.

"Miss Verna told me at the store," Luke said. "Said the deed is recorded as a perpetual municipal preserve. Said nobody can touch that water now until the mountain falls down."

He looked at Clara, who was standing on the other side of Tess, her hands in the pockets of her yellow oilskin, her dark hair curling in the damp air.

"You draw good maps, Clara," Luke said softly.

Clara looked at him, her gray eyes wide and clear as the pool below the dam.

"You built good corners, Luke," Clara said.

For a long time, the three of them stood together on the bridge deck in the quiet rain.

There was no awkwardness between them; there was no ghost of the court papers, no shadow of the six months of the shared calendar, no memory of the angry words that had lived in the room on the ridge two years ago.

The bridge was just an old iron bridge; the creek was just water running over mountain stone; and the three of them were just neighbors who had survived the winter and lived to see the clover come back.

Copper trotted up between their boots.

He pushed his wet red nose into Luke’s hand, then into Clara’s palm, and finally rested his chin across the toes of Tess’s boot with a deep, contented sigh that seemed to take the whole evening into his chest.

Luke reached down, scratched the dog behind his lone upright ear, and finished his coffee.

"The corn down on the bottom is eight feet high," Luke said, looking toward his small house. "I'm going to start chopping silage on Thursday if the ground dries off."

He tossed the dregs of the coffee into the creek and stepped back from the rail.

"You two want some fresh sweet corn for your supper?" Luke asked. "I got two dozen ears in the back of the truck that were pulled two hours ago."

"We'd like that, Luke," Tess said.

Luke walked to his truck, reached into the bed, and brought back a brown burlap sack that was cool and damp to the touch, smelling of corn silk, sweet sugar, and mountain earth.

He handed it to Clara.

"Tell Donnie I'll pick him up at six on Thursday," Luke said, climbing into the cab of the green flatbed. "We got sixty tons of silage to pack before the rain comes back on the weekend."

"I'll tell him, Luke," Clara said.

The big diesel engine caught with that familiar, hollow rumble.

Luke backed the truck around the bridge apron, raised his hand once in a clean, quiet mountain wave, and drove down the river lane into the mist, his red taillights glowing through the hemlocks until the sound of the engine was lost in the steady roar of the water over the stones.

### Chapter 40

The third October on the ridge was the season of the long light.

The mountain had turned into a tapestry of scarlet, gold, and deep, burnt bronze that stretched forty miles across the gaps under an autumn sky that was so blue, so vast, that it made the heart ache with its clarity.

It was four-thirty on a Thursday afternoon.

The clinic had closed early.

Sarah Mercer had taken the flatbed to a herd health check in Whitetop; Arthur was sitting on the bench in front of the post office, trading pocketknives with Clyde Henderson in the warm sun.

Tess and Clara walked up the hill from the house.

The new studio was quiet, its drafting table holding thirty sheets of the finished survey of the Smyth County historic chapels, the locust pegs shining like amber in the north light.

Copper ran ahead of them through the orchard.

He was eighteen months old, fast and sure on his three legs, his red coat glowing like a fox’s pelt in the golden grass, chasing a late yellow swallowtail butterfly that was fluttering among the fallen Virginia Beauty apples.

They reached the high shelf of the meadow.

The three slabs of limestone were warm, their gray surfaces covered in a dense, purple mat of wild mountain mint that filled the autumn air with that sharp, clean, peppery scent that tasted of stone and river water.

Tess sat down on the middle stone.

Clara sat beside her, her boots resting in the wild mint, her hands in her lap, looking out over the blue notch of Dutch Gap.

The town below was quiet.

The mercury light at the clinic had not yet turned on; the chimney smoke from forty houses was rising into the pale sky in thin, straight lines of blue thread; and down on Kettle Creek, three miles away, Luke’s black steers were moving slowly toward the water trough in the long shadows of the sycamores.

Copper reached the mound.

He did not sniff at the grass; he trotted straight to the three stones, circled once, and curled himself down between their boots, his red muzzle resting across Tess’s knee, his tail making two slow, contented thumps against the limestone before he went to sleep.

Tess laid her hand on the dog’s neck, feeling the steady, unasking beat of his heart.

She reached into her pocket.

Her fingers found the old brass dog whistle—the one that had lived on her key ring through three years of seasons.

She took it out.

The brass was worn smooth, darkened by forty years of fingers, its split ring cold against her palm.

She held it up to the late afternoon sun.

The golden light caught the tarnished metal, illuminating the tiny scratches where Roscoe’s teeth had touched it six years ago when he was a puppy chasing field mice in the tenant house on the creek.

Clara turned her head.

She laid her hand over Tess’s knuckles, her silver ring of hemlock needles gleaming against Tess’s skin.

"Do you remember the binder, Tess?" Clara whispered.

Tess smiled—a slow, quiet, peaceful smile that reached all the way to the bottom of her heart.

"I remember," Tess said.

"Six in the morning," Clara murmured, her voice soft as the wind in the hemlocks. "Eighteen breaths a minute. Yunnan Baiyao with the cheese. The five-thirty handoff at the bridge."

"The shared calendar," Tess said.

"It was the only thing we had," Clara said, her eyes wide, deep, clear as the water running over the milldam.

Tess slipped the brass whistle back into her pocket.

She turned to Clara, her hand coming up to cup the side of Clara’s jaw, her thumb brushing the small, pale scar near Clara’s hairline.

"No, Clara," Tess said softly. "It wasn't the only thing we had. It was just the thing that brought us to the edge of the woods so we could see what the mountain looked like."

Clara leaned in, her forehead resting against Tess’s temple, their breath mingling in the cool, sweet autumn air that was coming down off the high balds.

Below them, the gap began to fill with the evening shadows, the ridges turning from scarlet to deep, velvety purple as the sun slipped down behind the crest of the Iron Mountain.

From the woods behind the pump house came the clear, ringing call of a wood thrush—three clean, descending notes that hung in the twilight like brass bells.

The grief was there—it would always be there, resting six feet deep beneath the fescue, holding the three stones in its ancient, silent keeping—but it was no longer a wound.

It had become the foundation.

It was the mortar in the stone piers; it was the locust trunnel that pinned the bent; it was the water that turned the wheel in the millrace; and it was the forgiveness that let two women and an honest man look each other in the eyes without flinching under the open sky.

Tess wrapped her arm around Clara’s shoulders, holding her close against the mountain chill.

"We have forty years, Clara," Tess whispered into the dusk.

Clara tightened her fingers in Tess’s palm, her face turning toward the first white stars that were beginning to shine over the high ridges.

"Forty years, Tess," Clara said.

And there on the high mountain shelf, with the three limestone slabs at their feet and forty miles of Appalachians turning dark and vast beneath the canopy of heaven, the two women and the three-legged hound sat together in the quiet evening, at home in the good months that would never have to end.

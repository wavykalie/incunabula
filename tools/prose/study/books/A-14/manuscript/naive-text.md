# The Sound of Other People’s Doors

### Chapter 1

The building had nine doors onto the central well, and Arthur Cleary had lived behind the fourth of them for forty-one years, three months, and nine days. 

He did not need an alarm to tell him it was five in the morning. His right ankle, which had spent four decades depressing the stiff clutch-pedals of Leyland Nationals and Bristol VRs against the steep gradients of the High Battery, always delivered a dull, throbbing knot of heat to his Achilles tendon five minutes before the tide turned in the outer basin. If the wind was out of the west, off the salt-marsh and the chemical flats, the bedroom sash rattled with a dry, papery chatter; if it blew from the north, straight down the sea-reach from the skerries, the draft got under the heavy serge curtain and made the hem drag across the floorboards with the sound of a broom sweeping salt.

Then came the first door.

Ground floor, rear: Flat 1B. 

A sharp, two-stage brass snap—first the Yale cylinder giving under a key that had been filed down from an ill-cut blank, then the hard, impatient shoulder-shove against the warped oak rail where the wet from forty winters of river-fog had swollen the bottom corner. A pause of five seconds while Marek leaned back against the timber to slip his steel-capped boots into the heel-recess, the rubber soles squeaking against the communal quarry tiles; then the soft, muffled thud of the door being pulled shut with two fingers on the knob to spare the baby in the cot on the other side of the lath-and-plaster partition. 

Marek worked the six-o'clock gantry at the bulk terminal three miles down the spit. He was thirty-two, had wrists like hawser-thimbles, and never left without smelling of fried sunflower oil and yellow tobacco. He had lived in 1B for four years, and in all that time Arthur had spoken sixteen words to him: *Morning, lad; cold on the slip; watch that third step, the nosing’s come adrift.*

Arthur sat up on the edge of the mattress. The horsehair was grooved to the shape of his left hip, an indentation thirty inches long and six inches deep that had remained unchanged since 1996, the year his wife, Sarah, had put her suitcase on the late train to Preston and failed to get off it at the other end. He reached down into the dark, found his woolen socks by their darned heels, and pulled them over feet that had grown broad and flat from forty years of idling at red signals on the Quay Road.

The second door followed at twenty past five.

First floor, opposite his own: Flat 2B.

Tara. She was twenty-four, worked the cleaning shift at the ferry terminal cafeteria until two in the morning, slept three hours on a fold-out settee, and then took the quarter-to-six bus to the cold-store packaging sheds at the fish dock. Her door was modern—a cheap, hollow-core ply slab that the previous landlord had hung after the fire of 1998, using zinc butt-hinges that had never seen an oil-can. It did not snap; it screamed. It began with an agonizing, dry iron squeal that climbed a minor third before sticking on the head-casing, followed by the soft, wet slap of her palm against the face of the wood as she eased it into the frame with the infinite, terrified care of a mother who knew that if the spring-latch dropped with a bang, her four-year-old boy, Dylan, would be out of his bed and screaming before she had cleared the second landing.

Arthur stood, slipped his arms into his gray cardigan—the one with the leather buttons worn down to smooth horn at the knuckles—and walked to the kettle. 

The flat was thirty-eight paces from the inside of the hall door to the window that looked over the graving docks. A narrow passage, two strides wide, lined with books he had bought for fourpence each from the mission carts on Saturdays when the sailors were paid off; a square scullery with a chipped stone sink that sloped three-eighths of an inch toward the harbor wall; a sitting room where the ceiling rose twelve feet to an acanthus-leaf rose that had dropped half its plaster during the blitz of forty-one; and the bedroom, which smelled of cold linoleum, lavender soap, and the oilskin coat he had hung behind the door during the great gale of January 1987 and never quite found a reason to move.

He did not switch on the electric light. Electricity was twenty-eight pence a unit on the prepay key, and Arthur’s municipal pension was ninety-two pounds and six pence a week, supplemented by forty-four pounds of state basic that arrived on Tuesdays like an apology through the post-slot. He struck a match—one of the long red-headed ones from the shipyard supply store—and touched it to the ring of the gas ring.

The blue crown flared with a soft, hollow *pluff*.

Through the floorboards, through the twelve inches of dry timber, dead lime-mortar, and horsehair insulation that separated his scullery from the hallway, the third sound arrived.

Ground floor, front: Flat 1A.

Mrs. Corley. Eighty-six years old, daughter of an engine-fitter from the old iron-works, widow of a tug-master who had gone down with the *Barrow Lass* off the lightship in fifty-three. Her door was the oldest in the tenement: four inches of solid pitch pine with an iron rim-lock as large as a family Bible. When Mrs. Corley turned the key, it did not click; it ground. It was the sound of a shovel driven into coarse river gravel, a slow, five-second rotation that ended in a heavy, metallic *clonk* that shook the gas-pipes in Arthur’s cupboard. She only opened it six inches at this hour, just far enough to retrieve the pint of silver-top milk left on the stone ledge by the boy from the creamery, before the deadbolt went back into its keeper with the finality of a prison gate at sundown.

Arthur filled his enameled pot from the cold tap. The water had the sharp, iron taste of the reservoir up on the fells, carried fourteen miles through cast-iron mains that had been laid when Victoria was still wearing black for Albert. He set the pot on the blue flame and leaned his forehead against the window-pane.

The morning was blue-black, salted with a fine drizzle that had no weight to it, the sort of rain that did not fall so much as hang in the air until your eyebrows were gray with it. 

Down in the basin, where the timber-wharves had rotted into green slime thirty years ago, the lights of the redevelopment were already burning. 

They were not the orange sodium lights of his youth, the ones that had turned the wet slate roofs to the color of tinned peaches and given the diesel smoke an amber belly as it rose from the bus exhausts. These were white—a harsh, cold, daylight-balanced LED glare that turned the mud of the low-water channel into something dead and surgical. 

Three hundred yards out into the basin, where the old coal-staithes had once dropped four thousand tons a day into the holds of Dutch coasters, the first tower was already sixteen storeys high. The developers called it *The Spinnaker Reach*. On the large vinyl billboard tied to the chain-link fence along the dock road, a computer-rendered woman with hair the color of corn-silk stood on an eleventh-floor balcony holding a glass of white wine, looking out over an estuary that contained no mud, no bladder-wrack, no rusted anchor-chains, and no gulls picking through the plastic wrappers behind the fish-sheds.

*A New Horizon for the Maritime Quarter,* the sign said. *One, Two, and Three Bedroom Luxury Waterfront Residences. 85% Sold off Plan.*

Below the tower, huddled against the granite sea-wall like sheep behind a dyke, sat the four-storey brick block of St. Jude’s Terrace. 

It had been built in 1882 by the dock company to house twelve families of lock-keepers, line-handlers, and boiler-makers. Its bricks were not red anymore; they were the color of cold tea, crusted with ninety years of soot from the locomotives that had shunted coal along the quayside until the rails were torn up for scrap in seventy-two. It had no lift, no communal laundry, and no entryphone. 

If someone wanted you at St. Jude’s Terrace, they stood on the greasy cobbles of the lane and whistled up the well, or they came up the seventy-two stone steps, their boots ringing on the bull-nosed treads, until they found your door and knocked with their knuckles until the lath shook.

Arthur took his mug to the table. 

On the oilcloth, between the tin of salt and the brown earthenware sugar jar, lay a white envelope. It was not a bill; bills came with cellophane windows that showed his name misspelled as *Cleary, A. J.* in faint purple dot-matrix. 

This envelope was heavy, nine inches by four, made of cream paper that felt like the collar of a Sunday shirt. It had no stamp. It had been slipped under his door between four and five o’clock on the previous afternoon, while he had been down at the library reading the maritime shipping lists from 1964 to see if the *Crested Eagle* had ever actually called at Fleetwood or if he had imagined the gold funnel in the fog when he was nine.

The name across the front was written in blue ballpoint, in the neat, slanted hand of a young man who had been taught to form his letters with a plastic ruler:

*Mr. Arthur Cleary*  
*Flat 4, St. Jude’s Terrace*  
*Private & Confidential*

Arthur did not pick it up. He did not need to. He had already read the three paragraphs inside four times by the light of the gas-ring before going to bed.

The company was called *Vanguard Estuary Properties (No. 4) Limited*, with an address in a square behind the law courts in Leeds. The letter was signed by a man named Julian Croft, whose title below his signature was *Director of Special Acquisitions*. 

Mr. Croft had expressed his profound respect for Mr. Cleary’s long tenure in the district. Mr. Croft understood that Mr. Cleary had been a valued servant of the municipal transport undertaking for more than four decades, an anchor to the local community whose stability was an asset to the borough. Mr. Croft was keenly aware that St. Jude’s Terrace, while possessing "undeniable historic character," had reached the end of its structural lifecycle, its foundations having suffered from eighty years of salt-ingress and the vibration of the heavy container trucks using the new perimeter ring-road.

And then, on the second page, in the short, flat sentences that lawyers write when they want to make an execution sound like an act of municipal hygiene:

*As you are aware, the head-lease of St. Jude’s Terrace was acquired by our parent entity on the fourteenth of August. While the short-hold tenancies occupying the remaining seven units will terminate in accordance with statutory notices over the coming quarter, we are mindful of your exceptional status as an Assured Tenant under the 1977 Rent Act.*

*We do not wish, Mr. Cleary, to enter into an adversarial process that would cause you distress or deplete the resources of your retirement.*

*We have therefore reserved for your exclusive personal occupancy Apartment 304 in Phase One of The Spinnaker Reach. This unit (two bedrooms, south-west aspect, underfloor heating, secure allocated parking, and twenty-four-hour concierge service) will be transferred to you on a peppercorn lease for the term of your natural life, with all service charges and management levies discharged in perpetuity by this company.*

*In return, we require only your agreement to surrender your current tenancy on or before the thirty-first of October, and your undertaking to treat the terms of this transition as strictly confidential between yourself and our representatives.*

*Mr. Croft will call upon you at ten o’clock on Thursday morning to present the documentation and answer any questions you may have.*

Thursday was today.

Arthur picked up his tea. It was too hot to drink, but he held the earthenware between his palms until the heat pierced the thickened skin of his thumbs.

From the landing above him—the third floor, Flat 3A—came the fifth door.

Syd Corcoran. 

Syd had been a rigger on the floating crane *Mammoth* until his lungs had filled with the dust from the asbestos boiler-lagging they had stripped out of the old naval frigates in the dry docks back in the seventies. Syd’s door was an old four-panel deal that had shrunk two inches away from the frame at the top, so that it hung by its upper hinge like a broken wing. 

When Syd came out, there was no key. Syd never locked his door because Syd had nothing inside his room except a camp-bed, a paraffin heater that smoked like an oil-well, and twenty-four cardboard boxes of scale-model plastic warships he had never found the breath to glue together. 

The door opened with a soft, dry *clack* of the rim-latch, followed immediately by the three-beat cough—*huck, huck, hrr-ack*—that Arthur had heard every twenty minutes for fourteen years. Then the slow, dragging sound of Syd’s slippers on the cold stone of the stairs as he went down to the half-landing to spit into the zinc bucket that the council hadn't emptied since the previous Tuesday.

Arthur looked out the window again.

The light was changing. The high, gray clouds were turning the color of wet tin, and down on the pier the first gulls were dropping onto the slimy stones of the low-water slip. 

If he went out to the landing now, he would smell Syd’s paraffin, Mrs. Corley’s boiled milk, and the damp wool of Tara’s coat where she had brushed it against the wallpaper by the water-meter. 

If he stayed where he was until ten o'clock, a young man in a clean gray suit would come up the stairs, smelling of aftershave and laundry-steam, and offer him the ninth floor of the tower where the windows did not open and the doors were shut by hydraulic arms that made no sound at all.

### Chapter 2

The No. 7 bus had been Arthur’s route for twenty-two of his forty years behind the wheel. 

It started at the swing-bridge, ran three miles along the granite revetment of the West Quay, climbed the three-in-one gradient of the Salt-Gate where the horses used to founder in the frost, circled the red-brick barracks of the Territorial Army, and then dropped down through the railway arches into the marsh-villages where the chemical workers lived in rows of four-room cottages with slate roofs that turned purple when it rained.

A driver did not look at people’s faces. If you looked at their faces, you began to carry them home with you, and forty years was too long to carry two hundred people a day through the narrow streets of a three-speed gearbox. 

You looked at their knees. You looked at the way they stepped onto the ribbed aluminum platform: the heavy, rolling lurch of the dry-dock men whose hips had been ruined by thirty years of carrying three-inch staging planks across wet iron; the quick, tapping hop of the girls from the telegraph office who wore thin patent shoes that soaked through before they reached the shelter; the slow, trembling shuffle of the widows whose string bags smelled of cold tripe and damp newspapers.

You knew them by their shoes, by the weight of their five-penny pieces dropped into the brass tray of the ticket-machine, and by the sound their knuckles made against the glass partition behind your ear when they wanted to be let down between the official stops because their chests were bad and the hill was steep.

Arthur had been a good driver. He had never lost a reverse-gear in the snow on the Old Battery; he had never taken the paint off a coal-cart on the bridge; and he had only once, in the winter of sixty-nine, clipped the stone coping of the Customs House when the black ice came off the gut before the sand-drays were out.

"A driver," his father had told him in fifty-eight, when Arthur had first put on the blue serge uniform with the white plastic piping on the lapels, "is just a man who moves the town from where it can't bear to be to where it has to go. You aren't part of the town, Artie. You're the belt that turns the wheels. Keep your grease-gun in your pocket and don't argue with the inspectors."

Arthur had kept his grease-gun in his pocket. 

When the corporation buses were sold off in eighty-six to a consortium from Aberdeen that painted them the color of a cheap mustard-pot and cut the garage staff by two-thirds, Arthur had not joined the three-week strike. He had walked into the depot at five in the morning, clocked his card with the brass mechanical lever, checked his oil with a rag from his own scullery, and driven the six-fifteen down to the pier through a line of men who had been his schoolfellows at the Wesleyan academy. 

They had not thrown stones at him. They had known him too long for stones. 

They had just stood in the drizzle with their hands in the pockets of their donkey-jackets, their shoulders hunched against the dock wind, and watched him turn the big, black-enameled wheel into the lane. One of them—Billy Nancarrow, who had lived on the top floor of St. Jude’s Terrace before his legs went from the gangrene—had spat on the front nearside tire as Arthur crawled past in first gear. 

The spit had stayed on the gray rubber until Arthur hit the puddles at the Salt-Gate, where the marsh-water had washed it away without leaving a grease-mark.

Now, at half-past eight, Arthur put on his flat cap—the black woolen one with the leather band inside that had turned yellow from sixty years of salt-grease—and walked down the stairs.

The stairwell of St. Jude’s Terrace was an open cylinder of stone, five feet wide, that rose through the core of the building like an empty gun-barrel. The steps were not straight; they were winders, narrow at the spindle and broad at the wall, hollowed out in the center by sixty thousand journeys of iron-shod boots until each tread held a shallow pool of gray, greasy dust that never quite dried.

On the second landing, between his floor and the one above, he stopped.

Flat 3B. 

The door was painted the color of dried liver—an old lead-based enamel that had blistered into thousands of tiny, hexagonal scales near the bottom where the cat had scratched it in the sixties. 

This was the Beale door.

Behind it lived George Beale and his sister, Janice. George was seventy-one, three years older than Arthur, and had spent his life in the mold-loft of the Vickers yard, laying out the curves of cruiser hulls in white chalk on fifty yards of black-painted pine floor. He had not laid out a curve since eighty-two, when the yard stopped building hulls and began assembling prefabricated sections of nuclear submarines that arrived by barge from the Clyde. 

Now George had the softening. 

He sat in an armchair by the coal-grate with a piece of chalk in his thumb, drawing circles on the knees of his corduroy trousers until the cloth was gray-white, while Janice—who was sixty-four and had bad veins that made her ankles look like two sacks of turnips—boiled cabbage and scrubbed sheets in a zinc dolly-tub because they couldn't afford the seven pounds it cost at the laundrette behind the chapel.

Their door did not have a lock like Arthur’s or a bolt like Mrs. Corley’s. 

It had a wooden latch-string that hung through a gimlet-hole above the center rail, tied to an old iron horseshoe that George had picked up off the foreshore when he was seven years old. When the wind blew down the stairwell, the horseshoe tapped against the liver-colored paint: *tink... tink... tink*. 

It was the loneliest sound in the building. It sounded like an old watch that had lost its escapement and was running down three seconds at a time.

As Arthur stood on the landing, the latch lifted.

It did not rise smoothly; it came up in two jerky hops, as if the fingers on the other side were swollen at the joints. The door swung back six inches on its iron strap-hinges, which had been painted over so many times they looked like sausages of gray lead.

Janice Beale looked out. 

She was wearing a quilted nylon housecoat over two woolen cardigans, and her hair was pinned back with three aluminum clips that had lost their teeth. Her face was the color of a scrubbed floor-cloth, with that faint, yellow shine around the eye-sockets that comes to women who spend fourteen hours a day inside four walls with an old man who has forgotten his own Christian name.

"Arthur," she said. Her voice was flat, thin, carrying the dry, iron rattle of the lower parish. "You seen the boy from the gas?"

"No," Arthur said. He kept his hands behind his back, fingers hooked into his cardigan pockets. "Not since Tuesday."

"He was supposed to come for the meter," she said, looking down the dark drop of the stairs. "We’re four pound twelve in the red on the rebate. If they turn the screw on the street-cock, George won't keep his hands still. He has to have the fire, Arthur. If the room goes cold, he starts looking for his lines. He thinks the ship’s coming off the blocks at two o’clock."

"They won't turn it," Arthur said, though he knew perfectly well that the North Western Gas Board had already replaced three of the street-valves with modern electronic limiters that shut off automatically when the credit expired at midnight. "They haven't got the men for the walk-ups anymore. They don't like the stairs."

Janice looked at him for a second. Her eyes were small, watery, surrounded by red rings where the smoke from the wet slack had gotten into them. 

"You got one of them letters, Arthur?"

The question fell into the quiet between them like a lead sinker into forty feet of slack water.

Arthur did not move his feet. His boots were planted on the second tread down, where the stone had been worn away until his toes were an inch lower than his heels.

"What letter’s that, then?" he asked.

"The white ones," she said. She reached into the pocket of her housecoat and pulled out a scrap of paper that had been folded into four until the corners were soft and fuzzy. "A lad with a clean neck brought it yesterday dinner. Slipped it under the draft-felt. Says the whole row’s coming down before the frost."

Arthur looked at the paper. He did not take it from her hand. He could see the purple printing of the letterhead: *Vanguard Estuary Properties (No. 4) Limited*.

"What does it say?" he asked softly.

"Says we have to be out by the twenty-eighth of next month," Janice said. Her voice didn't rise; it had that terrible, quiet flatness of people who had been told their whole lives that they were in the way of something larger and more expensive than themselves. "Says they’ll give us eight hundred pound for the disturbance if we leave the keys on the mantelshelf and don't take the brass off the windows. Eight hundred pound, Arthur. You couldn't get a room in a cellar behind the pig-market for eight hundred pound. Not with George the way he is. They won't take him if he draws on the walls. I asked at the home on the Crescent in July, and the woman said four hundred a week, payable in advance by the family."

She stopped. Down in the courtyard, a diesel truck from the development site was backing up, its warning beeper sounding through the fog: *beep... beep... beep...* like an electric bird that couldn't find its nest.

"They can't just put you out," Arthur said. The lie felt thick and greasy in his mouth, like lard that had been kept in a tin too close to the paraffin stove. "There’s the council. There’s the housing officer down at the Town Hall."

"The housing officer went to Preston two years ago," Janice said. "There’s only that girl with the ring in her nose who sits behind the plastic screen and gives you them forms with the pink tops. I filled four of 'em in when the roof leaked over the scullery in March. She said they hadn't got no three-room flats on the ground floor. Said everyone’s waiting. Said there’s four hundred families from the flats on the marsh that are living in them tin caravans behind the railway."

She looked past him, down the stairs toward the street door where the gray light was creeping up the first four steps.

"What did yours say, Arthur?"

"Mine?"

"Your letter. You’ve been here forty year. Longer than anyone except old Mrs. Corley. You’ve got that paper from the old dock company, haven't you? The one with the blue wax on it?"

"It’s in the tin," Arthur said.

"They can't touch you," she said, and for the first time there was something like hope in her face—a small, pale, hungry thing that made him want to turn around and walk back up the twenty-two steps to his own room and drop the latch. "They can't knock the bricks down while there’s someone inside with one of them old papers. Our George said it when the men came with the measuring sticks in June. He said, *They’ll never shift Artie Cleary. Artie’s got the 1977. Artie’s like the foundations; they have to buy him out with gold before they can turn a spade.*"

Arthur took his right hand out of his pocket. The skin was dry, grayish-white at the knuckles, with two blue dots of ink under the skin of the forefinger from an old ballpoint that had burst during his first year on the No. 7 route.

"I have to go down to the chemist," he said. "My knee’s bad with the damp."

Janice looked at his hand, then nodded once, slowly. The hope didn't leave her eyes; it just settled there, like water in a footprint on the mudflats.

"You speak to 'em, Arthur," she whispered. "If that lad comes back today, you tell him we’ve paid the three-pound-ten every Monday since fifty-nine. We never missed a week, not even when the strike was on and George was picking sea-coal off the sands with an old coal-sack on his bicycle. You tell him that."

"Aye," Arthur said. "I’ll tell him."

She pulled the door to. 

The liver-colored paint met the frame with that soft, dry *clack*, and then the iron horseshoe tapped once against the timber: *tink*.

Arthur went down the remaining forty-eight steps into the street.

### Chapter 3

The city had lost its corners.

When Arthur was a boy, you could tell where you were with your eyes shut by the smell of the walls and the resistance of the wind. 

If you were by the graving docks, the air smelled of pitch, wet oakum, and the cold, green water that sat in the bottom of the caissons after the gates were pumped dry. If you were by the grain silos, there was a sweet, dusty flour-smell that got into the back of your throat like powdered malt, and the rats on the railway sidings were as fat as cats, with yellow bellies from eating the Argentine maize. If you were by the gas-works, the air was yellow and sour, tasting of ammonia and old iron, and you didn't need a coat because the heat from the retorts came off the brickwork in waves that dried your trousers while you walked.

Now, between St. Jude’s Terrace and the sea-wall, there was only the perimeter road.

It was four lanes of black asphalt that had no name, only a number on a green reflective sign: *A5092 Dock Link Road*. It had been laid across the old timber-yards three years ago, straight and wide, with concrete curbs that were nine inches high and concrete light-poles that stood like silver knitting needles in the drizzle. 

Nothing walked on the perimeter road except forty-ton articulated trucks with Dutch and German plates, carrying refrigerated containers of pork from the ferry terminal to the distribution centers on the motorway forty miles inland. They didn't stop. They moved at fifty miles an hour, their twelve tires making a high, tearing hiss on the wet stone-mastic surface, their headlights cutting through the salt-fog like white knives.

Arthur walked along the narrow gravel verge, his hands deep in his pockets, his cap pulled down until the brim touched his eyebrows.

To his right, behind six hundred yards of galvanized palisade fencing, lay the display village of *The Spinnaker Reach*.

It was not a building site anymore; it was an enclosure of green lawn that had been rolled out from plastic mesh three weeks ago, kept green by subterranean sprinklers that ran even in the rain. In the middle of the grass sat the sales pavilion—a cube of cedar-slat timber and plate glass that looked as if it had been dropped from a helicopter onto the mud.

A sign by the automatic glass sliding doors read:

*The Horizon Suite*  
*Marketing & Concept Gallery*  
*Visitors Welcome: Mon–Sat 9:30–17:30*  
*Coffee by Colonna & Small*

Arthur stood by the fence. 

Through the glass, the interior was warm and yellow, lit by hundreds of tiny halogen spots set into a suspended ceiling of perforated white metal. In the center of the room, on a circular plinth of black slate, sat the architectural model of the finished development.

It was six feet across, made of white perspex and tiny strips of frosted acrylic that glowed from within. 

There were five towers in all, arranged in an arc like the fingers of a man’s hand reaching out into the channel. *The Spinnaker* was the tallest—twenty-two storeys, with a sloping roof that ended in an aluminum needle pointing toward the North Star. Around the bases of the towers were tiny plastic trees with lime-green sponge foliage, tiny plastic people the size of grains of rice walking across tiny white promenades, and tiny plastic launches moored to tiny floating pontoons in a basin that was painted the bright, impossible turquoise of a swimming pool in Spain.

Where St. Jude’s Terrace stood—where his scullery, his gas-ring, his horsehair mattress, and Mrs. Corley’s pitch-pine door were currently holding up fifty thousand tons of Victorian sandstone—the model showed a sunken amphitheater with steps made of gray granite, leading down to a water-feature that looked like a row of glass fountains.

A label on the edge of the plinth read: *The Founders’ Basin: Public Plaza & Cultural Way.*

Inside the pavilion, a young woman in a navy-blue suit was wiping the chrome spout of an espresso machine with a yellow duster. She had long, pale hair pulled back into a horse’s tail by an ivory clip, and she moved with the slow, graceful indifference of someone who knew that the rain outside had nothing to do with her salary.

Arthur looked at his watch. 

It was twenty past nine.

He had forty minutes before the man with the clean neck arrived at his door.

He turned his back on the glass cube and walked out onto the old stone jetty that projected fifty yards into the low-water mud.

This was the Bell-Pier. It had been built in 1844, before the docks were dug, when the steam-packets from Liverpool had to land their passengers by open boat because there wasn't three fathoms of water inside the spit at half-tide. The stones were three tons each, gray limestone from the quarries at Silverdale, tied together with iron staples that had rusted into red blisters that looked like dried blood on the rock.

At the end of the pier, leaning against the rusted cast-iron bollard that had once held the spring-lines of the paddle-steamers, sat Ephraim Kalu.

Kalu was seventy-four. He had come to the port in 1961 from Port Harcourt, an assistant marine surveyor on an elder-dempster cargo boat that had caught fire in the Bay of Biscay and been towed into the graving dock for six months of hull-plating. He had stayed because he had met a girl from the Salvation Army home who played the cornet with red knuckles, and by the time she had died of the influenza in seventy-six, the palm-oil boats were gone and the docks were closing one basin every eighteen months.

He was wearing an old navy bridge-coat with four brass buttons down the front, their anchors worn down to flat gold smears, and a woolen scarf of red and green that had been knitted by a church-group in sixty-eight. 

Between his knees he held a brass-cased pocket compass that he polished with his thumb every six seconds: *rub... rub... rub*.

"Mr. Cleary," Kalu said. He didn't look up. His voice was deep, soft, with that rounded, West African cadence that forty years of North Lonsdale rain had never quite managed to flatten into the dialect. "You are walking fast for a man with a bad leg."

"The salt gets into the knee," Arthur said. He stopped three feet away, his back to the wind, looking down into the channel where the mud was beginning to shine as the water receded. "You’re out early, Ephraim."

"The tide is six inches lower than the book," Kalu said. He pointed his thumb toward the black wooden beacon that marked the edge of the training-bank. "The scour from the new cofferdam is taking the silt away from the jetty. Two years more, and these stones will fall into the gut. They have not driven the sheet-piles deep enough on the south side. They think because they have a computer in Manchester they know how the mud moves under the grease."

Arthur sat down on the flat top of the bollard. The iron was cold through his trousers, cold as a butcher’s block.

"A man came yesterday," Arthur said.

Kalu’s thumb did not stop. *Rub... rub.*

"The young man," Kalu said. "With the blue motor-car that makes no noise when it turns in the lane. I saw him from my window. He has shoes made of calf-skin that have never had a dubbin-rag on them. He walked around the terrace three times, looking at the cracks in the lime-mortar where the chimney-breast is pulling away from the gable."

"He left a letter," Arthur said.

"They all leave letters," Kalu said. "In sixty-six, the corporation left a letter about the widening of the High Street. In seventy-four, the railway left a letter about the coal-yard. In eighty-two, the trust left a letter about the dry docks. When an Englishman wants to take your chair from under your backside, Mr. Cleary, he always writes you a letter first to explain that the wood is rotten and you will fall down if you continue to sit upon it."

"They’re offering me a flat," Arthur said.

The words sounded strange out on the pier, small and thin against the crying of the gulls and the roar of the container trucks on the link-road.

Kalu stopped his thumb. 

He looked at Arthur with eyes that were yellow around the irises, like old sheep-parchment that had been kept in an oily drawer.

"In the tower?"

"The third floor," Arthur said. "Unit 304. Two bedrooms. Underfloor heating. A lease for my life."

Kalu looked down at his compass. He opened the glass face with his thumbnail, touched the needle until it swung wildly from north-north-west to east-by-south, and then let it settle back toward the fells.

"And Mrs. Corley?" he asked.

"Eight hundred pound," Arthur said. "For the disturbance."

"And the little girl with the boy? The one who washes the plates at the ferry?"

"Shorthold," Arthur whispered. "Section twenty-one. Twenty-eight days."

Kalu nodded once. He closed the compass with a sharp *click* that was louder than the water against the stones.

"My father," Kalu said, his voice dropping into that rhythmic, storytelling murmur that he used when the rum was in him on Saturday nights, "was a clerk for the United Africa Company at Opobo. He had sixty-four ledgers, all bound in green pigskin, with the names of every canoe-boy who took palm-kernels down the river from fifty-two to seventy-five. When the company left, the white man—a man named Mr. Hesketh, who had three fingers on his left hand from an old shrapnel wound—gave my father a silver pocket-watch with an inscription on the back: *For Faithful Service to the River Trade.*"

He looked out across the mud toward the white concrete needles of *The Spinnaker Reach*.

"Two weeks later," Kalu said, "the soldiers came down from the capital in three lorries. They took the ledgers out into the yard, threw them into the ditch where the water-hyacinths were growing, and set them on fire with four tins of kerosene. My father showed the lieutenant the silver watch. He said, *Look, the company has given me this watch. I am an established clerk of the first grade.*"

Kalu paused. He put the compass into the pocket of his bridge-coat and buttoned the flap over it with two fingers.

"What did the soldier do?" Arthur asked, though he knew the answer.

"He took the watch," Kalu said. "He didn't break it. He just put it into his pocket and said, *The company is in London, old man. This is the river.*"

Kalu stood up. His knees made three small, dry cracks, like twigs snapping in a dry lane. 

"You are sixty-seven, Arthur. Your hip is bad. If you stay in St. Jude’s Terrace, the roof will come down on your head in three years because the landlord will not spend five pounds on a slate-nail. If you go into the tower, you will have warm feet and an electric bell that sounds like a flute."

"And you?" Arthur asked. "Where will you go, Ephraim?"

Kalu smiled. His teeth were long, yellow, with two gaps at the side where the scurvy had taken them during his third year on the coasters.

"There is a cousin of my wife’s who has a greengrocer’s shop in Toxteth," he said. "Behind the park. He has an attic where they keep the onion-sacks in the winter. It has a window that looks at the chimney of the sugar refinery. It is not twenty-two storeys high, Mr. Cleary, but the stairs are wooden, and when someone comes up them, I will know whether it is the boy with the cabbage or the bailiff with the red paper."

He turned and began to walk back along the jetty, his boots finding the centers of the limestone slabs with the slow, rhythmic certainty of a man who had measured forty miles of chain-link in his life and never lost an inch.

Arthur watched him until his dark bridge-coat had blurred into the gray drizzle of the lane.

Then he looked at his watch. 

Quarter to ten.

He stood up, wiped the salt-spray from the knees of his trousers with his flat palm, and began the walk back to the terrace.

### Chapter 4

At ten o’clock precisely, the seventh door opened.

It was not one of the nine doors on the landings; it was the great street-door at the bottom of the stairwell—three inches of unpainted deal with an iron knocker shaped like a dolphin’s head that had been bolted through the center stile in 1890.

It did not open wide. It gave a short, heavy groan as the rusted lower hinge dragged across the semicircular track it had worn into the flagstones over a hundred and forty years, followed by the sound of smooth, rubber-soled leather shoes stepping onto the quarry tiles.

Arthur stood in his kitchen. 

He had not put on his coat again, but he had smoothed down his hair with his palm at the scullery mirror. His blue cardigan was buttoned to the throat, and the green carbon-copy tenancy agreement from October 1984 lay on the oilcloth between the salt-cellar and the envelope from Leeds.

The footsteps came up the winders.

They were not the footsteps of the tenants. 

Marek walked with his heels hard down, driving the iron tips into the stone like a man who was always three minutes late for a shift. Syd Corcoran dragged his toes with a dry, whispering shuffle that stopped every six steps while his chest caught up with his legs. Tara moved on the balls of her feet, light and quick, as if she were trying to cross the river on floating grease without breaking the skin.

These footsteps were rhythmic, dry, unhurried: *tap... tap... tap...* 

Every fifth step there was a brief, fractional pause while the man adjusted his weight to the curve of the stair, then the steady climb began again, rising through the dark well of the building like water coming up a pipe.

On the second landing, the steps stopped.

Arthur heard the soft *tink* of George Beale’s horseshoe as the man’s raincoat brushed against the liver-colored door. Then a quiet, polite clearing of the throat, the rustle of treated nylon, and the footsteps began the final ascent to the fourth floor.

They stopped outside Arthur’s door.

No whistle. No bang of the flat palm against the center panel.

Three distinct, dry knocks with the middle knuckle of a bare hand: *toc, toc, toc.*

Arthur waited three seconds. Then he walked the four paces to the door, turned the brass thumb-turn that had been bright when he moved in and was now the color of an old penny, and pulled the door inward.

Julian Croft was thirty or thirty-one. 

He was five feet ten inches tall, with the smooth, clean-shaven jaw of a young man who used an electric razor that had five heads and cost two hundred pounds. His coat was not black; it was a dark, slate-gray macintosh made of some fabric that had caused the salt-rain to bead into tiny, clear pearls on the shoulders without sinking into the weave. Under the macintosh he wore a dark blue suit of fine wool, a white shirt without a crease at the throat, and a silk tie the color of a wet slate.

In his left hand he carried a slim, black leather document case with two brass combination locks that had been set to 0-0-0.

"Mr. Cleary," he said. His voice was pleasant, low, carrying that neutral, southern-suburban precision that belonged to nobody who had ever lived within fifty miles of an estuary. "Good morning. I’m Julian Croft. Thank you for seeing me."

Arthur did not offer his hand. He stood in the doorway, his shoulder braced against the frame where the old cream paint had chipped away to reveal the yellow pine beneath.

"You’d better come in," Arthur said. "The landing’s cold."

"It is," Croft said, stepping over the threshold with a brief, automatic glance at the worn brass draft-excluder. "A difficult building to heat, I imagine. The thermal leakage through these old uninsulated brick cavities must be extraordinary."

He walked into the kitchen as if he had already studied the floor-plan in an office three hundred miles away—which, Arthur realized with a small, cold twitch in his belly, he certainly had. He did not look at the gas-ring or the enameled kettle; he walked straight to the window and looked out at the basin.

"Superb," Croft murmured, half to himself. "Even on a morning like this. The light off the low-water reach is quite unique. You’ll have essentially the same orientation in 304, Mr. Cleary, but with eleven meters more elevation. You’ll clear the top of the old grain elevator completely. On a clear day in March, you should be able to see the lighthouse on St. Bees Head."

Arthur shut the door. The latch dropped into the keeper with its customary, oiled click: *thluck*.

"Sit down," Arthur said, nodding toward the Windsor chair by the grate. "I haven't got any coffee. There’s tea in the tin if you want it."

"Nothing for me, thank you," Croft said. He did not sit in the Windsor chair; he sat on the straight-backed beech chair by the table, placing his leather case flat on the oilcloth, six inches from the green tenancy paper. He did not touch the green paper. He looked at it once, his eyes taking in the date—*14th October 1984*—and the purple rubber stamp of the *Barrow & District Steam Navigation Company Housing Trust*, then looked up at Arthur with a smile that was neither cruel nor kind, but simply efficient.

"I see you’ve found the original agreement," Croft said. "Excellent. Our legal team in Leeds was slightly concerned that the record might have been lost when the municipal undertaking was restructured in eighty-six. This simplifies matters considerably."

Arthur remained standing, his back against the cold iron of the small cooking-range that hadn't had a fire in it since the chimney was condemned in ninety-four.

"You’re putting them all out," Arthur said.

Croft’s expression did not change. He clicked the two brass latches on his case: *snick-snick*. The sound was very small, but it seemed to fill the room like the cocking of two pistols.

"We are regularizing the site, Mr. Cleary," he said mildly. "St. Jude’s Terrace has been in technical default of borough building regulations since nineteen ninety-nine. The wiring alone represents a catastrophic fire risk. The riser pipes are lead throughout. If the environmental health department had exercised their full statutory oversight five years ago, the building would have been condemned under a Section 11 notice and everyone would have been decanted into emergency accommodation on the marsh."

"They’ve lived here forty years," Arthur said. "Mrs. Corley’s eighty-six."

Croft drew a flat, white folder from the case. The cover was printed on heavy card, with the same silver-foil logo Arthur had seen on the billboards down by the dock: *Vanguard Estuary*.

"Mrs. Corley has been offered an ex-gratia relocation grant," Croft said, turning a page with his thumb. "Eight hundred pounds. For an unassured monthly tenancy with twelve months’ rent arrears recorded in ninety-seven, that is a very generous settlement. Most private landlords would have executed a standard possession order through the county court and left her to the local authority."

"She hasn't got twelve months' arrears," Arthur said. "Her husband died in fifty-three. The company told her she could stay for two bob a week until she went into the ground."

"The company," Croft said, looking at him with genuine, patient sympathy, "was liquidated in nineteen seventy-eight, Mr. Cleary. Its assets were purchased by a holding vehicle registered in Douglas, which in turn was absorbed by our own group four months ago. The paper you have there"—he pointed with a clean, silver-cased pen at the green document—"is the only surviving instrument with statutory protection under the 1977 Act. The others are all ASTs—Assured Shorthold Tenancies. They run from month to month. Under the ninety-six amendments, we are required only to provide eight weeks’ notice under Section 21."

He laid the folder open on the table.

Inside were four sheets of cream paper, printed with the same neat, slanted typography as the letter. At the bottom of the second sheet were two yellow adhesive tabs marked *Sign Here* in red ink.

Beside the papers lay a color photograph.

It showed an interior. 

The floor was pale wood—narrow planks of Scandinavian birch that had been oiled until they looked like silk. The walls were the color of wet lime, with no skirting boards, no picture-rails, and no pipes. At the far end of the room was a window that ran from the ceiling to the floor, twelve feet of unbroken glass looking out over a harbor that was blue and empty, with a white launch cutting a neat, V-shaped wake across the water toward the lighthouse.

In the corner of the room stood an electric heater that was set into the plaster like a gray slate tile, with a small digital screen that showed the number *21.5°*.

"This is 304," Croft said, his voice dropping into that quiet, reverent tone that people use when they are showing a child a very expensive clock. "It was completed on Tuesday. The carpets are wool-twist, eighty-twenty. The kitchen has an induction hob, an integrated dishwasher, and a combi-oven that self-cleans at four hundred degrees. The bathroom has a walk-in shower with a thermostatic valve that maintains the temperature within half a degree, regardless of the pressure in the mains."

He paused, letting the silence of the room settle around the photograph.

"You would have your own key-fob, Mr. Cleary. Encrypted. No one can enter the lift without one. There are two caretakers on duty from eight in the morning until eight at night. If a light-bulb fails in your ceiling, you press the button on the intercom and a man comes with a stepladder and replaces it within two hours. You will never have to buy an oil-can again. You will never have to listen to the gas-meter clicking when the wind comes out of the north."

Arthur looked at the photograph.

The room was clean. It was so clean it looked as if no one had ever breathed in it. There was no smell of boiled cabbage, no damp serge hanging behind the door, no horsehair poking through the ticking of the settee, and no sound of George Beale’s iron horseshoe tapping against the liver-colored paint.

"And what happens to this?" Arthur asked, looking around his kitchen.

"Demolition begins on the fifteenth of November," Croft said. "Phase Two requires the foundation pad for the leisure complex. We are driving eighty-foot concrete piles through the old cellar vaults down into the sandstone shelf. Nothing of the existing structure can remain."

He took the silver pen from his pocket and laid it across the cream paper, right beside the first yellow tab.

"If you sign today, Mr. Cleary, our movers will arrive on the twenty-fourth. You don't have to pack a thing. They provide the boxes, the bubble-wrap, the wardrobe cartons. They will take whatever you wish to keep and dispose of the rest. You can be in 304 before the first frost."

Arthur reached out his hand.

His fingers hovered over the silver pen. It was heavy, cool, made of machined steel with tiny grooves around the grip so that a man’s hand wouldn't slip if he were sweating.

"And if I don't sign?" Arthur asked.

Croft didn't pull the pen away. He didn't blink.

"Then we apply to the county court for a certificate of alternative accommodation," he said, his voice as calm and steady as the tide in the gut. "Under Schedule 4 of the Act, we are entitled to possession if we can demonstrate that suitable alternative premises of equivalent standard have been offered and refused without reasonable cause. The proceedings will take six months, perhaps eight. Our barristers will explain to the judge that we have offered you a brand-new, purpose-built two-bedroom apartment with a market value of three hundred and twenty thousand pounds, rent-free, for life."

He leaned forward slightly, resting his forearms on the table.

"The judge will look at you, Mr. Cleary. He will see a retired man of sixty-seven who has been offered the best retirement flat in the county, and he will look at us, and he will sign the order. It will take longer. It will cost our company about fourteen thousand pounds in legal fees, which we will not seek to recover from you because we are not unreasonable people. But in the end, Mr. Cleary, the result will be precisely the same."

He tapped the cream paper once with his finger.

"Except that under the court order, you will receive only a standard assured tenancy in the municipal pool at Black-Dyke, three miles behind the railway sidings, in a concrete maisonette with a flat roof that leaks every time the gulls sit on it."

Arthur looked down at his own knuckles. 

The two blue ink-dots under the skin were very dark against the white bone.

"Why do you want me to do it now?" Arthur whispered. "If you can get the judge to sign the order, why come here with the leather bag?"

Croft smiled. It was the first real smile Arthur had seen on his face—not the professional one from the brochures, but the smile of an apprentice who has finally learned how to set the points on a distributor without checking the manual.

"Because of the others, Mr. Cleary," Croft said.

"The others?"

"If you resist," Croft said, "the tenants’ association down at the Trades Hall will get involved. That red-headed woman from the university will come down with her petition. There will be banners on the railing. The local paper will run a photograph of you holding your forty-year-old rent book under the headline: *The Last Lock-Keeper of St. Jude’s.* The planning committee will get nervous about Phase Three. The council will defer the stopping-up order for the dock road, and every month of delay costs our investment syndicate eighty-two thousand pounds in facility fees to the bank."

He picked up the silver pen and held it toward Arthur, handle first.

"If you leave quietly on the twenty-fourth, Mr. Cleary, there is no story. An old man moves into a better home. The shortholds are cleared by standard administrative notice. By the time the Trades Hall realizes the terrace is empty, the windows will be tinned up and the roof-slates will be off."

He offered the pen again.

"Sign the deed, Arthur. Save yourself. You can't save them anyway. They’re already gone."

Arthur looked at the silver pen. 

From the landing below, through the floorboards, came the sound of Flat 2B.

Tara.

She had come back from the cold-store. The hollow-core door gave its high, screaming iron squeal as she pushed it open, followed by the soft, wet slap of her hand against the casing, and then the sound of the four-year-old boy, Dylan, laughing as his boots hit the linoleum in the passage.

*He’s awake,* Arthur thought. *She didn't get it shut in time.*

He reached out his hand and took the pen.

### Chapter 5

The silver pen had a balance that Arthur did not like. 

It was too light in the nose. A ticket-punch had weight; an old iron brake-handle had forty pounds of counterbalanced lead behind the bevel; even the brass key to the scullery press had a thick, square bit that filled the palm and reminded you that something was shut and stayed shut until you put your weight into it.

This thing felt like a needle you used for taking splinters out of a horse’s heel.

"The second page first," Croft said. His voice had lowered by half an octave, the tone men use in banks when the cashier is counting five-pound notes from the drawer with a wet sponge on his thumb. "The surrender of the head-lease rights. Just above the red mark."

Arthur did not sign. He laid the pen down on the white cream paper, exactly where the two sheets met.

"I’ll have my tea first," Arthur said.

Croft’s eyes narrowed by a fraction—not with anger, but with that sharp, analytical curiosity a surveyor shows when a plumb-line doesn't hang true against a wall he thought was square.

"Of course," Croft said, looking at his watch. It was a thin, gold disc with no numerals, just twelve tiny black notches on the rim. "I have twenty minutes before my conference call with the regional director. Take your time, Mr. Cleary."

Arthur turned his back on him and walked to the gas-ring. 

He didn't strike a match. The flame was already out, but he picked up the kettle by its cold wire handle, carried it to the stone sink, and turned the brass tap. 

The water didn't come with its usual heavy, thumping rush. It came in a thin, twisted ribbon, gray with air-bubbles, that rattled against the bottom of the enameled tin like dry peas dropped into a bucket. 

*They’ve turned the pressure down at the main,* he thought. *They’re doing it from the street-box.*

When he had half an inch of water, he set the kettle down on the cold iron of the range. He did not turn around. He stood with his hands on the greasy timber of the dresser, looking at the shelf where he kept his things.

There wasn't much.

A blue earthenware salt-crock with a chip on the rim that had been there since the winter of eighty-one, when Sarah had dropped it while the pipes were frozen. A tin of *Three Nuns* tobacco that had had no tobacco in it for thirty years, only six brass buttons from his corporation tunic, three spare mantles for the gas-lamp they had taken down in seventy-four, and a small, lead toy horse that his brother had found in the mud behind the dry-dock in nineteen forty-five. 

And, behind the crock, tucked into the dark space between the dresser and the chimney-breast, a photograph in a frame made of four pieces of matchboarding glued together with fish-glue.

It was not Sarah. 

Sarah had taken her photographs with her in the brown suitcase, along with the silver-plated fish-knives and the woollen blanket her mother had given them from the mill at Stalybridge.

It was the depot on the morning of the changeover: twenty-fourth of October, nineteen eighty-six.

There were eighteen men standing in front of Bus No. 42—the big, square-nosed Daimler with the double deck and the open platform at the back that they used for the workers' specials down to the steelworks. 

Arthur was in the second row, his cap straight on his head, his hands folded across his belly, looking straight at the lens with the blank, unblinking stare of a man who had been told by the photographer to hold his breath until the powder flashed. 

Beside him stood Billy Nancarrow, who had spat on his tire three weeks later; and on the lower step, with his boots on the running board and his cap cocked over his left ear like a sailor on shore-leave, stood Tommy Teague.

Tommy had driven the No. 12 out to the salt-marshes until his eyes went from the cataract in ninety-one. 

When Tommy’s wife had died of the growth in her breast, Tommy had stayed in his two rooms at the bottom of the lane for five years without coming out except to buy six penn'orth of stale bread from the back door of the co-op on Wednesdays. And when the water-rates men had come with the red summons in ninety-six, Tommy hadn't argued with them. He hadn't called the union. He had walked out onto the Bell-Pier at four in the morning, while the tide was running three knots through the gut, taken off his boots, laid his pension book inside them with a half-crown on top to stop the wind from taking the leaves, and walked off the end of the limestone into thirty feet of mud-water.

Arthur had been on the early shift that morning. 

He had seen the boots from the cab of the No. 7 as he came over the swing-bridge—just two black shapes on the white frost of the pier-head, looking like two small dogs waiting for a boat.

"Mr. Cleary?"

Croft’s voice was polite, but the thin edge was showing now, like the iron rail through the worn velvet on an old settee.

"I have to take this call at eleven," Croft said. "The deeds need to be logged with the registry by two if we’re to process the completion before the weekend."

Arthur turned around. 

The young man was still sitting by the table, but he had opened his leather case again and taken out a silver pen-holder that held three different colors of ink: black for the registry, blue for the company seal, and red for the cancel-stamp.

"You’ve got a mother, I suppose?" Arthur said.

Croft blinked once. The pen-holder stayed in his right hand, three inches above the cream paper.

"I’m sorry?"

"Your mother," Arthur said, his voice flat and level as a carpenter’s rule. "She still about?"

Croft gave a short, dry laugh that had no more breath behind it than the gas-ring when the meter was down to its last penny.

"My mother lives in Surrey, Mr. Cleary. In a village near Godalming. I don't quite see—"

"What kind of door has she got?"

Croft looked at him for five seconds. In the street below, another truck shifted gears with a heavy, wet snort of compressed air that rattled the tea-mugs on the dresser.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Her door," Arthur said, taking one step toward the table. "The front one. What’s it made of?"

Croft set the pen-holder down. His face had gone slightly tight around the nostrils, the way young men’s faces go when they think they are dealing with an old person whose wits have begun to slip into the fog.

"It’s oak, I believe," Croft said carefully. "A reproduction Georgian four-panel. With a brass knocker. It’s a very pleasant house, Mr. Cleary. But really, we have a considerable amount of business to conclude—"

"When she shuts it," Arthur said, "can she hear the people next door?"

"Of course not," Croft said, his voice sharpening. "It’s a detached property. There are four hundred yards of garden between her and the lane. Now, Mr. Cleary, if we could return to Section Three—"

"There’s nine doors in this well," Arthur said. He did not look at the papers. He looked at Croft’s macintosh, at the tiny drops of rain that were still sitting on the shoulder-seams like mercury. "I’ve lived behind this one forty-one years. When Marek goes out at five, I know if his girl’s teeth are coming through by whether he uses the Yale or the drop-latch. When Janice Beale’s brother gets the wandering in his legs, I hear the horseshoe hit the timber twice before she gets him by the cardigan and sits him back down by the hearth."

He stopped. He could hear his own breath—a thin, whistle in the left lung that had started three winters ago when the damp came through the brickwork behind his bed.

"If I go into that tower," Arthur said, "who lives in 303?"

Croft picked up his silver pen again. His fingers were perfectly steady.

"A senior project manager from the wind-turbine installation contractor," he said, speaking quickly now, reading from some internal inventory that had already cataloged every cubic foot of air between the mud and the clouds. "And in 305, an associate partner in an accountancy practice in Kendal. Highly respectable people, Mr. Cleary. Quiet. Professional. You won't hear a sound from them. The party walls are double-leaf plasterboard on acoustic isolation mounts, with ninety millimeters of mineral wool in the cavity. You could fire a twelve-bore cartridge in the lounge and they wouldn't hear it in the hallway."

"And when they go out?" Arthur asked. "What does it sound like?"

Croft stared at him.

"It sounds like a door, Mr. Cleary. A modern, fire-rated composite door with a three-point deadbolt and an automatic closer. It sounds like nothing."

Arthur looked at the green paper on the oilcloth.

Forty years.

Two pounds and four shillings a week when he signed it in the basement of the old dock office, with the clerk using an inkwell set into a block of lignum vitae that had been turned out of an old prop-shaft bearing. 

He had brought Sarah up the seventy-two steps that evening, carrying her small tin trunk on his shoulder while she held her hat against the harbor wind with both hands. She had stood in the empty scullery, looking at the chipped stone sink and the gas-bracket on the wall, and she had said: *It’s very high up, Artie. It’s like living in the top of a crane.*

And he had told her: *The bricks are four thick, Sal. They built 'em for the foremen. You couldn't knock 'em down with a twelve-pound shot.*

"Mr. Cleary," Croft said. He was standing now. His briefcase was shut, but the cream papers remained on the table, the silver pen lying beside them like a piece of bright wire. "I have to be in Lancaster by one o’clock. If you sign the surrender now, I will leave the preliminary cheque for two thousand pounds on the table. That is an advance against your disturbance grant, to cover your immediate expenses. The lease for 304 will be delivered to you by our solicitors on Monday morning."

Arthur looked at the silver pen.

He thought of the room in the photograph: the pale birch floor, the white walls, the little gray slate tile with *21.5°* glowing in green numbers through the dark. 

He thought of his hip on a Tuesday morning in January, with the rain coming down the chimney and the wind blowing the gas out on the ring while he tried to boil four inches of water for his tea. 

He thought of the eighty steps. 

Three years more, and he wouldn't be able to carry six pounds of potatoes up from the market without stopping three times on the winders to let his heart stop knocking against his ribs like a dry valve-lifter.

"And if I sign," Arthur said, "when do the men come for Janice?"

Croft’s face didn't move.

"The notices will be served on Monday morning," he said. "They have thirty days. Under the circumstances, given her brother’s condition, we will allow an extension of fourteen days if the social services department makes a formal application. We are not unreasonable, Mr. Cleary."

"Thirty days," Arthur repeated.

"It is the standard statutory period," Croft said. "Now, please. Your signature here, and your initials on the counter-deed."

He pushed the pen forward two inches.

Arthur reached out his hand. His fingers closed around the cold, smooth metal.

It was light. So light you could forget you had it between your knuckles. 

He brought the nib down to the yellow tab. 

Through the floorboards, through the twelve inches of dead lime and horsehair, came the sound of George Beale’s iron horseshoe.

*Tink.*

A long pause—five seconds, six seconds—while the old man’s slippers dragged three inches across the linoleum below.

Then the latch lifted.

Not the two jerky hops of Janice’s swollen fingers. 

A slow, heavy, rattling drag that went all the way to the top of the slot and stayed there, shaking with the palsy of a hand that had spent fifty years looking for the line of a bulkhead on a cold floor.

Then George’s voice. 

It was not loud, but it rose through the open chimney-breast with the terrible, hollow clarity of an old pipe speaking in an empty cellar:

"Janice? Janice, where’s the blue chalk? The slip’s clear, lass. They’re taking the wedges out from under the keel. If I haven't got the chalk, the ship’ll go down the ways without her name on her."

A door slammed below.

Not the Beale door. 

Flat 2B. 

Tara had run out onto the landing. Arthur heard her slippers slapping on the stone, three steps at a time, then her voice, sharp and high with the terror of a girl who had lived her whole life in rooms with thin latches:

"Mr. Beale! Get back inside! Dylan’s asleep, Mr. Beale—don't open the big door, there’s men on the stairs!"

Croft looked at the ceiling, his eyebrows lifting by a sixteenth of an inch.

"You see?" he murmured, his voice dropping into that quiet, reasonable tone again. "It’s untenable, Mr. Cleary. The building is simply not safe for these people. In a modern development, with professional security and controlled access, this kind of... disturbance simply cannot occur."

Arthur looked at the yellow tab.

The ink was black, shining wetly in the little window of the pen’s reservoir.

He looked at Croft’s shoes—the clean, calf-skin leather that had never seen a dubbin-rag, that had never stepped in the grease-troughs under an inspection pit or carried three stone of sea-coal up seventy-two steps in a wet sack.

"You’ve got a clean neck, Mr. Croft," Arthur said.

Croft frowned.

"I beg your pardon?"

Arthur laid the silver pen down on the oilcloth. 

He didn't drop it; he laid it down very gently, exactly parallel to the edge of the table, two inches from the green paper with the blue wax seal from nineteen eighty-four.

"I’m not signing," Arthur said.

### Chapter 6

The silence in the kitchen lasted seven seconds. 

Outside, on the dock road, a heavy transporter carrying four pre-stressed concrete bridge-beams hit the expansion joint at the entrance to the terminal. The iron in the road rang like a dead anvil, and the gas-brackets in Arthur’s scullery gave three small, dry rattles against the plaster.

Croft did not pick up the pen. He looked down at it, then at Arthur’s face, his eyes narrowing into that cool, administrative focus that men use when an adding machine has produced a total with an extra zero at the end.

"Mr. Cleary," he said, and his voice had lost its southern softness; it was flat now, dry as slate. "I don't think you quite understand the legal mechanics of your position."

"I understand 'em," Arthur said. He stayed where he was, his hands flat against the greasy wood of the dresser behind him. "You’ve got two barristers in Leeds with wigs on their heads and five hundred pounds an hour on their books. You’ve got a paper that says this house is seventy per cent air and thirty per cent damp. And you’ve got a judge in the county court who went to the same school as your chairman’s brother."

Croft’s lips thinned.

"The law is not a conspiracy, Mr. Cleary. It is a set of statutory definitions. Under Section 98 of the 1977 Act, your protection is contingent upon the absence of suitable alternative accommodation. We have offered you an apartment that exceeds the statutory definition of suitability by every metric known to the courts—space, heating, amenity, accessibility, and tenure. No judge in this circuit will allow you to maintain an Assured Tenancy in a building that has been scheduled for structural demolition under an approved urban regeneration masterplan."

"Then let him sign the order," Arthur said.

Croft took a breath. He reached down, picked up the silver pen, and slid it back into the leather loop inside his case: *snick*. 

"You think you’re helping them," Croft said, nodding toward the floorboards. "You think if you stay here like an old dog on a step, the bulldozers won't come. You think the company will say, *Oh, well, Mr. Cleary wants his tea in his old scullery, so we’ll just cancel an eighty-million-pound waterfront scheme and leave twelve families to rot in a nineteenth-century slum.*"

He stood up, tall and straight in his slate-gray macintosh, his leather case tucked under his left arm.

"They are going anyway, Arthur. On Monday morning, the bailiffs will serve the notices on Flat 1A, Flat 1B, Flat 2B, and Flat 3A. In four weeks, their tenancies expire. If they don't vacate, we apply for summary warrants under the fast-track possession list. By the end of November, there will be no one in this building except you and the rats."

He walked to the door, turned the brass knob, and pulled it open six inches.

"And then," Croft said, looking back over his shoulder, "we begin the asbestos remediation on the roof. Under the Health and Safety at Work Act, we are required to seal the building with polythene sheeting and disconnect the communal services to prevent contaminated particulate from entering the domestic supply. The water will be off for twelve days, Mr. Cleary. The gas will be capped at the meter-box in the lane. You will be sitting up here on your four-inch mattress with a candle and a bucket of water from the public fountain by the swing-bridge, waiting for your day in court."

He stepped out onto the landing.

"I will hold the offer open until five o’clock on Friday," Croft said. "After that, Unit 304 goes to the open market. There is a doctor from the hospital in Kendal who has already offered twenty-four thousand pounds over the guide price for the lease."

He pulled the door shut.

The latch dropped with that soft, oiled *thluck*.

Arthur did not move. 

He stood by the dresser, his feet planted on the worn linoleum, listening to the footsteps go down the winders.

*Tap... tap... tap...*

On the second landing, there was no pause. Croft didn't look at George Beale’s horseshoe. He didn't look at the liver-colored paint. He went down the stone steps with the quick, clean stride of a man who had four other appointments before lunch and didn't intend to let an old bus driver with bad knees make him late for his soup.

Then the great street-door groaned—that long, heavy, iron complaint that ended in the hollow *thud* of sixty pounds of unpainted deal hitting the jamb—and the building was quiet again.

Arthur walked to the table.

He picked up the green paper from nineteen eighty-four, folded it once along the old, soft crease, and put it back into his cardigan pocket. 

Then he picked up his cap.

### Chapter 7

The rain had stopped by noon, but the light hadn't improved. 

It was that dead, white sky that sits over the Irish Sea in late autumn, when the clouds are so low and flat that the smoke from the brickworks can't rise more than fifty feet before it turns sideways and drifts across the salt-marsh like dirty wool.

Arthur walked down the dock road toward the basin.

The sales pavilion was quiet. The young woman with the ivory clip was sitting behind her desk reading a glossy magazine with a picture of a house in Tuscany on the back. Outside, on the gravel perimeter, two men in yellow high-visibility vests were drilling holes in the concrete curbs with a pneumatic hammer: *rat-tat-tat-tat-tat.* 

Every time the steel bit bit into the aggregate, a puff of white stone-dust rose into the wet air, smelling of burnt lime and cold water.

Arthur walked past them, out onto the mud-flats behind the Bell-Pier.

At low water, you could walk three miles out into the channel if you knew the hard ground. 

The people from the new houses didn't know it; they thought the estuary was just mud and tide, a flat brown waste that smelled of sulfur when the wind dropped. 

But Arthur had walked it every Sunday for fifty years. 

There was the old ballast-bank, where the sailing brigs from Norway had dumped sixty thousand tons of gray granite boulders in the eighteen-forties before loading pig-iron for the Baltic; there was the training-wall, four feet of limestone blocks tied with iron crampons that ran like a submerged road toward the lighthouse; and between them lay the cockle-beds, where the sand was as hard and ribbed as an old washboard, firm enough to bear a horse and cart if you kept six yards clear of the black mud where the sewer-pipe came out from the hospital.

He found Syd Corcoran sitting on an upturned wooden fish-box behind the old boat-slip, where the wall gave some shelter from the north-west drift.

Syd had a piece of fine emery-cloth in his fingers, and he was rubbing the brass screw of a four-inch model of the cruiser *Penelope*. His fingers were brown at the tips from the nicotine, and his breath came through his teeth with a dry, whistling rattle that sounded like a bicycle-pump with a torn washer.

"Artie," Syd said. He didn't look up from the brass. "You seen that lad?"

"He was up at ten," Arthur said. He sat down on the granite coping of the slip, his heels dangling over the seaweed-covered mud six feet below.

"He was at mine at half-past," Syd said. *Scritch... scritch* went the emery against the little brass blades. "Offered me five hundred notes. Said if I went into the hostel behind the station, he’d pay the first month’s telly-license and give me a voucher for twenty-five dinners at the railway buffet."

Syd stopped rubbing. He held the little propeller up to his good eye, squinting against the white light from the clouds.

"I told him to put his voucher up his pipe," Syd said. "I told him I’d lived on the top floor of St. Jude’s since sixty-eight, and there wasn't a man in Leeds with a clean enough neck to tell me where I was going to cough my lungs out."

"He’ll bring the paper, Syd," Arthur said softly.

"Let him bring it," Syd said, his voice rising into that thin, cracked treble that old men get when their chests are full of water. "What’s he going to do? Put me in the lock-up? The lock-up’s warmer than my room anyway. They’ve got radiators in the cells now, Artie. My nephew was in for six weeks for the scrap-iron on the railway, and he said they give you three hot dinners a day and a color television in the day-room with the racing from Haydock."

He reached into his pocket, pulled out a crushed packet of *Woodbines*, and took out a cigarette that was bent like an elbow in the middle. He straightened it with his dirty thumbs, put it between lips that were bluish-gray at the corners, and struck a match on the side of his boot.

The sulfur flared, white and sharp, then the blue smoke curled up into his nostrils.

"Janice is packing," Syd said between two short, dry coughs.

Arthur turned his head.

"Packing what?"

"The crockery," Syd said. "She was down at the co-op at eleven, getting them cardboard banana-boxes from the lad by the fruit-bins. Said she’s going to take George’s chalk-boards down to her sister’s in Millom on the bus."

"Her sister’s got three rooms over a baker’s shop," Arthur said. "She’s got four kids of her own and a husband who works the shift at the quarry."

"Aye," Syd said, blowing smoke through his nose toward the gray water. "And George’ll be in the canal before Christmas. You know what George is like when he can't see the cranes. He thinks the town’s stopped if the jibs aren't turning."

They sat in the quiet for five minutes. 

A hundred yards out in the channel, a seal popped its head through the brown water, looked at them with its black, oily eyes for three seconds, and then slid back under the tide without leaving a ripple.

"He offered you the tower, didn't he?" Syd asked.

The emery-cloth started again: *scritch... scritch.*

"Aye," Arthur said.

"Three hundred?"

"304," Arthur said. "The third floor."

Syd nodded once. He didn't look angry; he looked tired, the way he had looked on the morning the floating crane had dropped its main-sheave into forty feet of dock-water and they had spent eighteen hours with the grapnels trying to find the wire in the dark.

"It’s warm up there, Artie," Syd said. "My sister’s girl went to see the show-flat on the Saturday they opened it with the free sherry. She said you can walk about in your stocking-feet and the floor feels like an iron that’s just been taken off the range. Said you don't even need a blanket on the bed. Just one of them white things with the feathers inside."

"I told him no," Arthur said.

Syd’s hand stopped. 

The little brass propeller fell into the gravel between his boots with a tiny, metallic *ting*.

He turned his head slowly, his neck-muscles standing out under the loose, gray skin like two lengths of old hemp rope.

"You what?"

"I told him I wasn't signing," Arthur said. He looked at his own boots, at the grease on the toes where he had kicked the brake-ratchet on the No. 7 bus for twenty years. "I told him he could go to the judge."

Syd stared at him. 

The cigarette burned down toward his yellow knuckles, the gray ash growing an inch long before the wind took it and scattered it across the mud.

"You’re a bloody fool, Artie Cleary," Syd whispered.

His voice wasn't loud, but it had a shake in it that Arthur had never heard before, not even when the Frigate boiler-lagging had caught fire in seventy-two and the smoke had filled the dry-dock until you couldn't see your own knees.

"You’re a bloody, stubborn old fool," Syd said. "What do you think you’re doing? Standing up for the terrace? Standing up for me? I’m dead, Artie. The doctor at the clinic told me in June I haven't got enough lung left to fill a bicycle-bell. In six months they’ll be carrying me down them seventy-two steps in a deal box with four handles made of cheap tin."

He leaned forward, his hands on his knees, his face two inches from Arthur’s shoulder.

"And Janice’ll be in Millom, and the little girl from the ferry’ll be in a council shed behind the gas-works, and you’ll be sitting up there in your scullery with the water turned off at the main, eating cold beans out of a tin because your gas-pipe’s been capped by the sheriff’s officer."

He reached down, picked up the little propeller, and threw it with all his strength out into the brown water of the channel.

It made a tiny splash—a white bubble that lasted half a second—and then the tide took it.

"Go back and sign the paper, Arthur," Syd said, his breath whistling so hard his shoulders shook under the donkey-jacket. "Take the warm floor. Take the little electric bell that sounds like a flute. There’s no one going to put a brass plate on the pier to say Artie Cleary died of the cold because he wouldn't let the derrick-men have his kitchen."

He stood up, turned his back on the basin, and began the long, dragging walk back toward the gap in the palisade fence, his boots kicking the white stone-dust from the drills as he went.

Arthur stayed on the coping.

The tide was rising now. 

The first gray tongue of salt-water was licking around the foot of the limestone blocks, creeping over the green bladder-wrack, filling the cracks where the crabs lived with a soft, sucking gurgle that sounded like someone drinking tea out of a deep saucer.

He reached into his pocket and touched the green paper from nineteen eighty-four. 

The paper was soft, almost greasy from forty years of being kept in the tin behind the salt-crock. It had no power in it. It was just sixteen square inches of dead wood-pulp with some blue ink on the front that had been written by a clerk whose bones were already white under the turf at the cemetery on the hill.

He pulled it out, looked at it for three seconds in the white light from the clouds, and then folded it back into four and put it into his waistcoat pocket, right over his heart, where the ticking of his old silver watch could strike against the wax seal every second until the dark came down.

### Chapter 8

At four o’clock, Arthur walked up the seventy-two steps for the third time that day.

The building smelled different. 

The smell of boiled cabbage was gone from the Beale landing. In its place was the sharp, sour reek of old newspaper and damp straw—the packing-straw that Janice had retrieved from the crates behind the greengrocer’s. 

Through the door of Flat 3B, which stood open two inches to let the dust out, Arthur could see three banana-boxes sitting on the horsehair settee. 

Inside the nearest box, wrapped in copies of the *North Western Daily Mail* from November nineteen ninety-one, were George’s drawing instruments: the long, brass-bound pear-wood splines he had used for striking the shear-lines of the cruisers; his heavy lead weights shaped like flat whales that held the wood against the chalk; and the three bone-handled dividers that had belonged to his grandfather when the yard was still building wooden schooners for the slate-trade.

George was sitting in the corner by the cold grate. 

He had his overcoat on—a heavy, black Melton coat with three horn buttons missing—and his cap was pulled down over his ears. He was holding a piece of white chalk in his right fist, and he was drawing a line along the wooden arm of his chair: *scritch... scritch... scritch.*

When he reached the end of the wood, his hand didn't stop; it dropped off the edge, hovered in the air for two seconds like a bird looking for a branch, and then went back to the joint by the cushion to begin the line again.

Janice came out of the scullery carrying a brown earthenware teapot wrapped in an old woolen scarf.

"Arthur," she said. Her face was yellow in the dusk, the skin under her chin hanging down in two soft folds that shook when she spoke. "The carrier’s coming at eight tomorrow. My cousin’s boy has got an open lorry with a tarpaulin. He says he can take the settee and the chest of drawers if we have 'em down on the pavement before the wardens are out."

Arthur stopped on the landing. He took off his cap and held it against his stomach with both hands.

"You’re going to Millom, then?"

"Aye," she said. She didn't look at him; she looked down at the tea-cosy, her thumbs smoothing the rough wool of the scarf. "There’s a room behind the wash-house. It’s got a concrete floor, but my cousin’s got an old oil-stove we can put in the corner. She says George can sit in the yard when the weather’s fair. He likes looking at the hens, Arthur. He used to have four black minorcas when we lived in the cottage by the rope-walk, before they widened the railway."

George’s chalk reached the end of the chair-arm: *scritch... drop.*

"The ship’s seventy feet in the beam," George said to the wall. His voice was remarkably clear, the deep, resonant voice of a man who was used to shouting through fifty yards of drafts in a loft with two hundred men working below him. "You can't bring her through the outer sill on the neaps. You have to wait for the Sunday tide, or the skeg’ll take the stone off the caisson."

"Hush, George," Janice whispered. "Drink your tea."

She looked at Arthur then, and her eyes were very small, very dry, like two gray pebbles that had been washed by forty years of salt-water until there was no grit left in them.

"That lad came back," she said.

Arthur’s fingers tightened on the brim of his cap.

"Croft?"

"No," she said. "Another one. A lad with yellow hair and a clipboard. Said he was from the council housing department. Said because we were leaving voluntary, they could give us forty-five pounds toward the lorry if we signed the cancellation of the register."

"Did you sign it?"

"I had to, Arthur," she said, and her voice didn't rise; it had that terrible, quiet flatness again. "The carrier wants thirty pound for the diesel from Millom and back. If I didn't have the forty-five, the furniture would have to stay on the cobbles, and the rain’d have it before the night was out."

She looked past him, down the dark well of the stairs toward the street.

"He said you’d signed, Arthur."

The lie hung between them in the cold air, smelling of old straw and George’s chalk.

Arthur did not answer for five seconds. 

He could hear the gas-brackets in the scullery, the faint *hiss-sh-sh* of the leak that had been in the elbow-joint since ninety-two, which the corporation had never come to fix because they said the pipes were too old to take the wrench.

"He told you that?" Arthur asked.

"He said you’d taken the key for the tower," she whispered. "Said you were going in on the twenty-fourth. Said you were a sensible man who knew when a house was finished."

She reached out her hand and touched his sleeve. 

Her fingers were cold, hard, with the thick, yellow nails of a woman who had scrubbed eighty thousand stone steps with a carbolic block and a zinc bucket.

"I don't blame you, Arthur," she said. "You’ve done your time. You were forty year on the buses. You deserve to have warm feet before you go. It’s only right. George said it when we had the letter: *Artie’s a corporation man. The corporation looks after its own.*"

Arthur looked down at her fingers on his coat.

He wanted to tell her. He wanted to reach into his waistcoat, take out the green paper from nineteen eighty-four, and show her that the seal was unbroken, that the red tabs were gone, that the silver pen was back in the young man’s leather case on its way to Lancaster.

He wanted to tell her that he was staying. 

That he would be here on Monday morning when the bailiffs came; that he would be here when the water was turned off; that he would sit in his four-inch horsehair groove with his cap on and his grease-gun in his pocket until the roof came down on his head.

But he looked at George’s chalk-mark on the arm of the chair.

He looked at the three banana-boxes on the settee, wrapped in forty-year-old newspapers.

He looked at the little room behind the wash-house in Millom, with the concrete floor and the hens in the rain.

If he told her he had stayed, what did it give her? 

It gave her two more weeks of the gas-meter clicking. It gave her two more weeks of George drawing lines on his trousers while the draft came under the door. It gave her a bailiff with a yellow paper and two policemen from the division who would carry George down the seventy-two steps by his elbows while the boys from the dock road whistled from the corners.

"Janice," he said.

His voice was very thick, like oil that had been kept in an unheated garage through a January frost.

"Aye, Arthur?"

"Watch the third step on the landing," he said. "The nosing’s come adrift. If the lad carries the settee down backwards, he’ll take his heel on it if he doesn't mind his footing."

She looked at him for three seconds, her hand dropping from his sleeve like a dry leaf from a wall.

Then she nodded once, slowly.

"Aye," she said. "I’ll tell him."

She turned back into the room. 

The liver-colored door didn't shut; it stayed open two inches, and through the gap Arthur heard the iron horseshoe tap against the paint as the wind came up the well:

*Tink.*

### Chapter 9

At eight o’clock that night, Arthur Cleary sat in his kitchen with the gas-ring out.

The building was very quiet.

Flat 1B was dark. Marek had not come home from the gantry; he had taken a double shift on the iron-ore unloader to get the overtime before the terminal closed for the weekend. 

Flat 1A was silent. Mrs. Corley had drawn her heavy iron bolt at five o’clock, and the only sound from her room was the faint, rhythmic ticking of the pendulum-clock her father had made out of an old steam-gauge from the locomotive sheds in eighteen ninety-four.

On the floor below, behind the hollow-core door that screamed on its hinges, Tara was singing.

She was not singing a church-song, and she was not singing one of the tunes from the radio down at the cafeteria. 

It was an old, nonsense rhyme from the west coast of Ireland that her grandmother had brought over on the potato-boat—four notes, repeated over and over with a soft, circular rhythm that rose through the floorboards like the sound of an old pump in a dry field:

*Go to sleep, my little boat,*  
*The water’s deep, the timber’s stout...*  
*The harbor-light is burning clear,*  
*And there’s no wind to bring us fear...*

Between the verses, the child made a small, snuffling sound, his breath catching once, twice, then settling into that long, soft sigh that children have when the room is dark and their mother’s hand is on the wool of their blanket.

Arthur sat at his table.

He had not taken off his boots. His feet were cold, the damp from the Bell-Pier having worked its way through the double-seams of the leather until his woollen socks were gray and heavy against his ankles.

On the oilcloth in front of him sat three objects.

The first was his brass key to the street-door—five inches long, with two square wards that had been filed by hand out of an iron bolt by a smith in ninety-two. 

The second was his pension book, with its sixty-four weekly vouchers, of which forty-one had been stamped with purple ink by the clerk at the post office behind the fish-dock.

The third was a postcard that had arrived in ninety-six, with a picture of the pier at Blackpool on the front and four lines of faded pencil on the back:

*Artie. The room is sixteen shillings. The sea is very gray today. I have bought two pairs of stockings from the shop by the tower. Sarah.*

He did not look at the postcard. He knew every stroke of the pencil; he knew the place where she had pressed too hard on the *S* in *Sarah* because the table in the boarding-house had an uneven leg that wobbled when the trams went past on the promenade.

He looked at the door.

His own door.

Forty-one years. 

He had painted it twice: once in eighty-five, with a tin of green coach-enamel that Billy Nancarrow had stolen from the corporation paint-shop in an old two-pound jam-jar; and once in ninety-seven, with some gray primer he had bought for three shillings from the ironmonger’s clearance sale when the shop closed down to make room for the new car-park.

Under the gray, where his thumb hit the style every time he pushed it shut, the green showed through—a small, dark oval of bright emerald that had the shape of his own knuckle-bone.

If he stayed, he would be the last.

Croft was right about that. The law was not a shield; it was a timetable. It was a schedule, like the winter route-sheet for the No. 7 bus: twenty-two minutes from the swing-bridge to the salt-marsh, two minutes to turn on the gravel at the church, four minutes for a cup of tea from the thermos, and then back into the slot behind the post-van at eight-fifteen.

You could sit in the cab with your arms folded and refuse to put the lever into gear, but the conductor would still ring the bell twice from the platform. 

The bell would ring, the passengers would look at the back of your head through the glass, the inspector would come out of his little wooden hut by the bridge with his yellow notebook in his fingers, and in the end the bus would move, or they would tow it into the ditch with the breakdown crane and put another man into the leather seat.

He stood up.

His right hip gave that dry, familiar pop—the sound of an old bronze bush that had lost its oil thirty thousand miles ago and was running dry on the steel shaft.

He walked to the window.

The lights of *The Spinnaker Reach* were burning into the white sky. 

On the ninth floor, behind the glass that ran from the ceiling to the floor, he could see a single yellow square where the electricians were testing the circuits. It looked very high. It looked as if it were floating in the drizzle, two hundred feet above the mud, disconnected from the earth, from the stone, from the seventy-two steps and the four-inch pine doors.

A man could live up there for twenty years and never hear an iron horseshoe tap against a panel. 

A man could die up there, and the caretakers with the encrypted fobs wouldn't know he was gone until the computer in the basement showed that the water hadn't been drawn from the cistern for seventy-two hours.

He looked down at the dark mass of St. Jude’s Terrace below him.

It looked small. 

From above, it looked like an old coal-lighter that had been abandoned on the bank when the tide went out, its ribs showing through the mud, waiting for the winter gales to break its back and scatter its planks along the reach.

He reached down into his pocket, took out his brass key, and laid it on the table beside the green paper from nineteen eighty-four.

Then he went into the scullery, took down his oilskin coat from behind the door—the one that had hung there since the gale of eighty-seven—and put his arms into the stiff, cold sleeves.

It smelled of linseed oil, salt, and thirty years of coal-smoke that had never quite left the canvas.

He did not strike a match. 

He walked down the narrow passage, his boots making no sound on the rag-rugs he had made from his old corporation trousers, reached the door, turned the brass thumb-turn, and stepped out onto the landing.

The latch dropped behind him with that soft, oiled click:

*Thluck.*

He stood in the dark well of the stairs for thirty seconds, his hand on the cold iron rail, listening.

From the third floor: Syd Corcoran’s three-beat cough—*huck, huck, hrr-ack.*

From the second floor: George Beale’s chalk on the wood—*scritch... scritch.*

From the first floor: Tara’s voice, very low now, almost lost in the draft from the street-door:

*The harbor-light is burning clear...*  
*And there’s no wind...*

Arthur Cleary pulled his cap down over his eyes, turned his face to the dark wind that was coming up the stone winders from the river, and began the seventy-two steps down to the street.

### Chapter 10

The lane at midnight was the color of a wet slate that had been rubbed with tallow.

Arthur did not take the perimeter road. He took the old track behind the coaling-stages—the one that ran between the back wall of the marine iron-works and the mud-gut where the lighters used to tie up during the spring freshets. 

The rails were still there under the nettles and the sea-aster: heavy, sixty-pound bridge-rails spiked down into half-round larch sleepers that had rotted into black sponge where the dogs had run along them for thirty years. Every fifty yards, his boot-toe caught on a rusted dog-spike or a piece of wire-rope that had worked its way out of the mud like an iron worm, but his knees knew the spacing. 

Three paces to the rail-joint; four paces to the switch-lever; six paces across the stone culvert where the warm water from the condenser-ponds used to steam into the ditch, smelling of boiled cabbage and carbolic.

The iron-works had been empty since seventy-six. 

Its roof was gone, the long, iron trusses standing against the white clouds like the ribs of a dead whale that had drifted ashore on the sand-spits and been picked clean by the gulls. Inside the perimeter wall, where thirty steam-hammers had once shaken the tea-mugs on the dressers of St. Jude’s Terrace every morning at six, there was only a forest of elder-bushes, four feet of yellow river-silt, and forty thousand rusted tin cans that had been dumped over the masonry by the boys from the council estate behind the gas-works.

At the corner of the dry dock, where the wall turned toward the river, he stopped.

There was a light burning in the watchman’s hut by the old caisson.

It was not an electric bulb; it was an old paraffin hurricane-lamp with a cracked globe that had been mended with two turns of copper wire, hanging from a rusted beam above an iron stove that had a stove-pipe sticking out through a sheet of corrugated zinc.

Inside sat Ephraim Kalu.

He was not asleep. He was sitting on a three-legged stool with his bridge-coat buttoned to his chin, his woollen scarf wrapped twice around his neck, holding his brass compass in his right hand and an old iron marlinspike in his left. 

On the rough deal plank that served as a table lay four pieces of white chalk, three rusted ship-bolts, and an old enamel plate containing the cold backbone of a salted herring with the tail still on it.

Arthur did not knock. There was no door to knock on; the hut was only three sides of matchboarding and a sheet of tarred felt that flapped in the river-wind with a dry, slapping sound: *flap... flap... flap.*

He stepped inside, his oilskin coat brushing against the post.

Kalu did not look up. He moved the marlinspike an inch to the left, using the point to draw a straight line through the grease on the table toward the herring bone.

"The tide is at the full," Kalu said. His voice was low, hollow, coming from deep inside the bridge-coat like the sound of water moving in an empty water-tank. "In twenty minutes, it will turn. If you are going out onto the training-wall, Arthur, you have five minutes before the wash from the ferry comes over the lower stones."

Arthur took off his cap and shook the drizzle from the brim onto the cold iron of the stove.

"I’m not going on the wall," Arthur said.

Kalu looked up then. 

His yellow eyes caught the orange light from the paraffin wick, reflecting two tiny, round flames that didn't move when he blinked.

"The young man came back?"

"No," Arthur said. "He won't come back. He’s in Lancaster by now, eating his dinner in an inn with white cloths on the tables."

Kalu picked up the marlinspike and balanced it across his two forefingers. The iron was pitted with rust, dark red and rough as shark-skin.

"You have given him the paper?"

"No," Arthur said.

Kalu watched the spike. It tipped slightly to the left, the heavy point sinking toward the grease, then he adjusted his knuckle by a sixteenth of an inch and brought it back to the level.

"Then you are going to the court?"

"I don't know," Arthur said. 

He sat down on an overturned grease-drum by the stove. The drum was cold, but it smelled of tallow and mineral oil—that old, heavy, honest smell that had lived in the pits under the buses, the smell of things that had to be kept from grinding together until the heat took the temper out of the steel.

"Syd’s thrown his propeller in the gut," Arthur said.

"Syd is an angry man," Kalu said softly. "When a man has no breath in his lungs, Arthur, he has only his anger to keep his blood warm. If you take his anger away from him, he is just an old coat on a peg that someone has forgotten to give to the mission."

"He told me I was a fool," Arthur said.

"You are," Kalu said. 

He laid the marlinspike down on the deal plank, picked up his pocket-compass, and opened the brass lid with that sharp, familiar *click*.

"The world does not want men who remember the sound of doors, Mr. Cleary. The world wants men who have numbers in their pockets and key-fobs that do not rattle when they walk. A door that speaks is an imperfect door. It means the joiner was drunk, or the timber was green, or the foundation has settled into the silt by three inches on the eastern corner."

He held the compass out toward the stove. 

The needle swung three points toward the iron, hovered for five seconds over the rusted damper, and then settled toward the corner where the cold river-wind was coming through the felt.

"In ninety-four," Kalu said, "they pulled down the mission-hall at the head of the basin. You remember the mission-hall, Arthur? With the two plaster angels over the porch that had lost their noses from the smoke?"

"Aye," Arthur said. "My brother was christened there."

"They had seven hundred names written in the communion-book," Kalu said. "Every man who had drowned between the Dudgeon and the Isle of Man since eighteen sixty-two. When the contractors came with the yellow crane, the parson—that young man from Cambridge with the glasses who had the bicycle with the basket—carried the book down to the town hall in a green canvas bag. He told the clerk: *This is the register of the parish. It must be kept in the strong-room under the library.*"

Kalu smiled, his long teeth shining in the orange wick-light.

"And where is it now?" Arthur asked.

"In ninety-eight," Kalu said, "the basement of the library flooded when the storm-sewer broke behind the market. The clerk told the committee that the paper had turned to brown mush and was producing an offensive odor that was distressing the young ladies in the lending section. So they put it into four plastic sacks and took it to the municipal tip behind the gas-works."

He closed the compass with a snap.

"The names were already gone, Arthur. The men were drowned in seventy-two, or eighty-four, or ninety-one. The book was only the grease that their widows had left on the pages when they turned them with their thumbs on Sunday mornings. Why keep the grease when the widows are under the sod and the ships are at the bottom of the channel?"

Arthur looked at his own boots. 

There was a smear of white stone-dust on the left toe, where he had walked past the drills at noon. It was already beginning to wash away in the drizzle, turning into a thin, milky paste that ran into the welt.

"I can't go into that tower, Ephraim," he said.

His voice wasn't loud, but it had that dry, hard grain in it that came from sixty years of speaking against the roar of the diesel exhaust through an open cab-window.

"Why not?" Kalu asked. "It is warm. Your legs are bad. If you stay in St. Jude’s, who will carry your coal when Syd is dead and Janice is in Millom?"

"I don't know," Arthur said.

"Then why?"

Arthur stood up. 

He walked to the opening of the hut and looked out into the basin.

The tide had reached the turn. 

For three minutes, the water did not move. It lay between the granite walls like a sheet of black glass, thirty feet deep, reflecting the white needle of *The Spinnaker Reach* and the dark, square bulk of the coaling-staithes. 

Then, two miles out in the estuary, the bell-buoy on the spit gave a single, hollow *tonk*.

The water began to move.

It didn't rush; it slid. A slow, silent drainage that drew the black surface toward the sea, carrying with it three plastic bottles, a baulk of timber with two rusted iron bolts sticking out of the end, and a thick skin of yellow foam from the chemical plant up the reach.

"Because if I go in there," Arthur said, "I’m the one who shut the door."

He did not look back at Kalu. He looked at the water.

"If they put me out with a court paper," Arthur said, "it’s their paper. It’s their judge, it’s their bailiff, it’s their yellow crane with the ball on the wire. I can sit on the curb with my box and my kettle, and I’m still Artie Cleary who drove the No. 7 and kept his grease-gun in his pocket."

He turned back to the hut, his hands deep in the pockets of his oilskin.

"But if I take the key from that lad with the clean neck," he said, "I’ve sold the landing. I’ve taken two bedrooms and an electric heater to let him have the stairs without a fight. I’d be living nine storeys up, looking down at the hole where George’s chalk was, knowing I took the warm floor while they were sleeping in an onion-loft in Toxteth or a wash-house in Millom with the hens in the rain."

Kalu looked at him for a long time. 

The wick in the hurricane-lamp gave a small, spitting pop as a drop of water fell through the chimney onto the glass, and the smell of hot kerosene filled the little space between the matchboards.

"You are an old fool, Arthur Cleary," Kalu said again.

His voice was very soft this time—so soft that it seemed to come from the water rather than the man.

"Aye," Arthur said. "I know."

Kalu reached into his bridge-coat, pulled out the pocket-compass, and laid it on the deal plank beside the herring-bone.

"Take it," Kalu said.

Arthur looked at the brass case. 

"I don't need a compass, Ephraim. I know which way the street runs."

"Take it," Kalu repeated, pushing it forward two inches with his thumb. "The glass is good. It was made in Glasgow in eighteen eighty-four by a man named James White who made the instruments for Lord Kelvin. It does not know where the towers are, Arthur. It does not know about the Leeds developers or the sixty-pound rails. It only knows where the cold ground is."

Arthur hesitated. 

Then he reached out his hand, picked up the round brass case, and slipped it into his waistcoat pocket, right beside the green paper from nineteen eighty-four.

It was heavy. It had the weight that the silver pen had lacked—a cool, solid presence that pressed against his ribs through the wool and the canvas like an old friend’s hand resting on your shoulder while you waited for the signal to drop.

"I’ll see you on the pier tomorrow, Ephraim?"

Kalu looked down at his table. 

He picked up the iron marlinspike, turned it once in his fingers, and began to scrape the grease away from the deal plank with the point.

*Scritch... scritch.*

"If the tide is right, Arthur," Kalu said. "If the tide is right."

Arthur stepped out of the hut into the rain.

### Chapter 11

By four o’clock on Friday morning, the frost had arrived.

It was not a clean, white country frost that sparkles on the hedgerows like sugar; it was the black, greasy rime of the estuary, that coats the stone-mastic of the roads with a skin of ice as slick as lard and turns the iron handrails into bars of frozen lead that stick to the skin of your palm if you touch them without a mitten.

Arthur woke at half-past four. 

His ankle didn't throb today; it had gone stiff, the joint locked into a right-angle that refused to bend until he had sat on the edge of the horsehair mattress for ten minutes, working the tendon with his thumbs until the blood began to move.

He did not strike a match. 

He didn't need the gas-ring. He had four inches of cold tea left in the earthenware pot from yesterday dinner, and he drank it standing by the window, watching the lights of the first shift down at the terminal.

The first door was late.

Flat 1B didn't open until twenty to six. 

When it did, there was no double-stage snap of the brass Yale. 

There was a heavy, wooden crash as the door was kicked open from the inside, followed by the sound of Marek swearing in Polish—short, sharp, barking oaths that bounced off the quarry tiles of the well like stones dropped down a dry pipe. 

Then the sound of two men carrying something heavy and awkward down the winders: the rhythmic, scraping *thud-drag... thud-drag* of a double-sprung mattress wrapped in plastic sheeting, hitting the lime-mortar on the corners and taking the old paint off in flakes as large as dinner-plates.

Arthur walked out onto the landing.

He didn't put on his slippers; he went in his woolen stocking-feet, making no sound on the cold stone.

He looked over the iron balustrade into the well.

The ground floor was full of yellow light from an open van parked on the cobbles outside the dolphin knocker. 

Two young men with shaved necks, wearing dark hooded jackets with the logos of a removals contractor from Preston on the sleeves, were hauling Marek’s chest of drawers across the pavement. 

Behind them stood Danuta, Marek’s wife. 

She was twenty-eight, but this morning she looked fifty. She was wearing an old sheepskin coat that had lost half its fleece at the elbows, holding the baby against her shoulder with her left arm while her right hand dragged a red nylon laundry-bag stuffed with children’s shoes and plastic toys across the wet flagstones.

Marek was standing by the street-door. 

He had his work-boots on, but he had no jacket, only a gray sleeveless vest that showed the blue anchor tattooed on the inside of his right forearm. He was holding an iron crowbar—a two-foot pinch-bar with a curved chisel-end—and he was levering the brass security-hasp off the doorframe of 1B.

Every time the steel bit into the pine, the wood screamed: *cr-r-rack.*

The dry, eighty-year-old timber splintered into long, yellow needles that fell onto the tiles like matchsticks. 

When the screws gave way, he didn't put them into his pocket; he threw them across the hall with a flick of his wrist. They hit the cast-iron umbrella-stand by Mrs. Corley’s door with three sharp, ringing *pings* that sounded like bullets hitting a boiler.

"Marek," Arthur called softly down the well.

His voice didn't carry much weight, but in the stone cylinder of the stairs it rolled down the winders like a marble in a groove.

Marek stopped. 

He did not look up immediately. He stood with the crowbar in his right hand, his chest heaving under the gray cotton, his breath coming in white, steaming puffs that drifted up toward the first landing.

Then he lifted his head.

His face was gray in the LED light from the street, with that dark, hollow stubble that comes to men who have worked thirty hours out of thirty-six in the rain.

"Mr. Cleary," he said. His English was good, but it was stiff, learned from the safety manuals at the container base and the television in the canteen. "You are awake early."

"You’re going, then?" Arthur asked.

Marek looked down at the crowbar in his hand. He turned it once, feeling the weight of the steel.

"The company has found two rooms in an old school in Kirkby," he said. "Behind the railway line. The manager says if we are not there by eight o’clock, he gives the room to two men from the fertilizer boat."

He spat on the tiles—a small, dark drop that landed right beside the splintered pine from his doorframe.

"They gave us six hundred pounds," Marek said. "The man with the clean neck. He came to the gantry yesterday at four o’clock. He had the money in an envelope with my name on the front. He said if I took the van this morning, he would not ask for the two weeks’ rent for the notice."

He looked up at Arthur again, his eyes small, dark, hard as two pieces of coke from the bottom of a bunker.

"You have the big flat, Mr. Cleary?"

Arthur gripped the cold iron rail with both hands. 

The rust got under his fingernails, sharp and gritty.

"No," Arthur said. "I haven't got it."

Marek didn't smile. He didn't nod. He just looked at Arthur for three seconds, his face completely blank, the face of a man who had seen too many papers with official stamps to believe that anyone ever refused a key if there was an electric fire behind it.

"He told my wife you had the key," Marek said. "He told her you were a friend of the director."

"He lied, lad," Arthur said.

Marek turned the crowbar again. 

Then he shrugged—a slow, heavy movement of his thick shoulders that seemed to take all the strength out of his back.

"It is no matter," Marek said. "The house is rotten anyway. The water in the kitchen smells like an old shoe. In Poland, my grandfather had a barn made of spruce logs that was better than this place. In the winter, the snow stayed on the roof, but inside the cows were warm and the milk didn't freeze in the bucket."

He turned his back on the well and walked out through the dolphin door into the lane.

The van engine started. 

It was an old, four-cylinder diesel with a loose big-end bearing that hammered against the crankcase with a heavy, hollow knock: *clack-clack-clack-clack.*

The blue smoke came in through the open door, thick and sour, filling the ground-floor hallway until Mrs. Corley’s iron rim-lock was lost in the fog.

Arthur stood on the landing until the van had backed out into the perimeter road.

He listened to the sound of the tires on the asphalt—that long, tearing hiss that grew fainter and fainter as the driver took the third exit toward the motorway.

Then the lane was quiet again.

Except that on the ground floor, the door of 1B was hanging wide on its warped hinges, showing the empty scullery, the bare floorboards with the square grease-mark where the cooker had been, and a single piece of yellow plastic clothesline hanging from an iron hook in the center of the ceiling.

### Chapter 12

At nine o’clock, the second removal arrived.

It was not a contractor’s van from Preston. It was an old three-ton Bedford lorry with a wooden flatbed, its cab painted the color of a bruised plum, with the words *J. H. Braithwaite & Sons, General Haulage, Millom* painted on the doors in yellow letters that had been touched up with an ordinary household brush.

The driver was a boy of nineteen with big, red wrists that stuck four inches out of the sleeves of his denim jacket. 

He didn't have any bubble-wrap, and he didn't have any wardrobe cartons. He had six greasy horse-blankets, three lengths of sisal rope that had been knotted together where they had broken under the strain of a hay-load, and a wooden tail-board that was held in place by two bent iron bolts.

Arthur went down to the second landing to help.

He didn't ask Janice. He just took off his cardigan, hung it on the knob of his own door, and walked into Flat 3B with his shirt-sleeves rolled to the elbow.

The room was nearly empty now.

The three banana-boxes were already sitting on the landing. 

On the horsehair settee sat George Beale, wearing his Melton coat and his flat cap, his two hands resting on his knees, his eyes fixed on the small square of linoleum between his boots where the pattern had been worn down to the brown jute base.

"George," Arthur said.

George didn't move.

"George, lad," Arthur said, louder this time, putting his hand on the rough wool of the old man’s shoulder. "We have to lift the settee."

George looked up slowly. 

His eyes were very clear today—not watery, but that pale, milky blue of the estuary when the frost is on the sand.

"Artie," he said. His voice was thin, but it had no shake in it. "The *Dido*’s in the basin."

"No, George," Arthur said gently. "The *Dido* was broken up at Faslane in fifty-eight. You remember, lad. We went up on the excursion train to see the breakers take the turrets off."

George looked at him for five seconds. 

A small, faint frown appeared between his white eyebrows, then cleared away like a breath off a razor.

"She was a good ship, Artie," George said. "Six-inch guns. Three boilers to a room. We laid her keelson down in thirty-nine, when the frost was so hard the chalk froze to the floorboards. We had to keep the chalk in our mouths to keep it soft enough to strike the line."

He reached into his pocket, took out a piece of white chalk no bigger than a thumbnail, and held it out to Arthur on his palm.

"Take it, Artie," George said. "I haven't got the room for it in the lorry. The boy says the tail-board won't shut if we have more than three boxes behind the cab."

Arthur looked at the chalk. 

It was yellowed around the edge from the tobacco in George’s waistcoat, and the end was shaped into a blunt chisel where it had rubbed against forty years of corduroy.

Arthur took it. 

He didn't put it in his pocket; he held it in his left palm, closing his fingers over it until the dry powder came off against his lifeline.

"Come on, George," Arthur said. "Let’s get you down the steps."

It took twenty minutes to get George down the seventy-two winders.

His knees were stiff, and he had forgotten how to turn his feet on the narrow ends of the treads. Every four steps he stopped, his left hand gripping the iron balustrade until his knuckles were white as lard, his right hand reaching out into the empty air of the well as if he were looking for a bulkhead in the dark.

Janice went before him, carrying the brown teapot and a plastic carrier-bag containing two loaves of white bread and six tins of sardines. 

She didn't speak. She didn't look back. She just moved down the winders one step at a time, her bad veins standing out on her calves like bunches of blue grapes under the brown nylon stockings.

When they reached the street-door, the boy from Millom was standing on the tail-board, hauling the horsehair settee up by its front legs with a rope around his middle.

The wood groaned. 

The back leg of the settee—the one that had been mended with an iron plate after the fire of ninety-eight—caught on the iron corner-iron of the lorry. There was a sharp, tearing crack as the old horsehair burst through the hessian backing, scattering forty years of gray dust and dry moth-balls across the wet cobbles.

"Mind it, lad!" Arthur shouted, his voice cracking with the strain of holding George’s elbow against the wind. "Mind the frame! It’s mahogany under the paint!"

The boy didn't look down. He gave another heave with his red wrists, his boots slipping on the wet larch planks of the bed, and the settee fell into the lorry with a heavy, hollow *whump* that shook the cab.

"That’s it," the boy said, wiping his nose with the back of his sleeve. "Get the old fella in the front. My uncle says we have to be over the bridge at Greenodd before the tide comes over the causeway at twelve."

Arthur helped George into the cab.

The seat was torn, the yellow foam-rubber sticking out through the gray vinyl like dry bread-crusts, and the floor was littered with rusted spanners, empty plastic oil-bottles, and old copies of the *Farmer’s Guardian*.

George sat by the door. 

He didn't look at the terrace. He didn't look at the dolphin knocker or the window of his own room on the second floor where the lace curtain was still hanging from two brass tacks. 

He looked at the dashboard. 

He reached out his right hand, found the round, plastic knob of the heater control, and began to turn it slowly with his thumb: *click... click... click.*

Janice climbed in beside him, pulling the door shut with both hands.

The lock didn't catch on the first slam. She had to hit it three times with her hip before the rusted bolt clicked home into the pillar.

She looked down at Arthur through the greasy glass of the window.

She didn't wave. She just pressed her flat palm against the pane for two seconds—the same gesture Arthur had seen Tara use on the hollow-core door on the first floor—then the boy let the clutch out with a jerk, the big Bedford diesel roared, and the lorry lurched forward into the lane, its wooden tail-board banging against the frame: *clatter-bang... clatter-bang.*

Arthur stood on the pavement.

He held the piece of chalk in his left hand and Kalu’s compass in his right.

Behind him, the dolphin door stood wide open to the frost.

From the hallway came the smell of cold quarry tiles, dry pine-splinters, and the blue diesel smoke that hadn't yet cleared from the corners of the ceiling.

And then, from the top of the well—four storeys up, through the open winders—came the sound of Syd Corcoran’s door.

*Clack.*

Then the cough. 

*Huck... huck... hrr-ack.*

Slower this time. 

Much slower. 

There was no spit at the end of it; there was only a long, rattling whistle that went on for four seconds, five seconds, six seconds, like the sound of an old boiler when the safety-valve has stuck on its seat and the steam has to find its way out through the rusted rivets around the fire-door.

Arthur turned his back on the empty lane and began the climb back up the stone.

### Chapter 13

By Saturday night, the silence had gotten into the walls.

A tenement is never truly quiet while people are breathing behind the lath-and-plaster. 

Even if they don't speak, even if they don't drop their boots or rattle the grate with the poker, there is a warmth that comes through the twelve-inch brickwork—a slow, living vibration that keeps the lime-mortar from freezing and makes the gas-brackets hum with a tiny, dry note when the pressure rises in the street-mains after the shops shut.

Now, St. Jude’s Terrace had the cold of an empty church.

The frost had worked its way up the stairwell from the open street-door, coating the iron spindles of the balustrade with a white, furry fur that came off on your fingers like salt-dust. 

On the ground floor, the door of 1B was still hanging wide, the black gap of the doorway looking like an empty eye-socket in a skull. 

On the second floor, the Beale door was shut, but Janice had taken the iron horseshoe with her in her apron pocket, and the wooden latch hung down through the gimlet-hole like a dead tongue, never moving even when the draft came up the well from the basin.

Only Arthur and Syd remained.

At eight o’clock, Arthur took a bowl of potato-soup up to the third floor.

He had made it on his single gas-ring—four small potatoes he had bought from the mission cart on Tuesday, half an onion that had gone soft at the root, and two spoons of lard from the grease-crock behind the kettle. 

He didn't have a tray; he carried the earthenware bowl in both hands, his thumbs wrapped in a clean tea-towel to keep the heat from his palms, his stocking-feet finding the centers of the winders in the dark.

Syd’s door was not shut. 

It was pushed to, but the broken upper hinge had dropped another half-inch, so that the top rail of the door was wedged tight against the plaster of the head-casing, leaving a triangular gap three inches wide at the bottom through which the blue smoke from the paraffin heater was drifting into the stairwell.

Arthur pushed it with his elbow.

The door groaned, its bottom rail dragging across the bare floorboards with a dry, screaming screech that sounded like an iron shovel being pushed through slag.

"Syd?"

The room was nine feet by twelve.

It had no wallpaper; the plaster had been painted with two coats of distemper the color of wet oatmeal back in the fifties, and where the rain had come through the roof-valleys behind the parapet, long, brown maps of water-stains ran from the cornice down to the skirting-boards like dirty rivers on a school-slate.

In the corner, on an iron bedstead with two missing castors that had been leveled up with two flat pieces of Yorkshire stone, lay Syd Corcoran.

He had three overcoats piled on top of his gray army blanket: his own donkey-jacket with the orange plastic patches on the shoulders; an old naval bridge-coat that had lost its lining; and a woman’s brown tweed coat with a rabbit-fur collar that had belonged to his sister before she went to the sanatorium in sixty-four.

He was not lying flat. 

He was propped up on four dirty pillows stuffed with wood-shavings that crackled every time his head moved, his chest bare under an unbuttoned flannel shirt that was gray with sweat around the armpits.

His skin was not white; it was the color of a wet turnip, with that faint, purplish-blue shadow under the cheekbones and around the mouth that means the heart has begun to pump the blood through the veins without waiting for the lungs to clean it.

Beside the bed, on an upturned wooden crate that had once held twelve tins of *Nestlé’s Condensed Milk*, sat the hurricane-lamp, its wick turned down to a blue bead no larger than a pea.

Around the lamp lay the pieces of the model cruiser *Penelope*.

They were not assembled. 

Syd had taken the hull out of the cardboard box—sixteen inches of gray polystyrene with the portholes molded into the plastic—and laid it across the wood. 

Beside it lay the three twin-gun turrets, their tiny plastic barrels pointing up at the ceiling like six white pins, and a small glass tube of polystyrene cement that had dried into a hard, clear bead at the nozzle.

"Syd," Arthur said. He set the bowl of soup down on the edge of the crate, moving the plastic turrets two inches to the left with his knuckle to make room for the hot earthenware. "I’ve brought you some broth."

Syd’s eyes opened.

They were very large, very dark, the pupils dilated until there was almost no brown left around them. 

He looked at Arthur, then at the steam rising from the bowl, then at the little gray cruiser on the wood.

"Artie," he whispered.

The word had no voice behind it; it was just a breath, dry and hot as the air from a bakehouse flue.

"Eat some soup, lad," Arthur said, sitting down on the edge of the iron bed. 

The springs gave a long, rusted creak under his weight: *scre-e-k.*

"Can't swallow," Syd said. 

He lifted his right hand two inches from the blanket. His fingers were swollen at the joints, the nails bluish-black, looking like five small mussels clinging to a wet rock.

"The water’s in the throat, Artie. It’s right up to the collar-bone. If I put the broth down, it’ll just come back up through the nose."

Arthur looked at the corner of the room.

The water-stain behind the bed was dark, shiny with damp that had frozen into a thin sheet of ice near the skirting-board.

"You should be in the infirmary, Syd," Arthur said softly. "I can go down to the police box by the bridge. They’ve got the telephone. The ambulance’ll be here in twenty minutes."

Syd’s head moved half an inch from side to side on the wood-shavings.

"No," he whispered.

"Syd, you can't stay up here with the frost. The window’s cracked in the corner."

"No ambulances," Syd said. 

A tiny bubble of white foam appeared at the corner of his mouth, stayed there for three seconds, and then burst with a faint, wet click.

"They put you in them rubber sheets, Artie. They take your trousers away and put 'em in a canvas sack with a number on the tag. And then you’re just an old fella in a bed with six women from the parish coming round with orange juice in plastic cups."

He closed his eyes.

His breath was coming faster now—short, shallow gasps that moved his ribs under the flannel shirt like the gills of a cod that had been left on the pier-coping in the sun: *huff... huff... huff.*

"You seen that lad?" Syd whispered.

"Croft?"

"Aye."

"No," Arthur said. "He won't be back until Monday."

"He was here," Syd said. 

His eyes didn't open, but his right hand moved across the blanket until his fingers touched the gray polystyrene hull of the cruiser.

"He stood by the door, Artie. At three o’clock. He had a big coat on. A coat made of black fur, like an officer on an ice-boat. He had a gold pen in his hand, and he said: *Mr. Corcoran, your name’s on the book. We’ve got the berth ready for you down at the dock.*"

"He was lying, Syd," Arthur said. "You were dreaming with the fever."

"No," Syd whispered. "He wasn't lying. I saw the ship, Artie. She was five hundred feet long, all painted white, with three yellow funnels and the steam coming out of the escape-pipes like flour. She had her lines out to the big bollards on the Bell-Pier, and the band on the promenade was playing that tune about the girl with the red ribbons in her shoes."

He stopped. 

His hand slipped off the plastic hull, his fingers curling inward until his knuckles touched his palm.

"Artie?"

"Aye, Syd. I’m here."

"What does it sound like?"

"What’s that, lad?"

"The door," Syd whispered, his voice so thin now that Arthur had to lean his forehead down until his cap brushed the old man’s gray hair to hear the words through the whistle of his breath. "The door in the tower. The one that doesn't make a sound."

Arthur looked at the little glass tube of glue on the crate.

He thought of the photograph Croft had laid on his table: the birch floor, the white walls, the little gray slate tile with *21.5°* glowing green in the corner.

"It sounds like nothing, Syd," Arthur said.

His voice was very steady, very quiet, the tone he had used when he had to tell the old women on the No. 7 bus that the bridge was up and they would have to wait twenty minutes in the rain before the boat cleared the gut.

"It’s made of composite wood," Arthur said. "It’s got three rubber seals around the edge, and two brass springs inside the casing that pull it shut with five pounds of pressure. When it meets the timber, the latch doesn't hit the brass; it goes into a pocket of neoprene foam that takes the knock out of the steel."

Syd’s breath hitched once: *huck.*

"No iron?" he whispered.

"No iron," Arthur said. "No hinges with the pins loose. No draft-felt dragging on the tiles."

Syd’s lips curved slightly at the corners—a tiny, pale shadow of that old, wicked grin he used to have when he had dropped a six-inch staging plank into forty feet of dock-water and the foreman had to spend three hours with the grapple-irons trying to fish it off the bottom before the superintendent arrived.

"Sounds bloody dull, Artie," Syd whispered.

Then he took a breath.

It was not a short breath. It was a long, deep, rattling inhalation that drew the air from every corner of the nine-by-twelve room—the cold air, the paraffin smoke, the smell of the potato-soup, and the salt-damp from the cracked window.

His chest rose three inches under the flannel shirt.

The three overcoats lifted.

Then he let it go.

### Chapter 16

The Hindpool Road depot had been built in 1898 to house ninety horses and forty-two horse-drawn omnibuses of the Barrow & District Tramways Company.

A gale in the spring of twenty-twenty-two had stripped thirty sheets of iron from the northern bay, and the rain had come down through the purlins ever since, turning the wooden floor-blocks into a black, greasy bog where dock-weeds and willow-herb were growing four feet high around the inspection pits.

"He told me to take the train to Morecambe," she said. "He said if I was on the two-fifteen from the station, he’d pay the first month’s rent on the room in the boarding-house. He said he had an arrangement with the landlord."

Arthur looked at the blue paper.

"What did he want for it, Tara?"

"He had a white paper," she said. "A little one. Said I had to sign it to say I had vacated St. Jude’s Terrace of my own free will, without any... what did he call it? Any coercion from the developers."

"Did you sign it, lass?"

Tara looked at the boy. 

Dylan was turning the steering-wheel with both hands, his little face purple with the effort of moving twenty-two inches of cold vulcanite against the seized King-pins below.

"I had to, Arthur," she whispered. "I hadn't got the four pound eighty for the train. I had one and fourpence in my tin, and Dylan hadn't had his dinner since yesterday morning."

Arthur stood up from the driver’s seat.

His municipal tunic was stiff across the shoulder-blades, the white plastic piping on the lapels cracking where the cold had gotten into the vinyl.

He walked down the three steps to the platform, reached down into his milk-crate, and took out his brass pocket-compass.

He didn't open the lid. He held the round, heavy case in his right hand, feeling the balance of the Glasgow brass against his knuckles.

"Come here, Dylan," Arthur said.

The boy stopped turning the wheel. He looked down at the old man with his round, dark eyes.

"What’s that?"

"It’s a compass, lad," Arthur said, stepping back up into the gangway. He pressed the little button on the pendant, and the glass face popped open with that sharp, singing *click*. "It belonged to an engineer from Africa. A man who measured forty miles of iron in his life and never lost a link."

The boy reached out two dirty fingers and touched the needle.

The steel swung three points, danced over the fleur-de-lis, and then pointed straight through the windscreen of Bus No. 142 toward the dark brick of the smithy wall.

"Is it gold?" Dylan asked.

"It’s brass, lad," Arthur said. "Brass doesn't turn into rust when the salt gets on it. You keep it in your pocket. And when you’re in that room in Morecambe, you look at the needle every morning before the sun comes up. If it points toward the water, you know where the town is."

He slipped the heavy brass case into the boy’s nylon pocket, right beside the paperclip on the zip.

The pocket sagged three inches under the weight, but Dylan pressed his hand over the bulge with a look of fierce, territorial pride that made his little jaw set like an old line-handler’s on a wet slip.

"Now," Arthur said, turning to Tara. "Get your coat buttoned, lass."

"Where are we going, Arthur?"

Arthur picked up his milk-crate with his left arm.

"We’re going to find a solicitor," Arthur said.

### Chapter 18

The office of *Gartside, Broadbent & Vance, Commissioners for Oaths*, was situated over an empty grain-merchant’s shop in the Old Market Place, three hundred yards behind the Custom House.

It had not been painted since nineteen seventy-one. 

The stairs were covered with brown linoleum that had worn through to the gray hessian backing at every bull-nose, held down by strips of zinc beading that were missing half their brass screws. 

On the first-floor landing, the smell of damp parchment, cold pipe-smoke, and dry tea-leaves was so strong it reminded Arthur of the parcel-office on a Saturday evening when the pigeon-baskets were waiting for the Crewe train.

A frosted-glass door bore the name:

*Mr. Hector Vance, LL.B.*  
*Notary Public*  
*(Walk In)*

Arthur pushed the door. 

It was the eighth door he had opened that day, and it had its own distinct voice: a long, hollow wheeze from an old pneumatic cylinder at the top that had lost its fluid in nineteen eighty-two, followed by the dry, metallic rattle of three copper latches falling into their brass cups: *snick-tock-click.*

Behind a mahogany counter that had three ink-stains the size of saucers set into the wood sat a woman of sixty-five with red hair that had been dyed the color of a new brick, wearing an electric-blue cardigan with plastic pearl buttons.

She was typing on an old Adler manual machine with two fingers, the steel type-bars hitting the ribbon with a furious, dry clatter that sounded like a machine-gun firing through a woollen blanket: *clack-clack-clack-ping.*

She did not stop typing when the door wheezed. She finished the line, threw the carriage with a sharp slap of her left palm—*sh-h-h-whack*—and then looked over her half-moon spectacles at Arthur’s municipal tunic.

"We don't take the parcel-deliveries here," she said, her voice sounding like two slates being rubbed together in an empty cellar. "The parcels go to the station master."

"I haven't got a parcel, Miss," Arthur said. He stayed by the counter, holding his milk-crate with both hands. "I want to see Mr. Vance."

"Mr. Vance is retired," she said, her fingers already hovering over the *A* and the *S* keys. "He has been retired since Michaelmas nineteen ninety-eight. He lives in Grange-over-Sands and only comes in on Thursdays for the probate signatures."

"Then who’s that behind the glass?" Arthur asked, nodding toward an inner door that stood open two inches, through which could be seen a green-shaded desk-lamp and a pair of feet in brown brogues resting on the bottom drawer of an oak filing-cabinet.

The woman sighed—a long, dramatic inhalation that caused her blue cardigan to rise three inches.

"That is Mr. Julian Vance," she said. "His nephew. He does the conveyancing for the small-holders and the repossession lists for the building society. But he doesn't see people without an appointment from the diary."

The inner door opened six inches further.

A man of thirty-five looked out. 

He was not like Croft. 

His suit was made of brown tweed that had seen twenty winters of damp mist on the fells, his collar was slightly frayed at the points, and his hair was that messy, sandy shock that belongs to men who spend four hours on a Saturday afternoon trying to get an old Ferguson tractor out of a bog with a rusty wire-rope.

He had a half-smoked pipe in his teeth, unlit, with a bowl carved into the shape of a bulldog’s head that had lost one of its glass eyes.

"What is it, Mrs. Braithwaite?" he asked, taking the pipe from his mouth.

"It’s an old person from the buses," she said, without looking up from her carriage. "With a box. And a girl from the fish-sheds."

Julian Vance looked at Arthur. 

His eyes were small, gray, surrounded by thousands of tiny wrinkles from squinting against the sea-wind on the salt-marshes. 

His gaze dropped from Arthur’s chrome badge—*Driver No. 142*—to the green paper with the blue wax seal that was sticking out of the corner of the milk-crate, between Sarah’s postcard and the enameled kettle.

He stopped. 

He took the pipe out of his mouth entirely, tapped the unlit ash into his palm, and walked to the counter.

"Is that a 1977 Act agreement?" Vance asked.

His voice had the broad, flat vowels of the Kent valley—the accent of men who knew the difference between an acre of moss-land and an acre of limestone-pavement without looking at the tithe-map.

"Fourteenth of October, nineteen eighty-four," Arthur said. "Barrow & District Steam Navigation Company Housing Trust. Registered with the rent officer on the twenty-first."

Vance reached across the mahogany counter.

His fingers were broad, hairy at the knuckles, with two brown stains from tobacco-juice on the second joint.

"Let me see it," he said.

Arthur did not give him the paper. He held the milk-crate tighter against his tunic.

"The girl signed a paper at one o’clock," Arthur said, nodding toward Tara, who was sitting on the wooden bench by the door, Dylan’s head resting against the blue nylon of her tabard. "In the cold-store. A lad gave her twenty pounds to go to Morecambe."

Vance looked past Arthur at the girl. 

He took in the blue tabard, the red hands, the four-year-old child with the paperclip on his zip, and the yellow slip from the housing department lying on her lap like a dead leaf.

"Croft?" Vance asked.

Arthur looked at him with a small, sharp twitch in his eyelid.

"You know him?"

Vance gave a short, dry bark of laughter that had no humor in it, only the sour disgust of an honest country attorney who has seen forty thousand acres of family farmland bought up by investment companies from the Channel Islands.

"Everyone in the district knows Julian Croft," Vance said. "He was at school with the clerk to the justices in Kendal. He has an office in Leeds with three secretaries who do nothing but download Land Registry titles and send Section 21 notices to people who can't read the small print on the back."

He lifted the flap of the counter.

"Come inside," Vance said. "Bring the girl. Mrs. Braithwaite, put the kettle on the gas. And don't use the chicory-essence; use the real tea from the tin behind the law reports."

Mrs. Braithwaite’s fingers stopped on the Adler keys. 

She looked at the ceiling as if she were waiting for an iron bowstring-truss to fall through the plaster, then reached into her handbag for the key to the gas-meter cupboard without saying a single word.

### Chapter 19

The room was twelve feet square, lined from floor to ceiling with black buckram-bound volumes of the *Chancery Reports* from eighteen seventy-two to nineteen fifty-five. 

On the desk, between a brass inkstand shaped like three hunting-dogs and a stack of yellow foolscap four inches thick, lay the paper Tara had signed in the cold-store.

It was not cream card; it was a single sheet of thin, white copy-paper, printed with that neat, slanted computer typography that Arthur had learned to hate more than the smell of dead haddock.

Vance read it twice, holding it close to his green lamp, his unlit bulldog pipe clamped between his yellow teeth.

"It’s an ex-gratia disclaimer," Vance said. He laid it down on the blotter and tapped it with the stem of his pipe. "She’s surrendered her statutory right to six months’ emergency review under the Homelessness Reduction Act in exchange for a discretionary relocation advance of twenty pounds."

He looked up at Tara.

"Did you take the twenty pounds, Mrs. Sullivan?"

"I had to," Tara said. Her voice was barely audible above the low, singing hum of the gas-bracket in the corner. "Dylan hadn't had his tea."

"Of course you did," Vance said, his voice surprisingly gentle. "That’s how they operate. They find a woman with five pounds in her purse and an ill-shod child, and they offer her the price of two bags of groceries and a single railway fare to get her off the borough register before the housing officer can log the possession warrant."

He reached out and picked up Arthur’s green document from nineteen eighty-four.

He did not read it quickly. 

He turned the leaves with the slow, reverent care of an archivist handling a fourteenth-century charter, his broad thumb testing the thickness of the paper and the impression of the blue wax seal where the ship’s wheel was stamped into the shellac.

"This is an Assured Tenancy under the seventy-seven Act," Vance said. "Schedule 15, Part One. It has statutory succession rights."

He looked at Arthur.

"You have no wife, Mr. Cleary?"

"She went in ninety-six," Arthur said.

"No children?"

"None."

"Then you are the sole surviving tenant of record for the entire curtilage of St. Jude’s Terrace," Vance said. "Under the sixty-nine lease, the common parts—the stairwell, the cellar-vaults, the water-risers, and the roof-parapets—are held in common trust by the tenants of the head-lease. If you have not executed a Deed of Surrender, Mr. Cleary, Vanguard Estuary Properties cannot legally sever the communal services or commence structural demolition without an order from the High Court."

"Croft said he had an interim order," Arthur said.

"Croft lied," Vance said, his pipe-stem clicking against his teeth. "Croft had a Certificate of Proposed Alternative Accommodation. That is an administrative filing, not a writ of execution. It allows him to apply for a hearing at the Preston County Court on the second Tuesday of next month."

He leaned back in his leather chair, the old mahogany frame creaking with that deep, comfortable groan that only furniture that had lived through four reigns could produce.

"They have tinned up the doors, Arthur," Vance said.

"I know," Arthur said. "I heard the saws from the slip."

"And they have Syd Corcoran in the morgue at the infirmary," Vance said. "The coroner’s officer called me at eleven to ask if I had the deed for the burial plot at St. Jude’s-on-the-Hill. Syd had no money, Arthur. He has three pounds twelve shillings in a post-office book that hasn't had an entry since nineteen ninety-four."

"I have forty pounds," Arthur said, reaching for his tunic pocket again.

"Keep it," Vance said. 

He stood up, walked to the tall window that looked down into the Old Market Place, and pulled the brown linen blind aside with two fingers.

Down on the cobbles, parked outside the butcher’s shop, was Croft’s blue car.

It was not an old car; it was a long, low, silent machine made of dark blue steel that looked like a drop of cold ink against the wet gray flags of the market. 

Inside the car, the small dashboard screens were glowing with yellow and red maps, and through the tinted glass, Arthur could see the shape of Croft’s head, his phone still pressed to his ear, his face turned up toward the first-floor windows of *Gartside, Broadbent & Vance*.

"He followed you," Vance said.

"He wants the green paper," Arthur said.

"He doesn't want the paper," Vance said, turning back to the room. "He wants your signature on the surrender. Because if he doesn't have your signature by four o’clock this afternoon, the facility loan from the bank in Frankfurt terminates under the default clause, and Vanguard Estuary has to pay sixty thousand pounds in demurrage fees to the piling contractors who are sitting on their barges in the basin."

He walked back to his desk, picked up the white paper Tara had signed, and tore it in half with a slow, deliberate movement of his thick thumbs: *cr-r-rack.*

He didn't throw it in the basket. He laid the two halves on the blotter, side by side, exactly an eighth of an inch apart.

"Mrs. Sullivan," Vance said.

Tara looked up, her hands tightening on Dylan’s blue nylon shoulder.

"You are going to take the boy down to the station," Vance said. "Mrs. Braithwaite will give you six pounds from the petty-cash tin. You will not go to Morecambe. You will take the three-o'clock bus to Greenodd, get down at the bridge, and walk up the lane to the home-farm at High Lowes. My sister has six rooms in the old granary. It is not twenty-two storeys high, and there is no underfloor heating, but the copper is hot and there is a pot of beef-broth on the range that has been simmering since yesterday morning."

Tara looked at the torn paper on the desk.

"And the council?" she whispered. "The woman with the gray hair?"

"Miss Garside," Vance said with that small, cold smile that showed his yellow teeth, "is my second cousin. She was at my wedding in eighty-nine, and she owes me two hundred pounds for the transfer of her mother’s orchard in Cartmel. If she mentions Section 193 to you again, tell her to call me at this desk before she has her tea."

He turned to Arthur.

"And you, Driver Cleary?"

Arthur picked up his milk-crate. 

The brass buttons on his municipal tunic caught the green light from the desk-lamp, shining like four small, gold pennies in the dark room.

"I’m going to Millom," Arthur said.

### Chapter 20

The road to Millom was twelve miles of narrow, single-track asphalt that wound through the salt-marshes of the Duddon estuary like an old black boot-lace dropped into a turnip-field.

Arthur did not take the bus. 

There was no bus; the No. 12 had been taken off the schedule when the iron-mines at Hodbarrow were flooded in nineteen sixty-eight, and the railway line had three halts where the grass was growing two feet high through the platforms and the signal-boxes were boarded up with corrugated iron.

He walked along the embankment of the old mineral line.

The slag was hard under his boots—sharp, purple lumps of furnace-refuse from eighty years of smelting the red hematite ore, crushed down into a road-bed that never held water, even when the tide came over the salt-marshes and turned the samphire-flats into an inland sea.

To his right, the high fells of Black Combe rose two thousand feet into the white clouds, their tops gray with the first sleet of the season. 

To his left, the channel was two miles of brown mud, shining in the afternoon light like wet whale-skin, with the gulls crying over the low-water gullies with that high, desolate scream that sounds like an iron bolt being drawn through a rusted staple.

He reached Millom at four o’clock.

It was an iron town that had forgotten what iron was for. 

Six rows of red-brick terraces huddled behind the slag-bank, their slate roofs turning purple in the dusk, their front doors opening straight onto the cobbles without a garden or a step. 

On the corner of Market Street, the old cooperative store had its windows tinned up with zinc sheets, and outside the railway station, three young men with shaved necks and grey tracksuits were sitting on an upturned wooden cable-drum, drinking beer from silver cans that they threw against the brickwork of the parcel-shed every time a train went past on the main line.

Arthur found the wash-house behind the baker’s shop in Wellington Street.

It was nine feet square, built of unmortared river-boulders with a corrugated zinc roof that had rusted through in the valleys, held down by four half-bricks laid on the ridges. 

The door was not painted; it was made of two-inch larch planks that had shrunk away from the jamb, leaving a gap an inch wide through which could be seen the blue flame of an old *Valor* oil-heater and the shape of George Beale’s Melton coat.

Arthur pushed the door.

It did not click; it groaned—a heavy, damp complaint of ungreased iron butt-hinges that had been fitted without a rebate: *cr-r-reak.*

Inside, the floor was cold, gray concrete, smelling of old flour-sacks, damp yeast, and the sweet, heavy reek of paraffin.

In the corner, on the horsehair settee with the ruptured hessian backing, sat George.

He had not taken off his flat cap, and his overcoat was still buttoned to the chin with the three horn buttons missing. 

He was sitting with his boots together, his hands flat on his knees, his eyes fixed on the whitewashed wall three feet in front of him.

On the lime-wash, three feet from the floor, a line had been drawn.

It was not a straight line. 

It began at the corner by the water-pipe, dipped three inches in the middle with a slow, magnificent curve that had the grace of a swan’s neck, and then rose toward the zinc roof with a long, sweeping sheer that ended right at the edge of the chimney-flue.

It was drawn in white chalk—the yellowed, dry chalk that Arthur had carried in his pocket since Friday morning.

Beside the line, written in George’s neat, sixty-year-old draft-loft hand, were three words:

*H.M.S. Penelope: Sheer at Aft Frame 14.*

Janice was sitting on a wooden flour-bin by the oil-heater, wearing her two cardigans and her brown nylon stockings, peeling four small onions into an enamel basin with an old bone-handled knife that had been ground down until the blade was no wider than a pencil.

She did not look up when the hinges groaned.

"Arthur," she said. 

Her voice had that thin, papery rattle of the wind through the larch planks.

"I’ve brought George’s chalk," Arthur said. He set his milk-crate down on the concrete floor between the settee and the oil-stove.

"He don't need it," Janice said, her knife taking a skin off the onion with a dry, papery *snick*. "He used the bit he had in his cuff. He’s been on that wall since three o’clock, Arthur. He thinks the ship’s on the slipway. He thinks the derrick-men are waiting for the offsets."

Arthur walked to the settee.

"George," he said softly.

The old man did not turn his head. His eyes were wide, clear, fixed on the white line on the river-stone.

"The rise of floor is three feet nine inches," George said to the lime-wash. His voice had that deep, resonant ring that filled the little stone wash-house until the zinc sheets on the roof vibrated with the sound. "You cannot bring the keel-blocks closer than forty inches on the centerline, or the plating’ll buckle when the water leaves her."

He reached out his right thumb, touching the white chalk-line where it rose toward the chimney.

"She was a lovely ship, Artie," George whispered. "She had thirty-two thousand horse-power on four shafts. When she went through the Firth on her trials in forty-one, she threw a bow-wave sixteen feet high that washed the cows off the shingle at Rothesay."

"Aye, George," Arthur said, his hand resting on the rough wool of the Melton coat. "She was a fine ship."

Janice dropped the last onion into the basin. The water gave a small, gray splash: *plip.*

"The lad came, Arthur," she said.

Arthur did not look around.

"Croft?"

"Aye," she said. "At two o’clock. He had that big blue motor-car parked in the lane by the bakehouse. The boys from the railway were looking at the wheels because they’ve got them little silver caps on the valves."

"What did he want, Janice?"

"He wanted George," she said. Her voice didn't rise; it was as cold as the concrete under her boots. "He had a paper with two red seals on it. Said if George signed the tenant-waiver, he’d give us a thousand pounds in five-pound notes. Said he’d pay the first six months on a little brick bungalow at Askam with a lawn and an electric fire."

She wiped her knife on the corner of her apron.

"Did George sign it?" Arthur asked.

Janice looked at the white line on the wall.

"George took the pen," she said. "That silver pen he has with the little grooves on the brass. George looked at it for three minutes, Arthur. He looked at the little nib, and he said: *This isn't an offset-pen, mister. This is a clerk’s toy. You couldn't strike a three-foot curve with this if you had two men to hold the spline.*"

She gave a small, dry sniff that had no tears in it, only the cold salt-mist from the estuary.

"And then he dropped it," she whispered. "Right down the drain-hole by the copper. It’s sitting in three feet of ditch-sludge under the flags, Arthur. That lad had his sleeve rolled up to the elbow, trying to find it with an old rake-handle, and his blue coat got covered in soot from the flue."

Arthur looked down at his own knuckles.

The two blue ink-dots under his skin were dark against the white bone.

"He’ll be back, Janice," Arthur said.

"Let him come," she said. She picked up her basin and walked to the door, her bad veins dragging on the concrete. "We’ve paid the three-pound-ten for sixty year, Arthur. We’ve had our tea, and we’ve had our wash, and George has got his line on the wall. There isn't a man in Leeds with a clean enough neck to rub that chalk off the stone."

She stepped out into the yard.

The door didn't shut; it stayed open two inches on its larch hinges, and through the gap Arthur could see the first flakes of the night’s sleet falling onto the corrugated zinc of the wash-house roof:

*Tick... tick... tick.*

### Chapter 21

At six o’clock, Arthur Cleary walked back into the lane outside St. Jude’s Terrace.

The street was dark now, lit only by the cold, white LED glare from the sales pavilion across the link-road. 

The police car was gone. 

The black van from the coroner’s office was gone. 

And on the front of St. Jude’s Terrace, over the ground-floor windows, over Mrs. Corley’s pitch-pine entrance, over the great dolphin knocker, were twelve sheets of eight-by-four galvanized corrugated steel.

They had been fastened to the Victorian sandstone with three-inch expansion bolts driven into the mortar-joints by pneumatic drills, their hex-heads smeared with red grease to keep the boys from turning them with an adjustable spanner. 

Across the center of the steel, stenciled in black spray-paint that had run down the corrugations in long, dirty tears, were three words:

*DANGER: STRUCTURE CONDEMNED*  
*KEEP OUT*  
*VANGUARD ESTUARY PROPERTIES*

Beside the steel, sitting on an overturned cable-drum by the curb, was Julian Croft.

His slate-gray macintosh was not clean anymore. 

There was a long smear of black soot across the right sleeve, where he had reached down Janice Beale’s drain, and his trousers were splashed to the knee with gray marsh-sludge from the mineral line. 

He had no pen. 

He had no leather document case. 

He was holding an unlit cigarette between two fingers that were gray with cold, his eyes fixed on the empty cobbles between his boots.

When Arthur’s boots hit the stone, Croft did not stand up. He lifted his head with a slow, heavy jerk, like an old horse that has reached the top of the hill with a four-ton dray behind it and knows that the brake has failed.

"Cleary," he said.

His voice was hoarse, scraped raw by twelve hours of speaking to solicitors, site-managers, and regional directors on a battery that was down to four per cent.

"The steel’s up," Arthur said. He set his milk-crate down on the curb, three feet from the cable-drum.

"I know," Croft said. "The contractors worked through the rain. It cost twelve hundred pounds in overtime to get the sheets from Preston before the light went."

He looked at Arthur’s municipal tunic—the brass buttons, the white plastic piping, the chrome badge: *Driver No. 142.*

"Where have you been, Arthur?"

"I’ve been to the depot," Arthur said. "And I’ve been to Millom."

Croft gave a short, ragged sigh that came out into the sleet as a pale plume of steam.

"The old man wouldn't sign," Croft said.

"No," Arthur said. "George doesn't like small pens."

Croft dropped his unlit cigarette onto the wet stones. It did not roll; it lay in a puddle of diesel-water, the white paper turning brown as the grease soaked through the tobacco.

"The bank has called the facility," Croft said.

He said it very quietly, without any of the legal terms he had used on Thursday morning—no *Schedule 15*, no *Section 21*, no *Deeds of Surrender*.

"The facility?" Arthur asked.

"The loan," Croft said. "Eighty-two million pounds. The German syndicate has invoked the vacant-possession warranty. If we haven't broken the foundations by midnight on the thirtieth, the interest rate steps up by four hundred basis points. The holding company goes into administrative receivership on Monday morning."

He looked at the twenty-two storeys of *The Spinnaker Reach*.

On the third floor, the lights of Unit 304 were still burning—that single yellow square in the glass tower, floating two hundred feet above the mud like a star that had fallen into an empty pond.

"You could have had the warm floor, Arthur," Croft whispered. "It was yours. I had the lease in my pocket. I had the keys with the gold tags on them."

"I know," Arthur said.

"Why didn't you take it?" Croft asked, and for the first time there was no professional balance in his voice; it was the question of a child who has built a house of cards on a carpet and watched someone open the door to let the cat in. "You’re sixty-seven years old. Your hip’s broken. You have ninety pounds a week and an enameled kettle with a wire handle. What were you saving, Arthur? What did you think was in this heap of rotten brick?"

Arthur sat down on his milk-crate.

He reached down into the box, found George Beale’s piece of white chalk, and rolled it between his thumb and forefinger until the dry dust came off against his palm.

"I wasn't saving the bricks, lad," Arthur said.

"Then what?"

Arthur looked at the twelve sheets of corrugated steel covering the front of the terrace.

Under the zinc, through the four-inch pine, through the twelve inches of dead lime and horsehair, he could hear the silence of the well.

Mrs. Corley was gone; they had taken her to the infirmary at three o’clock with a blanket over her face because her heart had stopped while the drills were putting the first bolt into her lintel.

Marek was in Kirkby, sleeping on a rubber mattress in an old school behind the railway.

Janice was in the wash-house in Millom, watching George draw cruisers on the lime-wash with his cuff.

Tara and Dylan were at High Lowes, sitting by a wood-stove that had been built for an old granary, watching the needle of an African compass point toward the cold water of the channel.

And Syd was in the cold-drawer at the hospital, with his three overcoats in a plastic sack under the gurney and a gray polystyrene cruiser between his dead fingers.

"A bus driver," Arthur said, his voice flat and steady as the tide in the gut, "doesn't own the road, lad. He doesn't own the passengers, and he doesn't own the sixpences in the brass tray."

He looked Croft in the eyes.

"He just drives the route. And when he comes to the end of the line, he doesn't leave the bus in the middle of the road with the lights off and the passengers sitting in the dark because someone offered him a cup of tea in a clean parlour."

He reached out his hand, took the green paper from nineteen eighty-four from his waistcoat pocket, and laid it on the cable-drum between them.

The blue wax seal was cold, hard as a pebble from the Bell-Pier.

"Take it," Arthur said.

Croft looked at the paper. He did not touch it.

"What is it?"

"It’s the surrender," Arthur said.

Croft’s eyes opened wide.

"You’re signing?"

"No," Arthur said. "I’m giving it to you. You can take it to your judge in Leeds. You can show him the ship’s wheel on the wax, and you can show him the date—*fourteenth of October, nineteen eighty-four.* You can tell him Artie Cleary’s gone off the route."

Croft looked at the green paper, then at Arthur’s hands.

"And you?" Croft asked, his voice shaking. "Where will you go?"

Arthur stood up from his milk-crate.

His right hip gave that dry, familiar pop—that old bronze bush that had done its forty years and had nothing left to lose.

He did not pick up the kettle. 

He did not pick up the salt-crock. 

He left the milk-crate on the curb, with Sarah’s postcard and the tin of *Three Nuns* tobacco sitting inside the wood like three pieces of ballast left in an empty hold.

"I’m going to the Bell-Pier," Arthur said.

He turned his back on St. Jude’s Terrace, on the twelve sheets of corrugated steel, and on the white needle of *The Spinnaker Reach*.

### Chapter 22

### Chapter 23

"The rectification notice remains on the file, Mr. Cleary," Bradley whispered, his voice trembling with that small, impotent hatred of a clerk whose authority has been overridden by someone who earns five times his salary.

### Chapter 32

The rain turned to sleet by noon, and by three o’clock the sleet had become the first real snow of the winter.

It was not the soft, silent snow that settles on the sheep-pastures of the fells; it was the horizontal, salt-scabbed drift of the estuary, driven at forty miles an hour off the Irish Sea, coating the north-west faces of the towers with an inch of gray slush that froze into ribbed sheets of corrugated ice as soon as it touched the cold glass.

Arthur did not stay in 304.

He had made his tea in Finch’s Swedish kettle—four mugs of strong, black Indian leaf that tasted of iron and hot tin—and then he had put on his oilskin coat, his black municipal cap, and his heavy leather boots.

He did not take the lift. 

He walked down the three hundred and twenty concrete steps of the fire-escape, his heels ringing on the ribbed steel nosings: *tramp... tramp... tramp.*

At the bottom, he pushed open the emergency exit into the service lane.

The piling rig had stopped. 

The eighty-foot mast was tilted back on its hydraulic rams, its blue steel white with frost, and the three men in the yellow vests were huddled inside the cabin of the work-boat, drinking soup from red plastic flasks while the snow accumulated on the deck around their boots.

Arthur walked to the perimeter road.

He did not take the bus. 

He had four ten-pound notes in his pocket—the remainder of the cash Hector Vance had extracted from the petty-cash drawer behind the law reports—and he walked through the slush to the old taxi-rank outside the railway station.

There were three cars on the rank. 

Two of them were modern, silver diesel saloons with private-hire door-stickers and roof-signs that were dark. 

The third was an old nineteen ninety-eight Ford Granada estate—six cylinders, two tons of rusted Essex steel, its rear wheel-arches patched with black fiberglass and pop-rivets, its roof-rack loaded with two spare wheels and an old wooden fruit-box containing four lengths of tow-rope.

The driver was sitting inside with the engine running to keep the heater going, reading an old copy of the *Evening Mail* by the light of the dashboard.

It was Billy Nancarrow’s boy, Jack.

He was fifty now, with the same red hair his father had had on the morning of the eighty-six strike, but his eyes were tired, the skin around his nose pitted with black grease from twenty-five years of mending old gearboxes in a shed behind the gas-works.

Arthur tapped on the window with his knuckle: *toc, toc.*

The window rolled down four inches with a dry, mechanical squeal from the winding-gear.

A cloud of blue roll-up smoke came out into the snow, smelling of cheap shag and menthol sweets.

"Artie?" Jack said, squinting through the glass at the municipal uniform. "What are you doing on the rank in this lot? The buses are all off. Stagecoach pulled the depot at two o’clock because of the ice on the Salt-Gate."

"I want to go to Millom, Jack," Arthur said.

Jack looked at him for five seconds. 

He took the cigarette from his mouth, pinched the glowing coal off between his greasy calloused thumb and forefinger, and dropped the dog-end into an empty sardine-tin on the transmission tunnel.

"Millom?" Jack said. "The causeway’s got three inches of salt-slush on the bends, Artie. If the back-end steps out on the Duddon bridge, we’ll be in the samphire before the warden can get his tow-chain on the axle."

Arthur reached into his tunic pocket and pulled out three of the ten-pound notes.

He laid them on the cold glass of the quarter-light.

"Thirty pounds, Jack," Arthur said. "And five pounds for the diesel when we get to the cross-roads."

Jack looked at the three blue notes. 

They were dry, crisp, clean as new sails.

He gave a short, wet spit that hit the rubber floor-mat by his pedals.

"Get in," Jack said, reaching over to kick the passenger door open with the heel of his boot. "The catch sticks on the latch. You have to heave it twice with your shoulder before the pin drops."

Arthur climbed into the passenger seat.

The Granada smelled of old damp carpet, transmission fluid, and thirty years of wet sheepdogs that had been carried between the fell-farms and the butcher’s yard.

Jack dropped the floor-shift into first gear.

The old Ford V6 roared with a deep, throaty, twin-carburetor snarl that had sixteen valves and no computer to tell the distributor when to fire. 

The rear tires bit through the snow down to the greasy cobbles, throwing two long plumes of black slush against the station wall, and the heavy car lurched forward into the storm, its wipers slapping against the glass with a slow, hypnotic clatter:

*Slap-sh-h... slap-sh-h.*

### Chapter 33

The wash-house behind the baker’s shop in Wellington Street was four degrees below freezing when they arrived.

The snow had drifted three feet high against the unmortared river-boulders of the north wall, and through the inch-wide gaps between the larch planks of the door, the white drift had formed long, ribbed tongues that reached across the concrete floor to the edge of the horsehair settee.

The *Valor* oil-heater was out.

The paraffin had run dry at noon, and the wick was charred into a black, greasy crust that gave off a thin, sour smoke that smelled of dead cabbage-leaves and cold soot.

George Beale was not drawing cruisers.

He was lying on the horsehair settee with his boots off, his feet wrapped in two old flour-sacks that had been tied around his ankles with lengths of binder-twine. 

He had four overcoats over him, but he was shaking—not with a violent tremor, but with that slow, rhythmic, deep-seated vibration that comes to old men when the core-temperature has dropped below ninety degrees and the heart begins to hesitate between the strokes.

His face was not white; it was the color of a wet slate that has been rubbed with chalk. 

His eyes were half-open, milky, unblinking, fixed on the lime-wash where the white sheer-line of the *Penelope* had begun to blur into gray mold where the damp had come through the stone.

Janice was sitting on the wooden flour-bin.

She had her coat on, her two cardigans, and an old woollen shawl pinned across her chest with a brass safety-pin that had belonged to a horse-blanket. 

Her hands were tucked into her armpits, her shoulders hunched until her neck had disappeared entirely into the wool, her breath coming in short, shallow puffs that didn't have enough heat to make steam in the freezing air.

When the larch door groaned back on its ungreased hinges, she did not lift her head.

"The boy’s gone to the pub, Arthur," she said. 

Her voice had no volume left; it was just a dry, papery whisper that sounded like dead leaves being blown across an empty yard.

"He said if we hadn't got the seven pounds for the paraffin, he wasn't going to carry the tin from the station in the blizzard."

Arthur stepped into the wash-house.

He did not look at the dead stove. 

He walked to the settee, knelt in the snow-drift that had come through the door, and put his hand on George’s forehead.

The skin was cold. 

It was like touching a piece of salt-beef that had been taken out of the brine-cask on a winter morning.

"George," Arthur said.

George’s eyelids gave a tiny, fractional flutter.

"Artie," the old man whispered. 

The voice didn't come from his chest; it came from the back of his throat, dry as lime-dust.

"The frames... the frames aren't square, lad."

"They’re square enough, George," Arthur said gently.

"No," the old man breathed. "The water’s... the water’s coming over the caisson, Artie. I can't find... I can't find the blue chalk to mark the dead-flat."

Arthur stood up.

He turned to the door where Jack Nancarrow was standing in the snow, his hands deep in his greasy coat pockets, his cigarette-end glowing red under the dripping brim of his flat cap.

"Take his feet, Jack," Arthur said.

Jack took the cigarette from his mouth. 

He looked at George’s face, at the four overcoats, at the two flour-sacks tied with binder-twine, then at the whitewashed wall where the white chalk curve was disappearing into the damp.

"He’s nearly gone, Artie," Jack muttered. "If we move him in the snow, he’ll die on the causeway."

"He’ll die here in two hours, Jack," Arthur said. 

His voice was flat, hard, the voice of the inspector who tells the breakdown crew that the bus has to come out of the ditch before the tide reaches the axle-boxes.

"Get the blankets out of the back of the Ford. The woollen ones from the sheep-pens. We’ll roll him in the middle, and Janice can sit in the front with the heater on her knees."

Jack looked at Arthur for three seconds.

Then he spat into the snow—a dark, yellow drop that bored a clean hole three inches deep into the drift.

"Right," Jack said.

It took ten minutes to carry George Beale through the drift to the Granada.

He had no weight. 

Seventy-one years of driving rivets, striking curves, and carrying three-inch staging planks had left him with nothing inside his bones except lime and dry air. 

He was like an old spruce spar that had been seasoned in a timber-shed until you could lift twenty feet of it with one hand.

They laid him across the back seat of the Ford, his head resting against the greasy vinyl of the door-trim, his feet tucked into the well behind Jack’s seat. 

Arthur pulled the two sheep-blankets over him, tucking the coarse, greasy wool around his neck until only his white nose and his milky blue eyes were showing.

Janice climbed into the passenger seat.

She did not look back at the wash-house. 

She did not look at the dead *Valor* stove, and she did not look at the banana-boxes sitting in the snow by the copper.

She held her plastic carrier-bag on her knees with both hands, her knuckles yellow and rigid as dead horn.

"Where are we going, Arthur?" she whispered as Jack let the clutch out and the big car began to fish-tail through the Wellington Street ruts.

Arthur sat in the back, on the transmission tunnel, his hand resting on George’s chest, feeling the small, shallow, irregular rise and fall of the old man’s ribs under the four overcoats.

"We’re going to the ninth floor, Janice," Arthur said.

"The tower?"

"Aye," Arthur said. "The tower."

She looked out through the side-window where the snow was whipping past the red-brick terraces of Millom like white shot.

"They won't let us in, Arthur," she said, her voice carrying that quiet, ancient certainty of the poor who know that every door with a clean glass panel is locked against them from the inside. "That lad with the clean neck. He’ll call the police from the station."

Arthur looked down at George’s face.

The old man’s eyes were shut now, but his mouth had relaxed by a fraction, his lower jaw dropping slightly as the heat from the Granada’s heater-duct began to blow warm air across his ankles.

"Let him call 'em, lass," Arthur said. 

His fingers tightened on the coarse wool of the sheep-blanket.

"I’ve got forty-one years of rent-receipts in the tin, and I’ve got twenty-four feet of dead rubber on the floorboards. If he wants to put George out into the snow, he’ll have to bring sixteen men from Leeds with bigger crowbars than the ones they used on the terrace."

### Chapter 34

*Ding.*

*Whish-t.*

*Whish-t.*

*Ding.*

### Chapter 35

In Unit 304, the underfloor heating had reached twenty-two degrees.

If you move him back into the cold before his core reaches thirty-six, his heart’ll fibrillate before you reach the bottom of the lift."

"You can't keep him here, Cleary. They’ll have an interim injunction by nine o’clock tomorrow morning."

He walked to the window, his clogs clicking on the birch, and looked down at the blue car in the lane.

"A lease is just paper, Arthur," Finch said softly. "I’ve seen thirty contracts on the North Sea that were signed by three ministers and five banks, and when the price of oil dropped six dollars a barrel, they were just sixty pages of scrap-paper in a shredder in Aberdeen."

He picked up George Beale’s wooden drawing-box.

"This tower isn't a ship, lad," Arthur said. "It has no keelson. It has no sheer. It has no lines to tell the men where the water meets the iron. It’s just sixty thousand tons of gray cement sitting on an old sand-bank, waiting for the river to take the silt out from under the piles."

*Ting-ng-ng.*

*Clack.*

Then the little metallic ring from the loosened strike-plate:

*Tink.*

### Chapter 36

### Chapter 37

By noon, Unit 304 had ceased to be an apartment; it had become an encampment of the dispossessed.

On the birch floor of the sitting room, Dylan had laid out his fleet:

"Aye," Arthur said. He stayed by the sink, his hands folded across his municipal belt.

Mrs. Heseltine, who was sitting on the horsehair settee beside Janice Beale with an enameled bowl of mutton-stew on her lap, looked up with a small, startled gasp that sounded like a bird caught in a chimney.

"Unit 305?" she cried. "My flat? I paid three hundred and forty thousand pounds for the lease in July! I have the certificate from the Land Registry in my drawer!"

Vane looked at her without a trace of anger or sympathy.

"Your lease, Lady Heseltine, was granted by a company that had no title to the common infrastructure," Vane said, turning another page with his yellowed thumb. "Under Section 54 of the Law of Property Act, your conveyance is voidable at the instance of the prior encumbrancer. You are an unsecured creditor for the purchase money, ranking alongside the scaffolding suppliers from Preston and the window-cleaners from Ulverston. If the liquidation proceeds to a standard realization, you will receive approximately four-point-two pence in the pound in three years’ time."

Mrs. Heseltine’s spoon fell into her stew.

The mutton-fat gave a small, gray splash: *plop.*

Her face went that dead, waxy yellow that Arthur had seen on Syd Corcoran’s cheeks on Saturday night.

"Four pence?" she whispered.

"Four-point-two," Vane corrected her, his teeth finding another antacid tablet: *scritch-crunch.* "Assuming the piling barge in the basin does not incur additional demurrage penalties exceeding fourteen thousand euros a day."

He looked back at Arthur.

"You have the holding-key, Cleary. The whole scheme is blocked behind your lease. If you take the twenty-four thousand pounds, the administrators can execute a compromise with the Bavarians. The receivers will take over Phase One, the German bank will write off forty million of the junior debt, and the contractor will resume the piling on Monday morning."

Arthur walked to the quartz island.

He did not touch the blue paper.

He looked down at his own knuckles—at the two blue ink-dots that had lived under his skin for forty years.

"And what happens to the school in Kirkby?" Arthur asked.

Vane blinked once.

"The school?"

"The pods," Arthur said. "Marek’s pod. And the other fourteen families with the children in the mud."

"They are not on the schedule," Vane said. "They are shorthold licenses terminated by administrative notice in October. They have no legal nexus with the insolvency."

"And the terrace?" Arthur asked. "The eighty-foot pile of red brick in the lane?"

"The rubble will be crushed for aggregate," Vane said. "To form the sub-base for the marina car-park. It is valued in the asset register at four pounds fifty a ton."

Arthur reached out his right hand.

He picked up Finch’s Swedish kettle. 

He filled his mug from the spout, the black Indian tea steaming into the cold air of the room, smelling of iron, malt, and sixty years of the No. 7 route.

"No, mister," Arthur said softly.

Vane’s jaw stopped moving. 

The antacid tablet lay half-crushed between his bicuspids, a white paste of magnesium carbonate showing at the corner of his thin lip.

"No?" Vane asked.

"I’m not taking your twenty-four thousand," Arthur said.

"Cleary, you are an old man," Vane said, his voice dropping an octave into that dry, clinical warning that accountants use when a client is about to do something that will void his life-insurance. "If you refuse this settlement, the administrator will apply to the Vice-Chancellor in Manchester for an order of sale under Section 30 of the Law of Property Act. It will take six months. It will cost seventy thousand pounds in legal disbursements, which will come out of the estate. And at the end of the day, the judge will order a statutory partition, the building will be sold by court auction to a vulture-fund from Grand Cayman, and you will be evicted by the High Court Sheriff with two hours’ notice."

"Let him bring his sheriff," Arthur said.

He set his mug down on the blue paper.

The hot earthenware left a wet, brown circle right across the words *Event of Default (Material Adverse Change)*: *s-s-s-s.*

"This building has forty flats, mister," Arthur said.

Vane looked at him.

"Forty-two," Vane said. "Thirty-six incomplete in Phase One."

"Then you’d better get the joiners back," Arthur said.

"The joiners?"

"Aye," Arthur said. He pointed with his thumb toward the corridor where the draft was whistling through the unsealed frame. "Marek’s a good joiner. He can hang forty doors in three days if you give him two pounds an hour and a hot dinner. And Jack Nancarrow can wire the ring-mains with his eyes shut."

He looked Vane straight in the eyes.

"You tell your German bankers that Artie Cleary’s got forty-two rooms on his route. And until every woman from the marsh and every man with an asbestos lung has got a key with an iron ring on it, there isn't a barge in Rotterdam that’s going to turn a screw in this basin."

Vane stared at him for ten seconds.

He did not chew. 

He did not take another tablet from his blister-pack.

He looked at Hector Vance, who was smiling into the ivory tail of his dolphin stick; he looked at Lady Heseltine, who was wiping her mouth with a paper napkin from the bakery; and he looked at Dylan, who was sleeping on the birch floor with his head resting on the gray polystyrene hull of the *Penelope*.

Then Quentin Vane closed his black ring-binder with a sharp, heavy *snap* that sounded like an iron trap springing shut in an empty cellar:

*CLACK.*

He picked up his plastic mug of cold station-coffee, turned on his heel, and walked out into the corridor, his Burberry coat flapping against his shins like the sail of an old coaster that had lost its moorings in the dark.

### Chapter 43

The spring surge came on the twenty-second of December.

It was not a storm from the west; it was that terrible, dead, north-westerly gale that comes down from the Hebrides once in forty years, when the moon is full, the perigee is at its lowest point, and the Atlantic Ocean is pushed into the funnel of the Irish Sea like three million tons of brine being forced through the neck of an enameled funnel.

At five o’clock in the afternoon, the tide was already four feet above the prediction in the Liverpool tables.

The Bell-Pier had disappeared.

The seventy-ton limestone blocks that had stood since eighteen forty-four were six feet under black water, and the gulls were sitting in the branches of the elder-bushes behind the old iron-works, their heads tucked into their feathers, waiting for the wind to drop.

In the basin, the water was fifteen inches below the granite coping of the quayside.

It was not blue, and it was not green; it was the color of liquid iron, thick with silt, carrying with it thousands of plastic bottles, baulks of creosoted timber, dead sheep from the Duddon marshes, and the yellow foam from the chemical works that sat on the surface in greasy cakes four feet thick.

Arthur stood at the window of Unit 304.

He was not alone.

The entire third floor was in his sitting room:

Alistair Finch, wearing his oilskins and his rubber sea-boots, his laptop connected to a portable battery-pack on the quartz island; 

Lady Heseltine, wearing an old donkey-jacket Finch had lent her over her cashmere cardigan, holding a silver torch with both hands; 

Janice and George Beale, sitting together on the horsehair settee under three blankets; 

Tara, Dylan, and Margaret Vance; 

And Marek, who had walked eight miles through the slush from Kirkby when the trains stopped at noon, his work-boots covered in yellow clay, his iron crowbar tucked into the belt of his corduroy trousers.

Finch was watching the screen.

His face was illuminated by the blue light of the harmonic telemetry, his mouth drawn tight over his teeth.

"The pore pressure’s spiking," Finch said. 

His voice was hoarse, strained against the roar of the wind that was hitting the triple-glazing with the sound of a gravel-train crossing an iron bridge: *sh-h-h-h-h-h.*

"The cofferdam on the southern excavation is deflecting fourteen inches," Finch said. "The sheet-piles have hit the ballast-bank, but they haven't penetrated the blue clay. If the tide rises another six inches, the water’ll get behind the interlocks, scour the gravel from under the road, and flood the basement raft of this building."

"How much time?" Arthur asked.

"Forty minutes," Finch said. "The high water’s at eighteen-forty. If the hydraulic head isn't relieved before then, the concrete foundation of this tower’ll be sitting in ninety thousand tons of fluidized quicksand."

A hand touched Arthur’s sleeve.

It was George Beale.

The old man had stood up from the settee.

He did not look like an invalid who had spent forty-eight hours in a wash-house with hypothermia. 

His Melton coat was unbuttoned, his flat cap was pulled down tight over his white hair, and his eyes—those pale, milky blue eyes that had seen forty miles of cruiser-hulls come off the ways—were as sharp and bright as two newly ground dividers.

"The culvert, Artie," George said.

His voice had that deep, magnificent bell-ring that filled the room until the Swedish kettle vibrated on the stone.

"What culvert, George?" Arthur asked.

"The eighty-eight culvert," George said, pointing with his long, yellowed fingernail down through seventy yards of rain toward the submerged spine of the Bell-Pier. "Under the third slipway. The corporation built it when the dry docks were flooded in the great November gale. It’s four feet of cast-iron, Artie. It runs straight through the limestone into the deep gut behind the lightship."

"It’s silted up, George," Arthur said. "It hasn't had water in it since sixty-eight."

"It isn't silted," George said, his hand tightening on Arthur’s arm with a grip that had forty years of staging-ropes behind it. "The sluice is shut, Artie. The worm-gear’s on the wall of the old pump-house behind the smithy. If you lift the gate four inches, the head in the basin’ll drop three feet in ten minutes."

He reached into his pocket, took out his piece of blue chalk, and turned to the great glass window.

With three quick, sweeping strokes of his arm, he drew a line across the rain-swept view:

A circle for the pump-house.

A long, straight dotted line for the cast-iron conduit.

And an arrow pointing toward the dark water of the gut where the red light was blinking through the spray.

Beside the arrow, he wrote two words:

*Low Sill: 1888.*

Finch looked from the drawing to his computer screen.

He brought up the digitized municipal drainage maps from eighteen ninety-nine.

His fingers stopped on the trackpad.

"He’s right," Finch whispered, his voice shaking with that pure, professional awe that engineers feel when they discover that their ancestors had solved their problem with eighty tons of Scotch iron a hundred and thirty years before the first microprocessor was etched. "It’s there. A four-foot gravity-relief culvert with an inverted siphon under the training-wall. If that sluice opens, it’ll drain twenty thousand gallons a minute straight into the low-water channel."

Arthur looked at Marek.

The Polish docker reached down, took the two-foot iron crowbar from his belt, and weighed it in his thick palm: *clack.*

"We have boots, Mr. Cleary," Marek said.

Arthur reached for his municipal cap.

He pulled the brim down until the leather sweatband touched his eyebrows.

He looked at Lady Heseltine.

"Hold the torch, ma'am," Arthur said. "We’re going down to the iron."

### Chapter 44

The pump-house of the Caledonian & Western Graving Dock was four feet under salt-water when they reached the bottom of the lane.

It was an octagonal tower of red sandstone, twelve feet across, its slate roof long gone, its iron-framed windows choked with bladder-wrack and dead dock-weeds. 

The water was up to Arthur’s thighs—black, freezing, tasting of salt, creosote, and the sour chemical scum from the marsh. 

Every wave that came over the granite sea-wall threw a sheet of white spray across the masonry that stung his eyes and tore the breath from his nostrils: *wh-o-o-osh.*

Inside the stone octagonal well, half-submerged in the swirling tide, sat the worm-gear.

It was three hundredweight of cast-iron—a four-foot horizontal capstan wheel mounted on a vertical manganese-steel spindle three inches thick, connected to a bronze sluice-gate sixty feet below in the bedrock.

The wheel had not moved since nineteen sixty-eight.

Its teeth were crusted with three inches of red rust, dried barnacles, and hard, black sea-coal that had wedged into the gearing like iron teeth.

Finch shone the beam of Lady Heseltine’s torch onto the spindle.

"The bronze is sound," Finch shouted against the thunder of the sea on the wall. "The manganese doesn't corrode! But the cast-iron teeth on the worm are seized solid in the housing!"

Marek stepped into the water.

The tide hit his chest, wetting his gray vest until the anchor-tattoo showed blue through the wet cotton.

He jammed the curved chisel-end of his crowbar between two teeth of the capstan wheel.

He heaved.

His neck-muscles stood out under his skin like two three-inch hawsers under eighty tons of strain.

His boots slipped on the green slime of the floor-flags: *scritch.*

The wheel did not move by a thousandth of an inch.

"It needs more leverage!" Finch yelled. "We need twelve feet of pipe! We haven't got the torque!"

Arthur stood in the water.

His hip was screaming. 

The cold brine had soaked through his municipal trousers, through his woollen drawers, right down to the bone of his joint, turning his old Achilles tendon into a knot of frozen iron that refused to bend.

He looked through the open door of the pump-house.

Across thirty yards of white foam and floating timber lay the skeleton of the old corporation depot at Hindpool Road.

Bay 4.

Bus No. 142.

Arthur did not run; he waded.

He moved through the three feet of water with that slow, heavy, rolling gait he had used when the snow-drifts were five feet deep on the High Battery in the winter of seventy-nine.

He reached the bus.

The platform was under six inches of water, the ribbed aluminum steps shining under the foam like silver fish-plates.

Arthur climbed inside.

He did not look at the fare-box, and he did not look at the route-blinds.

He walked down the flooded gangway to the rear emergency exit.

Beside the door, mounted on two spring-clips on the aluminum bulkhead, was the emergency towing-bar—ten feet of solid, forged carbon-steel, two inches in diameter, with two heavy eye-bolts welded to the ends that had been tested to thirty tons of direct pull on the Corporation’s dead-weight tester in nineteen seventy-six.

Arthur pulled the bar from its clips.

The steel came free with a sharp, ringing snap that echoed through the empty saloon:

*CLANG.*

It was heavy—seventy pounds of cold, greased steel that dragged his shoulders down as he carried it across the platform into the tide.

He brought it back to the pump-house.

Marek’s eyes widened in the torch-light.

"The bus," the Pole gasped.

"Aye," Arthur said, his voice level as an oil-bath. "The Leyland. Put the eye-bolt through the spoke, lad."

They slipped the ten-foot steel bar through the four-foot wheel.

Arthur took the outer end.

Marek took the middle.

Finch took the root.

"On three!" Finch roared against the squall. "One... two... THREE!"

They threw their weight against the steel.

Arthur’s boots found the joint between two limestone flags.

He did not use his back; he used his legs—the legs that had depressed thirty thousand clutch-springs against forty-degree hills.

His right hip gave a sound like a rifle-shot—a dry, sickening crack that tore through the tendon like a jagged wire.

For one second, two seconds, three seconds, nothing moved.

The salt-water swirled around their chests, smelling of the end of the world.

Then, deep in the stone beneath their feet—from sixty feet down in the Triassic sandstone—came a sound that was older than the town, older than the railway, older than the docks:

*CR-R-R-R-ACK.*

The red rust broke.

The sixty-year-old barnacles pulverized into white powder.

The capstan wheel turned two inches.

*Sque-e-e-k.*

"Again!" Finch screamed. "She’s taking the thread!"

They heaved again.

The wheel moved four inches. 

Six inches. 

A foot.

Then, from the bottom of the well, through the dark water, came a low, sucking, terrifying roar that shook the sandstone walls of the pump-house like an earthquake:

*GLUG-GLUG-GLUG-WH-O-O-OSH.*

The sluice-gate had lifted.

The sixty-foot vertical shaft became a whirlpool.

The black water in the lane began to slide toward the pump-house, drawn by twenty thousand gallons a minute of hydraulic head, rushing down into the cast-iron conduit, passing under the Bell-Pier, and exploding into the low-water gut two miles away in a roaring fountain of brown foam that tore the bladder-wrack from the training-wall.

Arthur let go of the steel bar.

He fell against the sandstone jamb of the door, his chest heaving, his hands clawing at the rough rock to keep his head above the swirling tide.

He looked out across the basin.

The water was dropping.

You could see the granite coping reappearing—one inch, three inches, six inches—as the ancient Victorian culvert swallowed the Atlantic Ocean and threw it back into the sea.

Finch was leaning on the capstan, laughing like an escaped lunatic, his oilskin dripping with slime.

"It’s holding!" Finch shouted, looking at his telemetry watch. "The foundation raft has stabilized! The pore pressure’s dropping through the floor! We’ve broken the head!"

Arthur did not laugh.

He stood with his back against the stone, his municipal cap in his hand, looking up through the sleet toward the third floor of *The Spinnaker Reach*.

Through the rain, two hundred feet above the mud, the unsealed door of Unit 304 was glowing with warm, yellow light.

And in the window, clearly visible against the dark clouds, stood the white chalk drawing of the *Barrow Lass*, its paddle-box riding high above the flood, its bow pointing toward the open sea where the red light was blinking against the fells:

Three seconds light.

Nine seconds dark.

Arthur Cleary slipped his municipal cap back onto his head, pulled the brim straight, and began the long walk back through the receding mud toward the tower.

### Chapter 45

The spring came to the estuary on the fifteenth of April.

It was not a sudden burst of warmth; it was that slow, salt-whitened thaw that turns the gray mud-banks into fields of pale green glasswort and makes the bladder-wrack smell of iodine and clean tide.

In the sales pavilion of *The Spinnaker Reach*, the perspex model had been removed from its black slate plinth.

In its place sat three large, wooden trestle-tables covered with hand-drawn plans on blue draft-paper, weighted down with George Beale’s lead whales and three pieces of red hematite ore from the Hodbarrow mine.

Behind the tables sat Hector Vance, his ivory-handled stick resting against his knee, his gold spectacles perched on his nose, looking at Quentin Vane across an enameled tea-tray.

"The court has sanctioned the scheme of arrangement," Vane said, his voice as flat and dry as ever, though he had abandoned his antacid tablets for a pipe of mild Dutch shag that smelled of dried plums. "The Bavarian syndicate has surrendered its secured debt in exchange for a forty-year leasehold debenture over the commercial marina."

He pushed a thick, blue-bound volume across the table.

"Phase One is transferred to the *St. Jude’s Maritime Housing Cooperative*," Vane said. "Forty-two units. Rent Act succession registered in perpetuity under the eighty-two covenants. The service charges are capped at four pounds ten a week per household, discharged from the berth-fees of the wind-turbine support vessels."

Hector Vance unhooked his spectacles.

"And the residents?" the old solicitor asked.

"Units 101 to 108 are occupied by the families from the Kirkby pods," Vane said, reading from the schedule without looking up. "Marek has been appointed site maintenance superintendent under a five-year contract. Unit 202 is allocated to Mrs. Tara Sullivan and her son, Dylan. Lady Heseltine has surrendered Unit 305 and taken Unit 203 to be next door to the child."

He looked out through the glass doors of the pavilion toward the quay.

"And Cleary?"

"Driver Cleary," Hector Vance said with a small, quiet smile that had seventy years of chancery practice behind it, "remains in Unit 304. Under his personal life-tenancy. With an unsealed door."

On the quayside, the sun was breaking through the high, white clouds.

Arthur was sitting on the granite coping where the Bell-Pier met the shore.

He was wearing his municipal tunic, but the weather was mild enough that he had unbuttoned it entirely, showing his clean white flannel shirt and his red braces.

Beside him sat George Beale.

George had his Melton coat off today; he was wearing an old blue linen work-smock with three brass pins in the lapel, his hands resting on his knees, his face turned toward the basin where the tide was running three knots through the opened sluice.

Dylan was sitting between them, his bare legs dangling over the water, holding Kalu’s compass in his palm, watching the needle dance as a small diesel tug moved past the pier-head with four timber-baulks in tow:

*Chug... chug... chug.*

"Where’s she going, Artie?" the boy asked, pointing with a dirty finger toward the tug.

"She’s going to the lightship, lad," Arthur said. "To take the coal out to the keepers."

"Will she make the turn?"

"Aye," George Beale said, his voice deep and steady as the limestone blocks below them. "She’ll make it. The channel’s sixty feet wide on the neaps. If he keeps her nose on the red beacon until he clears the spit, the water’ll carry her through the gut without a touch on the wheel."

Arthur looked up at the tower.

The twenty-two storeys of steel and acoustic glass were shining in the spring sun, reflecting the blue sky, the white gulls, and the gray fells forty miles away to the north.

On the third floor, seven doors stood wide open to the balcony.

Through the gaps, the sounds of the building drifted down to the water:

A child laughing on the tiles.

A woman singing in a kitchen.

A hammer hitting a shoe-heel on a last.

And from Unit 304, through forty-four millimeters of unsealed European ash, the little metallic ring of a loosened strike-plate answering the river-wind:

*Tink.*

Arthur reached into his waistcoat pocket.

He took out Sarah’s postcard from Blackpool.

He looked at the four lines of faded pencil for five seconds.

Then he reached out his hand, leaned over the edge of the limestone coping, and let the card drop into the salt-water below.

It did not sink immediately.

It lay on the surface of the tide for three seconds, the paper turning gray as the brine soaked through the fibers, the pencil-marks blurring into the dark river-mud:

*...the sea is very gray today... I have bought two pairs of stockings...*

Then the current caught it.

It slid toward the gut, drawn by the great, silent drainage that was carrying the river toward the open sea, passing over the old ballast-bank, over the sixty-pound rails, over the four-inch pine doors, and out into the deep Atlantic water where there were no schedules, no timetables, and no more stops along the road.

Arthur Cleary stood up from the granite stone.

His right hip gave a small, dry, familiar pop—that old bronze bush that had done its duty and had nothing left to answer for.

He put his hands in his pockets, turned his back on the river, and walked toward the open doors of the tower, his cap straight on his head, his boots firm on the stone, listening to the town begin its afternoon run.

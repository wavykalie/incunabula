# The Record

### Chapter 1

The platen of the machine was silent, but the twenty-two keys made a sound like dry elderberry twigs snapping under felt slippers.

Ruth Kelleher did not look at her hands. If she looked at her hands, the speed fell from two hundred and forty words a minute to one hundred and eighty, and at one hundred and eighty the room overtook her. A twenty-year-old assistant prosecutor from the county attorney’s office could speak at one hundred and ninety when he was nervous; a patrol sergeant describing the trajectory of a shattered tail-light could touch two hundred and ten without clearing his throat; but a mother testifying about the last time she saw her son on a porch in the Lower Fourth could drop into a breathless, sobbing stutter that hit two hundred and sixty-five before the judge could lift his gavel.

Ruth sat three feet below the bench, to the left of the witness box, her knees angled forty-five degrees from the jury rail so her elbows would not brush the mahogany modesty screen. Her lower back rested against the stiff lumbar pad of a pneumatic swivel chair she had bought with her own money in 2014 and wheeled from Courtroom 3B to Courtroom 2A whenever the docket shuffled.

On the narrow LCD display of the Luminex, three lines of raw English text floated in green phosphor:

`THE COURT: WE WILL TAKE THE RECESS UNTIL TWO.`  
`MR. VANCE: THANK YOU YOUR HONOR.`  
`THE WITNESS: CAN I GO OUTSIDE TO SMOKE?`  

Ruth tapped the chord for *proceedings suspended*—`P-R-S` on the initial bank, thumbs dropping together onto the double vowels—and let her wrists fall into her lap.

The air in Courtroom 2A smelled of steam heat, damp wool, and lemon oil rubbed over seventy-year-old red oak that had dried out during the February freeze. Outside the arched leaded-glass windows, three stories above the corner of Market and Fourth, the rain was coming down off Lake Erie, gray and particulate, washing the diesel grit from the cornices of the Blackstone County Courthouse.

Judge Harlan stood up, his black robe rustling like dry corn husks. He was seventy-one, with liver spots the size of nickels across his temples and a heart valve that had been replaced during the 2021 winter recess. He did not look at Ruth, nor at the defendant, nor at the two deputy sheriffs standing by the latch of the holding cell. He stepped through the heavy velour curtain behind the bench without a word, his orthopedic heels squeaking once on the parquet floor of chambers.

"Ruthie," said Dan Kowalski, the court crier. He was sixty-two, wore an off-the-rack navy blazer with brass buttons that had lost their lacquer, and had been carrying the Bible for jury oaths since Ruth had smooth skin on the backs of her knuckles. "He’s going to take ninety minutes. You want a turkey club from down the street?"

"No, Dan. Thank you."

"You look gray, Ruthie. The light in here is like a morgue since they put those fluorescent tubes behind the ceiling molds."

"It’s just the damp," Ruth said. Her voice was quiet, kept down in the register that did not carry into the microphones. For eighteen years she had trained herself to make no noise in a room. She did not cough; she did not clear her throat; she did not wear bangles or shoes with steel shanks that could tick against the metal crossbar of her tripod. In the county directory she was listed under *Judicial Support Personnel, Grade 8*, but inside the walls of the third floor she was what she had always been: the ear that did not forget.

She reached down, unthreaded the USB cable from the side port of her machine, and turned the knurled brass thumbscrew that locked the steno head to the telescoping aluminum stalk. The machine weighed four pounds. She held it against her ribs like an accordion as she walked through the side aisle, past the empty defense table where two yellow legal pads lay with their top pages torn off into serrated fringes.

The hallway smelled of wet floor runners and floor wax that had never fully cured over the terrazzo. Down at the end of the corridor, past the heavy fire doors of the Clerk of Courts, a man was standing beside the water fountain.

He was holding an eight-and-a-half by eleven volume bound in blue pressboard with a black tape spine.

Ruth stopped three paces from the brass doorplate of the Reporters’ Pool.

The man was Julian Cross. He was thirty-eight, with thinning reddish hair cut down to the scalp and spectacles with thin titanium wire frames that sat slightly crooked on a broken bridge. He had been an appellate defender with the state capital post-conviction unit in Columbus before he came back to Blackstone to open a solo shop over a dry cleaner on South Main. He wore suits that had been pressed too many times, the fabric on the lapels shiny from the dry-cleaner’s iron.

"Ms. Kelleher," Cross said. He did not raise his voice, but the acoustic tiles in the ceiling had been removed for asbestos remediation two months earlier, and the bare concrete above them caught his syllables and bounced them off the marble baseboards.

"Mr. Cross," Ruth said. "If you need a copy of the suppression hearing from this morning, it won’t be scoped until tomorrow evening. Judge Harlan hasn’t ruled on the firearm chain yet."

"I don't need this morning," Cross said. He held the blue volume with both hands, his thumbs hooked over the binding edge. "I need *State against Thorne*."

A small, cold drop of moisture fell from the radiator valve overhead and clicked against the floor between them.

"Marcus Thorne was sentenced in November," Ruth said. "The appeal went to the Seventh District in January. The record was filed four months ago."

"I know," Cross said. "I have the record right here. The certified transcript of the trial. Volume Two of Three. Page four hundred and fourteen."

Ruth looked at the blue pressboard cover. On the front, framed within the standard window cut out of the cardstock, the white title page was visible through the clear acetate:

`IN THE COURT OF COMMON PLEAS`  
`BLACKSTONE COUNTY, OHIO`  
`CASE NO. 23-CR-882`  
`STATE OF OHIO, PLAINTIFF,`  
`v.`  
`MARCUS D. THORNE, DEFENDANT.`  
`TRANSCRIPT OF PROCEEDINGS (VOLUME II)`  

Below the title, centered in ten-point Courier, was her name, her credentials, and her license number:

`RUTH M. KELLEHER, RPR, CRR`  
`OFFICIAL COURT REPORTER`  
`LICENSE NO. OH-4109`  

"What about it?" Ruth asked.

Cross didn't open the book. He turned it over once in his thin, scrubbed hands, as if he were weighing the linen thread that held the signatures together.

"You signed the jurat on page six hundred and eighty," Cross said. "The seal is the red embossed seal of the Clerk of Courts, signed by Paul Slocum as deputy. You attested that these six hundred and eighty pages are a true, accurate, and verbatim transcription of the shorthand notes taken by you upon the trial of said cause."

"That is what the certificate says," Ruth said. "That is what I type on every record that leaves this building."

"Did Marcus Thorne testify that he saw the gun on the floorboard before he reached for the registration, or did he testify that he didn't know the gun was in the car until Sergeant Miller pulled it out from under the spring housing?"

Ruth did not blink. A court reporter did not answer questions about testimony from memory; that was the first rule drummed into the students at the old Academy of Steno Arts down in Dayton before the school went bankrupt and sold its desks to an accounting firm. *The reporter has no memory. The reporter has only the notes.*

"I took four thousand pages of testimony in November, Mr. Cross. I took three civil malpractice depositions, a six-day toxic tort arbitration over the chemical plant in Mingo Junction, and forty-two arraignments. If you want to know what the witness said, read the lines."

"I did read the lines," Cross said. He stepped closer. His eyes behind the wire frames were pale hazel, the whites threaded with tiny red veins that came from sitting up under fluorescent lights with appellate briefs until three in the morning. "I read lines twelve through fourteen on page four-fourteen. Then I went to the county jail and I read them to Marcus Thorne through the perforated steel partition in Visiting Booth C. And Marcus began to hit his forehead against the cinderblock wall until the guards came in and put him in four-point restraints."

Ruth shifted the Luminex against her ribs. The aluminum stalk had grown cold against her forearm.

"Defendants don't like transcripts," she said.

"Marcus isn't an idiot," Cross whispered. "He knew he was looking at twenty-four years without paper if the jury thought he had knowledge of the weapon. That was the whole case. That was the only issue on the Rule Twenty-nine motion. If the gun was his, it was armed career criminal. If he didn't know it was under the seat, it was a misdemeanor joyriding charge on his cousin's Buick."

Cross reached out and peeled back the blue pressboard cover. His thumbnail was bitten down to the quick. He flipped past the index of witnesses, past the marking of the state's exhibits, past the direct examination of the patrolmen, until he stopped at a leaf with a dog-eared lower corner.

"Look at line eleven," Cross said.

Ruth did not want to look. Every instinct in her body—eighteen years of muscle memory, eighteen years of sitting thirty inches from the witness chair while men described torn livers, shattered femurs, thirty-dollar drug debts settled with cheap chrome-plated revolvers behind a bowling alley—told her to turn her shoulder, unlock the Reporters’ Pool door, and pull the brass bolt shut behind her.

Her eyes dropped to the paper.

The font was Courier New, twelve-point, ten pitch, twenty-five lines to the page, exactly as prescribed by Ohio Rule of Superintendence 40. The margins were one and three-quarter inches on the left, three-eighths on the right.

`10    Q.  When you put your hand down between the cushions,`  
`11        did you feel the steel of the receiver?`  
`12    A.  I felt the steel right away. I knew the gun was`  
`13        down there before the officer reached through the glass.`  
`14    Q.  And you didn't tell him?`  
`15    A.  No. I didn't say nothing.`  

Ruth stared at lines twelve and thirteen.

A strange, hollow sensation opened in the pit of her stomach, not unlike the feeling when an elevator cable slipped half an inch on the hoistway sheaves in the county tower.

She knew Marcus Thorne’s voice. She had listened to it for two days. He had a deep, slow, gravelly baritone that came from smoking unfiltered Kools behind his uncle’s auto repair shop since he was fourteen, and he spoke with the flat, dragging cadence of the hill country down along the West Virginia border. He did not say *right away*. He never used the phrase. When the prosecutor had pressed him on the timing of the stop on Route 7, he had used the word *directly* four times in ten minutes, until Judge Harlan had told him to speak plain English.

And she knew the strokes.

In steno, *right away* was a two-stroke brief or a single chord if you wrote Phoenix theory: `RAEU` on the initial keys, `WAEU` on the final. But Ruth didn't write Phoenix. She had been trained in 1998 on StenEd, the old, heavy, phonetic system where every vowel had to be accounted for with the thumb cluster. To write *I felt the steel right away*, her fingers would have had to drop into four separate movements:

`EU` (I)  
`TP-LT` (felt)  
`T-` (the)  
`STAOEL` (steel)  
`RAOEUGT` (right)  
`A/WAEU` (away)  

Her thumbs did not remember those strokes.

The fingers of a court reporter are not like the fingers of a typist. A typist thinks of letters; a stenographer thinks of pressure and sound. If you have typed seven million words of criminal testimony, your hands possess an autonomous, physical archive of every violent event that has been spoken into a microphone in your county. If a witness said *I felt the steel right away*, her left middle finger would have a slight ghost-ache from the double-depression of the `T-P` bank.

There was no ache.

"That's what's in the volume," Ruth said. Her throat was dry.

"Is that what he said, Ruth?"

She looked up from the paper into Cross’s pale, bloodshot eyes.

"The transcript is certified," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else’s voice played back through an answering machine speaker across an empty room. "My certificate is on page six hundred and eighty. If you have an issue with the record, you file an App.R. 9(E) motion to correct the record with the Seventh District, and the court will hold a hearing."

"A hearing before whom?" Cross said softly. "Before Harlan? Harlan sentenced him to twenty-two years. Harlan said on the record that the defendant’s admission on page four-fourteen demonstrated a total lack of remorse and an active intent to ambush a peace officer."

Cross took the blue book back and let the cover snap shut against the pages. The sound was like a small pistol shot in the empty, tiled hall.

"I called the Clerk's office yesterday," Cross added, his voice dropping another notch. "I asked for the electronic backup. The RTF file. The ASCII text you upload to the county server when you file the hard copy. You know what the clerk told me?"

Ruth felt the blood leave her ears. "The Clerk's office handles the digital archive."

"Paul Slocum told me the server had a drive failure in January," Cross said. "He said the backup array for the fourth quarter of twenty-three was corrupted during the cloud migration. He said the only official, legal, existing record of *State against Thorne* is the paper volume filed on January sixteenth. The one with your red stamp. The one with your name on the linen spine."

Cross took a step back, turned, and began to walk down the hall toward the elevator bank. His rubber soles made a rhythmic, sticky sound on the damp runner: *shuck, shuck, shuck*.

Ruth stood by the door of the Reporters’ Pool. She took her key out of her wool cardigan pocket, her hand trembling so slightly that the brass tip scratched twice against the escutcheon before the cylinder turned.

### Chapter 2

The Reporters’ Pool on the third floor had no windows.

Forty years ago, when the courthouse was remodeled during the Carter administration, the county had divided the old law library with floor-to-ceiling partitions of yellowed gypsum board covered in burlap fabric that had long since faded to the color of dried oatmeal. Four desks were squeezed between two rows of gray steel filing cabinets.

Two of the desks were empty. One belonged to Betty Miller, who had taken early retirement in 2022 after her wrists developed bilateral carpal tunnel so severe she could no longer hold a tea mug with one hand. The other had belonged to young Greg Chen, who had lasted seven months before taking a remote job with a deposition agency in Chicago that paid twice the county rate and didn't require him to sit through autopsy photos of three-year-olds found in the river.

The third desk belonged to Marsha Klein. Marsha was sixty-one, had her gray hair pinned up with plastic tortoise-shell combs, and was sitting with a foot pedal beneath her orthopedic shoes, transcribing a probate hearing on an ancient IBM clone whose cathode-ray tube monitor cast a greenish pallor over her knuckles.

Marsha didn't stop typing when Ruth came in. The pedal went *thump-thump-thump* under her heel.

"Harlan keep you late?" Marsha asked through her headset. She wore a single-ear transcription foam donut, the left ear bare to the room.

"Suppression," Ruth said.

"They ever find that Glock in the culvert?"

"No," Ruth said. "The patrolman lost the evidence bag between the ditch and the cruiser."

"They'll convict him anyway," Marsha said, her hands dancing across the keyboard with the sharp, uneven staccato of a standard QWERTY machine. "The county needs the bed days. If they don't keep the third floor of the jail at eighty percent, the federal grant for the opioid task force gets cut in October. Slocum had a memo about it on the board this morning."

Ruth walked to her desk in the corner.

Her workstation was different from Marsha’s. It was completely silent. On her desk sat a twenty-four-inch Dell monitor mounted on a hydraulic arm, a high-speed document scanner that she used for marking exhibit receipts, and the docking cradle for her Luminex.

She set the steno machine down into the padded cradle. The USB connection engaged with a soft, electronic double-tone.

On the screen, her CAT software—*Case CATalyst Version 21*—woke from its power-saving sleep. The dialog box appeared:

`FILES DETECTED ON REMOVABLE MEDIA (SD-CARD 1):`  
`2026-11-02_COURT2A_HARLAN.sgx (4.2 MB)`  
`DO YOU WISH TO READ AND TRANSLATE?`  

Ruth didn't click *Read*.

She reached into the lower right drawer of her desk, beneath the box of spare rubber key-pads, the bottles of lens cleaner, and the three unopened bottles of naproxen sodium she kept for her forearms, and pulled out a small, flat black plastic case with a velvet lining.

Inside the case were nine high-capacity SanDisk Extreme SD cards, each labeled with a strip of white masking tape and a fine-tipped black Sharpie.

Every modern steno machine has two memory cards. Card One is the working card: the machine writes to it while you type, and when you plug the machine into the computer, the software reads the file, runs it against your personal translation dictionary, and turns the chords into English words. When the card gets full, you format it and start over.

Card Two is the internal card. It sits behind a small steel plate on the underside of the chassis, held in place by two micro-Torx screws. It records raw binary steno. Not words. Not English. It records the exact microsecond timestamp of every key depression, the depth of the stroke in millimeters, and the hexadecimal value of the twenty-two switches. It records the physical event of your hands.

Nobody ever looks at Card Two. In eighteen years, Ruth had never known an attorney, a judge, or a clerk who even knew the plate was there. When a reporter died or retired, the county auctioned off the machine or traded it in to the factory in Elmhurst, Illinois, where a technician with a magnetized screwdriver stripped the internal memory and wiped the board with a diagnostic ROM.

Ruth opened the plastic case.

She ran her thumb along the white tape labels until she reached the card marked: `2023_OCT-DEC_RAW_BKUP`.

Her hand was cold. She could feel the pulse in the pad of her middle finger—that steady, heavy throb that came after four hours of continuous high-speed writing.

"Marsha," Ruth said.

The *thump-thump* of the foot pedal stopped. Marsha turned her head, the gray bun shifting on her collar.

"Yeah, Ruthie?"

"When you filed the year-end transcripts for the grand jury in January, did you drop the text files on the shared network drive, or did you give the thumb drives to Paul Slocum directly?"

Marsha pulled off her foam headset and let it dangle around her neck like a stethoscope. She reached for a plastic bottle of diet ginger ale that had gone flat on her blotter.

"Slocum came around with that blue external drive of his," Marsha said, taking a sip. "The one with the yellow rubber bumper around it. Said the commissioners were auditing the storage costs on the county server. Said they were moving all the completed records off the local network into the state archival cloud down in Columbus."

"Did he give you a receipt?"

Marsha laughed. It was a dry, coughing bark that smelled of menthol lozenges.

"A receipt? From Paul? Since when does Paul Slocum give a receipt for anything smaller than an air-conditioning compressor? He told me if I didn't have the grand jury files ready by the fifteenth, he’d report the pool to the presiding judge for a docket backlog. I just handed him the two Kingston sticks and told him to sign the log-book on the wall."

Ruth looked at the wall beside the heavy fire door. A clipboard hung from a bent brass nail, holding four sheets of carbonless transfer paper.

"Did he sign it?"

"He put his initials," Marsha said, slipping the headset back over her ear. "P.V.S. Same as he’s done since he was twenty-two and running the mail cart for old Judge Cleary. Why? Somebody lose a record?"

"No," Ruth said. "Just checking an exhibit reference."

Marsha nodded, her foot found the pedal, and the room filled again with the dry, mechanical chatter of probate hearings from three weeks ago: *I direct that all my just debts, funeral expenses, and the costs of the administration of my estate...*

Ruth took a high-speed multi-card reader from her pencil cup and plugged it into the secondary USB port on the front of her tower.

She slid the SanDisk card into the slot.

A small blue LED on the reader began to flicker.

On her monitor, a file directory opened:

`E:\RAW_DATA\2023\`  
`  10-14-23_CR-771_STATE_V_BOWERS.raw`  
`  10-22-23_CV-109_MILLER_V_OHIO_POWER.raw`  
`  11-06-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D1.raw`  
`  11-07-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D2.raw`  
`  11-08-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D3.raw`  
`  11-09-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D4.raw`  

Ruth stared at the fourth file: `11-09-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D4.raw`.

November 9, 2023. Day Four of the trial. The day the defense called Marcus Thorne to the stand.

She did not open it through *Case CATalyst*. If she opened it through the software, the program would automatically apply the user dictionary currently residing in her registry—a dictionary that had been updated three hundred times since November.

Instead, she opened a low-level diagnostic utility—a small, bare-bones command-line program called *StenoRead* that an old court reporter from Philadelphia had written in C in 2004 and distributed on an internet bulletin board for certified examiners.

She typed the command into the black terminal window:

`stenoread -x -t "11-09-23_CR-882_STATE_V_THORNE_D4.raw" > dump.txt`

The terminal flickered. Seven million lines of hexadecimal code resolved into a two-column plain text file.

Ruth scrolled down.

The left column contained the timestamps, down to the thousandth of a second. The right column contained the raw steno chords, represented by the standard keyboard characters separated by forward slashes.

She hit `Ctrl+F`.

She typed: `STAOEL` (steel).

The cursor jumped three-quarters of the way down the page.

`14:22:08.112  /W-PB/  (When)`  
`14:22:08.415  /U/  (you)`  
`14:22:08.620  /POT/  (put)`  
`14:22:08.890  /KW-R/  (your)`  
`14:22:09.115  /HAPBD/  (hand)`  
`14:22:09.340  /TKOUPB/  (down)`  
`14:22:09.610  /TWEPB/  (between)`  
`14:22:09.880  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:10.125  /KUGS/  (cushions,)`  
`14:22:10.740  /TK-D/  (did)`  
`14:22:10.910  /U/  (you)`  
`14:22:11.105  /TPAOEL/  (feel)`  
`14:22:11.310  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:11.580  /STAOEL/  (steel)`  
`14:22:11.820  /OF/  (of)`  
`14:22:12.015  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:12.310  /RAOEFR/  (receiver?)`  

Ruth leaned forward until her forehead was four inches from the glass of the display.

Her breath fogged the lower bezel.

Here was the witness's answer:

`14:22:13.410  /EU/  (I)`  
`14:22:13.680  /SK-P/  (didn't)`  
`14:22:13.910  /TPAOEL/  (feel)`  
`14:22:14.125  /TPHOEG/  (nothing)`  
`14:22:14.390  /KW-L/  (cold.)`  
`14:22:14.810  /EU/  (I)`  
`14:22:15.040  /TPHOT/  (never)`  
`14:22:15.280  /TPHOU/  (knew)`  
`14:22:15.510  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:15.740  /TKPWHUPB/  (gun)`  
`14:22:15.980  /WAS/  (was)`  
`14:22:16.210  /TPHO/  (in)`  
`14:22:16.440  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:16.690  /KAR/  (car)`  
`14:22:17.110  /SKP-T/  (until)`  
`14:22:17.380  /T-/  (the)`  
`14:22:17.610  /TPAEUS/  (officer)`  
`14:22:17.920  /P-LD/  (pulled)`  
`14:22:18.140  /T-/  (it)`  
`14:22:18.390  /OUT/  (out.)`  

Ruth sat back in her chair.

The burlap walls of the cubicle seemed to draw inward, absorbing the light from the fluorescent tubes overhead.

The raw stroke log was clear. Marcus Thorne had never said *I felt the steel right away*. He had never said *I knew the gun was down there before the officer reached through the glass*.

He had testified: *I didn't feel nothing cold. I never knew the gun was in the car until the officer pulled it out.*

Those were the twenty-two words he had spoken under oath on November 9, 2023, at two twenty-two in the afternoon, while the rain beat against the arched glass of Courtroom 2A. Ruth’s fingers had registered those exact chords. The microsecond intervals between strokes showed the rhythmic hesitation of a Southern-born boy trying to remember whether he had touched the seat belt buckle: three hundred and twenty milliseconds between *car* and *until*.

Someone had taken her certified file—the electronic transcript that carried her digital certificate, her license number, and her professional jurat—and had replaced those twenty-two words with twenty-four words of pure, fatal confession.

And then they had printed the volume on the heavy sixty-pound bond paper used for appellate records, stamped it with the mechanical seal of the Court of Common Pleas, and filed it with the Seventh District Court of Appeals.

And on the last page of that volume, under the solemn oath that certified she would forfeit her license and face five years in the Marysville Reformatory for Women if she knowingly filed a false record, was her own signature.

Written in blue Pilot G-2 rollerball ink.

The exact pen she had used every day of her life since her thirty-fifth birthday.

### Chapter 3

Ruth did not go down to the cafeteria for lunch.

She took the small velvet case, the SD card reader, and the printout from the diagnostic utility, put them into her leather satchel, and walked out the back stairwell of the third floor.

The stairs in the old annex were cast iron, painted over with thirty coats of industrial battleship gray that had chipped away at the nosings to reveal the yellow primer underneath. The shaft was cold, ventilated only by an unglazed louvre at the roofline that let in the sound of traffic from the interstate bridge half a mile south.

She came out on the basement level, where the floor turned from terrazzo to bare, sealed concrete that had cracked into map-like networks of white salt lines.

The office of the Clerk of Courts occupied four thousand square feet beneath the old probate vaults. It was divided by rows of heavy green metal shelving on mechanical carriages—the *Spacesaver* system that moved along steel tracks set into the floor whenever someone turned the three-spoked aluminum wheels at the ends of the aisles.

The air smelled of old sulfur-bleached wood pulp, decomposing glue, and the ozone that drifted from the industrial Xerox machines running five hundred pages a minute in the back copying room.

Behind the counter sat Brenda Vance. She was fifty-two, wore silver-rimmed bifocals on a beaded lanyard around her neck, and was sorting traffic citations into plastic bins with the quick, indifferent rhythm of a postal clerk.

"Paul in?" Ruth asked, her hand resting on the smooth laminate counter.

Brenda didn't look up immediately. She finished sliding a yellow three-part carbon form into the *A-D* basket before she pushed her glasses up her nose with the side of her thumb.

"Back in the micro-film bay," Brenda said. "They’re trying to clear out the ninety-four domestic relations files before the auditors come down from Cleveland on Monday. He’s been in a mood since eight this morning."

"Thanks, Brenda."

Ruth walked down the central aisle, between the high rows of domestic relations filings from the nineteen-eighties. The blue cardboard file jackets stood shoulder to shoulder on the shelves, thousands of broken marriages, child-support defaults, and restraining orders held together by rusted two-pronged Acco fasteners that stained the manila folders with orange rings.

At the end of aisle seven, where the ceiling pipes were wrapped in horsehair insulation and white canvas jackets, a single fluorescent fixture hummed over a gray metal desk.

Paul Slocum was sitting on a swivel stool with a cup of lukewarm gas-station coffee in his hand.

He was fifty-six years old. He had been a high school running back for Blackstone High in the late eighties, and his shoulders still had the broad, thick, forward-slumping posture of a man who had spent three decades carrying heavier loads than he had been built for. His hair was iron-gray, cropped close to his skull to hide the receding corners, and he wore a white short-sleeved dress shirt with a gray polyester tie that was loosened two inches at the collar.

He was looking at a yellow legal pad covered in pencil figures—the storage fees the county owed to an iron-mountain facility outside Akron.

"Paul," Ruth said.

Slocum didn't start. He was not a man who started. He had worked in the courthouse since the year the county jail was placed under federal consent decree for overcrowding, and he had seen three county commissioners indicted, two sheriffs removed for election fraud, and a common pleas judge carry his own furniture out to a pickup truck at midnight after a state ethics investigation.

He turned slowly on the stool, his pencil resting between his first two fingers.

"Ruthie," he said. His voice was a quiet, dry rasp, the result of two packs of Chesterfields a day smoked on the loading dock behind the boiler room. "Harlan done with you for the afternoon?"

"He's in chambers until two."

"Good. He shouldn't be pushing those afternoon dockets anyway. His face looked like a beet yesterday during the Miller arguments. If he drops on the bench, we've got ninety days of visiting judges from Steubenville who don't know where the light switches are."

He took a sip of the coffee, grimaced, and set the paper cup down on a metal filing cabinet.

"What do you need?"

Ruth did not sit down. There was an empty folding chair beside the microfilm reader, but she remained standing, her satchel hanging from her shoulder, her hands clasped loosely in front of her cardigan.

"Julian Cross was on the third floor twenty minutes ago," she said.

Slocum’s eyes did not leave her face. He did not blink. A small muscle in the corner of his left jaw, right above the hinge of the bone, gave a single, almost imperceptible twitch.

"Cross is an annoyance," Slocum said evenly. "He thinks because he spent four years in Columbus eating box lunches with the federal public defenders that he can come down here and tell us how to file our records. He’s two months behind on his docket fees for that habeas petition on the West Side arson."

"He had Volume Two of the Thorne trial," Ruth said.

"Did he?"

"He had the bound volume with my jurat on page six hundred and eighty."

Slocum picked up his pencil again. He began to turn it between his fingers, tapping the pink rubber eraser against the green blotter with a slow, metronomic cadence: *tap, tap, tap*.

"Cross has a client who's doing twenty-two years at Mansfield," Slocum said. "When a boy goes to Mansfield on a twenty-year stretch, his lawyer tries to find a typo in the jury instructions or a missing signature on the warrant return. It’s what they do. You know that, Ruthie. You’ve seen forty of them come and go."

"It's not a typo, Paul."

The pencil stopped.

The silence in the basement was heavy, filled only with the low, hydraulic hum of the sump pump in the corner drain and the distant, rhythmic clank of the steam pipes running up to the courtrooms above.

"What is it, then?" Slocum asked.

"Lines twelve and thirteen on page four hundred and fourteen," Ruth said. Her voice remained down in that flat, even, professional register that she used when an attorney asked her to read back a question to an obstinate witness. "Marcus Thorne testified that he didn't know the gun was in the car. He testified that he didn't feel anything cold between the cushions. The transcript filed with the Seventh District says he felt the steel right away and knew the gun was there before the sergeant reached the glass."

Slocum looked down at his yellow legal pad.

He drew a neat, straight pencil line beneath a column of figures, then another line parallel to it, double-underlining the total.

"Transcripts have errors," he said. "You take two thousand words an hour for four days straight, fingers slip. CAT software makes a mistranslate. You write *not* and the machine reads *now*. It’s why the appellate rules have a procedure for correction. Cross can file his motion."

"My fingers didn't slip, Paul."

Ruth reached into her satchel. She did not take out the diagnostic printout, and she did not take out the SD card. She took out her reading glasses—a pair of inexpensive tortoiseshell frames from the drugstore—and held them between two fingers.

"In StenEd, *felt the steel right away* isn't a mistranslate for *didn't feel nothing cold*," she said. "They don't share a single stroke. They don't share a single initial consonant. The chords are on opposite sides of the board. The machine didn't write those lines. A human being sat at a word processor, highlighted twenty-two words of my ASCII file, typed in twenty-four different words, and recompiled the PDF."

Slocum leaned his elbows on the desk. He folded his hands together, his thick, blunt fingers interlocking over the gray tie.

The fluorescent tube overhead flickered twice, its ballast giving a faint, bacon-fat sizzle before settling back into a sickly greenish glow.

"Who recompiled it, Ruth?" Slocum asked softly.

"You told Cross the server had a drive failure in January," Ruth said. "You told him the fourth-quarter backup array was corrupted during the cloud migration."

"It was," Slocum said. "The IT contractor from Youngstown dropped the ball on the mirror. The commissioners wouldn't pay for the redundant off-site storage. We lost two terabytes of draft filings and audio tracks from three courtrooms. It’s in the county administrative minutes. It was an open meeting. The *Gazette* had three paragraphs on it on page six."

"The backup array didn't lose the Thorne file," Ruth said.

"How do you know that?"

"Because the Thorne file was filed on January sixteenth," Ruth said. "The cloud migration wasn't approved by the Board of Commissioners until February second. I checked the docket book on the counter before I walked back here. The journal entry for the Thorne transcript was signed by you on January sixteenth at nine-fifteen in the morning. Two hours before you took the hard copies to the post office."

Slocum looked at her for a long time.

His eyes were small, dark, and deep-set beneath thick gray brows that had grown wild with age. There was no anger in them. There was no fear. There was only the dull, heavy, immovable weight of an animal that had lived inside a stone barn for forty years and knew the thickness of every wall.

"You're forty-five, Ruthie," he said.

The shift in his tone was so slight that if she had not spent eighteen years tracking the microscopic transitions of voice in cross-examinations, she would have missed it entirely.

"What does that have to do with page four hundred and fourteen?"

"You've got twenty-six years in the Public Employees Retirement System," Slocum said, speaking with a slow, deliberate clarity, as if he were dictating a formal entry to a probate clerk. "You started in ninety-eight as a floater in the municipal court down in Bellaire. You transferred up here in two thousand and six, right after your girl was born."

He paused, letting the mention of her daughter hang in the damp air between the rows of old divorce records.

"Four more years," Slocum continued. "At thirty years of service, with your Grade Eight longevity steps, you pull seventy-two percent of your highest three years for the rest of your life. That’s forty-eight hundred dollars a month. Health insurance through the state system. Dental. Vision. You get to keep your machine. You can do private arbitrations down in Wheeling two days a month for cash money and take your granddaughter to the state park in the summer."

Ruth felt her throat constrict. Her knuckles tightened on the strap of her satchel until the brass buckle pressed into the bone of her thumb.

"Paul," she whispered.

"You’re a good reporter, Ruthie," Slocum said. He stood up from the stool. He was not tall, but he was thick through the chest and his waist had not widened like the other men who sat behind the desks on the second floor. He stood with his feet twelve inches apart, his weight balanced, like a man waiting on the line of scrimmage in the mud of a November afternoon thirty-five years ago.

"The best we've had since old man Varner died," he said. "You don't take smoke breaks every forty minutes like Betty did. You don't leave untranslates in the record that look like alphabet soup. When you hand in a transcript, Harlan signs the journal entry without even looking at the page count."

He walked three steps toward her, stopping at the edge of the gray metal desk.

"Marcus Thorne," Slocum said, "is a piece of garbage."

"He didn't say those words, Paul."

"Marcus Thorne," Slocum repeated, his voice dropping into a flat, gravelly whisper that seemed to come from the floorboards, "shot a twenty-four-year-old girl through the cheek behind the Sunoco station on Route 7 in twenty-twenty-one. Everybody in the Fourth Ward knows he shot her. The girl knows he shot her. But her brother was in the same car, and her brother was selling fentanyl for Thorne’s uncle, so when the detectives put her in front of the grand jury, she got amnesia. She couldn't remember if the shooter was tall or short, black or white, driving a Buick or a tractor."

He leaned forward slightly.

"The only thing that kept Marcus Thorne from putting three more people in the ground was that stop on Route 7. Miller pulled him over. The gun was under the seat. It had the girl’s blood on the extractor pin. But because the search was thin on probable cause—because Harlan was scared the Seventh District would bounce the suppression—the case hung on whether Thorne knew the weapon was in the vehicle."

"You changed the record," Ruth said.

"I didn't change a thing," Slocum said.

He reached out his right hand. He didn't touch her. He pointed one blunt, gray-haired finger toward the ceiling—toward the floor of Courtroom 2A overhead.

"You signed it, Ruthie."

"I signed what I thought was my file."

"Your signature is on page six hundred and eighty. Your red seal is on the jurat. The county’s name is on the tape. If that record is false, it’s not false because Paul Slocum sat at a computer. Paul Slocum doesn't know how to run a steno program. I don't know an RTF from an egg salad sandwich. The only person in this building who has the software, the dictionary, and the administrative key to compile a certified transcript for Case Twenty-three CR Eight-eighty-two is Ruth M. Kelleher."

He let his hand drop back to his side.

"And if that transcript is fraudulent," he said, "then the person who committed tampering with government records—a felony of the third degree under Revised Code Twenty-nine Twenty-one Thirteen—is the person whose name is on the certificate."

"I have the raw card," Ruth said.

The words came out before she could stop them. They were small, sharp, and cold as glass.

Slocum didn't move. He didn't look surprised.

He smiled. It was a terrible, thin stretching of his gray lips that did not reach his eyes.

"You have an SD card," he said. "A little piece of black plastic you keep in your purse. You know what an expert from the Bureau of Criminal Investigation will say if Julian Cross drags that card into a hearing?"

Ruth said nothing.

"They'll say it's an unverified draft," Slocum whispered. "They'll say a court reporter who realized she made a catastrophic error in a capital murder trial panicked, went home to her personal computer, edited her stroke directory, and generated a secondary file to cover her backside because she was terrified of a civil suit."

He took another half-step closer. Ruth could smell the Chesterfields on his breath, the stale chicory in his coffee, and the faint, sweet smell of the floor wax that clung to his shoes.

"They'll look at your history, Ruthie."

Ruth’s heart stopped.

The basement around her seemed to lose its sound. The hydraulic hum of the sump pump disappeared. The clanking of the steam pipes was gone. There was only the gray face of the man who had been the chief deputy clerk for thirty-two years, looking at her with the terrible, patient intimacy of a priest in a dark box.

"They'll look at the summer of two thousand and sixteen," Slocum said.

### Chapter 4

Ruth walked out of the courthouse through the basement delivery dock.

The rain was heavier now, drumming against the galvanized steel awnings where the Sysco trucks unloaded fifty-pound sacks of frozen tater tots for the county jail. The alley was narrow, paved with old vitrified paving bricks that had sunk into ruts where the water pooled in dark, iridescent slicks of motor oil and road salt.

She walked four blocks north, away from the square, her head down against the wind coming off the river.

Her car was parked in the gravel lot behind the abandoned train depot—a 2017 Honda Civic with two hundred and ten thousand miles on the odometer and rust bubbling around the rear wheel wells like dried brown sugar. She had parked there every workday for eleven years because the county charged fifty dollars a month for a permit in the paved garage behind the annex, and fifty dollars was two weeks of Teresa’s asthma medication.

She got into the front seat, pulled the door shut, and locked it.

The windows fogged almost instantly. Inside the car, the air smelled of wet upholstery, old coffee grounds, and the cedar block she kept in the glove compartment to keep the damp out of her spare steno ribbon cartridges.

She set the satchel on the passenger seat.

*Two thousand and sixteen.*

She had thought that year was dead. She had thought it had been buried beneath five hundred felony dockets, eighty thousand pages of testimony, two changes of the presiding judge, and the slow, grinding erosion of forty-five thousand dollars paid into a second mortgage that had finally cleared the liens on the house in the Lower Fourth.

Ten years ago, her sister Teresa was twenty-eight.

Teresa had worked the swing shift as a dispatcher for the county emergency management agency, sitting in the basement of the sheriff's annex with a headset on, logging ambulance calls from the townships along the river. She had lived in a small, damp apartment over an auto parts store on Front Street, with three cats and a boyfriend named Donnie Vance who had done eighteen months in the federal camp at Morgantown for distributing methamphetamine out of a boat trailer in Wellsville.

One night in August 2016, Donnie Vance had come through the back door of the apartment with three bullet holes through the tailgate of his Dodge Ram and an aluminum briefcase that smelled of ether and burnt lithium.

Twenty minutes later, two deputies from the sheriff's road patrol had kicked the door off its hinges.

They didn't find Donnie. Donnie had gone over the back fire escape and across the railroad tracks before the cruisers had their cherries on. But they found Teresa sitting on the kitchen floor with twenty-eight grams of crystal meth in her purse, four thousand dollars in five-dollar bills stuffed into a coffee tin behind the stove, and an unregistered nineteen-eleven Colt pistol under her mattress that had its serial numbers filed off with a chainsaw file.

In Blackstone County, in 2016, twenty-eight grams and an altered firearm was an automatic eight years at Marysville. No paper. No treatment. No judicial release.

Ruth had sat in that same basement office on a Tuesday evening, two days before the grand jury was scheduled to hear the indictment.

She had sat where she had stood twenty minutes ago—beside the green *Spacesaver* carriages, beneath the sweating horsehair pipes.

Paul Slocum had been there. He had held the two-page narrative report written by Deputy Miller—the same Miller who had arrested Marcus Thorne seven years later.

*Miller's report had two paragraphs on the weapon,* Slocum had said, his voice quiet in the empty basement. *He says he found it under the mattress. But his search warrant was for the trunk of the Dodge. The warrant didn't have the apartment on it. The deputy prosecutor who drafted the affidavit forgot to check the box for the residence.*

Ruth had looked at Slocum then with the desperate, blind, animal terror of a woman whose only family was about to be dropped into a state prison forty miles south of Columbus.

*What does that mean?* she had asked.

*It means,* Slocum had said, *if the grand jury hears that Miller found the gun in the apartment, they'll indict on the firearm spec. Once the spec is on the paper, the prosecutor can't drop it without a written journal entry from the judge, and Harlan won't sign an entry that looks like he's going soft on a girl who was sleeping with Donnie Vance.*

*What do I do, Paul?*

And Slocum had looked at her with those small, dark, ancient eyes.

*You're taking Grand Jury Room B tomorrow,* he had said. *Miller is testifying at nine-thirty. Miller doesn't read his report when he's on the stand; he just talks off the cuff. If the reporter’s transcript of the grand jury testimony doesn't mention the apartment—if the transcript says the weapon was located in the truck bed alongside the batteries—the firearm spec doesn't attach to the girl. It attaches to Donnie.*

*That's tampering,* Ruth had whispered.

*That's family,* Slocum had replied. *Donnie’s already got four warrants. He’s going to Mansfield anyway. Teresa’s got a girl who needs her teeth straightened. You want to see that child raised by the county down at the children's home on Route 4?*

Ruth had gone into Grand Jury Room B the next morning at eight-fifteen.

She had set up her old Protegé machine—the mechanical one with the paper tape that folded itself into neat, rectangular stacks in the plastic basket beneath the keys.

When Deputy Miller testified, he had said: *We went up the stairs, we pushed the door, and I saw the butt of the weapon sticking out from under the box spring in the front bedroom.*

Ruth’s fingers had typed:

`WE/WEPBT/UP/T-/STAEURS`  
`WE/P-B/T-/TKAOR`  
`SKP/EU/SAU/T-/PWUT/OF/T-/WEP`  
`ST-G/OUT/TPR/UPBD/T-/TK-P/PW-D`  

And then, that evening, when she went back to her cubicle to scope the day's record—when the room was empty and Marsha Klein was down at the bingo hall in Martins Ferry—Ruth had opened the file in *Case CATalyst*.

She had highlighted those four lines.

She had replaced them with:

`WE/WENT/TO/T-/TRUK`  
`SKP/EU/SAU/T-/PWUT/OF/T-/WEP`  
`ST-G/OUT/TPR/UPBD/T-/TAEUL/TKPWAET`  

*I saw the butt of the weapon sticking out from under the tailgate.*

The indictment came down without the firearm specification. Teresa pled to a third-degree misdemeanor of attempted possession, received two years of unsupervised probation, thirty days of community service picking up beer cans along the river park, and a five-hundred-dollar fine that Ruth had paid out of her Christmas savings account.

Teresa had lived. She had gotten clean. She was living in Zanesville now, working as an assistant manager at an Aldi, her daughter in high school band, her teeth straight, her life ordinary and safe and small.

And Paul Slocum had kept the paper tape.

Ruth had looked for that paper tape in the incinerator behind the annex three days after the plea. She had asked the janitor if the grand jury bags had been burned.

*Slocum came down and took them himself,* the janitor had said. *Said the court administrator wanted to verify the chain of custody on the narcotic exhibits before the state police picked them up.*

Ruth rested her forehead against the hard plastic of the steering wheel.

The horn gave a faint, muffled grunt under her weight.

Ten years.

For ten years, Slocum had kept that roll of folded paper tape—the green-ruled, two-inch steno paper with the violet ink marks from the ribbon of her Protegé. Her machine had had a chipped left vowel key—the `A` key had a tiny notch on the upper edge where her ring had caught it during a municipal court trial in 1999. Every stroke on that paper had that microscopic notch. A forensics examiner from the state lab could match that notch to her machine in seven minutes with an optical comparator.

If she spoke to Julian Cross—if she went to the Seventh District Court of Appeals with the raw binary dump from Card Two of her Luminex—Slocum would not go to prison alone.

Slocum wouldn't go to prison at all.

Slocum would open the safe behind his desk in the micro-film bay. He would pull out the brown envelope containing the grand jury tape from August 14, 2016. He would hand it to the county prosecutor, and he would say: *Ruth Kelleher has been doctoring transcripts for ten years to protect her family and cover her own incompetence. Here is the first one she did.*

And who would believe her?

The court reporter who had altered a grand jury transcript to save her drug-addict sister from an eight-year sentence, now claiming that the chief deputy clerk—a pillar of the county Democratic committee, a man who had served under five presiding judges, a man whose brother was the president of the county bank—had somehow hacked into her software and altered twenty-four words in a murder trial?

The Seventh District would deny the Rule 9 motion in three sentences.

Harlan would hold her in summary contempt before the lunch recess.

Her pension would be revoked under the Ohio public corruption statute.

And at forty-five, with her hands shaking and her name dragged through every small-town paper from East Liverpool to Marietta, she would sit in a cell at the Marysville Reformatory, thirty miles from where Marcus Thorne was eating stew off a plastic tray in the segregation block at Mansfield.

### Chapter 5

At one-fifteen, Ruth walked back into the courthouse through the front entrance on Fourth Street.

The metal detectors in the vestibule were old, their gray plastic casings smeared with grease from the winter parkas of three hundred defendants a day. A deputy sheriff named Henderson—a man whose father had been the county coroner when Ruth was in middle school—nodded to her as she set her satchel on the inspection belt.

"Cold one out there, Ruthie," Henderson said.

"Wind’s picking up," she said.

She didn't wait for him to look inside the satchel. A court reporter was exempt from the search; she had the white laminate pass with the blue state seal clipped to her lapel, the pass that allowed her to carry recording equipment, batteries, and micro-screwdrivers past the magnetometers without an escort.

She climbed the stairs to the second floor.

Courtroom 2A was still locked. The frosted glass panels in the double doors were dark, reflecting the yellow light of the hallway fixtures.

Ruth didn't go to the Reporters’ Pool. She turned left, down the short corridor that led to the law library.

The county law library had been abandoned as an active research facility ten years ago, when the commissioners canceled the subscription to the hardbound *Ohio Official Reports* and bought two LexisNexis terminals that sat in the clerk’s office. Now it was used mostly by the visiting judges for lunch, or by the juvenile court officers when they needed a quiet place to read psychological evaluations before a detention hearing.

The room was vast, fifty feet long, with high, vaulted plaster ceilings that had been painted with murals of Roman lawgivers in 1912. The figures of Justinian and Cicero were dark with coal smoke and seventy years of cigar ash that had settled into the varnish before the courthouse banned smoking in the nineties.

At the far end of the long mahogany conference table, beneath a green glass banker’s lamp that was not turned on, Julian Cross was sitting with three red-rope expansion folders open in front of him.

He was drinking black coffee from a styrofoam cup that had begun to dissolve at the rim.

He looked up when the heavy oak door clicked shut behind her.

"Ms. Kelleher," he said. He didn't stand up. He looked smaller here, surrounded by ten thousand volumes of the *North Eastern Reporter* bound in decaying sheepskin that shed brown dust whenever a leaf was turned. "Did Harlan change his mind about the recess?"

Ruth walked to the edge of the table.

She set her satchel down on the mahogany surface. The leather was damp, leaving a faint, cloudy oval of moisture on the wax.

"Sit down, Mr. Cross," she said.

Cross hesitated. He looked at her face—at her eyes, at the tight, gray line of her mouth, at the way her thumbs were tucked into the pockets of her cardigan to keep them from twitching.

He reached out and pushed one of the red-rope folders to the side, clearing a space on the table.

"Did you check the software?" he asked.

Ruth did not sit down. She pulled out the chair opposite him—a heavy, leather-backed armchair with brass upholstery tacks—and rested both hands on the top rail.

"If I tell you what's on my card," she said, her voice dropping into that low, flat whisper that did not carry into the corners of the high room, "what are you going to do with it?"

Cross leaned back. The chair groaned under him.

"I file an App.R. 9(E) motion in the Seventh District," he said. "I attach an affidavit from you. You state under penalty of perjury that the transcript filed on January sixteenth contains an unauthorized alteration on page four-fourteen, lines twelve and thirteen. You provide the raw stroke log. We ask for an evidentiary hearing. We subpoena the clerk’s server logs. We subpoena the printer."

"There are no server logs," Ruth said.

"They have to have an image—"

"There are no logs, Mr. Cross. The server was wiped in January. Slocum took the drives himself. The county has a certificate of destruction from an e-waste contractor in Canton dated February third. The only digital file in existence is the PDF that Slocum uploaded to the appellate docket from his own terminal."

Cross stared at her. The pale hazel of his eyes seemed to narrow, the small red veins standing out against the sclera like cracks in cheap porcelain.

"Then we put Slocum on the stand," Cross said.

Ruth let out a short, quiet breath through her nose. It was not a laugh; it was the sound a person makes when someone suggests putting the river on the witness stand to ask why it drowned the cornfields.

"Paul Slocum has lived in this county for fifty-six years," she said. "His uncle was the sheriff who arrested the men who bombed the union hall in sixty-eight. His brother is the chairman of the county board of elections. His daughter is married to the administrative assistant to the presiding judge of the Seventh District. You've been back from Columbus for eighteen months. You have four court appointments a month and thirty thousand dollars in law school debt that you're paying off with thirty-five-dollar-an-hour county vouchers."

"That doesn't change the law, Ms. Kelleher."

"The law doesn't live in the *North Eastern Reporter*, Mr. Cross. It lives on the third floor of this building. It lives in the fact that Judge Harlan’s son-in-law gets the county title work for every road widening from here to Cadiz. It lives in the fact that if Paul Slocum tells the presiding judge that a transcript is verified, the presiding judge signs the journal entry while he’s eating his ham sandwich."

Cross reached out and picked up his styrofoam cup. He held it with both hands, his fingers pressing into the white beadboard until it dented.

"Marcus Thorne," he said, "is twenty-four years old."

"I know how old he is," Ruth said. "I typed his birth certificate into the record on Day One."

"He has a four-year-old boy," Cross said. "The boy’s name is Leo. Marcus’s sister is keeping him in a trailer behind the steel mill in Steubenville. Every Saturday she puts that child into an eight-year-old Chevrolet Cavalier that has no third gear and drives seventy miles to Mansfield. She sits for two hours in a room that smells of industrial floor stripper while Marcus talks to the boy through a pane of four-inch Plexiglas."

He looked up at Ruth, his jaw tight, his voice trembling with an anger that was too small, too weak, too thin to move anything in this courthouse.

"He didn't have the gun, Ruth."

"I know he didn't have the gun," she whispered.

"Then give me the affidavit."

Ruth took her hands off the back of the leather chair.

She stood upright. Her back was straight, her shoulders level—the posture she had maintained through ten thousand hours of human degradation, through three hundred murder trials, through the endless, gray, unchanging procession of men who had done terrible things and men who had done nothing at all, all of them sliding down the same greasy mahogany chute into the state vans that waited in the alley behind the jail.

"If I give you that affidavit," she said, "I will be arrested before the grand jury rises on Friday."

Cross froze.

"For what?"

Ruth looked up at the ceiling—at Justinian, sitting on his marble bench with his scroll and his sword, his face cracked with coal dust, his Roman eyes looking down at the empty mahogany table with the magnificent, ancient indifference of an empire that had been dead for fifteen centuries.

"For what I did," she said, "ten years ago."

### Chapter 6

At two o'clock, Courtroom 2A was cold again.

Judge Harlan was back on the bench. His face was slightly less red than it had been at noon, but his right eyelid had begun to droop—the small, ischemic warning sign that his cardiologist had warned him about three times since the holidays.

The defendant—a twenty-two-year-old boy named Kevin Shattuck who had stolen sixteen thousand dollars worth of copper pipe from an abandoned cold-storage warehouse—sat with his chin on his chest, his orange jumpsuit three sizes too large for his shoulders.

"Mr. Vance," Harlan said, his voice thick with the phlegm of seventy-one years in the Ohio Valley. "Are you prepared to call the chain-of-custody witness, or are we going to spend another forty-five minutes discussing the inventory log from the sheriff’s garage?"

The assistant prosecutor stood up. "The state calls Detective Miller, Your Honor."

Ruth’s fingers rested on the keys of the Luminex.

She did not look up when the door from the holding cell opened.

Detective Miller walked in. He was forty-eight now, six feet two, with a heavy, thick gut that hung over his duty belt and a graying flat-top haircut that had not changed since he was a sergeant on the road patrol in 2016. He wore a brown tweed sport coat with leather patches on the elbows, a gold detective’s shield pinned to his breast pocket, and the heavy, black-soled boots of a man who spent his mornings walking through muddy creek beds looking for discarded crowbars.

He stepped into the witness box.

He did not look at Ruth. He had sat three feet from her eighty times in his career. He knew her like he knew the cigarette machine in the basement or the water cooler on the second floor: an appliance that had been installed before he made detective and would probably be there until the county tore the building down.

"Raise your right hand," Dan Kowalski said, holding the Bible out with his liver-spotted fingers.

Miller raised his hand. "I do."

He sat down.

Ruth stroked:

`T-/W-PB/` (The witness)  
`KW-R/S-D/` (was sworn)  

Miller cleared his throat. He had a deep, resonant voice that filled the room without the microphone—the kind of voice that juries liked, the voice of a high school football coach who would tell you the truth about your son’s blocking assignments even if it hurt your feelings.

"State your name and assignment for the record," Vance said.

"Detective Robert Miller, Blackstone County Sheriff’s Office, Detective Bureau. Assigned to Special Investigations."

"Detective Miller, directing your attention to the morning of November fourth, twenty-twenty-three..."

Ruth’s fingers moved.

`STK-PW-R`  
`TPAOEU-B`  
`K-RT`  
`PH-L`  

The strokes were smooth. Her machine was a good machine; she had cleaned the key-stems with isopropyl alcohol on Sunday night, and the stroke depth was set to three point two millimeters—the shallow, sensitive setting that required only eight grams of downward pressure to trigger the optical sensors.

*Directing your attention.*

She had typed that phrase four thousand times. It was a single stroke: `TK-R-GS`.

Her hands did not need her brain. Her brain was somewhere else.

Her brain was in Mansfield.

Mansfield Correctional Institution was a concrete fortress set among the flat, gray cornfields of Richland County, eighty miles north of Columbus. She had driven past it once, fifteen years ago, on her way to see her aunt in Sandusky. She remembered the high chain-link fences topped with spirals of razor wire that looked like silver caterpillars in the summer sun, the six concrete guard towers with their tinted searchlight bays, and the low, flat, windowless brick blocks where two thousand five hundred men were kept in cells seven feet wide by ten feet long.

Marcus Thorne was in Cell Block Three.

If the Seventh District affirmed his conviction—and the Seventh District affirmed ninety-four percent of criminal convictions from the rural counties—he would stay in Cell Block Three until the year 2045.

He would be forty-six years old.

His son, Leo, would be twenty-six.

His boy would grow up without a father who had ever held him without a guard watching through a glass partition. The boy would grow up in the Lower Fourth, on the same streets where Marcus had grown up, where Donnie Vance had sold his meth, where the patrol cars cruised at four in the afternoon with their searchlights angled toward the front porches to see who was sitting on the plastic milk crates.

And why?

Because Marcus Thorne had been in the car with a gun that had killed a girl two years earlier, and the state had not been able to prove it.

Because Paul Slocum had decided that twenty-two years for an innocent stop was better than six months of an unsolved homicide on the county clearance rate.

Because Ruth Kelleher had wanted her sister Teresa to have thirty days of community service instead of eight years in Marysville.

`Q.  Did you examine the serial number on the copper pipe?`  
`A.  There wasn't any serial numbers. The jackets were stripped.`  
`Q.  How did you identify the lot?`  
`A.  By the manufacturing stamp on the elbows. It had the Mueller brass mark from nineteen-eighty-four.`  

Ruth’s left thumb dropped onto the vowel cluster: `A-O-E-U`.

### Chapter 19

### Chapter 33

The cross-examination of Fire Marshal Varner by the public defender—an old, half-blind lawyer named Frank Corcoran who had done forty capital cases from an office over a harness shop in Barnesville.

She picked up her leather satchel.

She walked down the aisle, stopped behind Maya’s chair, and laid her hand on the girl’s shoulder—that quiet, firm, steady hand that had survived forty million words of human misery.

"Don't ever let them touch the dictionary, Maya," Ruth said.

Maya looked up, her young eyes bright, her face flushed with the victory of the two hundred words.

"What?"

"The dictionary," Ruth said softly.

She looked at the twenty-two black keys of the Cybra—the keys that had no letters, the keys that were blank as river stone.

"They can buy the machines," Ruth said. "They can buy the buildings. They can buy the judges with thirty thousand dollars in campaign contributions from the paving companies."

She leaned down until her breath touched the girl’s ear.

"The words belong to you," Ruth said. "And as long as you keep them true, the state has to listen."

### Chapter 46

On the first Tuesday in March, Ruth traveled to Terre Haute, Indiana.

The United States Penitentiary sat three miles south of the Wabash River, on fifteen hundred acres of flat, gray clay that had been drained by tiled ditches in the nineteen-thirties.

It was a maximum-security institution—the federal death house, where the lethal injection chamber stood behind three sets of airlock doors in the center of the brick compound.

The air outside smelled of limestone dust from the gravel quarries, diesel exhaust from the semi-trucks on Interstate 70, and the cold, wet, iron scent of the Wabash in the spring melt.

Ruth sat in Visiting Room B.

The room had eight booths divided by four-inch panes of laminated ballistic glass that had been scratched with razor blades by forty generations of bank robbers and drug conspirators.

She sat on a backless round stool of stainless steel that was welded to the concrete floor.

The heavy steel door at the back of the prisoner corridor opened with a pneumatic hiss that sounded like a steam iron.

Paul Slocum walked in.

He was wearing the orange cotton jumpsuit of a high-security federal inmate—`BOP REG. NO. 44109-061`—stenciled across his breastbone in three-inch black numbers.

His wrists were handcuffed to a belly chain; his ankles were shackled with three-pound leg irons that clicked against the concrete with a slow, heavy rhythm: *shuck, shuck, shuck*.

He had aged ten years in nine months.

His hair was completely white, cropped down to the scalp by the intake barber at Terre Haute, showing three jagged scars on his crown where he had been hit with a baseball bat during an election fight behind the union hall in 1984.

His shoulders had fallen.

The thick, muscular bulk of the high school fullback was gone, replaced by the hollow, stringy, withered frame of an old man whose lungs were failing him under the weight of thirty-five years.

He sat down on the steel stool opposite her.

He did not pick up the black plastic telephone receiver on the wall.

He leaned forward until his forehead was three inches from the scratched glass.

He looked at Ruth’s face.

His small, dark, deep-set eyes were bloodshot, the yellow whites clouded with the cataracts that had begun to form across the lenses like frost on pond water.

"Ruthie," he said through the perforated stainless-steel speaking baffle set into the glass.

His voice was a dry, whistling gasp, the voice of a man who was breathing through five millimeters of scarred bronchial tissue.

"Paul," Ruth said.

She did not pick up the receiver.

She sat with her hands folded in her lap, her gloves resting on her leather satchel, her back straight against the cold of the room.

"They're tearing down the old jail," Slocum said.

He did not look at her eyes; he looked at her knuckles, at the blue veins that ran across the backs of her hands.

"The commissioners signed the contract last week," he said. "They're putting a parking lot where the boiler room was. Three hundred spaces for the health department. They’re going to plant flowering pears along the alley where the Sysco trucks used to unload."

Ruth said nothing.

"Harlan's daughter came to see me before they moved me from Milan," Slocum continued.

His jaw worked twice, the loose gray skin under his chin trembling with a slow, dry rhythm.

"She said Harlan didn't leave a dime," he whispered. "The medical center in Wheeling took the house in Cadiz for the nursing bills. She said they found forty boxes of old civil dockets in his garage that had been eaten by rats. She had to pay eight hundred dollars to have an e-waste company haul them to the landfill."

He leaned his forehead against the glass.

The cold surface fogged with his breath—a small, gray, circular oval that shrank and expanded with every shallow heave of his chest.

"Why did you do it, Ruthie?" he asked.

It was not anger.

It was not the hatred he had carried down the third-floor corridor in November.

It was the bewildered, petulant, childlike confusion of an old clerk who had spent his life keeping the columns straight in a ledger that had been thrown into the river.

"I gave you twenty years," Slocum said. "I kept your sister out of Marysville. I made sure Harlan signed your voucher every month on the first. You had forty-eight hundred dollars a month coming to you for the rest of your life. You could have sat on the porch and watched the barges."

Ruth looked at him through the glass.

She saw the reflection of her own face in the laminate—the gray hair pinned behind her ears, the small lines around her mouth, the clear, pale gray eyes that had looked at three hundred murder trials and had not blinked.

"Marcus Thorne was twenty-four years old, Paul," she said softly.

"Marcus Thorne was trash," Slocum whispered through the steel grille. "The Fourth Ward has ten thousand Marcus Thornes. They come out of the trailers; they sell their bags on Route 7; they shoot each other in the culverts behind the gas stations. If it wasn't Marcus, it would have been someone else’s boy."

Ruth stood up from the stool.

She did not touch the glass.

She stood straight, her leather satchel over her shoulder, her hands resting against her coat.

"It wasn't someone else’s boy, Paul," she said.

Her voice had that quiet, absolute, final resonance that had ended forty-eight capital trials.

"It was the boy whose name was on page four-fourteen."

She turned toward the heavy steel door that led back to the visitor lobby.

Slocum rose half an inch from his stool, his belly chain clinking violently against the metal rim of the partition.

"Ruthie!" he called through the baffle.

His voice cracked, a high, desperate, whistling rattle that sounded like a bird caught in a chimney flue.

"Who’s going to remember?" he cried. "The building is gone! Cleary is dead! Harlan is dead! Nobody in that county cares what was typed on that paper!"

Ruth stopped at the door.

She did not turn her head.

She looked at the small rectangular window of wire-reinforced glass that looked out over the Indiana prairie—over the flat, black, fertile earth that was waiting for the spring plows, over the road that ran straight as a plumb-line toward the west.

"The machine remembers, Paul," Ruth said.

She touched the buzzer on the frame.

The green light flickered once, the lock released with a heavy, pneumatic *chunk*, and she walked out into the cold March wind.

### Chapter 47

The graduation recital of the College-Conservatory of Music was held in Corbett Auditorium in Cincinnati on the second Sunday in May.

The hall was vast, twelve hundred seats of crimson velvet beneath a circular ceiling of acoustic plaster designed to reflect the sound of eighty instruments without amplification.

Ruth sat in the tenth row of the orchestra.

She wore a navy blue linen dress that Teresa had helped her choose at the department store in Zanesville, a simple silver locket around her neck that held a photograph of her father in his postal uniform, and her new reading glasses with the thin tortoiseshell frames.

Beside her sat Teresa.

Teresa wore a cream-colored silk blouse and a dark skirt, her brown hair cut short around her face, her hands resting quietly in her lap.

On the other side sat Marcus Thorne.

He was wearing a dark charcoal wool suit that Julian Cross had bought him for the occasion, a white spread-collar shirt, and a burgundy silk tie held with a plain silver clip.

His hair was neatly trimmed; his thick, grease-stained fingernails had been scrubbed with pumice until the skin was pink; and his five-year-old son, Leo, sat on his knee, holding a printed program with both hands, his yellow boots swinging three inches above the red carpet.

The lights in the hall dimmed.

The circular chandelier above the stage faded into a soft, amber glow that smelled of warm filaments and polished brass.

The conductor—a tall woman with graying hair in a black tailcoat—walked onto the stage, followed by sixteen members of the conservatory chamber orchestra.

They took their seats.

The oboe gave the A—four hundred and forty vibrations per second, the clean, pure, razor-like tone that set the pitch for every instrument in the room.

Then Katie walked through the stage door.

She was seventeen now.

She wore a long black velvet gown that touched the tops of her patent-leather shoes, her French horn held in the crook of her left arm like a golden child.

The horn was new.

A professional Yamaha 667, with yellow brass tubing, unlacquered, the metal hand-hammered by an old craftsman in Hamamatsu, reflecting the amber stage lights like a curved mirror of liquid gold.

She stepped to the center of the stage, beside the conductor’s podium.

She did not look at the twelve hundred people in the hall.

She looked up at the tenth row.

She saw her mother.

She saw Marcus Thorne.

She saw the boy with the program.

And then her eyes found Ruth.

The girl did not smile.

She nodded once—a quick, proud, solemn movement of her chin that was identical to the nod her aunt had given to thirty-two visiting judges before the first witness took the stand.

She raised the horn to her lips.

Her right hand slipped deep into the flare of the bell.

Her left hand found the three rotary valves:

Index finger on the first.

Middle finger on the second.

Ring finger on the third.

Her left thumb rested lightly on the trigger valve—the four-millimeter lever that switched the horn from the F-tube to the B-flat tube.

The conductor lifted her baton.

A silence fell over the hall—that deep, breathless, sacred silence that comes when twelve hundred people wait together for the first sound to be spoken into the light.

The baton came down.

The horns of the orchestra gave two soft, muted chords in B-flat major:

*Dah-dum.*

And then Katie played.

It was the Mozart Third Concerto—the *Allegro*, the first movement, the theme that Mozart had written on rough paper in Vienna in 1787 for an old friend who had played the horn in an opera orchestra for thirty years.

The sound was not like the courtrooms.

It was not the scream of a mother in the Second Ward; it was not the dry, desperate lie of a detective who had forgotten to read his search warrant; it was not the clicking of twenty-two keys snapping like elderberry twigs under felt slippers.

It was pure, human, uncorrupted breath.

It rose from the wooden floorboards of the stage, it climbed the crimson walls of the auditorium, it filled the circular acoustic dome with a wide, soaring, golden resonance that seemed to gather every tear, every lie, every cold compromise of eighty years in the Ohio Valley and wash them clean in the river of the sound.

Ruth closed her eyes.

She did not listen like a reporter.

She did not count the syllables; she did not measure the intervals; she did not look for the untranslates in the green phosphor of a screen.

She rested her hands in her lap.

Her fingers were loose, warm, still.

The thumb of her right hand rested against the palm of her left, where the skin was soft, healed, whole.

The music moved through the room like the wind through the wild phlox on Eleventh Street—slow, steady, beautiful, and free.

And beneath the sound, beneath the brass and the wood and the stone, the record was complete.

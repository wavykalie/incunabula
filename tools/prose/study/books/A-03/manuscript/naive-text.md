# The Golden Trug

### Chapter 1

The principal hazard of driving Julian Foss anywhere outside the North Circular was his conviction that the English countryside was a decorative backdrop maintained exclusively for his aesthetic approval.

"Look at that copse, Polly," he said from the back of the hired Volvo, lowering his tinted driving glasses by an eighth of an inch with a ringed middle finger. "That is an Elgar copse. You don’t get copses of that temper in the Home Counties anymore. The Forestry Commission has poisoned the skyline with Norwegian spruce. It’s all matchwood now. No moral spine."

"It’s very nice, Julian," Polly Vance said. She did not look at the copse. She was looking at the battery icon on her phone, which had turned an angry, inflamed crimson at three o'clock and was now hovering at four percent. "The turning for the hotel should be after the second bypass roundabout. Keep an eye out for a brown heritage sign."

"An Elgar copse," Julian repeated, louder this time, in the baritone register he had developed in 2014 for the third series of *The Blackfriars Murders* and had never entirely relinquished. "Woodland with an interior life. One feels one could find an abandoned cello under those beeches."

"I’m sure one could," Polly murmured, tapping the glass of her phone to prevent the screen from timing out. 

The screen flickered. The signal bar in the corner dropped from one faint notch of 3G into a stubborn, void-like *No Service*. 

Polly took a short, disciplined breath through her nostrils. It was an exercise taught to her by an osteopath in Finsbury Park after her second month in Julian’s employment: four seconds in, hold for two, release through parted lips while mentally listing three objects in the room that were inanimate and therefore incapable of ruining her afternoon. *Steering wheel. Glove compartment. Bottle of San Pellegrino.*

She was thirty years old, had a second-class honours degree in Comparative Literature from the University of East Anglia, and currently possessed five separate linen handkerchiefs in the zip pocket of her rucksack, three of which were embroidered with the initials *J.F.* and two of which had been used earlier that morning to wipe spilled macchiato off Julian’s suede driving loafers. For four years and eight months she had managed his schedule, his dry-cleaning, his prescription beta-blockers, his gluten anxieties, and the fragile, translucent balloon of his professional self-esteem.

Her official title on the headed invoices she submitted to Julian’s agent was Personal Associate. Julian preferred *Executive Aide-de-Camp*, which he occasionally shortened to *My Good Right Arm* when introducing her to casting directors or bar tenders who looked as though they might require intimidation. 

"Polly," Julian said, his tone shifting from rural reverie to the thin, sharp whine that presaged an administrative inquiry. "The invitation said seven for seven-thirty, didn't it? The red carpet doesn't start before quarter past, surely?"

"Seven for seven-thirty, yes," she said, squinting through the windscreen at the rain-lashed hedgerows of Wiltshire. Or Somerset. The satellite navigation had lost its bearings forty minutes ago near a poultry farm outside Warminster, and they were currently following a succession of increasingly narrow B-roads flanked by mossy drystone walls and signs warning of amphibious crossings.

"Because if there’s a photolithography line," Julian said, "I don’t want to be arriving behind the streaming people. You know what happens. The provincial photographers get exhausted by the reality television boys and by the time someone with three decades on the stage steps out of the car, they’ve packed their flashes into their boots. It’s bad for the agency. It looks like an afterthought."

"There won't be reality television boys, Julian."

"You don't know that. They put them in everything now. You go to a premiere for a Restoration comedy and half the front row is people who sell protein powder on the internet. It degrades the medium. I told Marcus: if there are influencers, I want a separate green room. I’m not sharing an antipasti platter with a boy who has a ring-light attached to his forehead."

"Marcus assured us it was an industry gathering," Polly said, though Marcus—Julian’s third agent in five years, an overworked young man named Marcus Webb who operated out of a glass cubicle in Soho that smelled of vape liquid and oat milk—had delivered that assurance during a telephone conversation while simultaneously ordering a flat white and arguing with an Uber driver. 

*The Glass Orchard*, Julian’s latest cinematic endeavour, was an independent Anglo-Belgian co-production about a retired glassblower in nineteenth-century Ghent who may or may not have poisoned his sister-in-law with powdered cobalt. Julian had spent four damp weeks in a warehouse in Ostend during the previous November playing the role of Uncle Pieter, a character whose dialogue consisted almost entirely of guttering coughs and ominous references to the north wind. The film had not, so far as Polly was aware, secured theatrical distribution in any major territory, but Marcus had called three weeks ago in a lather of synthetic excitement to announce that it had been selected as the opening gala feature for "the South-West Festival."

"A regional spotlight," Marcus had said, his voice crackling over Polly’s headset while she was queuing at the post office to return three pairs of Italian trousers Julian had deemed "insufficiently breathable." "Very boutique. Very curated. They’ve got lottery funding, Polly. Big dinner, civic dignitaries, regional press, tribute to the independent spirit. They specifically asked for Julian. They want him to cut the ribbon, say a few words about the craft, collect the commemorative piece. Black tie. Full velvet if he’s got it. It’s five hundred quid plus travel and subsistence. In this climate, that’s not nothing for a Friday evening."

It was not nothing. In fact, given that Julian’s residual cheques from the 2011 German syndication of *The Blackfriars Murders* had dwindled to eleven euros every four months, five hundred pounds represented two weeks of groceries from the high-end farm shop in Marylebone and the monthly installment on his storage locker in Bermondsey.

"The velvet is in the boot," Polly said, turning the Volvo onto a tarmac lane bordered by a sagging post-and-rail fence. A wooden fingerpost pointed into the drizzle: *MARKET MELBURY 2. GOLF CLUB 1½.*

"The midnight blue?" Julian asked. "Not the bottle green? The green has the silk grosgrain lapels. It catches the key-light much better."

"The midnight blue. You said the green made you look like an expensive billiard table."

"Did I? When did I say that?"

"In the cab on the way to the Laurence Olivier Awards after-party in 2022."

"I was in a very vulnerable state then, Polly. My humours were disturbed. You shouldn't hold a man to statements made in a London taxi." Julian leaned forward, his silver-tipped temples catching the dull grey light from the passenger window. He patted his breast pocket. "Where is the speech?"

"In the clear plastic wallet on the back of my seat. Three pages. 14-point type, double-spaced. I highlighted the Latin quotation in yellow."

Julian reached over, extracted the sheaf of paper with the crisp, theatrical rustle that Polly had heard in rehearsal rooms, radio studios, and the waiting area of the British Airways executive lounge at Heathrow. He cleared his throat twice—a soft, dry *hrrm-ph* that set the small brass crucifix hanging from his watch chain swinging against his waistcoat.

"*'Art,'*" he read, his voice dropping into its most resonant, mahogany timbre, "*'is not a luxury we hang upon the wall of civilization. It is the loam from which our shared humanity takes root.'* Good. That’s good, Polly. Did you write that or did I?"

"You said something like it when we were looking at the bathroom tiles in Wickes," Polly said. "I rounded off the edges."

"It has weight," he declared, satisfied. "Loam. A very muscular word. The regional people like words like that. It makes them feel that London isn't entirely populated by people who drink matcha."

The lane widened abruptly into a bypass lined with newly planted saplings protected by green plastic spiral guards. Ahead, through the mist of rain thrown up by a passing tractor, rose the red-brick bulk of a modern hotel. It stood between an Esso service station and a veterinary surgery, its entrance marked by three flags bearing the logo of the Mercure group, hanging sodden and limp from white aluminum poles.

Polly felt the first cold droplet of professional foreboding strike the back of her neck.

"The Mercure Royal Oak & Spa," Julian read from a roadside hoarding, his glasses dropping another fraction. "A bit... commercial, isn't it? Marcus said boutique. He specifically used the word *abbey*. 'A converted abbey with extensive grounds.'"

"He might have meant the surrounding area," Polly said, though she knew with absolute, icy certainty that Marcus had not meant anything of the sort. Marcus had read the confirmation email on his phone while walking down Wardour Street, hit *Forward* to Polly with the single word *Go*, and instantly erased the event from his memory.

She swung the Volvo into the hotel entrance. The driveway did not feature a red carpet. It featured a row of yellow wheelie bins, an electric vehicle charging post with an out-of-order bag taped over its nozzle, and a chalkboard propped against a concrete tub of dying pansies.

The chalkboard bore an inscription in yellow and white chalk, decorated with small, carefully drawn illustrations of root vegetables:

**WELCOME DELEGATES!**  
**THE 48TH MID-SHIRE ALLOTMENT & REGIONAL PRODUCE GALA**  
*(Wessex Cucurbit Federation & Allied Growers)*  
**AWARDS DINNER: CONDUIT SUITE (LOWER GROUND)**  
**PRIVATE PARTY: NO PARKING IN COACH BAYS**

Julian stared at the board. Polly stared at the board. The windscreen wipers gave two rhythmic, rubbery squeaks against the glass, clearing a semicircle through which the chalk parsnips seemed to gaze back at them with quiet, agricultural insolence.

"Polly," Julian said. The mahogany tone had evaporated. His voice was thin, small, and sharp as a paper cut.

"Yes, Julian."

"Why does that board mention cucurbits?"

"I don't know, Julian."

"What is a cucurbit?"

"I believe," Polly said, her gaze fixed straight ahead on the automatic glass doors of the Mercure Royal Oak, "it is the botanical family that includes the squash, the cucumber, and the marrow."

A silence descended upon the interior of the Volvo, broken only by the tick of the cooling manifold and the muffled roar of the A36 in the middle distance.

"Polly," Julian whispered. "Where is the Ghent glassblower?"

### Chapter 2

The administrative catastrophe, when Polly finally managed to reconstruct it twenty minutes later in the disabled toilet on the ground floor, possessed the hideous, pristine symmetry that only modern digital efficiency can produce.

Her phone, revived by eight minutes of emergency charging from a wall socket behind the vending machine, revealed the truth in two parallel threads of correspondence dated three weeks prior.

Thread One was between Marcus Webb and the coordinator of the Bath & North Somerset Independent Film Forum, regarding the gala screening of *The Glass Orchard* at the Little Theatre Cinema in Bath. Marcus had agreed the date, agreed the fee, and then, with the casual negligence that had seen him lose two clients to rival agencies that very spring, had failed to hit *Submit* on the digital booking contract, leaving it sitting in his drafts folder like an unexploded mine.

Thread Two was between the events secretary of the Mid-Shire Allotment & Regional Produce Association—a Mrs. Enid Bream—and an entertainment agency in Bristol called *West-Country Speakers & After-Dinner Talent Ltd.* Mrs. Bream had been seeking a celebrity to present their annual trophies following the sudden and untimely hip replacement of Mr. Bob Craddock, the retired weather presenter for BBC Points West who had performed the duty with genial competence for nine consecutive years.

*West-Country Speakers* had, in turn, contacted Marcus Webb’s agency seeking a "mature character actor with classical credentials and a rural affinity" who might be available on the third Friday of October for five hundred pounds cash in an envelope and standard petrol allowance. 

Marcus’s intern—a gap-year student named Barnaby whose grandfather had once owned an orchard in Worcestershire—had seen the words *Gala Dinner, Somerset, Friday 24th, 500 quid* on both digital memos, assumed they were the same event, and merged the calendar entries under the heading: *FOSS: PROV/GALA/BATH.*

The confirmation sent to Mrs. Bream had read:  
*Julian Foss (Inspector Dalrymple in 'The Blackfriars Murders') confirms attendance for the Annual Banquet. Will provide own dinner suit. Please ensure vegetarian option contains no nightshades.*

The confirmation sent to Polly had read:  
*All set for the West Country premiere, babe. Full black tie. Big crowd. They're thrilled to have him.*

Polly leaned her forehead against the cool, graffiti-etched laminate of the disabled toilet door. Above her, a fluorescent strip light hummed on a frequency that seemed designed to induce mild cerebral hemorrhage. 

In the corridor outside, a gentleman in a brown tweed hacking jacket and green corduroys walked past, carrying a plastic washing-up bowl lined with damp tea towels from which protruded three extraordinarily long, perfectly straight carrots with their feathery green tops still intact.

"They're scrubbing well, George," a voice said near the water cooler. "The sand in the trench made all the difference. Not a trace of canker on the shoulders."

"It's the lime, Arthur," a second voice replied, rich with the phlegmy satisfaction of the West Country soil. "You get the pH up to seven-point-two and the wireworm won't look at them. Where's the staging?"

"Conduit Suite. Down the service stairs. Mind the fire doors, the spring's fierce."

Polly pushed herself away from the door, adjusted the lapels of her grey wool cardigan, and walked out into the reception area.

Julian was standing near the revolving doors, frozen in the posture of a man who has stepped out of an airlock onto an uninhabited moon. He was still wearing his camel-hair car coat, though he had unbuttoned it to reveal the midnight-blue velvet dinner jacket beneath. The jacket was exquisite: cut by an elderly tailor in Poland Street who had once made waistcoats for Peter O’Toole, with roll collars of heavy silk and jet buttons that caught the flat, fluorescent glow of the hotel foyer with a sullen, aristocratic lustre. 

Around him swirled the human tide of the Mid-Shire Allotment & Regional Produce Association. There were men in beige knitwear with leather elbow patches; women in fleece gilets embroidered with the crests of local horticultural societies; retired bank managers carrying wooden seed trays with the reverent two-handed grip of Byzantine priests carrying icons; and a small, fierce woman in an electric-blue trouser suit who was clutching a clip-board and shouting at an elderly porter about a lack of trestle tables.

Nobody was looking at Julian. A man carrying an entire wooden crate of heritage shallots bumped against Julian’s elbow, said, "Watch your step, mate, these are the exhibition sets," and maneuvered past him toward the lift without breaking stride.

Julian turned his head slowly as Polly approached. His eyes, usually an alert, theatrical hazel, had acquired the glassy, unseeing stare of a stuffed trout.

"Polly," he said.

"Julian."

"There is a turnip on the reception desk."

"I saw it, Julian."

"It is the size of a human infant. It has whiskers."

"It’s an exhibition swede, I believe. A heritage variety."

Julian took a small, shuddering breath. "We are in the wrong place, aren't we?"

Polly looked down at her rucksack. In the side pocket, the emergency tin of throat lozenges rattled faintly against her portable charger. "There appears to have been an administrative... convergence, Julian."

"An administrative convergence," he repeated. He did not shout. Julian rarely shouted when he was genuinely terrified; he retreated into an icy, brittle articulation that sounded like someone walking on frozen celery. "Marcus has sent me to a greengrocer’s convention."

"An allotment gala, Julian. The forty-eighth annual."

"In Wiltshire."

"Technically Somerset. We’re four miles from the border."

"I am dressed," Julian whispered, looking down at his lapels with mounting horror, "for the premiere of a Belgian art film. I have spend forty minutes in the car preparing an analysis of the moral culpability of Flemish glass artisans. I have three pages on the spiritual resonance of the Northern Renaissance."

"It’s very good material, Julian. Perhaps some of the themes—"

"The themes, Polly? The themes? What possible thematic intersection exists between Ghent in 1880 and a crate of subterranean tubers?"

"Loam," Polly pointed out cautiously. "You did mention loam."

Julian closed his eyes. His nostrils flared. "Get the car."

"Julian—"

"Get the car, Polly. We are leaving. We will drive back to London. We will stop at the services on the M4, I will consume a pasty of inferior provenance in the dark, and tomorrow morning I will personally go to Marcus Webb’s office with a heavy brass fender."

"We can't leave, Julian."

He opened his eyes. "Why can we not leave? We are adult citizens in a constitutional monarchy. We are not tethered to the swede."

"We can't leave," Polly said, lowering her voice to a discreet whisper as two women carrying jars of pickled shallots brushed past them, "because the contract was executed through *West-Country Speakers*. The deposit was paid into your account yesterday. If we pull out now without a certified medical emergency, there is a five-hundred-pound cancellation penalty, Marcus’s agency will be liable for the replacement compère, and under clause seven of your representation agreement, Marcus can deduct the entire cost of the gala’s disruption from your winter theatrical retainer."

Julian stared at her. "Marcus has no right—"

"Marcus has the right under paragraph twelve, section B, which you signed in the Wolseley when you were distracted by that woman who looked like Kristin Scott Thomas."

Julian’s mouth opened, then closed again with a faint, dry click. His gaze drifted across the foyer, taking in a trestle table where two women in matching aprons were arranging a pyramid of Bramley apples with the solemn concentration of bomb-disposal experts.

"How much was the deposit?" he asked quietly.

"Two hundred and fifty pounds. Marcus has already taken his commission from it."

"So if we leave, I am out of pocket by seven hundred and fifty pounds?"

"Plus petrol," Polly said. "And the hire car has to be back in King’s Cross by ten tomorrow morning or they charge us for an additional weekend."

Julian stood very still for several seconds. In the silence between them, the distant, brassy blast of a hotel public-address system crackled through the ceiling speakers:

*“Attention all competitors in Class 4, Heaviest Marrow. Staging closes in fifteen minutes. Will the owner of the blue Morris Minor in the coach bay please move it immediately, you are blocking the loading ramp for the Wessex champion.”*

Julian turned his head toward the stairs leading to the Conduit Suite. 

"Polly," he said, and for the first time she detected the faint, pathetic tremor of an actor who realizes the curtain is rising and he is wearing the wrong trousers. "Who is presenting the awards?"

"You are, Julian."

"And who... who was supposed to be doing it?"

Polly checked the bottom of Mrs. Enid Bream’s confirmation email. "A Mr. Bob Craddock. From the television."

Julian’s eyebrows knitted together in an expression of intense, wounded professional pride. "Bob Craddock? The man with the beige cardigans who points at the rain over Gloucester?"

"He has a very loyal following in the cider-growing districts," Polly noted.

"I have played Hamlet," Julian said, his voice rising dangerously. "I have played Coriolanus at the Chichester Festival. I have died of typhus in three different BBC adaptations of George Eliot. And I am replacing a man whose primary professional accomplishment is informing the population of Swindon that it will be overcast on Tuesday?"

"He’s had a hip replacement, Julian. The public is very sympathetic."

"Well, I haven't had a hip replacement," Julian spat. "I am in full possession of my joints. And I am not going down those stairs to hand silver-plated egg cups to men who smell of fish-blood-and-bone."

"Mr. Foss?"

The voice was crisp, female, and possessed the carrying power of a naval starter’s pistol.

Polly froze. Julian, whose professional reflexes were as deeply ingrained as the breathing reflex, automatically rearranged his features from homicidal despair into an expression of gracious, aristocratic condescension before turning toward the speaker.

Standing at the foot of the reception desk was a woman of fifty-two or three, dressed in a sharply tailored charcoal skirt-suit with silk piping. She was holding a clipboard, a silver mechanical pencil, and a glass of warm white wine. Her dark hair was cut into a severe, architectural bob that Polly recognized with an instant, sickening thud of memory from the framed photographs that Julian kept—or had kept, until their acrimonious disposal in a skip in 2017—on the sideboard of his flat in Belsize Park.

"Diana," Julian said.

The word came out not as an greeting, but as the sound a man might make if an anvil had fallen from a great height directly onto his diaphragm.

Diana Foss—now, as Polly recalled with sudden, horrific clarity from a long-ago telephone row regarding alimony arrears, Diana Cloutier-Smith—did not drop her clipboard. She did not spill her wine. She merely raised her dark, razor-sharp eyebrows until they nearly touched the fringe of her bob, her gaze travelling slowly from Julian’s midnight-blue velvet lapels down to his polished patent-leather dress shoes, and then back up to his horror-stricken face.

"Julian," she said, her voice clear, musical, and entirely devoid of human mercy. "How delightful. I didn't know you’d taken up the cucurbits."

### Chapter 3

The Green Room of the Conduit Suite had once been a linen-sorting cupboard and had retained its original ambience with remarkable fidelity.

It contained three grey plastic stacking chairs, an ironing board propped against a fire exit, an industrial dehumidifier that produced a sound like a distant concrete mixer, and a low melamine coffee table upon which someone had placed a plate of iced party rings and a laminated card bearing the Wi-Fi password for 2019.

Julian was sitting on the center plastic chair, his knees drawn together, his camel-hair coat draped over his shoulders like the ceremonial mantle of a deposed monarch. He had not removed his gloves.

"She’s doing the compèring," he said to the wall. "Diana is doing the compèring."

"She’s the vice-chair of the regional trust, Julian," Polly said, pacing the narrow strip of lino between the ironing board and the dehumidifier. "Her uncle was the president of the Wessex Allotment Federation for thirty years. When he died, she took over the charitable trust that administers the prize fund. She does it every year."

"She divorced me," Julian said, his voice hollow. "She took the cottage in Salcombe. She took the Regency sideboard. She took the first edition of *The Seven Pillars of Wisdom* with the spine damage. And now she has taken the Wessex cucurbits."

"She didn't take them, Julian. She was already here. We are the ones who invaded."

"Why did Marcus not know this?"

"Because Marcus is twenty-six years old and thinks anyone over forty lives in a communal cloud of non-specific antiquity," Polly said. She pulled her rucksack onto her lap and began sorting through the contents: three pens, a lint roller, a packet of paracetamol, a miniature bottle of gin she had stolen from an Aer Lingus flight in August, and Julian’s spare dress shirt. "The important thing is that we have forty minutes before the soup course. We need to look at the running order."

"I am not going out there, Polly. I cannot. The woman knows every weakness in my psychological perimeter. In 2015, during the previews of *The Cherry Orchard*, she told me my third-act monologue sounded like an audition for a gravy advertisement. I couldn't look an onion in the face for six months."

"You were very good in *The Cherry Orchard*, Julian."

"I was adequate," he corrected miserably. "The *Guardian* said I was 'sturdy.' A man of my training does not wish to be described as sturdy, Polly. Oak tables are sturdy. Shire horses are sturdy. An actor should be combustible."

There was a brisk, double knock at the door, and before Julian could pull his coat over his head, it swung open to admit Mrs. Enid Bream.

Mrs. Bream was seventy if she was a day, built along the lines of a low-profile brick outhouse, with iron-grey hair set in rigid, corrugated waves that looked as though they could withstand a direct strike from a spade. She was wearing an emerald-green polyester dress with a lace collar pinned by a brooch in the shape of a watering can.

"Right," she said, marching in and planting herself before Julian with the unyielding authority of an agricultural inspector. "You're the turn."

Julian blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The turn. The talent. Bob's replacement." She consulted a sheaf of pink carbon paper clipped to a masonite board. "Mr. Foss. Julian. We had you down as arriving at five for the sound check, but George said you were loitering by the swedes looking lost."

"I was not loitering," Julian said with a sudden, frosty dignity. "I was observing the exhibits. As an artist, I find the tactile reality of the soil—"

"Quite," Mrs. Bream interrupted, not looking up from her papers. "Well, the sound check's done. Young Kevin from the leisure center has set the roving microphone to channel three. Don't tap it. Whatever you do, don't tap it. The last time someone tapped it, the amplifier in the skittle alley blew a fuse and we had seventy pensioners sitting in the dark through the brassicas."

She peered over the top of her reading glasses at Julian’s dinner jacket.

"You're very smart," she observed, her tone suggesting that this was an error of judgment bordering on moral delinquency. "Bob usually wore his beige fleece. The one with the embroidered robin on the breast. It puts the competitors at their ease. Some of the older members from the marsh districts get nervous around velvet. They think you're from the Inland Revenue."

"I assure you, madam," Julian began, "this jacket was tailored in—"

"Never mind that now," Mrs. Bream said, thrusting a printed booklet into Polly’s midriff. "Here’s the schedule. Dinner is at seven-forty-five. Vegetable broth—leek-based, naturally, supplied by Arthur Pendelton from his second-prize trench—followed by cold cuts and potato salad. The vegetarian option is a stuffed marrow. Which I understand you don't eat."

She looked at Julian with an expression of profound, personal disapproval.

"The nightshades," Polly interposed smoothly, stepping between Mrs. Bream and Julian before the latter could explain his intestinal lining. "Mr. Foss has an inflammatory response to the *Solanaceae* family. Potatoes, tomatoes, aubergines."

"He's at a produce gala," Mrs. Bream said flatly. "Half the room has spent six months spraying copper sulfate on their King Edwards to keep the blight off, and he won't eat a potato?"

"A green salad will be delightful," Polly said. "With olive oil, if the kitchen has it. Or lemon."

Mrs. Bream made a sharp, wet scratch on her pink paper with a yellow pencil. "They've got salad cream. If he doesn't want that, he can have a roll and butter. Now: the speeches. Diana will open. She’ll do five minutes on the trust's capital grant for the new drainage ditch at the north allotments, then she’ll introduce you."

Julian stiffened. "And what, precisely, is Diana intending to say by way of introduction?"

Mrs. Bream gave him a look of weary pity. "Whatever she likes, I expect. She's the chair's niece. Then you do ten minutes. General remarks. Nothing political. Don't mention the council's policy on polytunnels, it’s a sore subject after the planning meeting in March, and keep off the badger cull. We have two members from the Vale who had three rows of sweetcorn flattened by badgers in August and they're still not speaking to the people from the wildlife trust."

"My remarks," Julian said, drawing himself up to his full seated height, "will concern the eternal dialogue between human labour and the classical earth."

Mrs. Bream paused, her pencil hovering over the paper. She looked at Julian for three long, terrifying seconds.

"Keep it to eight minutes," she said. "The raffle is at nine, and the butcher’s voucher has to be drawn before the meat warms up. Then the cups."

"The cups," Polly echoed.

"Thirty-two classes," Mrs. Bream said. "Starting with Class 1, The Novice Shallot, and finishing with Class 32, The Open Championship Heaviest Marrow. You hand the trophy to the winner, Diana reads the weight from the card, you shake the hand, you hold the marrow for the local paper."

Julian’s face drained of what little color had returned to it. "I hold the marrow?"

"For the *Mid-Shire Herald*," Mrs. Bream said, as if explaining the alphabet to a backward spaniel. "Bob always held the marrow. He put his arm round it like a baby. The readers like it. Shows you're not afraid of a bit of soil."

"Mrs. Bream," Julian said, his voice dropping an octave into pure, quivering tragedy. "The heaviest marrow... what sort of weight are we contemplating?"

"Arthur’s is seventy-four pounds two ounces," Mrs. Bream said crisply. "Reggie Thorpe’s is seventy-one eight, but there's a soft patch near the stem that the judges are looking at with a magnifying glass. If Reggie’s disqualified for fungal ingress, Arthur takes it. Either way, it's over five stone. Bend your knees when you lift it. Bob didn't bend his knees in 2018 and he spent the whole of the autumn sitting on a rubber ring."

She turned on her heel and marched toward the door.

"Ten minutes, Mr. Foss. Don't touch the microphone. And if you have to visit the facilities, use the ones by the skittle alley. The handle on the ladies' in the Conduit suite has come off again, and if you get stuck in there, George will have to take the hinges off with a crowbar, and we haven't got the time."

The door clicked shut behind her.

Julian sat in his plastic chair. He did not move. He looked like an Edwardian gentleman who has just been informed that his bank has failed, his wife has run away with the groom, and the house is on fire, but whose breeding forbids him from uncrossing his legs.

"Five stone, Polly," he whispered.

"You have very good core stability, Julian. You’ve been doing Pilates on Thursdays."

"Pilates with a foam roller, Polly! Not seventy-four pounds of damp, unpasteurized gourd!"

"We’ll manage it," Polly said, though her own heart was performing a strange, syncopated rhythm against her ribs. She picked up her notebook. "We need to rewrite the speech. The Ghent glassblower has to go."

"I loved the glassblower," Julian moaned. "He had dignity. He had an inner life."

"He had powdered cobalt," Polly said firmly, clicking her ballpoint pen. "These people don't want powdered cobalt. They want drainage ditches, potassium levels, and someone who understands the tragedy of wireworm. We have eight minutes to turn you into a champion of the allotment movement."

"I don't know anything about allotments," Julian said, burying his face in his hands. "I have a window box in Belsize Park that contains three dead geraniums and an empty cigarette packet left by the window cleaner."

"Then we shall improvise," Polly said. "You're an actor, Julian. Play an allotment holder."

Julian lifted his head from his hands. His eyes were wild. "What does an allotment holder sound like?"

"Like someone who has spent forty years waiting for rain," Polly said, "and is furious that it arrived on a Sunday."

### Chapter 4

The Conduit Suite was situated in what had clearly been, prior to the hotel’s 1994 refurbishment, a basement storage area for industrial refrigeration units. 

It was long, low-ceilinged, and paneled in that specific shade of simulated teak veneer that seemed designed to absorb all known forms of light. At the far end, upon a low wooden riser that smelled faintly of dry rot, sat the top table, draped in a white paper tablecloth that was already showing signs of grease saturation near the bread baskets. 

Around the room, eight circular tables were packed with ninety-two members of the Mid-Shire Allotment & Regional Produce Association, all of whom appeared to have arrived directly from their plots without the intermediate intervention of a shower. The air was a dense, humid cocktail of damp Harris tweed, vinegar from the pickled shallot judging, lukewarm brown Windsor soup, and the unmistakable, slightly sweet stench of seventy-four pounds of prize marrow slowly coming to room temperature under forty-watt spotlights.

Polly stood near the service entrance, her back pressed against a stack of spare banqueting chairs, her rucksack between her feet. 

At the top table, Julian sat between Mrs. Enid Bream and a massive, silent man in a blue nylon windbreaker who had been introduced as Mr. Arthur Pendelton. Julian was not eating his roll. He was staring straight ahead at a floral arrangement consisting of three gladioli, two sprigs of parsley, and an ornamental kale that looked as though it had died in captivity.

To his right, Diana Cloutier-Smith was presiding with the effortless, terrifying poise of an empress holding court in a provincial abattoir.

She had already delivered her opening remarks. They had been concise, witty, and had included two sharp, surgical references to "certain elements from London who believe that food originates in small plastic punnets at the back of an M&S Simply Food." The room had roared with laughter. Diana had smiled her cold, diamond-edged smile, adjusted her pearl earrings, and sat down to consume her vegetable broth with the delicate precision of a surgeon dissecting a small bird.

Now, she rose again. She tapped the side of her wine glass with her silver mechanical pencil. The sound, sharp and crystalline, cut through the wet rumble of ninety allotment holders chewing their cold cuts.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Diana said, the microphone held at precisely the correct four-inch distance from her chin. "Growers. Exhibitors. Friends of the Wessex soil."

A murmur of communal satisfaction rolled through the Conduit Suite.

"Every year," she continued, "we gather to celebrate not merely the harvest of our plots, but the values that sustain our community. Patience. Diligence. The refusal to be defeated by the carrot fly."

Laughter. A man at table four shouted, "Hear, hear!" and banged his fork on the table.

"For nine years," Diana said, her tone softening into a masterclass of civic sentiment, "we were privileged to have with us our dear friend Bob Craddock. Bob understood us. Bob knew that when a man spends forty-eight hours sitting beside a pumpkin with an oil heater and an old duvet, he is not exhibiting signs of mental distress; he is defending the honour of the Vale."

Roars of approval. Arthur Pendelton nodded his massive, grey-stubbled head with the solemnity of an Easter Island statue.

"Sadly, as you know, Bob is currently in the orthopaedic wing at Southmead Hospital, recovering from what I am told was a very successful ceramic hip replacement." Diana paused, letting the ripple of sympathy subside. "We sent him a basket of heritage pears from the trust's orchard on Tuesday. He sent back a message saying he hoped the judging in the runner beans was cleaner than it was in 2023."

A collective, knowing gasp ran around the tables. Several people glanced toward a table in the corner where two elderly men in matching beige cardigans were glaring fixedly into their potato salad.

"However," Diana said, and here her voice dropped into a register of exquisite, predatory amusement that made Polly’s fingers tighten around the strap of her bag until her knuckles turned white, "the committee has been fortunate. Through a series of... extraordinary administrative occurrences, we have secured for this evening a guest from the wider world of the dramatic arts."

She turned her head slowly, luxuriously, toward Julian.

Julian did not move. He looked like a man who has heard the click of a tripwire in a jungle path and is calculating whether the punji sticks are poisoned.

"Many of you," Diana said, "will remember the television drama of the early two-thousands. A simpler time. A time when murder investigations took three whole episodes and were conducted by men with leather elbow patches who drove Morris Minors through the Cotswolds."

A few vague nods. An elderly woman near the front squinted at Julian through thick bifocals and said, "Is he the one from *Heartbeat* who had the dog?"

"No, Margaret," Diana said smoothly, her eyes never leaving Julian’s face. "He is not the one from *Heartbeat*. He is Mr. Julian Foss. Mr. Foss spent three series playing Inspector Dalrymple in *The Blackfriars Murders*, a production memorable for the quality of its rain-effects and the tragic cancellation of its fourth series due to declining demographic interest."

A wince ran through Julian’s frame, so delicate it was visible only to Polly and possibly Arthur Pendelton.

"Mr. Foss," Diana continued, "has journeyed down to us today from London. In full evening dress. Because he was under the impression—an impression we must all do our best not to disturb too brutally—that he was attending an international cultural summit."

A dry chuckle began at the top table and spread outward.

"He has kindly agreed," Diana said, her voice dripping with honeyed malice, "to set aside the lofty concerns of the theatrical stage and share with us a few thoughts on our humble endeavours. Ladies and gentlemen... Mr. Julian Foss."

Diana sat down. She did not applaud. She picked up her wine glass, leaned back in her chair, and looked at Julian with the expression of a cat that has not only caught the mouse, but has verified that the mouse has an outstanding tax liability.

Julian stood.

For three terrible seconds, nothing happened. The microphone, resting in its cradle before him, let out a low, ominous hum on channel three. The ninety-two members of the Mid-Shire Allotment & Regional Produce Association stared at his velvet lapels with the stony, unimpressed scrutiny they usually reserved for imported Spanish tomatoes.

Julian reached out. He picked up the microphone. He did not tap it. 

He looked down at the three pages Polly had rewritten for him on the back of the hotel’s conference stationery—pages containing three carefully researched references to potassium deficiency, a joke about slug pellets, and an earnest paragraph about the spiritual value of community compost.

Then, with the slow, deliberate grace that Polly had seen him use only when he was about to do something utterly catastrophic on a live radio broadcast, Julian Foss folded the papers in half, slipped them into the inside pocket of his midnight-blue velvet jacket, and looked directly at his ex-wife.

"Diana," he said, his voice rolling out across the Conduit Suite like a heavy fog across the Solent, "is, as ever, unsparing in her accuracy."

The room went completely quiet.

"It is true," Julian continued, stepping out from behind the top table and moving toward the small open space between the vegetable display and the judging dais. "I arrived this evening expecting the premiere of a film. A film about a glassblower in nineteenth-century Ghent who suffers a great deal in the rain and achieves very little of commercial value."

A snort of laughter came from a table near the door.

"I wore this jacket," Julian said, touching his lapel with two fingers, "because in my trade, we are vain, insecure creatures who believe that if our silk is sufficiently heavy, the world will not notice that we are pretending to be people we are not."

He paused. He looked down at Arthur Pendelton’s seventy-four-pound marrow, which sat on its wooden pallet four feet away, gleaming under the strip lights like a beached whale.

"Your chairman," Julian said, his voice dropping into a tone of quiet, almost ecclesiastical intimacy, "has spent six months producing that."

He pointed at the marrow.

"Seventy-four pounds," Julian whispered. "Five stone of solid, uncompromising vegetable matter. It does not pretend to be Hamlet. It does not attempt to play Coriolanus. It does not ask for four stars in the *Sunday Times* or an Arts Council development grant. It simply *is*."

Arthur Pendelton looked up. His watery blue eyes, half-buried under eyebrows that resembled two clumps of dried sphagnum moss, fixed upon Julian with sudden, intense focus.

"It is the product," Julian declared, his arm rising in a gesture that had once commanded the battlements at Elsinore, "of labour. Of dung. Of vigilance in the small hours of the morning when the slug creeps forth from the ditch to devour the hopes of man."

A low, resonant grunt of assent rose from the third table.

"I have spent thirty-five years," Julian said, his baritone swelling until it vibrated the glass water jugs on the sideboards, "speaking the words of other men. Dead men. Men who sat in comfortable rooms in Stratford or Dublin and wrote about kings who lost their crowns. But who writes the tragedy of the blossom end rot?"

"Nobody!" shouted a man with a beard full of tobacco crumbs. "Not a bloody word!"

"Who commemorates the quiet, heroic struggle of the man who discovers that his shallots have been infected by the white rot?" Julian demanded, his eyes flashing with the authentic, demented fire of a theatrical veteran who has found his audience and will not let them go until they weep. "Is that not drama? Is that not the eternal dialogue between human will and the indifferent soil?"

Arthur Pendelton hit the table with his open palm. The cutlery jumped two inches into the air. "By God," he said, his voice like two gravel carts colliding in a quarry. "He's right."

"Diana tells you that I am from London," Julian said, turning his head toward his ex-wife, whose smile had frozen into a thin, pale line of absolute incredulity. "She tells you that I belong to a world of small plastic punnets. She forgets that before I was an actor, before I was an inspector in a synthetic tweed jacket solving fictional crimes in Gloucestershire, I was a boy in Shropshire whose grandfather had three rods of heavy clay behind the gasworks."

This, Polly knew with mathematical certainty, was an unmitigated lie. Julian’s father had been an ophthalmic surgeon in Cheltenham and his grandfather had owned a timber-importing business in Bristol; the nearest Julian had ever come to three rods of heavy clay was falling out of a punt at Oxford in 1991.

"My grandfather," Julian whispered, the microphone trembling slightly in his grip, "knew that the marrow is not a vegetable. The marrow is a test of the soul."

He stepped forward. He stood directly above Arthur Pendelton’s green behemoth.

"Therefore," Julian said, looking around the room with an expression of profound, almost tearful humility, "I do not apologize for my velvet. I wear it tonight not for the glassblowers of Ghent, but for the growers of the Mid-Shire. For the men and women who look upon the slug and say: *Not today, my friend. Not upon this plot.*"

He lowered the microphone.

For two seconds, the Conduit Suite hung in a silence so complete that the hum of the dehumidifier sounded like an aircraft engine.

Then Arthur Pendelton stood up. He did not applaud. He raised his half-pint glass of brown ale toward Julian and roared: "Good on yer, mate!"

The room exploded. Ninety allotment holders surged to their feet, banging their soup spoons against their water glasses, thumping the tables with their horny, soil-blackened palms, cheering with the wild, unrestrained fury of people who have spent forty-eight years being patronized by television weather presenters and have suddenly found an apostle who understands the theological significance of mulch.

At the top table, Diana Cloutier-Smith took a very slow, very deliberate sip of her warm white wine. Over the rim of her glass, her eyes met Polly’s across the width of the room.

Diana did not blink. She merely tilted her head by a fraction of a millimeter, as if acknowledging that while the opening skirmish had been lost to an unexpected display of theatrical guerrilla warfare, the campaign was still very young, and the seventy-four-pound marrow had not yet been lifted.

### Chapter 5

"The problem," Polly said twenty minutes later, leaning against the cold metal casing of the ice machine in the service corridor, "is the engraving."

"The engraving?" Julian asked. He was leaning against the opposite wall, his velvet jacket unbuttoned, his silk tie loosened by a half-inch of triumphant exhaustion. He was drinking a glass of cider that Arthur Pendelton had pressed into his hand with the insistence of an elder brother offering holy water. "What is wrong with the engraving?"

"Mrs. Bream showed me the cup," Polly said. She pulled her notebook from her rucksack and opened it to a sketch she had made while the raffle tickets were being sold. "The Wessex Cucurbit Trophy is a perpetual shield. It has twenty-four silver plaques around the rim for the historical champions, and a central plate for the current year."

"And?"

"The local committee," Polly said, looking at him with deep, professional sorrow, "does not like to waste time on the night. The engraver in Market Melbury is a ninety-year-old retired watchmaker named Mr. Cloutier."

Julian stopped his glass halfway to his mouth. "Cloutier? As in... Diana’s uncle?"

"Her second cousin," Polly corrected. "He engraved the plate on Wednesday afternoon. Based on the advance booking notice sent by *West-Country Speakers* before Bob Craddock had his hip operation."

Julian lowered the cider. "Polly. What does the central plate say?"

Polly read directly from her notes:  
*“WESSEX CUCURBIT FEDERATION – 2026 CHAMPIONSHIP. PRESENTED BY BOB CRADDOCK, MBE. 'HE KNOWS WHICH WAY THE WIND BLOWS.'”*

Julian closed his eyes. The triumph of his eight-minute improvisation on the spiritual nobility of dung evaporated from his features, leaving behind the pale, doughy reality of a fifty-four-year-old actor in a hotel basement.

"I cannot present a trophy," he whispered, "that states I am Bob Craddock."

"You could present it," Polly suggested, "with your thumb over the lower half of the plate."

"My thumb is not two and a half inches wide, Polly! And what about the quote? 'He knows which way the wind blows'? It sounds like a testimonial for an extractor fan!"

"It’s Bob’s catchphrase," Polly explained. "He’s used it since the 1987 hurricane. Apparently, it has a very soothing effect on the elderly."

The fire door at the end of the corridor swung open with a pneumatic hiss, and Diana Cloutier-Smith stepped through.

She had discarded her clipboard. She was holding a small silver trophy shaped like an urn with two scrolled handles, and a blue velvet box that looked as though it contained something either very old or very lethal.

"A tour de force, Julian," she said, coming to a halt three feet from them. "Truly. The Shropshire grandfather was an inspired touch. Was that the one who left you the brass-mounted microscope you sold in 2012 to pay your osteopath?"

Julian drew himself up. "My grandfather was a man of the soil, Diana. In his soul, if not in his tax returns."

"Your grandfather was an underwriter for Lloyd's who spent his weekends playing bridge in Malvern," Diana said crisply. She turned to Polly, her eyes narrowing with professional assessment. "You must be the assistant. Polly, isn't it?"

"Polly Vance," Polly said, holding out a hand that was slightly damp from the ice machine.

Diana looked at the hand, then at Polly’s face, with the detached curiosity of an entomologist examining a new variety of aphid. "You wrote the bit about the dung, didn't you?"

"The loam was mine," Polly said defensively. "The dung was Julian’s own development."

"I thought as much. Julian hasn't used the word 'loam' spontaneously since he tried to convince the director of *The Bill* that his character should have an interest in landscape gardening." Diana set the silver urn on top of the ice machine. "We have a crisis."

"We know about the engraving," Julian said stiffly. "And I have no intention—"

"Not the engraving," Diana said, cutting him off with a flick of her mechanical pencil. "The engraving is a minor clerical embarrassment that can be masked by a piece of green baize and a quick apology about municipal scheduling. The crisis is Reggie Thorpe."

Polly frowned. "The runner-up?"

"Reggie," Diana said, "is seventy-six years old. He has won the Heaviest Marrow class for four consecutive years. This year, Arthur Pendelton’s marrow is seventy-four pounds two ounces. Reggie’s is seventy-one pounds eight ounces."

"A clear victory for Arthur," Julian said, taking a sip of his cider. "A triumph for the organic method."

"Reggie," Diana continued, her voice dropping into a register of icy, bureaucratic doom, "has formally lodged a protest under Rule 14, Section C of the Wessex Federation Regulations."

"What is Rule 14?" Polly asked.

"Rule 14," Diana said, "governs the structural integrity of the specimen at the time of weighing. A marrow that exhibits soft rot, stem-split, or internal cavitation exceeding one-half inch in depth is deemed non-viable and must be disqualified from the championship class."

Julian stared at her. "Are you telling me that Arthur’s marrow is... non-viable?"

"Arthur’s marrow," Diana said, "has a fissure near the underside. It was noticed during the staging by the steward for roots and tubers. Reggie claims that Arthur has been feeding the marrow with a wick soaked in sugar water through a hypodermic needle inserted into the secondary stem."

"A hypodermic needle?" Polly gasped. "In a marrow?"

"It’s an old trick from the Somerset coalfield," Diana said, as if discussing the use of chemical weapons in the Crimea. "It adds three to four pounds of liquid weight in the final forty-eight hours. The skin swells, the sugar draws moisture from the atmosphere, and by Friday afternoon you have an extra half-stone of unearned mass. But if the skin tears under the pressure, the interior begins to ferment."

She looked at Julian.

"The judges are in the Conduit Suite now," Diana said. "They have Arthur’s marrow on the scales. Reggie is standing there with a penknife and a magnifying glass, demanding an exploratory incision."

Julian’s cider glass remained motionless. "And what does Arthur say?"

"Arthur," Diana said, "is standing by the fire exit with a four-pronged digging fork, informing the room that if anyone puts a penknife into his *Dreadnought*, he will personally invert them into the water butt."

A heavy, muffled crash echoed from the far side of the double doors, followed by the sound of twenty elderly people shouting simultaneously in varying West Country dialects.

"They're starting on the bread rolls," Diana observed calmly. "If the committee cannot resolve this in eight minutes, the police will be called from Warminster. And if the police are called, the *Mid-Shire Herald* will not run a picture of an actor holding a silver cup; they will run a picture of Julian Foss being escorted from a Mercure basement by two constables while an elderly man in corduroys throws fermented squash at his velvet jacket."

Julian turned to Polly. His face was the colour of skimmed milk.

"Polly," he whispered. "The contract."

"Clause nine," Polly said, her mind working with the cold, desperate speed of an air traffic controller whose monitors have just failed. "If the event is terminated due to civil disorder or breach of the peace arising from the conduct of the participants, the appearance fee is forfeit and the guest is responsible for his own extraction."

"We lose the five hundred pounds," Julian said.

"We lose the five hundred pounds," Polly confirmed. "And we have eighty-five miles of petrol to pay for, plus the additional day on the Volvo."

Julian set his glass on the ice machine. He pulled his cuffs down with two sharp, symmetrical tugs. He looked at Diana, whose expression was one of cool, clinical anticipation.

"Where," Julian said, his baritone dropping to its most terrifying, authoritative depth, "is the fork?"

### Chapter 6

The scene in the Conduit Suite had ceased to resemble an agricultural banquet and was now hovering dangerously close to the storming of the Bastille as re-enacted by the members of an allotment society.

At the center of the room, Arthur Pendelton stood before his marrow like a Scottish chieftain defending his clan’s last bull. His four-pronged fork was held at the port-arms position, its forged steel tines gleaming under the strip lights. 

Five feet away stood Mr. Reggie Thorpe: small, wiry, wearing a brown shop coat over a Fair Isle jumper, his face purple with competitive rage, brandishing an ivory-handled folding knife with a three-inch blade.

Between them stood Mrs. Enid Bream, holding her masonite clipboard before her chest like a breastplate, her corrugated waves quivering with indignation.

"You touch that skin, Thorpe," Arthur was bellowing, his voice shaking the suspended ceiling tiles, "and I’ll have you through that fire door backwards! It’s thirty-two inches of solid flesh! Grown on horse manure from the brewery stables! No sugar! No wicks! Pure, honest Wiltshire loam!"

"It’s weeping!" Reggie screeched, pointing his knife at the marrow’s posterior. "Look at the belly! It’s got a drip on it like a faulty tap! That’s sugar-damp, that is! It’s fermenting from the core out! By midnight it’ll smell like a cider press!"

"It’s natural condensation!" Arthur roared. "The room’s ninety degrees because the hotel won't turn the radiators off! She’s sweating! Any decent vegetable sweats in this company!"

The surrounding growers were divided into two distinct, hostile camps. The men from the North Plots—largely retired railway workers and municipal bus drivers—were shouting support for Arthur; the growers from the West Allotments—an alliance of retired schoolmasters, postmen, and a fierce woman who grew award-winning shallots in raised gravel beds—were backing Reggie’s demand for an exploratory puncture.

"Order!" Mrs. Bream shouted, her voice cracking under the strain. "Rule 14 cannot be applied without the consent of the Chief Steward! Where is George? Has anyone seen George?"

"George is in the car park," someone shouted from the back. "He’s trying to get the battery out of the Morris Minor!"

The double doors swung open.

Julian Foss entered the room.

He did not walk; he processed. He had re-buttoned his midnight-blue velvet jacket, adjusted his black silk tie into a state of razor-sharp symmetry, and was carrying under his left arm the green baize cloth that had previously covered the raffle table.

Behind him walked Polly, carrying Julian’s spare handkerchief in one hand and the silver mechanical pencil she had quietly confiscated from Diana in the other.

"Gentlemen," Julian said.

He did not shout. He did not raise his voice above the pitch he had used in 2008 when addressing the Senate of Rome in a three-week revival of *Julius Caesar* at the Sheffield Crucible. But there was something in the timbre—a dry, acoustic whip-crack that had been trained in drafty church halls in Newcastle forty years ago—that cut through the horticultural fury like a cold knife through soft butter.

The shouting stopped. Reggie Thorpe’s knife remained suspended three inches above the marrow’s flank. Arthur Pendelton lowered his fork by an eighth of an inch, his watery blue eyes narrowing in suspicion.

Julian walked slowly toward the trestle table. He did not look at the fork. He did not look at the knife. He looked exclusively at the marrow.

"A magnificent specimen," he murmured, his voice rich with an almost sensual appreciation. "Magnificent. Look at the ribbing, Polly. Look at the transition from the dark jade of the crown to the pale, milk-green belly."

"Exquisite, Julian," Polly said, stepping into place at his elbow like a seasoned stage manager attending an unpredictable star.

"It is not magnificent," Reggie Thorpe snapped, though the authority had drained from his tone. "It’s got a split in the bilge. Rule 14 says—"

"I am familiar," Julian said, turning his head with glacial slowness toward Reggie, "with Rule 14."

He was not familiar with Rule 14. He had learned of its existence four minutes ago in the service corridor.

"Rule 14," Julian continued, stepping between Reggie and the vegetable, "is an administrative instrument designed for small minds who believe that nature can be measured by the calipers of the clerk."

A gasp ran around the room. Mrs. Bream looked as though she had been struck in the face with a wet leek.

"What did you say?" Reggie demanded.

"I said," Julian whispered, leaning forward until his face was six inches from Reggie’s nose, "that you are looking at the work of an artist through the eyes of an excise officer."

He turned to the room, his velvet lapels catching the light as he swept his arm toward the sixty competitors who were watching him with open mouths.

"Is this," Julian cried, his voice filling every corner of the Conduit Suite, "what the Wessex Federation has become? A committee of dissection? A gathering of men who look upon a triumph of the earth and seek only to find where they may stick their little knives?"

Arthur Pendelton gave a low, rumbling growl of satisfaction.

"Mr. Thorpe claims," Julian said, his eyes flashing, "that there is sugar in this marrow. He claims that this majestic growth—this *Dreadnought* of the Wiltshire soil—is a fraud sustained by a hidden wick."

"It has a split!" Reggie insisted, though he took a half-step backward from Julian’s imposing velvet bulk. "Look at the moisture!"

"I will look at the moisture," Julian declared.

He reached into his pocket. He took from Polly the embroidered linen handkerchief. 

With the slow, surgical reverence of a high priest approaching an altar, Julian Foss knelt upon the beer-stained nylon carpet of the Conduit Suite. He did not mind the carpet. He did not mind the smell of cold vegetable broth. He was an actor in the third act of an impossible play, and the house was packed.

He extended his right hand. With the corner of his monogrammed handkerchief, he gently, delicately touched the droplet of liquid that was trembling upon the marrow’s lower seam.

He lifted the handkerchief. He looked at it.

Then, before Polly could reach out to stop him, before Mrs. Bream could consult her pink sheets, and before Diana Cloutier-Smith could step through the doors to prevent the utter destruction of his intestinal flora, Julian Foss touched the damp linen to the tip of his tongue.

The entire room held its breath. Arthur Pendelton leaned forward so far that the four tines of his fork nearly pierced his own boot.

Julian closed his eyes. His jaw moved once, twice. His brow furrowed in intense, agonizing contemplation.

He opened his eyes. He stood up. He smoothed the front of his jacket with both hands.

"Iron," he said, his voice ringing with absolute, biblical certainty. "Iron. River silt. A faint trace of wood-ash from an apple-wood fire."

He looked directly at Reggie Thorpe.

"There is no sugar here, sir," Julian said, his baritone dropping into a tone of devastating, aristocratic contempt. "There is only the sweat of an honest man who has fed his soil with the labour of his own hands."

A cheer went up from the North Plots table that rattled the fire doors.

"You're lying!" Reggie yelled, his face turning an alarming shade of damson. "You're a London actor! You don't know sugar from lime!"

"I know the taste," Julian said, stepping forward until Reggie was forced back against the display of intermediate shallots, "of integrity. And I know the taste of envy. Put your knife in your pocket, Mr. Thorpe. Before I am obliged to ask your chairman why a member of this association is carrying an offensive weapon into a room dedicated to the cucurbit."

Reggie Thorpe looked at Julian. He looked at Arthur Pendelton, who had raised his fork by three inches and was grinning with the terrifying benevolence of an executioner who has been granted a half-day holiday. He looked at Mrs. Bream, who had closed her pink folder with a snap that sounded like an executioner’s axe.

Reggie’s knife clicked shut.

"It's a farce," Reggie muttered, turning on his heel and snatching his coat from the back of his chair. "The whole thing's a bloody London farce. I'm taking my parsnips and I'm going home."

He marched toward the fire exit, his boots squeaking furiously on the lino, and slammed through the door into the rain.

The room erupted. Arthur Pendelton dropped his fork, lunged forward with both arms outstretched, and engulfed Julian in an embrace that compressed the midnight-blue velvet jacket against forty square inches of damp blue nylon and three days of unwashed Wiltshire fleece.

"You're a gentleman, sir!" Arthur bellowed, tears of pure, agricultural emotion welling in his red-rimmed eyes. "A proper gentleman! You tell 'em! You tell the buggers!"

Julian, whose ribs were creaking under a pressure that Polly estimated at three hundred pounds per square inch, managed to emit a strangled, aristocratic gasp that sounded like: "The... loam... my dear fellow... the loam."

Across the room, Diana Cloutier-Smith stood by the service doors.

She was not smiling. But as her eyes met Polly’s, she slowly, deliberately raised her glass of warm white wine by two inches, took a single sip, and allowed her head to tilt in an unmistakable, sardonic gesture of professional salute.

### Chapter 7

"The weight," Diana said into the roving microphone ten minutes later, her voice smooth, calm, and utterly untroubled by the near-riot that had preceded it, "is confirmed by the stewards."

The Conduit Suite was packed to the walls. The competitors who had previously been drinking in the skittle alley had crowded into the doorway, their pints of heavy bitter held high, their faces illuminated by the yellow glow of the stage spots.

"The winner," Diana announced, "of the Wessex Cucurbit Perpetual Trophy for 2026... with a confirmed weight of seventy-four pounds, two ounces... is Mr. Arthur Pendelton of the North Meadow Allotments!"

The applause was deafening. It was not the polite, pattering applause of an independent film premiere; it was the thunderous, table-shaking roar of eighty people who have seen justice done to a very large vegetable.

Julian stood beside Diana on the riser. He had survived Arthur’s embrace, though Polly had been obliged to spend four minutes in the corridor with a wet paper towel removing two distinct smears of chicken manure from his left shoulder. He had refused to take off the jacket. He had refused the beta-blockers. He was running on pure, unadulterated theatrical adrenaline—the specific, lethal intoxication that comes to an actor who knows he has saved a terrible production from opening-night disaster by sheer force of personality.

"To present the trophy," Diana said, turning to Julian with a smile that would have chilled champagne, "we have Mr. Julian Foss. Who has kindly agreed to stand in for our dear Bob."

She picked up the trophy from the small side table.

Polly, standing at the foot of the riser, held her breath.

The trophy was not a cup. It was a massive, electro-plated silver shield mounted on an oak base that looked as though it had been salvaged from a sixteenth-century gunboat. In the center, freshly engraved in letters three-quarters of an inch high, gleamed the fatal words:

**PRESENTED BY BOB CRADDOCK, MBE**  
*'HE KNOWS WHICH WAY THE WIND BLOWS'*

Diana handed the shield to Julian. As she did so, she leaned in until her lips were two inches from his ear.

"Let’s see you get out of this one, Dalrymple," she whispered.

Julian did not flinch. He took the shield with both hands, his fingers curling over the silver rim. He did not attempt to cover the plate with his thumb. He did not look at the baize cloth.

He turned to face the room. He held the shield high above his head, the electro-plate catching the light and blinding the photographer from the *Mid-Shire Herald*, who had just arrived in a yellow oilskin and was frantically adjusting his flashgun.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Julian said, his baritone filling every crack in the simulated teak paneling. "Look upon this silver."

The room quieted.

"Look upon this name," Julian commanded, lowering the shield so that the central plate was illuminated by the spotlight. "Bob Craddock."

A respectful murmur passed through the crowd.

"Bob Craddock," Julian said, "could not be with us tonight. His hip—that noble joint that has borne the weight of regional meteorology for three decades—has failed him."

Laughter, warm and sympathetic.

"Bob knows," Julian said, reading the engraved motto with the slow, resonant gravity of an archbishop reciting the Nicene Creed, "*which way the wind blows*."

He looked at Arthur Pendelton, who was standing on the floor below the riser, his massive hands clasped before him like a schoolboy waiting for a prize.

"The wind," Julian said, "has blown through this room tonight. It has blown away the doubts. It has blown away the small suspicions of the envious. It has left behind only the great, enduring reality of Arthur’s marrow."

### Chapter 13

The Wordsworth Suite at two in the morning was even less hospitable than the linen cupboard they had occupied earlier.

He did not wake like an ordinary mortal, by opening his eyes and stretching his limbs; he emerged from sleep in three distinct, staged phases, beginning with a deep, tragic moan that seemed to originate in his diaphragm, followed by a dramatic fluttering of his silver-tipped eyelashes, and culminating in a sudden, vertical bolt of his torso that sent the cellular blanket sliding onto the parquet floor.

"Polly," he croaked, clutching his throat. "The air is thin. Have we climbed into the Apennines?"

"You're in Belsize Park, Julian," Polly said, walking in from the kitchen with a mug of hot water containing a slice of lemon and three drops of ginger tincture. "You’ve had two hours of sleep."

Julian took the mug with two hands, his fingers trembling slightly against the ceramic. He took a sip, winced as the ginger hit his mucous membranes, and looked around the drawing room with the vague, unfocused gaze of an old dog who has forgotten where his bone is buried.

"I had a dream, Polly," he whispered. "A terrible, mythological dream. I was standing in the Colosseum in Rome. The Emperor was there—he looked remarkably like the casting director for *Midsomer Murders* who rejected me for the vicar in 2017—and they released the beasts from the subterranean cages. But they weren't lions, Polly. They were immense, ribbed squash. Rolling toward me across the sand with a terrible, wet squelching sound."

"It wasn't a dream, Julian," Polly said, sitting on the opposite armchair and opening her notebook. "You lifted seventy-four pounds of marrow at midnight, you saved Arthur Pendelton from a disqualification under Rule 14, and you are currently trending on three major social platforms under the hashtag *#MarrowMan*."

Julian stopped his mug halfway to his lips.

"I am... what?"

"Trending, Julian."

"Trending?" The word tasted strange in his mouth; he pronounced it with a foreign, suspicious inflection, as if referring to an obscure variety of continental cheese. "Is that... desirable?"

"It means two million people have watched you taste Arthur’s marrow on the floor of the Conduit Suite."

Julian went entirely white. He set the mug on the low walnut coffee table with such sudden carelessness that water slopped over a 1994 edition of *Who's Who in the Theatre*.

"My career is finished," he whispered. "The National Theatre will never call. Nicholas Hytner will cross the street if he sees me outside the Garrick. I am a laughing-stock. A grotesque. An internet buffoon."

"Nicholas Hytner hasn't run the National since 2015, Julian," Polly said patiently. "And you're not a laughing-stock. The BBC wants to commission a six-part prime-time series about you visiting allotments in the West Country."

Julian blinked. The white drained from his cheeks, replaced by a faint, delicate flush of salmon pink that rose from his collar to his cheekbones.

"A series?" he murmured. "For BBC Two?"

"Peak-time. Eight o'clock. Sunday evenings. After the antiques."

Julian sat up straighter. His shoulders went back. His spine, which had been curved into a posture of acute lumbar collapse, achieved an alignment that would have satisfied his Finsbury Park osteopath.

"Well," he said, his baritone swelling with that sudden, effortless majesty that Polly had witnessed a thousand times. "Of course. One shouldn't be entirely surprised. The public has an instinct for truth, Polly. They are weary of these glossy, synthetic presenters who look like estate agents and speak with the vocabulary of an air-fryer manual. They want maturity. They want gravitas. They want a man who can look at a root crop and understand its place in the great, unbroken tapestry of the British imagination."

"Quite," Polly said.

"Did Marcus say what the title might be? Something simple, I hope. *The Loam of England*, perhaps? Or *Foss: A Journey Through the Furrows*?"

"Marcus didn't mention a title," Polly said. "He mentioned a taster tape."

"A taster tape? Naturally. A few minutes of footage to establish the visual language. I shall wear the camel-hair coat, perhaps with a soft woolen scarf in peat-brown. We can shoot it in Richmond Park. Under the ancient oaks."

"Not Richmond Park, Julian," Polly said, looking directly into his hazel eyes. "On your allotment."

Julian’s chest remained expanded for three seconds before deflating with a slow, wheezing hiss.

"On my... what?"

"Your allotment, Julian."

"Polly," Julian said, his voice dropping into a confidential, slightly irritable whisper. "You know perfectly well that I do not have an allotment. I have never had an allotment. The very idea of having an allotment—of traveling to a municipal plot on a Tuesday afternoon to hoe a trench in the rain—is utterly incompatible with the lifestyle of a working character actor."

"The BBC believes you have one," Polly said. "Because Arthur Pendelton told the *Mid-Shire Herald* that you were an experienced grower from the Shropshire borders, and Marcus confirmed it to the commissioning editor forty minutes ago."

Julian stared at her. His hands gripped the arms of the leather sofa until the hide creaked.

"Marcus told the British Broadcasting Corporation that I possess an agricultural plot?"

"He said you had three rods in north London," Polly said, reading from her notes. "He told them you were an organic purist who specialized in rare Victorian alliums and that you spent your autumns preparing heritage seed beds."

Julian looked toward the window. 

Outside, a grey north London drizzle was falling on the plane trees. A siren wailed faintly in the direction of the Royal Free Hospital. On the narrow fire escape, a solitary, rain-soaked pigeon was pecking disconsolately at the empty plastic bird feeder.

"Polly," Julian whispered.

"Yes, Julian."

"Where am I going to get three rods of heritage alliums before Monday morning?"

Polly closed her notebook. She reached into her coat pocket, extracted the small, cream-coloured business card that Diana had handed her through the car window at five o'clock that morning, and laid it gently on the walnut coffee table beside the wet *Who's Who*.

"There is," Polly said, "only one person in the United Kingdom who possesses both the legal authority to grant an emergency municipal agricultural lease and a sufficient degree of malice toward your dignity to make the process interesting."

Julian looked down at the card. 

His eyes widened as he read the embossed black letters:  
*DIANA CLOUTIER-SMITH*  
*CHIEF COMMISSIONER, WESSEX HERITAGE & ALLOTMENT TRUSTS*  
*SPECIAL ADVISER TO THE REGIONAL OMBUDSMAN*

"No," Julian moaned, burying his face in his hands. "Not Diana. Polly, I beg of you. I would rather shoot the taster tape in a municipal park with a plastic trowel."

"The BBC," Polly said, rising from her chair and picking up her phone, "does not shoot taster tapes with plastic trowels, Julian. And if you want eight o'clock on Sunday evenings, you are going to have to apologize for the Regency sideboard."

### Chapter 19

The offices of Cloutier-Smith Heritage & Land Management were located in a converted Georgian malthouse on the outskirts of Bradford-on-Avon, twenty miles north of Market Melbury.

It was ten o'clock on Sunday morning.

The rain, which had followed Polly and Julian from Somerset to London on Saturday, had now followed them back down the M4 in a hired Volkswagen Golf—Avis having flatly refused to provide another estate car until the forensic report on the Volvo’s boot had been concluded.

Diana Cloutier-Smith was sitting behind a vast, four-meter desk of reclaimed English elm, drinking Earl Grey from a bone-china cup that looked as though it had been purchased at an auction of items from an impoverished ducal estate. 

She was wearing a charcoal cashmere roll-neck jumper and small, perfectly spherical pearl earrings. Behind her, through three arched windows that had once illuminated the germination of barley, the River Avon flowed swollen and brown between weeping willows.

Julian sat in a low leather armchair opposite the desk. He had spent two hours in the car trying to decide between his tweed hacking jacket and his dark navy reefer coat, eventually choosing the tweed on the grounds that it made him look "like an enlightened landowner who has recently sold an outbuilding to fund a local literacy initiative."

Polly sat beside him, holding her canvas bag and a large, flat cardboard box she had collected from a specialist organic nursery in Chiswick at seven that morning.

"So," Diana said, setting her cup in its saucer with a tiny, porcelain click that seemed to echo through the entire malthouse. "The *Guardian* calls you 'The Velvet Colossus.'"

Julian cleared his throat. "A journalistic exaggeration, Diana. The press is always prone to hyperbole in the autumn. The lack of major political conferences creates a void."

"Two point four million views," Diana noted, looking at an iPad that lay beside her desk blotter. "The video of you tasting the marrow has been re-posted by the Danish Ministry of Agriculture as an example of British rural eccentricity. They seem to think it was part of a harvest festival ritual involving human sacrifice."

"The Danes," Julian said stiffly, "have always had a very literal approach to the dramatic arts. Look at their cinema. It’s all people sitting in kitchens with thirty-watt bulbs, waiting for their marriages to collapse."

"And now," Diana continued, her cool, dark eyes turning slowly toward Julian, "you want an allotment."

"A temporary lease," Julian corrected. "A mere administrative accommodation. A location for thirty-six minutes of broadcast-quality filming. Marcus assures me that the BBC requires only a modest visual backdrop: a small shed with some authentic tools, a compost heap of suitable maturity, and a few rows of... of things that have already occurred."

"Things that have already occurred?" Diana repeated, her eyebrows rising. "You mean vegetables?"

"Mature specimens," Julian said, waving an airy hand. "Vegetables that have achieved their potential. I don't want to be filmed looking at seeds, Diana. Seeds are boring. Seeds are for young people with patience and gardening gloves. The public wants to see the result. They want to see me standing beside an established leek, reflecting on the brevity of human existence."

Diana leaned back in her high-backed chair. She picked up a heavy, silver paperweight shaped like an acorn and turned it slowly in her fingers.

"Julian," she said.

"Yes, Diana?"

"The waiting list for a municipal allotment in the Wessex Federation area is currently seven years."

"Seven years?" Julian’s baritone squeaked. "For a strip of dirt?"

"For nine rods of Grade II agricultural loam with access to a shared standpipe," Diana said crisply. "There are four hundred and twelve people on the list in Market Melbury alone. Two retired headmistresses, forty-six civil servants, and the entire committee of the local Labour Party. If I were to allocate a plot to an actor from London who hasn't held a spade since 1989, Reggie Thorpe would have me before the Ombudsman before your cameras had even unloaded their tripods."

"Reggie Thorpe," Julian sneered, "is a defeated man. His knife is folded. His shallots were mediocre."

"Reggie Thorpe," Diana said, "is the secretary of the Ratepayers' Alliance. He spends his Sundays checking the planning register for unauthorized chicken coops. If a BBC production truck turns up at the North Meadow plots without a Section 38 permit signed by the Town Clerk, Reggie will have the police there within nine minutes."

Julian sank back into his armchair. The enlightened landowner posture evaporated, leaving behind the familiar, hollow-eyed despair of the Sunday evening train back from an unsuccessful audition in Leeds.

"Then I am undone," he whispered. "The BBC will cancel the meeting. Marcus will drop me. I will spend my declining years doing voiceovers for regional double-glazing firms in the Midlands."

Polly reached down, picked up the flat cardboard box from Chiswick, and placed it on the reclaimed elm desk.

Diana looked at the box. "What is that?"

"Heritage brassicas," Polly said. "And six roots of sea-kale. Pre-germinated. Organic certification from the Soil Association."

Diana’s gaze flicked from the box to Polly’s face. "Where did you get sea-kale on a Sunday morning?"

"I have a cousin who manages a community nursery in Petersham," Polly said without blinking. "And in my bag, Mrs. Cloutier-Smith, I have the receipt for the two-hundred-and-fifty-pound appearance fee from Friday night."

Diana did not touch the box. Her eyes remained fixed on Polly. "And what am I supposed to do with the appearance fee?"

"You endorse it over," Polly said, "to the North Meadow Drainage Fund. As a charitable donation from Mr. Foss to commemorate the late Uncle Robert’s contribution to culvert maintenance."

A profound silence descended upon the malthouse. Outside, the Avon gurgled past the weeping willows, carrying a dead branch toward the weir.

Diana looked at Julian. Julian was looking at his shoes, his fingers pleating the wool of his tweed jacket with nervous, rhythmic precision.

Then Diana looked back at Polly.

"You really are very good, aren't you?" Diana said softly.

"I am thirty years old, Mrs. Cloutier-Smith," Polly replied, her voice steady and completely devoid of inflection. "I have spent four years managing a man who believes that hotel radiators are personally controlled by the Director-General of the BBC to spite him. A drainage fund donation is a relatively minor administrative maneuvers."

Diana picked up her silver pencil. She clicked it once.

"Plot 12B," she said.

### Chapter 20

Plot 12B at the North Meadow Allotments in Market Melbury had not been cultivated since the late spring of 2021.

It stood in the low, waterlogged corner of the site, bounded on three sides by a drainage ditch choked with hairy bittercress and on the fourth by the rusty chain-link fence of the sewage treatment plant. 

The plot itself—nine rods of heavy, unyielding Kimmeridge clay—was an impenetrable jungle of five-foot stinging nettles, creeping buttercup, and mare’s-tail that had grown with the terrifying, prehistoric vigor common to all neglected English ground. In the center of this botanical wasteland stood a shed constructed from three corrugated iron sheets, two pallets from an electrical wholesaler, and an old front door painted in peeling salmon-pink gloss with the brass letterbox still intact.

It was four o'clock on Sunday afternoon.

The light was failing rapidly, the grey clouds sinking down toward the willow trees along the river until the church tower of St. Jude’s was swallowed in mist.

Julian stood on the muddy grass track outside the plot, wearing a pair of brand-new Hunter Wellington boots that Polly had purchased at an agricultural supply merchant in Devizes forty minutes earlier. The boots were so clean, so glossy, that they looked as though they had been dipped in liquid liquorice.

"It’s a swamp, Polly," he said, his voice flat with despair. "It’s the Great Dismal Swamp. Look at those nettles. They have teeth. One feels that if a man stepped into that jungle, he would be reduced to a skeleton and a pair of brass buttons before the eleven o'clock news."

"It has character, Julian," Polly said, pulling a pair of heavy-duty gardening gloves from her bag. "The BBC people love character. Marcus says the director specifically asked for 'a plot that looks as though it has witnessed the struggle of the generations.'"

"It hasn't witnessed the struggle of the generations, Polly! It has witnessed the abandonment of all human hope! Look at that door! Why is there a letterbox on a potting shed? Who is delivering letters to the stinging nettles?"

"The previous tenant," a voice rumbled from behind them, "was old Fred Cloutier. Diana’s third cousin on the mother’s side."

Arthur Pendelton emerged from the mist along the grass track, pushing a battered green wheelbarrow containing a four-pronged fork, a heavy wooden rake, and a brown earthenware flagon with a cork stopper.

He had changed out of his nylon windbreaker into a thick, grease-blackened donkey jacket with leather shoulder patches. His face was weathered, red, and beaming with the profound, conspiratorial affection of a conspirator meeting a comrade behind enemy lines.

"Arthur," Julian said, his baritone automatically rising into its aristocratic register. "My dear fellow."

"Mr. Foss," Arthur said, touching the brim of his flat cap with two soil-stained fingers. "Diana told me you was coming. Said you needed the plot squared up for the London cameras."

"A mere visual establishment," Julian said smoothly. "A modest demonstration of the craft."

Arthur looked over the chain-link fence at the five-foot sea of nettles. He spat into the grass with the relaxed, unhurried accuracy of a man who has considered the problem of vegetation for sixty years and concluded that it is largely a matter of lever-action.

"She's a bit foul on the ditch side," Arthur observed. "Fred let the couch-grass get into the subsoil when his knees went in nineteen. But the clay’s good underneath. Deep. Full of lime from the old brickworks. You put the fork into her and she turns over like black butter."

"Black butter," Julian repeated, tasting the phrase with theatrical relish. "A marvellous phrase, Arthur. Very evocative. Almost Seamus Heaney."

"Never heard of him," Arthur said, reaching into the barrow and producing the four-pronged digging fork. "Right then, Mr. Foss. Grab the shaft."

Julian froze. His eyes drifted from the fork to his brand-new Hunter boots, and then to Polly.

"The... shaft?"

"If you're going to be on the television tomorrow morning," Arthur said, thrusting the ash handle into Julian’s gloved hands, "you’ve got to have the clay on the tines. The London people might be daft, but they know what a fork looks like when it’s been in the ground. You can't turn up with a shiny tool, Mr. Foss. It looks like you bought it in a garden center in Bath."

"Arthur is quite right, Julian," Polly said firmly, checking her watch. "The light will be gone in forty minutes. We need to clear three yards of the frontage, plant the sea-kale, and make the shed look as though you’ve been meditating inside it since the third series of *The Blackfriars Murders*."

Julian looked down at the fork. The steel tines were dark, pitted with age, and cold as iron grave-markers. 

He took a breath. He looked out across the misty allotment field, where the smoke from two distant bonfire heaps was drifting through the willow trees, carrying the sharp, sweet smell of burning potato haulms into the dusk.

"Very well," Julian Foss said, his voice dropping into the solemn, heroic register of Henry V before the walls of Harfleur. "Into the clay."

He stepped off the grass track.

His right Hunter boot sank four inches into the Wiltshire mud with a soft, hungry, sucking sound that resonated through the damp silence of Plot 12B.

### Chapter 21

At seven o'clock on Sunday evening, the Conduit Suite of the Mercure Royal Oak & Spa was once again dark, but the small public bar on the ground floor—known as the *Cavalier Lounge*—was populated by eight locals, a commercial traveler who sold dental supplies from a Vauxhall Insignia, and Julian Foss.

Julian was sitting in an inglenook fireplace beneath a horse-brassy beam that bore a brass plate stating that King Charles II had once hidden in an outhouse forty miles to the west.

He had not removed his Hunter boots, which were now encrusted with two inches of authentic Kimmeridge clay, several strands of couch-grass, and a dead dock leaf that had adhered itself to the buckle. His tweed jacket smelled powerfully of wood-smoke and wet ditch-water. His hands, even through the gloves, had acquired that faint, greyish patina that comes from four hours of intimate physical contact with the subsoil of Somerset.

He was drinking a pint of Wadworth 6X with Arthur Pendelton.

"The trick with the sea-kale," Julian was saying, his voice rich, mellow, and vibrating with the effortless authority of an elder statesman of the allotment movement, "is the blanching. One must deny it the light, Arthur. One must cover the crown with an inverted terracotta pot—or an old chimneypot, if the aesthetic of the plot demands it—so that the stem grows pale, crisp, and entirely devoid of bitterness."

Arthur nodded slowly, his mug of bitter halfway to his mouth. "That’s what Fred used to do. Used an old zinc wash-tub with a brick on top to keep the foxes off."

"Precisely," Julian said, taking a long, deep draught of his ale. "The zinc tub. An authentic, vernacular solution. None of this plastic membrane from the DIY sheds. The plant needs to breathe within its darkness, Arthur. It needs to feel the weight of the traditional vessel."

Polly sat at the small copper-topped table beside the fireplace, eating a packet of salt-and-vinegar crisps and watching Julian with the detached, analytical admiration of a trainer whose horse has just cleared a five-foot fence after refusing three water jumps.

Her phone buzzed on the table.

It was Marcus Webb.

"Polly," Marcus said, his voice now purring with the smooth, oily satisfaction of an agent who has just secured an option agreement on a property he doesn't own. "Everything is set. The car is picking up the director and the commissioning editor from Paddington at seven-thirty tomorrow. They’ll be in Market Melbury by nine-fifteen. Is the location ready?"

"Plot 12B is prepared, Marcus," Polly said. "We have cleared six square yards of ground. We have installed the sea-kale. And Julian has achieved an acceptable level of soil contamination on his outerwear."

"Fabulous, babe. Absolutely stellar. Now, listen: the director is a young woman named Cleo. Very visual. Very *Sight & Sound*. She did that documentary about the female cooper in Orkney that won the short-form BAFTA last year."

"The cooper?"

"She likes textures, Polly. Wood-grain. Rust. The sound of water running through an old pipe. She told me she wants Julian to do a monologue to camera about 'the silence of the seed.' Can he do the silence of the seed?"

Polly looked over at Julian, who was currently demonstrating to Arthur Pendelton how an eighteenth-century ditch-digger would have held a spade to prevent inflammation of the median nerve.

"He can do forty minutes on the silence of the seed, Marcus," Polly said. "With gestures."

"Brilliant. And Polly?"

"Yes, Marcus?"

"Has the ex-wife signed the release?"

Polly froze. Her hand stopped halfway to the crisp packet.

"What release, Marcus?"

"The location release, babe! For the BBC legal department! We can't shoot on a municipal site without the signed authority of the Trust Commissioner! Paragraph seven! If we don't have the countersignature by eight-thirty tomorrow morning, the crew can't even take the cameras out of the flight cases!"

Polly closed her eyes.

Through the pub window, across the dark, rain-swept bypass, the single red light on top of the mobile phone mast blinked against the black sky of Wiltshire.

"Marcus," Polly said softly.

"Yeah?"

"Why did you not mention this at three o'clock?"

"I was in a meeting with a boy who does impressions of air fryers on YouTube, Polly! My bandwidth was totally compromised! Just get her to sign it! She’s his ex-wife, for God’s sake! How difficult can it be?"

The line went dead.

Polly lowered her phone. She looked at Julian, who was now explaining the structural advantages of the three-pronged drag over the standard Canterbury hoe to a fascinated dental-supplies salesman.

Then she reached into her bag, took out Diana’s cream business card, and looked at the private number printed in small, elegant sans-serif type.

"Polly," Julian called out, his eyes shining with ale and rural fraternity. "Come and tell Arthur what you found in the shed! The brass tap! Tell him about the brass tap from the Great Western Railway!"

Polly did not answer. She stood up, pulled her coat around her shoulders, and walked out into the cold, damp hallway by the payphone to make the most dangerous telephone call of her professional life.

### Chapter 22

The kitchen of Diana Cloutier-Smith’s house in Bradford-on-Avon was everything Julian’s flat in Belsize Park was not: warm, stone-flagged, illuminated by recessed spotlights that did not hum, and smelling faintly of beeswax, rosemary, and an expensive wood-burning stove from Denmark.

It was nine o'clock on Sunday night.

Diana was sitting at an island unit made of honed Welsh slate, slicing a loaf of sourdough bread with a Japanese knife that made a sharp, clean *crunch* every time the blade met the crust. 

On the slate beside her lay the BBC Location Agreement Form 4B, pristine, white, and completely unsigned.

Polly sat on a high bar stool, holding a glass of sparkling water that Diana had poured from a chrome tap set directly into the stone.

"No," Diana said, without looking up from the bread.

"Mrs. Cloutier-Smith—"

"No, Polly. I have spent eight years removing Julian Foss from my life. I have removed his debts. I have removed his tax demands. I have removed three boxes of his unread stage plays from the attic of the cottage in Salcombe. I am not signing a document that turns a municipal allotment trust into a television set for his third-act career revival."

"It's not just his revival," Polly said quietly.

Diana paused, the knife resting on the cutting board. She looked up, her dark eyes sharp and reflective in the warm light of the kitchen.

"Oh? And whose revival is it, then? Marcus Webb’s?"

"Mine," Polly said.

The silence in the kitchen lasted for four seconds. The wood stove gave a soft, dry *pop* as a birch log settled into the ash.

Diana laid the knife down. She leaned against the slate island, her arms folded across her charcoal roll-neck, studying Polly with the detached, clinical scrutiny she usually reserved for disputed property boundaries.

"You're thirty," Diana said.

"Thirty next month."

"And you have a degree from East Anglia."

"Comparative Literature," Polly said. "Upper second."

"And you have spent four years carrying his beta-blockers, wiping his lapels, and lying to theatrical producers about his availability."

"Four years and eight months."

"Why?" Diana asked. The word was not cruel; it was genuinely curious, with that flat, forensic interest that characterized all Diana’s inquiries into human folly. "He's not a genius, Polly. He was an adequate television actor who had three good years in the nineties because the lighting director on *Dalrymple* knew how to hide his double chin. He has no money. He has no loyalty. If a better agency offered him five hundred pounds more a year, he’d leave you at a railway station with three suitcases and an unpaid hotel bill."

"I know that," Polly said.

"Then why are you sitting in my kitchen on a Sunday night, begging for a signature on a BBC release form?"

Polly looked down at her glass of sparkling water. The small, silver bubbles rose through the clear liquid, breaking against the rim with tiny, silent explosions.

"Because if this series happens," Polly said, "I am the associate producer."

Diana’s eyebrows lifted by a fraction of an inch. "Is that in writing?"

"In an email from the commissioning editor," Polly said, taking her phone from her pocket and laying it on the slate. "Clause three. 'Subject to the delivery of the taster tape and the execution of the location agreements, Miss Vance will be credited as Associate Producer and receive standard Pact-Equity development fees.'"

Diana looked at the phone. She didn't pick it up, but her eyes scanned the screen with the speed of a seasoned administrator.

"Development fees," Diana murmured. "Twelve thousand pounds?"

"Fourteen," Polly corrected. "Plus expenses. It’s enough to clear my overdraft, pay off the deposit on a flat in Stoke Newington, and never have to buy an exhibition shallot on a Sunday morning for the rest of my life."

Diana stood very still for several seconds. 

She walked over to the wood stove, picked up a pair of small iron tongs, and adjusted the birch log until the flame caught the bark with a sharp, yellow flare.

"He doesn't know, does he?" she said, her back to Polly.

"Julian?"

"He thinks this is all for him. The velvet. The loam. The silence of the seed."

"Julian believes," Polly said softly, "that the world is an audience that has temporarily stepped into the foyer for a drink, and that it is his duty to call them back to their seats."

Diana turned around. A faint, dry smile—the first genuine smile Polly had seen on her face—touched her lips.

"He always was an idiot," Diana murmured.

She walked back to the island unit. She picked up her silver mechanical pencil. She did not read the terms of the BBC Location Agreement Form 4B; she had drafted similar agreements for heritage trusts for twenty years and knew every indemnity clause by heart.

She signed her name at the foot of page three with a sharp, elegant flourish: *Diana Cloutier-Smith, Chief Commissioner.*

Then she slid the paper across the slate to Polly.

"Nine o'clock tomorrow, Polly," Diana said. "Not a minute earlier. And tell young Cleo from the BBC that if her camera crew tramples the comfrey patch on Plot 11, I will impound their van under the Commons Registration Act of 1965."

Polly picked up the form. Her hands, for the first time in three days, were completely steady.

"Thank you, Mrs. Cloutier-Smith."

"Diana," she said. She picked up her Japanese knife and returned to her sourdough. "And Polly?"

"Yes?"

"When the series goes to six episodes," Diana said, the knife crunching through the crust with surgical precision, "ensure that you get the producer credit. Never let an actor think he’s the only one who understands the loam."

### Chapter 23

Monday morning at the North Meadow Allotments broke with that rare, translucent clarity that sometimes visits the West Country in late October: a pale, eggshell-blue sky washed clean by three days of rain, the willow trees along the river glowing like pale gold wire, and the white frost on the cabbage nets melting into thousands of diamond droplets in the early sun.

At twenty minutes past nine, two silver Mercedes vans bearing the discreet logos of the BBC Factual Division were parked in the lane beside Arthur Pendelton’s Morris Minor.

On Plot 12B, the transformation was complete.

The five-foot nettles had been cleared from the frontage by Arthur and his nephews at dawn; the path had been dressed with three barrows of clean river gravel from the brewery yard; and Julian Foss stood beside the newly installed sea-kale bed, wearing his tweed hacking jacket, his clay-stained Hunter boots, and a soft, dark-green woolen scarf that Polly had sprayed with three drops of lavender water to counteract the lingering smell of the Mercure basement.

Cleo, the young director—who was twenty-eight, wore an enormous navy-blue fisherman’s smock, and had three small silver hoops in her left ear—was peering through the viewfinder of a handheld Arri Alexa with an expression of intense, almost mystical reverence.

"The texture, Julian," she whispered, her voice hushed as though they were in a cathedral. "Look at the way the light catches the rust on that latch. It’s so... uncompromised. It has such an ancient, melancholy weight."

Julian did not look at the latch. He looked into the middle distance, toward the church tower of St. Jude’s rising through the willow mist, his jaw set, his silver hair ruffled by the morning breeze from the downs.

"The soil, Cleo," he murmured, his baritone dropping into that low, thrilling register that had once made him the third most popular television detective in the West Midlands, "does not bargain with us. It waits. It accepts our labour, it accepts our vanity, and in the end... it accepts our bones."

Behind the camera, the sound recordist—a man in a high-visibility beanie holding a four-foot boom pole—nodded slowly, his eyes wide with professional admiration.

"Cut," Cleo whispered. "Oh, God. That was magnificent, Julian. Truly. 'It accepts our bones.' Did you write that?"

Julian smiled his small, aristocratic, slightly crooked smile. He reached down with one gloved hand and gently, delicately touched the crisp, green leaf of the Chiswick sea-kale.

"One doesn't write these things, Cleo," Julian Foss said softly, his voice carrying across the quiet allotments to where Polly was standing by the gate, checking the time on her phone. "One simply listens. To the loam."

Across the fence, Arthur Pendelton leaned on his four-pronged fork, spat into the drainage ditch, and looked at Polly with a slow, deliberate wink of his rheumy eye.

Polly did not wink back. 

She looked down at her phone screen, where a new email had just arrived from the BBC production office in Bristol, confirming the preliminary dates for the six-part series, with a separate contract attached for the Associate Producer.

### Chapter 25

### Chapter 27

### Chapter 28

The headquarters of the National Vegetable Society (South-West Branch) occupied the rear room of an abandoned railway weighbridge behind the cattle market in Chippenham.

The room smelled of damp carbon paper, creosote, methylated spirits, and forty years of unventilated nylon knitwear. He looked at the 1892 map. He looked at the Chiswick invoice. 

Then he looked at Julian Foss.

He sat in his midnight-blue velvet dinner jacket, his hands clasped before him on the table, his silver hair catching the dull light of the weighbridge office, looking at Hector with the calm, sorrowful, absolute authority of a man who has played Thomas More at the Globe and knows that the axe cannot touch the soul.

Then he handed it back to Reggie Thorpe.

"Reggie," Hector said.

"Hector!" Reggie squeaked. "You can't let him get away with this! He’s an actor! He’s wearing evening dress in Chippenham!"

She tossed the newspapers onto the table beside Marcus’s manila folder.

"Page three," Diana said, leaning over Polly’s shoulder.

Polly unfolded the paper.

Beneath a photograph of the newly restored priory orangery ran the headline:

**WESSEX HERITAGE ACCORD: HISTORIC ALLOTMENT CHARTER RATIFIED AS COUNTY COUNCILS SIGN TRI-VALE PACT**  
*(Mr. J. Foss and Mrs. D. Cloutier-Smith Appointed Joint Wardens of the Lower Rhyne)*

"Joint Wardens," Polly read aloud, looking up at Diana.

Diana did not smile, but her dark, razor-sharp eyes softened by an eighth of an inch as she looked out toward the walled garden where Julian’s figure was visible among the espaliers.

"It carries an annual stipend of four pounds ten shillings," Diana said. "And the right to pasture six geese on the common land below the church. I told Julian he could keep the geese, provided he didn't attempt to teach them verse speaking."

### Chapter 58

On Plot 12B, the afternoon was warm, lazy, and filled with the low, continuous drone of the bees in the dandelion heads along the drainage ditch.

Julian Foss sat on an upturned wooden apple box, wearing his faded tweed trousers, his Hunter boots, and an old cotton smock that Diana had discovered in the priory laundry—a genuine nineteenth-century Wiltshire carter’s smock of heavy unbleached linen with honeycomb smocking across the breast.

He was holding a pair of long, curved brass vine-scissors.

Before him, upon the dark, crumbly loam of the compost mound, the six seedlings of *The Dalrymple* had grown into a dense, vigorous emerald-green carpet that covered twenty square feet of the soil.

Arthur Pendelton knelt beside him, his massive hands resting on his corduroy thighs, his flat cap pushed back on his grey head.

"Now then, Mr. Foss," Arthur rumbled, his thick index finger pointing into the heart of the foliage. "Look at the crowns. You see that one there? The one with the double rib on the stem?"

Julian leaned forward, his reading glasses perched on his nose, examining the leaf-axil with the intense, breathless concentration of a watchmaker adjusting an escapement.

"The double rib, Arthur," Julian whispered. "A sign of structural density?"

"A sign of power," Arthur said. "That’s the *Dreadnought* coming through. She’s got the shoulder of her grandmother. The others... they’re nice little plants, Mr. Foss. Very pretty. Good for a show in a village hall. But that one... that one’s a fighter."

Julian raised the brass scissors.

He hesitated, the steel blades hovering two inches above the secondary shoots.

"Must I, Arthur?" he murmured, his voice trembling with genuine, painful reluctance. "It feels... it feels like the third act of *Richard III*. To prune away the innocent... to sacrifice the tender shoots for the ambition of the one."

Arthur looked at him with his rheumy, wise, seventy-two-year-old eyes.

"You can't have six champions on one mound, Mr. Foss," Arthur said gently. "The earth only gives so much strength in a summer. If you try to feed 'em all, you end up with six forty-pounders that won't win a ribbon at a parish fete. You have to choose, sir. You have to give the whole heart of the dung to the one that can carry it."

Julian took a slow, deep breath through his nostrils.

He looked out across the allotment field: past the salmon-pink door of his shed, past George Vane’s runner beans, past Reggie Thorpe’s cold frames where the glass caught the sun like small mirrors on the grass.

He looked down at the chosen shoot.

Its broad, hairy, shovel-shaped leaves were dark green, thick as leather, and reached toward the light with an unhesitating, fierce vitality that belonged to the earth itself.

*Snip.*

The scissors closed.

One by one, Julian Foss removed the competing shoots, laying the green leaves upon the gravel path with the solemn, surgical reverie of an elder brother preparing the way for the heir.

When it was finished, only the single vine remained, rooted in the center of the mound, drawing its life from the deep, rich darkness of the brewery dung below.

Julian laid the brass scissors in his apple box.

He reached down with his bare hand and gently, delicately brushed a crumb of dry Kimmeridge clay from the base of the stem.

"Grow, then," Julian Foss whispered into the soil. "The world is waiting."

### Chapter 59

The European documentary crew arrived from Brussels on the first Monday in May.

There were eight of them, traveling in two black Mercedes vans that had navigated the narrow stone arch of the priory gatehouse with considerable difficulty, scraping their wing mirrors against the seventeenth-century limestone.

The director was not Jean-Luc De Coster; Jean-Luc was in Antwerp preparing an opera about salt-miners.

The director was a young woman named Camille, who had studied at the INSAS film school in Brussels, wore a black leather bomber jacket, and carried a light-meter around her neck on a length of yellow parachute cord.

She walked into the walled garden at ten o'clock, stopped by the dolphin fountain, and looked around the two acres of brickwork and pear blossom with the sharp, clinical eye of a northern European intellectual assessing the decadent ruins of the British Empire.

Polly Vance walked out from the orangery to meet her.

Polly was wearing her navy trousers, her flat leather boots, and her silver digging-fork brooch was pinned to the collar of her white linen shirt.

She did not carry a clipboard. She carried an iPad mini in a dark leather case and a single sheet of paper containing the agreed shooting schedule under the Franco-Belgian co-production treaty.

"Miss Vance?" Camille said, her English rapid and accented with the dry, Parisian cadence of the Belgian film schools.

"Welcome to Melbury, Camille," Polly said, shaking her hand with a firm, professional grip. "The light is best between two and four on the south wall. We have cleared the filming permits with the county heritage office, the power drops are in the potting shed, and Mr. Foss is currently in his study reviewing the text for the voiceover."

Camille looked at Polly. 

Her gaze travelled from the neat, calm line of Polly’s collar down to the silver brooch with the green tourmaline seed, and then to the quiet, unruffled confidence in Polly’s grey eyes.

"You are the producer," Camille said. It was not a question; it was an observation of authority.

"I am the producer," Polly said.

"And Mr. Foss? Is he... difficult?"

Polly smiled—a wide, warm, brilliant smile that had five years of luggage, three series of *Dalrymple*, and seventy-seven pounds of vegetable marrow behind it.

"Mr. Foss," Polly Vance said softly, "is an artist, Camille. If he becomes agitated, give him two drops of cider vinegar in warm water and ask him about his grandfather’s shallots. He will be entirely manageable."

Camille looked toward the gamekeeper's lodge, where Julian was walking down the grass path between the apple trees, wearing his tweed jacket and carrying his reading glasses in his hand.

"He has the face," Camille murmured, her professional skepticism dissolving into artistic admiration. "The face of an ancient landscape."

"He has the face," Polly agreed, turning back toward the refectory table where Diana was already pouring the morning coffee. "And we have the camera."

### Chapter 60

The formal installation of the Wessex Cucurbit Trophy in the library of Melbury Priory took place on a Saturday afternoon in late May, during the annual open garden fete for the benefit of the church roof fund.

The library was an immense, panelled gallery seventy feet long, lined with six thousand leather-bound volumes that smelled of old calfskin, cedar-oil, and three hundred years of dry paper.

At the far end of the room, beneath a seventeenth-century portrait of a Lord Chancellor in an ermine collar who bore an uncanny, unfortunate resemblance to Reggie Thorpe, stood a newly constructed oak plinth.

Upon the plinth, mounted upon a slab of green Purbeck marble, sat the perpetual silver shield.

The central silver plate, freshly polished with Goddard’s foam by Mrs. Enid Bream, gleamed in the sunlight that fell through the stone mullions:

**WESSEX CUCURBIT FEDERATION**  
*PERPETUAL REGIONAL SHIELD*  
*PRESENTED BY BOB CRADDOCK, MBE*  
*'HE KNOWS WHICH WAY THE WIND BLOWS'*

And beneath it, engraved in letters three-eighths of an inch high by Mr. Cloutier in Market Melbury:

*2026: ARTHUR PENDELTON (74 LBS 2 OZ)*  
*DEFENDED BY JULIAN FOSS, ESQ. (77 LBS 8 OZ)*  
*AT THE THREE COUNTIES SHOWGROUND, MALVERN*  
*'THE LOAM REMAINS'*

The room was packed with eighty people.

There were allotment holders from the North Meadow; there were railway workers from Westbury; there were women from the floral decoration society; and standing by the marble fireplace, drinking cider from a half-pint glass, was Bob Craddock, leaning comfortably on a single black walking stick with a carved duck-head handle.

"It’s a grand piece, Julian," Bob said, nodding toward the silver shield. "Grand. Uncle Robert would have been pleased. He always said a trophy shouldn't sit in a bank vault; it should sit where people can see the names."

"A monument, Bob," Julian said, standing beside him in his charcoal corduroy suit, his hand resting lightly on the marble plinth. "A record of human endeavour. Every name on that plate represents an autumn of vigilance."

Diana stepped forward from the crowd. 

She was wearing a silk dress of peacock blue, her silver pencil tucked behind her ear, holding a tray bearing three glasses of dry sherry.

She handed one to Julian. She handed one to Bob. 

She turned to Polly, who was standing by the tall sash window, looking out at the visitors wandering among the espaliered pears in the warm sun.

"Polly," Diana said.

Polly took the third glass. "Diana."

"The Bristol lease," Diana said softly. "The new edit suites in Spike Island. The contracts were signed this morning."

Polly looked at her over the sherry. "And Alistair?"

"Alistair," Diana said, her voice dry and perfectly controlled, "has agreed to be Director of Production. He grumbled for forty minutes about the cost of the ergonomic chairs, and then he ordered two tons of coal for the stove in the reception hall. He says it will keep the London commissioners from lingering too long after the rough-cuts."

Polly laughed—a clear, ringing laugh that echoed off the leather bindings of the library.

Across the room, Julian raised his glass.

He looked at Bob Craddock. He looked at Arthur Pendelton, who was standing by the door in his best blue suit, eating a cucumber sandwich with immense, solemn bites. 

He looked at Diana.

Then Julian Foss turned his head and looked directly across the crowded library at Polly Vance.

He did not raise his voice to its theatrical pitch. He did not call upon Shakespeare. He did not summon the glassblowers of Ghent or the bishops of the medieval church.

He spoke with the quiet, simple, unvanquished dignity of a man who had traveled a long road through the mud and had found, at the end of the journey, that he had arrived precisely where he was meant to be.

"To the assistant," Julian Foss said softly, his glass held high in the Wiltshire sunlight. "Who became the producer. To the diary, that is now our own. To the luggage, that is safely home. And to the loam... that forgives us all."

The room went still for one beautiful, suspended second.

Then eighty glasses were raised toward the window, eighty voices echoed the toast through the ancient house, and Polly Vance took a sip of her sherry, smiled at the man in the corduroy suit, and knew that the opening act was finally over, and the real story had just begun.

# The Iron Frisket

### Chapter 1

The lye in the washing tub had a thin skin of greenish ice over it, so tough that Katharina had to break it with the head of the wooden lye-brush before she could drop the first handful of lead types into the vinegar water.

The crack of the ice was small and sharp in the vaulted cellar, no louder than the snapping of a dry twig under a boot, but seven-year-old Lucas flinched where he sat on the low three-legged stool by the charcoal brazier. His hands were tucked inside the ragged cuffs of an adult doublet Pieter had worn three winters ago; the sleeves had been sheared off at the elbow with shears, leaving gray wool threads trailing against his thin, ink-stained wrists.

"It is only the tub," Katharina said without looking at him. Her voice had the dry, gravelly scrape that came from sleeping in the cellar damp and waking with the smoke of smoldering peat caught in the back of the throat. "Sort the nonpareil *m*s, Lucas. If they lie in the pi-box until noon, the ink will set like pitch in the counters, and we shall spend all of tomorrow picking them out with a cobbler’s awl."

"My fingers won't bend, Mother," the boy whispered. He did not say it as a complaint; it was a matter of physical accounting, stated in the flat, reasonable manner of children who had spent two years watching adults weigh bread against copper pence.

"Blow on them," she said. "Hold them over the pan for five breaths, no more. Peat is nine stuivers the basket at the Kipdorppoort, and Master Claes swore by the Holy Blood he would not carry another sod down the ladder until the feast of Saint Catherine."

Lucas held his hands over the red glow of the coals. His nails were rimmed with lampblack and dried walnut oil, the indelible badge of the trade that no amount of tallow-soap ever touched. On his left forefinger, where the soft skin met the quick, was the gray, calloused notch that every compositor carried after his first hundred galleys—the place where the sharp brass of the composing stick bit into the flesh as the line was justified.

Behind him, in the hollow beneath the wooden stairs that led up to the cooper’s drying shed, Clara stirred beneath the pile of mended wool quilts. She was barely four, small enough that her entire body made no more than a gentle heave under the gray frieze cloth. She gave a thin, wet cough, twice, swallowed in her sleep, and turned her face toward the cellar wall. The stones there were slick with saltpeter that bloomed like white moss in the wet months of the year, bitter on the tongue and cold as ditch-water.

Katharina plunged her bare hands into the tub.

The cold hit her wrists like a pair of iron bracelets. The lye was old, brewed from beech ashes and stale chamber-lye saved from the tenants above, sour and biting. It bit into the cracks around her knuckles, into the split skin along her thumbs where the cold had opened little red mouths that wept clear lymph when she flexed her hands. She did not snatch them back. She took the horsehair brush and scrubbed the face of the lead types, sixty letters at a time, held tight in an iron turtle. The ink—boiled linseed oil, resin, and the soot of burning turpentine—loosened slowly, bleeding into the gray water in greasy, iridescent swirls that smelled of a burnt church.

It was eight in the morning by the bell of the Cathedral of Our Lady, three hundred paces to the west over the rooftops of the Cammerpoort. Eight strokes: dull, bronze hammers falling through a fog so thick that the sound seemed to come from under the earth rather than out of the sky.

After the church bell came the drum.

It began at the Citadel, the great five-pointed monster of earth and brick that the Duke of Alba had planted in the south of the city twenty years ago, and rolled down the High Street toward the Town Hall. A slow, heavy beat: *drr-um, drr-um, drr-um-um-um*. The Spanish garrison changing the watch at the gates. Then the clatter of twenty pairs of iron-shod hooves on the paving stones of the Kammenstraat, passing the narrow alleyway that led to their courtyard.

Katharina held the horsehair brush motionless under the surface of the lye.

She did not breathe. Lucas did not breathe. Even the boy’s hands over the brazier stayed suspended, the red light tinting the underside of his chin.

The horses went by. The sound was different from the dray horses that hauled the tanners’ bark or the wine casks from the quays; these were heavy Gelderland geldings, iron-corsleted men in the saddle, their carbines slung across their knees, their scabbards clinking rhythmically against the brass bosses of their stirrups. They rode two abreast, the patrol of the *Amman*—the city bailiff—and the Provost’s provosts, sniffing for smoke where there should be no chimneys, looking for cellar grates that had been blacked out with felt or greasy parchment.

When the last iron shoe had clicked off toward the Veemarkt, Katharina let her breath go. It came out white, a pale feather against the dark vaulting.

"Get to work," she said softly.

Lucas reached into the box. *Click. Click. Click.* The tiny lead letters, cast from antimony and tin, fell into their proper compartments in the lower case: *a, e, i, o, u*, the thin spaces, the thick quadrats, the commas with their tails like flea-bites. He was quick, quicker than Pieter had been at his age, because Pieter had been raised in an open shop in the days of the old Emperor, when a master printer kept four presses on the ground floor, eight journeymen at the balls, three apprentices to damp the quires, and a sign swinging from the iron bracket over the street: *In the Sign of the Golden Compass* or *The White Dove*.

Pieter had been eighteen when the troubles came. Katharina had been twelve when the Spanish soldiers had broken the dykes and burned the suburbs, and twenty when she married him in the dark side-chapel of Saint Andrew’s, with the priest whispering the Latin so fast the words sounded like dry peas rattling in a bladder.

Now there were no signs. The guild-book of Saint Luke, which had once held the names of eighty masters of the art of typography, lay in the vault of the Town Hall with thirty-two red crosses drawn through the margins—men who had fled to Leiden, men who had gone to the scaffold at the Grote Markt, men who had died of the jail-fever in the Steen before their process could be drawn.

And the press.

Katharina lifted her head and looked across the cellar.

It stood in the deepest recess of the undercroft, where the brick groins met the foundation of the old medieval town wall. It did not look like the great double-screw presses that Christopher Plantin ran in his palace on the Vrijdagmarkt—great monsters of seasoned oak and polished iron that could strike off twelve hundred sheets between matins and vespers.

This was a half-press, built low to the earth, its cheeks fashioned from two rough-hewn baulks of Flemish elm that Pieter had bought from a ship-breaker’s yard by the dock. The winter after the Spanish took the city, when the Duke of Parma had closed the Scheldt and half the houses in the Cammerpoort were standing empty with their roofs rotting into the floor joists, Pieter had carried the timber into the cellar one plank at a time, by night, under a load of salt turnips.

He had turned the iron screw himself on a blacksmith’s lathe in the Klapdorp, working by stealth while the smith kept watch at the door with a draw-knife. The platen—the flat plate of iron that pressed the damp paper against the inked type—was only ten inches by twelve, just large enough to take an octavo forme or a single sheet of broadsheet cut in half.

The main press, the great three-pull press with the gilded lion on the cap that had belonged to Pieter’s father, had been broken in the courtyard eighteen months ago.

She could still see the marks of the sledges on the stones outside the cellar hatch. They had not taken it away. The Provost’s men did not waste oxen on unlicensed timber; they carried twelve-pound iron mauls and four-sided cold-chisels. They had split the cheeks down the grain, sheared the brass nuts from the winter-beam, and smashed the coffin until the marble bed broke into five jagged pieces like loaves of dry bread. Then they had taken the type-cases—eight cases of Robert Granjon’s beautiful roman, four of Hendrik van den Keere’s heavy black-letter, the italic that had cost thirty silver florins from the foundry in Frankfurt—and dumped them into the mud of the gutter, shoveling three buckets of night-soil over the lead so that the metal could not be gathered and recast without poisoning the furnace.

They had left Pieter on his knees in the sludge, his apron torn from his chest, his spectacles crushed into his cheek.

*“Let the ink dry on your tongue, heretic,”* the sergeant had said. He was a Walloon named Baude, who had lost an ear at the siege of Tournai and wore a strip of black velvet pinned to his hat to cover the scar. *“The next time you press an alphabet, it will be the Lord’s Prayer on the back of your skin with an iron branding-wire.”*

They had not known about the elm press in the vault. They had not known about the three hidden cases of pica and italic buried beneath the salt-herring barrels, wrapped in oiled pigs’ bladders to keep the brine from pitting the faces of the type.

And they had taken Pieter away anyway.

Not for what he had printed that week—they had found nothing on his drying-lines except four dozen receipt-books for a timber factor on the quay and three hundred copies of a Latin grammar for the Latin school at Saint James. They had taken him because his name was written on an old slip of Italian paper that a clerk had found in the desk of an apothecary who had been strangled in the Steen three days before.

Katharina wiped her dripping hands on her wool apron. The rough gray hemp was stiff with cold, smelling of her own sweat and the stale fish-meal they had boiled for yesterday’s dinner.

She walked to the foot of the stairs, her felt slippers making no sound on the packed dirt. She reached up into the gap between the third riser and the stone foundation wall, where the darkness was absolute and thick with spiders’ webs that clung to her hair like cold lace.

Her fingers found the tin canister.

It was an old spice-box that had once held ginger from the Portuguese ships, its red lacquer peeled away in scabs, the lid sealed with mutton-tallow to keep out the damp. She pried the lid off with her thumbnail.

Inside lay the single sheet of paper.

It was not Dutch rag paper, coarse and yellow, but fine Genoa linen, thin as an onion skin, bearing the watermark of a three-tiered crown and an anchor. On it were six lines of Latin, written in the cramped, beautiful, vicious hand of the city chancellery, with the broad seal of the Bailiff of Antwerp pressed into red wax at the corner.

The seal was cracked across the center, where the Spanish lion’s paw reached for the shield, but the signature beneath it was undamaged. It was written with an iron-gall ink that had eaten slightly into the paper, leaving the strokes dark and brown as dried blood:

*Iudicatum per D. Balthasarem van Cloet, Notarium et Commissarium ad causas typographicas.*

*Balthasar van Cloet.*

She did not need to read the Latin. She had read it forty times by the light of a single tallow candle while Pieter was still breathing in the cellar of the Steen, before the cold and the bad water from the river had done the work that the executioner’s hemp had been cheated of.

*Forasmuch as Pieter Huysman, printer of this town, hath been found suspect of harboring, composing, and giving forth to the public ear sundry wicked, seditious, and schismatic papers touching the honor of our Sovereign Lord the King and the Holy Mother Church... let him be held in close ward without bail, book, or paper, until his confession be perfected...*

He had stayed in the Steen seven weeks.

She had sold the brass pots from the kitchen to pay the turnkeys for clean straw. She had sold her mother’s silver marriage-spoons to buy him two small pots of small beer each week, because the water in the lowest wards was drawn straight from the canal where the wool-washers dumped their potash.

On the eighth Tuesday, the turnkey had met her at the outer iron gate by the river stairs. He had not taken the four groats she held in her fist. He had simply pointed with his iron key toward the cart that was standing in the sleet, with four dead men lying across each other like bundles of wet sailcloth, waiting for the lime-pits outside the Kronenburg Gate.

*“Take the boots if you want them, widow,”* the man had said. *“The bailiff’s boy has already had the doublet.”*

Katharina put the tin canister back into the crevice.

She pressed the loose stone into place until it sat flush with the mortar. Then she took three steps across the cellar to where the elm press stood in the gloom, reached out her hand, and laid her palm against the flat oak cap.

The wood was cold as ice, but it was solid. When Pieter had framed it, he had pinned the joints with three-inch oak trunnels, soaked in vinegar so they would swell and never draw. The timber had not warped. The bed was true.

"Mother," Lucas said.

She turned.

The boy was standing by the brazier. He was holding his composing stick in his right hand, the brass shining where his thumb had rubbed it clean.

"There is someone at the cooper’s wicket," he said.

### Chapter 2

The cooper’s yard was silent, save for the dripping of the mist from the eaves of the barrel-sheds.

Master Claes was an old man who had survived four changes of religion by becoming stone deaf whenever an armed horseman rode down the street and by keeping three different banners rolled up in the thatch of his stable—one with the red Burgundian cross, one with the Prince of Orange’s orange-and-blue stripes, and one with the Virgin Mary sitting on a silver moon. He did not care what happened in the cellar beneath his split-oak hoops so long as his floorboards did not catch fire and the rent was paid in coin that did not crumble between the teeth.

The wicket gate that led from the alley into the yard did not have an iron bell; it had a dry sheep’s knuckle-bone hung on a cord, which tapped three times against the timber when the latch was lifted from the outside.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Katharina moved without thinking. It was an old dance, rehearsed until the limbs followed the pattern without the brain intervening.

She caught Lucas by the shoulder and pushed him toward the low partition where the peat was stacked. "Under the sacks," she whispered. "Take Clara. If you hear wood splinter, do not run toward the lane. Run through the sewer vault into the cellar of the three herring-casks. You know the grating that is loose?"

"Yes, Mother," the boy said. He did not cry. His eyes were very wide and dark, reflecting the dying coals of the brazier. He slipped into the space behind the dry birch-logs, moving like an otter into its holt, his small body disappearing beneath the gray sacking until only the tip of his wooden shoe was visible. Katharina kicked a handful of sawdust over the shoe.

She reached for the heavy iron fire-shovel that stood by the lye-tub.

It was three feet of forged charcoal-iron, weighing six pounds, with an edge that Pieter had ground sharp on the sandstone wheel to scrape the ink-stones. It was an awkward weapon for a woman who did not stand five feet high, but if a man came down the cellar ladder head-first, six pounds of iron on the nape of his neck would break him like a hare.

She stood in the shadow of the elm press, behind the great upright cheek, her shoulder pressed against the cold wood.

The cellar hatch—two heavy leaves of green oak, covered with tarred canvas to keep out the rain—creaked on its iron straps.

A thin line of daylight cut through the dark of the cellar, gray and dirty as greasy dishwater. With the light came the smell of the street: horse dung, boiling cabbage from the cook-shops by the bridge, and the raw, salt stench of the low-tide mud from the river.

A pair of boots came down the ladder.

They were not soldiers' boots. They were not the heavy, iron-pegged brogues of the Provost's archers, nor the high, yellow leather boots of the Spanish horsemen with their jingling spurs.

They were shoes of black Spanish cordovan leather, square-toed, clean around the welts, fastened with small buckles of dull steel that had been lacquered black so they would not catch the light. Above the shoes were stockings of heavy black wool from Douai, thick and expensive, the kind that cost three guilders the ell and were never sold to craftsmen.

The man came down four steps, then paused. He did not come further into the vault. He stood with his head just beneath the level of the cooper’s floor, his face obscured by the broad, stiff brim of a beaver-skin hat. Over his shoulders he wore a short cloak of dark Flemish broadcloth, trimmed with a narrow border of marten-fur that was wet around the hem with mist.

"Mistress Huysman," the man said.

The voice was not loud. It was dry, precise, the voice of a man who spent ten hours a day reading Latin depositions in a room where two clerks sat behind screens dipping goose-quills into copperas-gall.

Katharina did not lower the shovel. Her hands were sweating inside the wool mittens; the iron handle felt greasy and cold.

"There is no printer here," she said. Her voice came out harsher than she had intended, echoing off the damp brick groin behind her. "Master Claes rents this pit for his split stave-wood. The press that was here was broken by the officers of the Town Hall eighteen months ago. If you want old iron, go to the smith in the Klapdorp."

The man did not move. He reached out with one gloved hand—the glove was of fine deer-skin, dyed black, with five small bone buttons at the wrist—and laid his fingers against the rough elm ladder-pole.

"Master Claes does not keep two cases of Plantin’s cicero roman in his stave-room," the man said. He spoke Dutch, but it was the Dutch of the chancellery, smooth and Latinized, without the broad, gutter-rolling vowels of the river-people. "Nor does split oak smell of boiled walnut oil and turpentine. Put down the shovel, Katharina. If I had brought the archers with me, there would be six men at the grating in the alley and two with crowbars at the cooper’s hatch."

He took another step down.

Now he was inside the vault. The light from the hatch fell across his shoulder, cutting across his face.

He was fifty years old, perhaps fifty-five. His hair, where it showed beneath the black brim of his beaver hat, was cut short according to the Spanish fashion, iron-gray and stiff as boar’s bristles. His beard was trimmed to a narrow, sharp spade that ended two inches below his chin, salted with white around the corners of his mouth. The skin of his cheeks was yellowish, like old vellum that had been kept in a box with dried cloves, marked by two deep, vertical folds that ran from the wings of his nostrils to the corners of his lips—the lines of a man who had not smiled at a table for twenty years.

Katharina did not drop the shovel.

Her eyes traveled from the fur border of his cloak down to the leather pouch that hung from his girdlestead on three steel chains. It was a notary’s satchel, square, stiffened with pasteboard, its flap secured by a small brass clasp shaped like a folded leaf.

She had seen that satchel before.

She had seen it resting on the corner of the oak table in the small examination chamber behind the Amman’s court, while two turnkeys in leather jacks held Pieter’s arms behind his back so that his elbows touched behind his spine, until the joints made a sound like dry birch-wood breaking under a cooper’s mallet.

She knew the ring on the man’s middle finger: a heavy oval of dark bloodstone, carved with three water-lilies and an open book.

*Balthasar van Cloet.*

The breath died in her chest. A cold, hollow ache opened behind her ribs, so sudden and vast that her knees trembled against the wool of her skirts. For a fraction of a heartbeat, she thought the earth was slipping away beneath her slippers, that the river had broken through the sluices and was filling the vault with black Scheldt mud.

"You," she whispered.

Van Cloet did not flinch. He looked at her with eyes that were neither cruel nor angry; they were the pale, watery gray of winter pond-water, completely flat, taking in the shovel, the lye-tub, the three cases of type under the canvas, and the two small wooden shoes half-hidden under the peat-sacks.

"I," he said.

"Pieter is dead," she said. She did not raise her voice; it was too thin to carry. "He has been in the lime-pit at the Kronenburg since the second week of Lent. There is nothing left here to take. You took the copper kettle. You took his Bible with the German plates. You took the five silver florins he had saved to buy paper from the mills at Huy. If you have come to break the elm wood, do it yourself. The maul is behind the door. I have no money to pay the executioner’s fee."

Van Cloet looked at the elm press. He walked past her—not quickly, with the slow, measured pace of a man inspecting an archive—and laid his black-gloved fingers upon the iron spindle.

He did not look at her while he spoke.

"The pitch on this thread is too coarse," he said, his finger following the spiral of the iron. "A common press needs three inches of drop to the quarter-turn. If you cut it shallower, the boy who pulls the rounce must strain his belly against the bar to get an impression on damp rag. Pieter was always an indifferent smith. He understood the casting of letters, but his iron-work was rude."

"He was an honest master," Katharina spat. "He did not sell his ink to the highest alderman."

"Honesty," Van Cloet said, his hand remaining on the wood, "is an expensive humor in a town where the garrison eats forty sacks of rye each day and the river is stopped with Spanish hulks from Rupelmonde to the sea. Christopher Plantin is not honest. He prints the King’s Polyglot Bible with eight presses on the ground floor, and in his private room he prints the *Mirror of the World* for the heretics in Frankfurt under an anagram. That is why Plantin has twelve journeymen and three thousand gold florins on the exchange, and Pieter is in the pit."

"Did you come here to tell me about Plantin?"

Van Cloet turned. He reached beneath his cloak, his hand disappearing into the deep pocket of his doublet.

Katharina’s fingers tightened on the shovel handle. If he drew an iron whistle to summon the patrol, she would strike him across the bridge of the nose before the silver touched his lips.

He did not draw a whistle.

He drew a small, heavy bag of unbleached Holland linen, tied around the neck with a leather thong. He did not toss it; he set it down on the clean pine stone-bed of the press, between the iron frisket and the wooden coffin.

The bag hit the timber with a dull, heavy *chink*.

Not the thin, tinny clatter of copper mites or base-metal groats. The sound was dense, oily, the unmistakable dead clink of thick, unclipped silver.

"There are twenty-five Brabant daalders in that linen," Van Cloet said. "Full weight, minted at Antwerp before the fall of the town. None of the Duke of Parma's pot-metal coin that turns black if a horse pisses near it. Each one is worth thirty-two stuivers on the exchange."

Katharina stared at the linen bag.

Twenty-five daalders.

In the market at the Grote Markt, a basket of dry split birch was five stuivers. A bushel of sound rye, free of ergot and weed-seed, was twenty-four. A tub of salt herring from the Zeeland boats was two daalders. With twenty-five daalders, a woman could buy four bags of meal, two loads of peat, eight pounds of lard, and three yards of heavy English frieze to make warm cloaks for two children whose ribs were beginning to show through their skin like the wickerwork of a fish-trap.

It was the price of an entire winter. It was life until the ice went out of the Scheldt in March.

"Why?" she said.

Van Cloet unbuckled the brass leaf-clasp of his satchel.

"I have a copy," he said. "It is twelve leaves in octavo. Small pica, leaded if you have the spaces, or set solid if you are short of metal. Three hundred sheets. No more, no less."

"What copy?"

"A memorial," he said. He took from the satchel a bundle of folded sheets, tied with a strip of blue linen tape. The paper was clean, fresh, the corners sharp. "An account of certain proceedings held in the Town Hall between the feast of Saint Michael and the first day of this month. Specifically, the names, properties, and secret contracts of the five commissioners of the confiscated goods who ordered the demolition of the twelve printshops in the Cammerpoort, the seizure of the paper-stocks in the Hopland, and the death of four men whose names you know."

Katharina did not reach for the paper. Her eyes remained fixed on his face.

"You are the commissioner," she said.

"I was," Van Cloet said. His voice did not alter by a fraction of a tone; it was still the dry, measured scrape of a pen on sheepskin. "Until Thursday noon. On Thursday noon, the Lord of Champagney and the Spanish treasurer found that my accounts of the salt-duties did not balance by six thousand florins. My house in the Meir is sealed with the King’s wax. My two clerks are in the bottom of the Steen, where Pieter was. If I am found in the streets after the third drum, the Provost will not trouble himself with an examination in the small chamber. He will hang me from the iron crane on the wharf by my belt."

"Then you are a ruined thief," Katharina said.

"I am an alderman who knows where the other four thieves keep their ledgers," he replied. "And you are a woman with two children who will have nothing to eat next week except the grease from their father’s ink-balls. Will you take the copy, or shall I go to the Klapdorp and give it to the journeyman who has two fingers missing from his right hand?"

Katharina looked at the linen bag on the press-bed.

From behind the peat-stack, there was a tiny, muffled sound—the sound of Clara shifting her cold feet against the sacks, her small teeth chattering in her sleep.

"Give me the paper," Katharina said.

### Chapter 3

Van Cloet did not leave by the cooper’s wicket.

He waited until the tenth stroke of the Cathedral bell had died away and the sound of the noon-carts had filled the Kammenstraat with the rumble of iron-tired wheels and the swearing of the carters. Then he drew his beaver hat down until the brim touched the bridge of his hooked nose, pulled his cloak close around his throat, and climbed the elm ladder with the dry, deliberate agility of a spider returning to its crevice.

The oak hatch clicked shut. The bolt dropped into place from above.

Katharina did not touch the silver.

She stood in the center of the vault, her arms hanging by her sides, the heavy iron fire-shovel still resting against her wool skirts. The silence in the cellar was different now; it was no longer the empty, waiting cold of the morning, but something thick and dangerous, like the air in a room where a jar of vitriol had been overturned on the hearth.

"Lucas," she said.

The peat-sacks rustled. The boy crawled out on his knees, his hands gray with dust, his hair full of dried moss. He stood up, shaking himself like a terrier, his dark eyes looking first at the empty ladder, then at the press, and finally at the linen bag that lay on the clean pine wood.

"He was the man from the court," Lucas said.

Katharina looked at him sharply. "You were under the stairs when they took your father. You did not see his face."

"I saw the shoes," the boy said. His voice was very quiet, but steady. "He had the black buckles. When they held father against the well-curb in the yard, that man stood by the water-butt and wrote in a book with a horn ink-pot tied to his button. He did not look up when father shouted. He just wiped his pen on his sleeve and turned the leaf."

Katharina closed her eyes.

For a moment, the cellar smelled of that morning eighteen months ago—not the sour lye and the damp stones, but the sharp, oily reek of burning linseed oil, the sulfur of the torches the Provost’s men had carried to search the hay-loft, and the wet, earthy smell of the courtyard mud where Pieter’s forehead had been pressed into the stones.

*“Remember his hands,”* Pieter had whispered to her two years before he died, on the night they had hidden the elm press in the vault. *“The men with the swords are only dogs, Katharina. A dog bites because his master kicks him from behind. It is the man with the ink-pot you must watch. The man with the ink-pot is the one who remembers which house has the timber, which house has the type, and which child is small enough to starve before the frost breaks.”*

She walked to the press.

She reached out and picked up the bundle of papers that Van Cloet had left beside the silver.

The blue linen tape was knotted with a small, neat slip-loop. She pulled it loose. The paper unfolded with a crisp, dry rustle that sounded loud in the vault.

It was twelve leaves, octavo, written on both sides in a small, cursive secretary hand. It was not the loose, sprawling draft of an author who scratched out three words for every two he kept; it was an engrossed copy, clean, without a single blot or erasure, each line justified to a measure of eighteen pica ems, with the catchwords written neatly in the lower right-hand corner of every leaf, precisely as an experienced compositor would wish to find them.

The title ran across the top of the first page, set out in large, formal black-letter capitals that had been drawn with a square-cut reed:

*A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST OF THE INJURIES, ROBBERIES, AND UNLAWFUL PROCEEDINGS EXECUTED UPON THE POOR BURGHERS AND CRAFTSMEN OF ANTWERP BY THE FIVE LORDS OF THE COMMISSION.*

Beneath it, in a smaller, closer hand, came the text:

*“To all honest magistrates, elders of the guilds, and faithful citizens who desire the ancient liberties of this town to be preserved from the greed of those who have made their market out of our blood: Know ye that on the seventeenth day of September, in the year of our Lord 1585, there was gathered in the secret chamber of the Town Hall a certain private company, calling themselves the Commissioners for the Pacification of the Arts…”*

Katharina carried the leaves to the light that filtered through the small pavement-grating above the lye-tub.

The grating was set flush with the paving of the alleyway; through its iron bars she could see the muddy hems of women’s petticoats passing, the iron-rimmed wheels of the beer-sledges, the gray bellies of the pigs that scavenged the cabbage-stalks from the gutters. A thin drizzle had begun to fall, turning the dust on the glass into greasy streaks that smelled of horse-manure.

She turned the first leaf.

*“...First, Alderman Joost van Valckenisse, who did by his own private warrant seize into his hands forty-two bales of white printing paper from the warehouse of Gillis Coppens, under pretense that the said paper was bought with the money of the heretics in Zeeland; which paper the said Joost did straightway sell to Christopher Plantin for two hundred and forty gold carolus florins, which sum was never entered into the treasury of the town…”*

She turned the second leaf.

*“...Item, Master Hendrik van den Berghe, who caused the three presses of the widow of Willem Silvius to be broken with sledges, and the metal thereof, being six thousand weight of cast type, to be melted down in the cannon-foundry at the South Gate, under color of making ball for the King’s field-pieces; yet did the said Hendrik take from the furnace forty pigs of clean tin, worth eighty pounds Flemish, and conveyed them by night to his own country-house at Deurne…”*

Her breath came short and quick.

It was not a religious tract. There was not a word here of the Confession of Augsburg, not a phrase from Calvin’s *Institutes*, no dispute over the Sacrament or the authority of the Bishop of Rome.

It was something far more dangerous.

It was the account-book of the ruin of the town.

It had the dates, the hours, the names of the carters who had hauled the stolen quires, the names of the smiths who had taken the ironwork from the broken presses, the amounts paid to the informers, and the secret shares divided between the five magistrates in the back parlor of the Golden Fleece after each raid.

And on the seventh leaf, halfway down the page, her eye caught the name:

*“...Item, on the fourth day of May, in the year 1586, was the shop of Pieter Huysman, printer in the Cammerpoort, entered by the archers of the Amman, and his goods spoiled to the value of four hundred florins. In which matter it is to be noted that the said Pieter was not condemned by any sentence of the bench, nor had any witness sworn against him touching any matter of heresy; but only that the said Joost van Valckenisse had covetous desire of the house wherein the said Pieter dwelt, which house he hath since granted to his own nephew, a notary of the Council…”*

Katharina’s fingers clamped on the margins of the paper until the dry linen groaned under her nails.

*Not for heresy.*

Not even for the old Latin grammar, nor the schoolbooks, nor the three forgotten letters from his cousin in Frankfurt that had lain behind the chimney-piece for seven years.

He had died because Joost van Valckenisse wanted the house.

The house was gone anyway. The nephew had lived in it three months, until a mortar-bomb from the Dutch fleet at Lillo had fallen through the roof during the siege and burned the upper floors to a blackened shell of joists and plaster. Now it was only an open cellar where the neighborhood dumped their broken crockery and the offal from the pork-butchers.

Pieter had died in the cold water of the Steen for forty yards of blackened timber and an open pit where the dogs fought over sheep-heads.

"Mother?"

Lucas was at her elbow. He had climbed onto the three-legged stool to look over her shoulder. His thin, dark face was close to the paper; he could read the larger letters, the black-letter titles, though the small cursive was still too difficult for his eyes.

"Is father's name there?" he asked.

"Yes," she said.

"Does it say where they took him?"

"No," Katharina said. Her voice was steady now, hard and cold as the flagstones under her feet. "It says who took him."

She laid the manuscript down on the composing stone.

Beside it lay the linen bag of silver. Twenty-five daalders. It sat on the gray pine timber like an iron shot that had been fired into the wall of a church, heavy, blunt, and impossible to ignore.

"We have no paper," Lucas said. He was looking at the press with the practical, narrow wisdom of a child who had grown up in an empty shop. "There are only three quires of the gray wrapping-paper from Master Claes under the stairs, and it is full of oat-chaff and knots. If you put that on the forme, the ink will run like grease on a trencher."

"We will have paper," Katharina said.

"And the ink," the boy said. "The varnish in the stone jar is two years old. It has turned to liver. If we thin it with turpentine, the smoke will come through the cellar-grating into the lane. The watch will smell it before the first sheet is pulled."

Katharina turned toward the ladder.

She walked to the crevice behind the stairs, reached in, and took down the spice-box once more. She did not take out the warrant; she took out three small iron keys tied to a copper wire, and a leather purse that contained twelve copper mites and a single silver groat with the head of the old Emperor Charles, worn so smooth that the face looked like an egg.

"Stay with Clara," she said to the boy.

"Where are you going?"

"To the Hopland," she said. "To the cellar of Master Gillis. He has five bundles of French crown-paper that he hid under his hay-stacks before the Spaniards took the gates. If he is still alive, he will sell me two reams for three daalders."

"The patrol is in the street," Lucas whispered.

"The patrol is at the Town Hall," she said. "They are changing the guard for the noon-meal. They do not look at women with market-baskets. Put the fire out in the brazier, Lucas. Do not leave a single spark. If a wisp of smoke goes through that grating while I am gone, the cooper will bring the constables with him before I reach the bridge."

She took her heavy wool shawl from the nail behind the door, wrapped it twice around her head and shoulders, and tucked the linen bag of silver into the deep pocket of her petticoat.

The weight of the coins banged against her thigh with every step she took—heavy, cold, and hard as lead.

### Chapter 4

The street of the Hopland smelled of burned malt and wet thatch.

Three years ago, it had been the wealthiest quarter of the inland trade, where the great merchants from Liège and Cologne kept their warehouses of wool, madder, and printing paper from the mills of Champagne. Now two houses out of five were charred timber frames, propped up with fir baulks to keep them from tumbling into the road; the gardens behind the brick walls, where the rich burghers had grown their tulips and winter-cabbages, were trampled into a gray marsh of ruts and horse-litter.

Katharina walked with her head down, her wooden-soled slippers clicking on the greasy stones.

In her right hand she carried an old wicker market-basket with a torn lid, covered with a rag of salt-stained linen that smelled of smoked sprats. Under the sprats lay six of Van Cloet’s silver daalders, wrapped in a scrap of wool so they would not clink together when she walked.

A company of Walloon infantry was lounging under the stone archway of the old Cloth Hall.

They were dirty, lean-jawed men in iron caps that had gone brown with rust, their doublets patched with greasy sheepskin, their bare legs red and chapped above their low shoes. They sat on the mounting-blocks, playing dice on the head of an empty herring-tub, using lead bullets for counters. One of them, a boy no older than eighteen with a squint and a great carbuncle on his lip, looked up as Katharina passed.

*“Where are you going, mother?”* he shouted in his thick, mangled French. *“Bring us some beer from the Red Lion, or we’ll search your basket for fat bacon.”*

Katharina did not look at him. She did not quicken her pace. To run was to invite a halberd between the shoulder-blades; to speak was to invite a hand under her shawl. She kept her eyes on the mud two paces before her feet, her slippers dragging slightly, her back curved into the round, beaten hunch of a fishwife who had carried thirty pounds of sprats from the quays since four in the morning.

The boy laughed, spat into the gutter, and rolled his dice again.

She turned into the narrow lane behind the Church of Saint Walburga.

Here the houses were older, built of timber and wattle, their upper floors jutting out until the eaves almost touched across the lane, cutting off the gray sky and leaving the alley in a perpetual, stagnant dusk.

At the sign of the *Crowned Stork*, the door was closed.

It was a heavy door of split oak, studded with round-headed iron nails, with a small iron grille at the height of a man’s eye. The shop had once sold writing-ink, Dutch quills, parchment from the tanners of Dinant, and fine linen paper from the mills of Troyes. Now the shutters were nailed fast with spruce battens, and across the lintel someone had smeared a cross of red ochre—the mark that showed the master had been fined for non-attendance at the Mass of the Restoration.

Katharina did not knock on the door.

She walked down the narrow passage beside the house, where the eaves dripped into a stone trough full of green scum, to the low cellar door that opened under the kitchen stairs.

She tapped with the iron heel of her slipper: twice, a pause, then three times quick.

From within the house came the sound of a heavy chain being dragged across a flagstone. A bolt grated in its socket, stiff with rust. The door opened four inches, held by a short iron check-strap.

A face appeared in the gap.

It was an old man’s face, thin and yellow as an apothecary’s parchment, with a tuft of white beard under the lip and two small, red-rimmed eyes that blinked against the dull gray light of the alley. He wore a greasy skull-cap of black velvet and a fur-lined gown that had lost half its fur, showing the moldy canvas beneath.

"Master Gillis," she said.

The old man stared at her through the gap for three heartbeats. His mouth worked, his tongue wetting his dry lips with a faint clicking sound.

"Pieter’s wife," he said.

"Yes."

"Pieter is dead," the old man whispered. He looked past her shoulder into the empty passage, his eyes darting from the stone trough to the chimney-stacks above. "They took him to the Steen. I told him not to keep the Italian paper. I told him three times at the guild-meeting: the Italian paper has the watermark of the Duke of Ferrara, and the Duke is a friend to the Prince of Orange. A man who keeps Italian paper in his loft is a man who wants to sleep with his neck in an iron collar."

"I have not come for Italian paper," she said. "I have come for crown-paper. Two reams of the French rag from Troyes."

The old man laughed—a dry, wheezing cough that brought a string of yellow phlegm to his lip.

"Crown-paper," he said. "She wants crown-paper. Why not the Emperor’s satin from Venice? Why not silk from the Levant? The Spaniards have burned the mills at Huy. The barges from Champagne have not come down the Meuse since the feast of Saint John. If a man has ten sheets of clean rag in this town, he sells it to the Chancellery for three groats the sheet to write death-warrants on."

Katharina reached into her basket.

She pushed her hand under the salt-stained cloth, drew out two of the silver daalders, and held them between her thumb and forefinger through the four-inch gap in the door.

The silver caught the dull light of the passage.

Old Gillis stopped coughing.

His eyes widened until the red veins in the corners looked like little spiders. He reached out with two long, yellow fingers, the nails black with ink and garden-dirt, and touched the rim of the nearest coin. He did not take it; he felt the edge, testing the milling with the pad of his thumb.

"Brabant silver," he whispered. "Good metal. Where did you get this, Katharina?"

"My brother is a carter at Mechelen," she lied without a second’s hesitation. "He sold two hogsheads of salt beef to the garrison at the bridge. Will you give me the paper, or shall I go to the French merchant by the Red Gate?"

"The French merchant has nothing," Gillis said quickly, his fingers curling around the coins. "The French merchant had his throat cut by the mutineers three weeks ago, and his cellar was cleared to the bare gravel. Wait."

The door did not open further. The strap stayed in its iron staple.

The old man turned into the darkness of the cellar. She heard him moving among the empty crates, his slippers shuffling through what sounded like dry straw, followed by the clinking of iron bars and the heavy, sliding sound of a timber being hauled across a floor.

He was gone ten minutes.

Katharina stood in the passage. The mist had turned to a steady, freezing drizzle that soaked through her wool shawl, trickling down the back of her neck in a thin, cold thread. Every sound from the street—the distant rumble of a dray, the barking of a dog, the clatter of a halberd against a gate-post—made the muscles in the back of her calves bunch like cords.

If a patrol came down the alley now, she was trapped between the brick wall and the stone trough. There was no second way out. They would find the silver in her basket; they would find the old man with the paper; they would have them both in the small room at the Town Hall before the Cathedral bell struck one.

The door creaked.

Old Gillis reappeared in the opening. He was breathing hard, his chest rattling with each breath like an empty cart on cobbles.

In his arms he carried two packages, wrapped in coarse, tarred canvas and tied with hemp twine. They were flat, square, about fifteen inches by twelve, each package containing one ream—five hundred sheets—of royal crown-paper, trimmed to the small octavo measure.

"It has been under the hay since the winter of the siege," the old man muttered. He pushed the first package through the gap. It was heavy, dense, smelling of tar and old clover. "The margins are a little foxed with the damp around the edges, but the centers are clean. Pure linen rag. You can strike it with black ink and it will not sink through to the back like the German stuff from Basel."

Katharina took the package and set it into her basket. It filled the wickerwork from side to side; she had to press it down with both hands to get the lid to shut over the canvas.

She held out the remaining four daalders.

Gillis snatched them from her palm. His fingers were hot and dry, like old bread fresh from the hearth. He bit the edge of one coin with his three remaining teeth, nodded once with a sharp, bird-like jerk of his chin, and slipped the silver into the lining of his gown.

"Do not bring it back," he said. "If the archers find you with it, tell them you bought it from the dead Frenchman’s boy. If you name my house, Katharina, I will swear by the seven wounds of God that you stole the silver from my chest while I was at the chapel."

"I will not name your house," she said.

"What are you printing?" he asked suddenly. His hand stayed on the edge of the door, his watery eyes narrowing as he peered at her through the gray drizzle. "It is not schoolbooks. A woman does not pay six silver daalders for crown-paper to print the *Distichs of Cato*."

Katharina looked him in the eyes.

"Pieter’s memorial," she said.

The old man went very still. The yellow skin of his forehead gathered into three deep, horizontal furrows.

"You are a fool, girl," he whispered. "Pieter is in the lime. The lime does not care what is printed on crown-paper. Go home and buy bacon for your children."

The door slammed shut. The iron bolt shot home with a sound like a pistol-shot.

Katharina lifted the basket.

The weight was immense—forty pounds of wet canvas, linen rag, and wickerwork, hanging from a single hemp handle that bit into the flesh of her fingers through her wool mittens. She swung the basket onto her right hip, hitched her shawl over her head until her face was buried in the wool, and stepped out of the alley into the muddy torrent of the street.

### Chapter 5

By three in the afternoon, the cellar had become a furnace of black grease and boiling oil.

Katharina had built up the fire in the brazier with small pieces of dry beech-charcoal, which burned without smoke if the draught was kept low. Over the coals sat an iron pipkin containing three pints of old linseed oil, two lumps of rosin no larger than walnuts, and four ounces of Venice turpentine that she had scraped from the bottom of an earthenware jar Pieter had hidden behind the water-butt.

The smell was frightful. It was thick, sweet, and suffocating, coating the tongue with an oily film that tasted of old varnish and resin.

Lucas stood beside her, his face pale, holding a wooden spatula with a long handle.

"Keep it moving," she said, her voice muffled by the linen rag she had tied across her nose and mouth. "If it catches the flame, it will throw a sheet of fire to the ceiling. If you see it begin to froth like milk, take the pipkin off the coals with the iron tongs. Do not touch the pot with your hands."

"Yes, Mother," the boy said. His eyes were watering from the fumes, but he stirred the dark, bubbling liquid with a steady, rhythmic circle, his thin shoulder rising and falling beneath his doublet.

Katharina sat at the composing stone.

The light from the pavement-grating was almost gone now; the winter dusk had come down over the Cammerpoort, blue and cold, turning the greasy glass above her head into a square of dark slate. She had lit two tallow dips—not the fine wax candles of the churches, but the cheap, yellow dips made from sheep’s fat that smelled of a slaughter-house and sputtered every three minutes with a sharp *spat* that threw black flakes across the bench.

Beside her, on the wooden galley, lay the first forme.

She had set forty lines of the *Declaration*.

She worked with a speed that came from twelve years of watching Pieter at the frame, but her fingers were smaller than his, more agile among the tiny subdivisions of the pica case. She held the iron composing stick in her left hand, her thumb holding the letters against the brass rule as she set them:

*A - D - E - C - L - A - R - A - T - I - O - N*

*space.*

*A - N - D*

*space.*

*M - A - N - I - F - E - S - T*

Every letter was reversed, upside-down and back-to-front. To an untrained eye, the line of lead was an illegible thicket of gray notches and jagged shoulders; to Katharina, it was as clear as the Latin inscription over the gate of the Town Hall. She read it as she set it, her eye catching the nick on the belly of each type to ensure that no letter was turned upside-down:

*...Item, the said Joost van Valckenisse did by his own private warrant...*

She justified the line. It was too long by half a thick space.

She took the small steel tweezers and lifted the third space between *private* and *warrant*, replacing it with a thin space of beaten brass. She tested the line with her thumb. It was snug; it did not rattle, yet it was not so tight that the lead would bow when the iron chase was locked with the wooden quoins.

She laid the brass rule over the face of the letters, lifted the next handful from the case, and began again:

*...seize into his hands forty-two bales of white printing paper...*

"Mother," Lucas whispered.

The oil in the pipkin had begun to smoke. A thin, blue vapor was rising from the surface, smelling sharply of burned bread.

Katharina dropped the composing stick onto the stone, took the iron tongs, and lifted the pot off the coals. She carried it to the stone slab where the vine-black was laid out.

The vine-black was the secret of Pieter’s shop. Anyone could make ink with lampblack from the chimney-flues, but common soot was greasy; it smeared when the damp paper was folded, and turned yellow after two years in the light. Pieter had made his black from the charred twigs of Champagne grapevines, burned in a sealed iron pot until they were reduced to a charcoal so soft and light that a single grain could be crushed between two fingernails into an impalpable powder.

She poured the hot oil over the black heap.

*Hiss-s-s.*

A cloud of black steam puffed into her face, making her choke behind her linen mask.

She took the heavy granite muller—a stone shaped like an enormous turnip, flat on the bottom, with a handle carved out of the rock—and began to grind the mass.

*Scritch. Scritch. Scritch.*

Round and round, with the full weight of her shoulders behind the stone. The oil and the soot met, fought, and blended, the gray powder turning into a black, lustrous paste that rolled before the stone like melted pitch. It had to be ground until there was not a single grit remaining, not a speck of uncarbonized wood, until the ink was as smooth as butter and drew out from the palette-knife in long, glistening threads that did not break for six inches.

Her arms burned. The muscles across her back, between her shoulder-blades, knotted into an ache that made her teeth click together.

"Clara is waking," Lucas said.

Katharina stopped the stone. She wiped her forehead with the back of her sleeve, leaving a long, black streak of vine-oil across her temple.

The child was sitting up beneath the quilts. Her cheeks were flushed red, not with health, but with the dry, burning heat of the cellar-fever. Her eyes were glazed, staring at the yellow flame of the tallow dip with the wide, empty wonder of a sick dog.

"Bread," the little girl murmured. "Mother. The bread is hard."

Katharina walked to the bench.

She had bought two loaves of rye bread from the baker by the bridge with four of the copper mites she had left in her purse. She had cut off a crust, soaked it in a little warm beer from the pipkin, and sprinkled it with three grains of coarse salt.

She sat on the edge of the low pallet, took Clara onto her lap, and fed her the mash with a horn spoon.

The child’s skin was hot through her wool shift, burning against Katharina’s ribs like a hot brick wrapped in flannel. She swallowed three spoonfuls, then turned her face away, leaning her forehead against Katharina’s breast.

"Cold," she whispered.

"The bed will be warm soon, heart," Katharina murmured. She rocked the child with a slow, mechanical sway, her hand patting the thin little shoulder through the rough frieze. "Mother has the silver now. Tomorrow we will have three loads of peat from the Kempen. We will have a fire in the hearth that will burn until the bells ring for Christmas. We will have white bread from the French baker, and butter from the polders."

Clara did not answer. Her eyes closed; her breathing was rough, rattling in her small pipes with the sound of wind through dry reeds.

Katharina laid her down again. She pulled the quilts up to the child’s chin, tucked the edges tight against the cellar wall to keep out the draught that came from the loose stones, and stood up.

"Lucas," she said.

"Yes, Mother?"

"Get the sheepskin balls."

The boy went to the corner behind the press.

He brought out the two inking balls—two hemispheres of turned beech-wood, stuffed with uncarded sheep’s wool and covered with pelt from an uncastrated ram, soft and oily. The leather was stiff from months of disuse, dry and yellow around the nails that held it to the wood.

"We must grease them," Katharina said. "Take a piece of tallow from the candle-end and rub it into the grain until the skin is soft as an old glove. If the pelt is dry, it will pull the letters out of the chase when you rock it across the forme."

She walked back to the stone.

She picked up the composing stick once more.

*...Item, the said Joost van Valckenisse did take from the house of the said Pieter Huysman his Bible with the German plates...*

The line was set.

She lifted it with her thumb and two fingers—a block of forty lead types, three inches long, weighing less than two ounces—and carried it to the iron chase.

Her hands did not shake.

The cold was coming through her slippers from the dirt floor, numbing her toes until they felt like pebbles, but her fingers were hot, sticky with the vine-oil and the varnish.

She was thirty years old.

She had twenty-five silver daalders in the pocket of her skirt. She had two sick children in a cellar where the water rose through the flagstones at every spring-tide. She had twelve leaves of treason on her composing stone, written in the hand of the man who had killed her husband.

She set the line into the chase.

*Click.*

The lead met the lead with the sound of a key turning in an iron lock.

### Chapter 6

At two in the morning, the city was dead.

The frost had come down with the midnight tide, sharp and black, freezing the slush in the gutters into ridges of jagged ice that broke under a horse’s hoof with a sound like pistol-fire. Over the roofs of the Cammerpoort, the great spire of the Cathedral stood up against the stars like a spear of black iron, its bells silent between the third watch and prime.

In the cellar beneath the cooper’s shed, the only light was a single tallow dip, shielded behind a broken piece of slate so that its beam fell only upon the tympan of the press.

The forme was locked.

Katharina had planed it with the wooden planer, striking the block with the mallet until every letter sat flat upon the marble bed. She had wedged the elm quoins tight with the shooting-stick, tapping them home until the chase rang like an iron bell when she struck it with her knuckles.

She had lifted the forme by its corners. Not a letter had dropped out. The justification was true; the eighty lines of pica were held in the iron frame as if they had been cast in a single block of metal.

"Lucas," she whispered.

The boy stood on the low platform of birch-logs that Pieter had built so that a child could reach the rounce.

He looked old in the dim light. His eyes were sunk deep into his skull, surrounded by dark, bluish circles of fatigue, but his hands were steady on the handles of the sheepskin balls. He had worked them on the ink-block until the surfaces were black, glossy, and completely even, coated with a skin of ink so thin that it did not show a single bead when he held it to the candle.

"Damp the paper," she said.

She took the first sheet of French crown-paper from the pile between the wet boards.

It was cold, limp, smelling of linen and stream-water. She laid it upon the parchment of the tympan, fitting the two small holes in the margin over the brass register-points that Pieter had filed from old pins.

She folded down the frisket.

The frisket was an iron frame covered with stout parchment, in which Pieter had cut small square windows with a sharp knife, exactly corresponding to the pages of type. It fell over the paper with a soft, leathery *slap*, covering the clean white margins so that no stray smear of ink from the chase could touch the edges.

"Ink," she said.

Lucas stepped forward.

He worked with both hands, rocking the two balls across the face of the type with a quick, twisting motion: *pat-pat-pat, roll-roll-roll*. The leather met the metal with a soft, sucking sound—the sound of clean ink biting into clean lead. He did not hit the type; he kissed it with the sheepskin, leaving every letter black, wet, and sharp against the dark iron of the chase.

Katharina folded down the tympan.

Now the paper lay against the inked metal, held between the parchment and the lead.

She caught the rounce with her left hand—the wooden crank that turned the spit and ran the carriage beneath the platen.

*Crank. Creak.*

The carriage rolled forward on its iron ribs, the greased leather straps tightening beneath the bed with a low, groaning sigh.

She caught the bar with her right hand.

The bar was three feet of ash-wood, fitted into the iron eye of the screw. It was smooth, dark with the grease of Pieter’s palms, worn flat where his thumb had pressed against the wood for seven years.

She set her feet against the dirt floor. She leaned her weight into the bar.

*Pull.*

The screw turned. The iron platen came down upon the tympan with the slow, monstrous leverage of the three-inch thread.

She felt the resistance through the ash-wood—the cushion of the wool blankets inside the tympan yielding first, then the hard, unyielding resistance of the lead types meeting the wet linen paper.

She did not jerk the bar. She pulled it home with a long, steady heave of her back, holding it for the space of one heartbeat while the ink sank into the pores of the rag.

*Clack.*

The screw hit the stop.

She threw the bar back. The counter-weight on the lever lifted the platen with a sharp, iron rattle.

She turned the rounce backwards. The carriage rolled out from the shadow of the press.

She lifted the tympan. She lifted the frisket.

She took the sheet by the corners with her fingertips, her nails keeping clear of the printed space, and lifted it from the pins.

The paper came away from the lead with a faint, tearing hiss—the sound that every printer knows better than his wife’s voice, the sound of clean rag parting from inked metal.

She carried it to the candle.

The black was magnificent.

It was not the watery, brownish smudge of the cheap pamphlets that the peddlers sold under their cloaks at the bridge; it was deep, velvety, dense as a priest’s cassock, sitting upon the cream-colored linen like letters carved out of jet. The impression was even from the top of the page to the bottom; the counters of the *e*s and the *a*s were open and clean, without a single blur or bead of grease.

*A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

The words stood up from the paper in tiny, crisp ridges that she could feel with the pad of her thumb—the true, three-dimensional bite of typography, the mark that no scribe with a reed could ever imitate.

"It is clean, Mother," Lucas whispered. He had leaned down from his platform, his ink-stained face inches from the wet sheet.

"It is clean," she said.

She laid the sheet upon the drying-rack behind the stairs.

"Two hundred and ninety-nine," she said.

The Cathedral bell struck three.

Outside in the lane, there was a sudden, sharp sound.

The rattle of an iron-shod scabbard against a stone wall.

Katharina did not drop the paper. She laid it flat upon the laths, reached down with her left hand, and pinched the wick of the tallow dip between her wet thumb and forefinger.

The light died.

The cellar was plunged into an absolute, black darkness that smelled of hot sheep’s fat, vine-oil, and the sour frost that was creeping through the grating in the pavement.

Above their heads, through the two inches of oak that formed the cooper’s floor, came the sound of boots.

Two men. They were walking slowly down the center of the lane, their iron heels clicking on the frozen ruts. They stopped directly over the pavement-grating.

Katharina reached out in the dark.

Her hand found Lucas’s shoulder. She pressed down with her fingers, hard, until the boy sank down onto the birch-logs, his breath held in his throat.

Through the iron bars of the grating, five feet above her head, came the red, smoky glow of a pine-torch.

The light cut through the dirt on the glass, throwing a grid of black bars across the vaulted brickwork of the ceiling. With the light came the smell of the torch—pitch-pine and sulfur—and the sound of a man spitting into the gutter.

*“There is no smoke here,”* a voice said. It was Walloon French, rough and guttural. *“The chimney is cold as an old ditch.”*

*“The old man said he heard a carpenter’s hammer,”* a second voice replied. This one was Flemish, the voice of a city watchman from the ward of Saint Andrew. *“He swore by the Virgin there was knocking under the stave-shed since the second watch.”*

*“Old Claes is eighty years old and drinks three pots of small beer before he goes to his straw,”* the Walloon said. *“He hears his own knees cracking and thinks it is the Prince of Orange with a spade. Come on. The corporal has a pot of hot wine at the gate-house, and my feet are like two lumps of lard.”*

The red light flickered on the ceiling.

The boots moved. The iron scabbards clinked.

The sound went down the lane toward the river, slower and slower, until it was swallowed by the dull, distant roar of the water rushing through the piers of the bridge.

Katharina did not move for five minutes.

She stood in the black vault, her hand on the boy’s shoulder, listening to the small, regular click of the water dripping from the lye-tub into the stones.

Then she took her tinder-box from her pocket.

*Strike. Strike.*

The spark caught the charred linen. The pale blue smoke rose from the tin. She blew on it until the ember glowed red, touched the sulphur-match to the coal, and lit the tallow dip once more.

The little yellow flame stood up, straight and steady in the cold air.

She looked at the press.

The forme was waiting on the carriage, its black letters shining in the tallow-light like the scales of an adder. The paper lay under the wet boards.

She took the second sheet from the pile.

"Lucas," she said, her voice dropping into the quiet, rhythmic cadence of the shop. "Ink the forme."

The boy picked up the balls.

*Pat-pat-pat.*

The iron screw turned in its socket. The ash-wood bar came home.

*Pull.*

### Chapter 7

By noon of the second day, the last sheet had been lifted from the pins.

Three hundred copies of the *Declaration*, gathered into twenty-five quires of twelve leaves each, lay upon the drying-poles beneath the floor joists of the cooper’s shed. They hung like white banners in the gloom, their pages curling slightly as the cold air carried off the moisture of the damp rag, filling the cellar with the clean, sharp smell of dried linen and boiled oil.

Katharina sat on the edge of the stone bed, her knees drawn up to her chin, her hands tucked inside her sleeves.

She had not slept for thirty-six hours.

Her eyes felt as if someone had thrown a handful of fine Scheldt sand into the sockets; every time she blinked, the lids dragged across her eyeballs with a dry, scratching heat that made her temples throb. Her right shoulder, where the strain of the three-hundred pulls had centered, was a knot of dull, throbbing pain that reached down into the muscles of her forearm.

Lucas lay across the foot of Clara’s pallet, his face buried in the wool quilt, his arm thrown over his little sister’s chest. He was sleeping so deeply that not even the thunder of the noon-cannon from the Citadel had made him stir.

Clara’s fever had broken with the dawn.

Her skin was cool now, clammy with a sour sweat that smelled of vinegar, but her breathing was slow, deep, and untroubled. When Katharina had touched the child’s forehead an hour ago, Clara had murmured something about a yellow bird, turned her cheek into the pillow, and slept again.

The oak hatch creaked above.

Katharina did not reach for the shovel this time. She sat still, watching the rectangle of gray light open in the ceiling.

Van Cloet came down the ladder.

He wore the same dark cloak, the same beaver hat, the same cordovan shoes with the blackened buckles. But he moved differently today; his movements were quicker, more abrupt, his head turning toward the drying-lines before his feet had touched the bottom rung.

He stopped in the center of the vault.

He looked at the white sheets hanging from the poles.

He did not speak. He walked to the nearest line, reached up with his gloved hand, and took one of the leaves between two fingers. He brought it close to his eyes, his pale, watery gaze traveling across the twenty lines of the title-page:

*A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

He turned the leaf. He examined the register, holding the paper up toward the faint light from the pavement-grating to see if the lines on the front of the sheet aligned with the lines on the back.

They were true to a hair’s breadth. Pieter’s brass pins had not slipped.

A faint, curious sound came from the man’s throat—a dry, clicking breath that might have been a sigh or the beginning of a laugh that had died before it reached his lips.

"The work is good," he said.

"The work is Pieter’s," she said from the shadow of the press. "The letters are Granjon’s. The paper is from Troyes. The ink was made with Champagne vines. There is no better work coming out of the Golden Compass this winter."

Van Cloet folded the sheet once, then twice, with the deft, practiced fold of a notary, and slipped it into his breast-pocket.

"Where are the rest?" he asked.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin toward the drying-lines. "They are dry enough to pack. If you keep them in sheets, they will lie flat in two sacks of malt. If you want them gathered and stitched into pamphlets, it will take another day, and I must have twelve pence for the thread."

"No," he said. "They must not be stitched. A stitched book is an executioner’s exhibit. A loose sheet can be passed from hand to hand in the market, under a cabbage-leaf or folded inside a pair of stockings. Are the names clear?"

"The names are as you wrote them," she said. "Every letter. I did not leave out an alderman. I did not leave out your friend Master Joost van Valckenisse."

Van Cloet’s eyes flicked to her face.

For the first time since he had entered the cellar, something like an expression moved across his yellow skin—a tightening of the lips, a small twitch at the corner of his left eye that made the scar beneath his cheekbone stand out white against the stubble.

"He is not my friend," Van Cloet said.

"He was your friend when Pieter was taken," she said.

Van Cloet did not look away. He stood before her with his hands behind his back, his cloak drawn back to reveal the black hilt of a short hunting-dagger at his hip.

"Pieter was an obstruction," he said, his voice flat as ditch-water. "He printed for the consistory of the Dutch church. He printed for the merchant factors who were sending money to the Prince in Delft. If we had not taken his press in the spring of eighty-six, the Duke’s provosts would have burned this entire street to the cellars. We took four men to save forty."

"You took four men to save your house on the Meir," Katharina said.

"My house on the Meir is sealed with the King’s wax," he said. "The furniture was sold at the Town Hall yesterday morning. My library—three hundred volumes of the civil law, bound in calf by the masters of Louvain—was burned in the courtyard to heat the bailiff’s soup. Do not speak to me of houses, widow. In three days, Joost van Valckenisse will have my head on an iron spike over the Red Gate, or I will have his in the Steen."

He took from his pocket a second bag of linen.

It was larger than the first, tied with a red silk ribbon that had come from some legal process in the chancellery. He laid it upon the stone bed beside the empty composing stick.

"There are fifty daalders in that purse," he said. "The balance of the contract, and twenty-five more for the paper."

Katharina did not look at the silver.

"What will you do with the sheets?" she asked.

"Tonight is the eve of Saint Martin," he said. He spoke looking at the drying-lines, where the three hundred copies were rustling faintly in the draught from the hatch. "The Spanish garrison has four hogsheads of wine from the Bailiff to keep them warm on the walls. At midnight, there are forty boys from the Latin school who will drop these sheets through the grates of every magistrate’s house between the Kipdorp and the Scheldt. Five hundred copies will be nailed to the doors of the four parish churches before prime. By ten in the morning, the Lord of Champagney will have read the names of the five commissioners who took the King’s money and sold the town’s bread to the Dutch."

"And you?"

Van Cloet turned toward the ladder.

"I have a boat at the Blue Tower," he said. "A skiff with four oars and a muffled rowlock. If the wind stays in the south, I shall be at Lillo before the morning drum. If it turns north, the provost’s horsemen will have me at the bend of the river."

He put his foot on the bottom rung of the ladder.

Then he stopped.

He reached into his pocket once more, his fingers hesitating for a fraction of a second before they drew out a small object. He turned and laid it upon the wood of the elm press, right upon the center of the platen where the iron thread entered the nut.

It was an iron signet ring, old, the face carved with the arms of the town—the two severed hands and the castle with three towers.

Beside it lay an ivory-handled scraper, such as notaries used to erase false words from sheepskin.

"What is that?" she asked.

"It is the seal that was on Pieter’s warrant," he said. He did not look at her; he looked at his own boots on the ladder. "It was the only one. The commission had no second seal. If anyone asks where the paper was printed, Katharina, tell them you found the seal in the gutter by the Town Hall."

He did not wait for her answer.

He climbed the ladder, his shoes clicking on the elm rungs, and pushed open the hatch.

The gray daylight flooded the cellar for a single heartbeat—cold, brilliant, showing every speck of ink on Katharina’s hands, every streak of soot on Lucas’s sleeping face, every white page of the three hundred sheets hanging from the roof.

Then the wood slammed shut.

The bolt fell.

The cellar was dark again, save for the single tallow dip that burned beside the press, its small flame casting the shadow of the great iron screw across the brick ceiling like an enormous, outstretched hand.

### Chapter 8

The storm came from the west, off the North Sea, driving the salt mist of the estuary up the broad channel of the Scheldt until the river smelled of rotting bladder-wrack and dead cod.

By ten that night, the rain had turned to sleet—a thick, gray mush that coated the cobbles of the Kammenstraat with grease, putting out the torches of the link-boys and driving the Spanish sentries into the shelter of the tavern doors.

Katharina worked in the dark.

She had taken the sheets down from the poles two hours ago. They were dry now, stiff and flat, packed into two large sacks of coarse hemp that Master Claes had used for carrying split willow-bands. Over the paper she had laid four inches of dry shavings from the cooper’s draw-knives, smelling of clean green oak, so that if a hand were thrust into the mouth of the sack, it would find nothing except the curly, white ribbons of the cooper’s craft.

The boy who came for the sacks was not from the Latin school.

He was a journeyman cooper named Frans, nineteen years old, with a broken nose and a great blue scar across his neck where a Spanish pikeman had grazed him during the sack of the town. He did not speak. He came down the ladder with his sleeves rolled up, took the first sack onto his shoulder with an easy, rolling heave of his back, and vanished up the hatch.

Five minutes later, he came back for the second.

"Is the lane clear?" Katharina whispered, her hand on the boy’s sleeve.

Frans looked at her with eyes that were red from beer and cold.

"The lane is full of horses," he muttered in his broad Antwerp street-speech. "The Amman’s men are riding toward the Meir. They say someone has thrown a dead dog into the well of the Alderman van Valckenisse, with a paper tied to its tail."

He grinned—a sudden, white flash of teeth in the gloom—and hauled the second sack up into the rain.

The hatch closed for the last time.

The work was done.

Katharina stood in the center of the vault.

The cellar was completely empty now. The drying-lines hung slack from their iron hooks, swaying slightly in the cold draught that came from the loose stones by the sewer. The press stood on its elm baulks, silent, its bed clean, its frisket open like a pair of black wings.

On the pine table lay the seventy-five silver daalders.

They were divided into three heaps: twenty-five for the paper and the ink, twenty-five for the winter’s meal and the peat, and twenty-five that she would bury beneath the hearthstone against the time when Clara would need shoes or Lucas would be old enough to buy his freedom in the guild.

Beside the silver lay the iron signet ring and the notary’s scraper.

She picked up the ring.

It was heavy, made for a man’s thick thumb, the iron dark and pitted with small spots of rust around the crest. The two hands of Antwerp—the hands that the giant Antigonus had cut from the boatmen who refused his tribute, according to the old story the nurses told by the peat-fires—stood out from the iron in low relief, their fingers stiff and straight above the battlements of the castle.

*The town that cuts off hands.*

She walked to the lye-tub.

The ice had formed again over the surface, thin and gray as horn. She broke it with the handle of the wooden brush, took the iron ring between her fingers, and dropped it into the water.

It fell through the green depths with a small, hollow *plop*, striking the lead types that lay in the bottom of the tub with a faint clink, and was still.

Then she took the scraper.

She sat at the composing stone, pulled the single tallow dip close to the edge of the wood, and took from the crevice behind the stairs the tin canister that held Pieter’s warrant.

She unfolded the fine Genoa paper.

The six lines of Latin looked back at her:

*Iudicatum per D. Balthasarem van Cloet, Notarium et Commissarium...*

She set the flat, sharp edge of the ivory scraper against the name.

She did not scrape it away.

She did not rub the iron-gall ink from the fibers of the paper until the surface was white and blank, as a notary would do to mend an error in a lease.

She took the small steel bodkin that Pieter had used to lift letters from the chase—a three-inch point of hardened iron, set into a handle of boxwood—and she dipped the point into the black ink that still lay in the corner of the stone.

Beneath the signature of Balthasar van Cloet, in the narrow white space between the red wax seal and the edge of the sheet, she wrote with the iron point.

Her hand was small, but it was steady. She did not write in the cursive hand of the chancellery, nor in the loose script of the merchants.

She wrote in the formal, square capital letters of the roman lower case, exactly as Pieter had taught her when she had first learned to hold the composing stick twelve years ago:

*PRINTED IN THE CAMMERPOORT BY KATHARINA HUYSMAN, WIDOW, IN THE MONTH OF NOVEMBER, IN THE YEAR OF OUR LORD 1587.*

*THREE HUNDRED COPIES.*

*THE PRESS STANDS.*

She waited three minutes for the vine-black to dry into the paper.

Then she folded the sheet twice, pressed the creases flat with the bone folder, and placed it back into the tin canister.

She walked to the foot of the bed.

Lucas was still sleeping, his forehead pressed against his sister’s hair. The child’s hand had reached out from under the quilt, her small, round fingers curled around a wooden spoon that she had played with before the fever took her.

Katharina took off her shoes.

She blew out the candle, pulled the heavy frieze cloak over her shoulders, and lay down beside her children on the straw.

Above them, through the two feet of brick and the heavy oak timbers of the cooper’s shed, the Cathedral bell began to strike the four hours of the morning watch.

One. Two. Three. Four.

The bronze notes fell through the dark, cold and heavy as iron shot, rolling out over the frozen roofs of the town, over the silent alleys of the Cammerpoort, over the black, rushing waters of the Scheldt, toward the sea.

Katharina closed her eyes.

Her hand reached out beneath the wool quilts, found Lucas’s cold fingers, and held them tight.

In the dark of the cellar, the elm press stood in its corner, its iron screw greased, its bed clean, waiting for the light.

### Chapter 9

The morning brought no sun, but the rain had stopped, leaving the city locked in a rime of gray, greasy frost.

The first sound that woke Katharina was not the Cathedral bell, nor the drum from the Citadel; it was the shouting from the Kammenstraat.

It was not the panic of a riot—there were no cobblestones flying against shutters, no shrieking of women dragging bedding into the lanes—but the low, continuous roar of three hundred men gathered in a narrow place, their voices bouncing between the high brick facades like the hum of a swarm of wasps trapped in an empty cask.

Katharina sat up on the straw.

Her limbs were stiff, her right shoulder aching as if a heavy dray had rolled across the joint, but her head was clear. The fever-smell that had hung over Clara’s pallet for five days was gone, replaced by the sharp, clean cold of the frost and the lingering odor of dried linseed oil.

Lucas was already awake. He was sitting on the three-legged stool by the lye-tub, his feet off the ground, looking up at the pavement-grating.

Through the iron bars, pieces of white paper were drifting down from the alley like snow.

"Mother," he whispered without turning his head. "Look."

Katharina walked to the grating.

She reached her hand through the bars, her fingers stretching into the slush of the gutter. Her nails caught the corner of a wet sheet that had been trampled into the mud by an iron-shod boot.

She pulled it through.

It was page four of the *Declaration*.

The ink was black and unyielding, untouched by the melting frost. Across the top of the leaf, the names of the aldermen stood out from the dirty rag like iron spikes:

*...Joost van Valckenisse... Hendrik van den Berghe... Gillis de Schot...*

Across the printed words, someone had drawn a heavy line with a piece of red chalk, and beneath it, in a rude, sprawling hand that had been scratched with a rusty nail, were three words in the broad dialect of the quays:

*HANG THE DOGS.*

From the street above, a voice rose above the general clamor—the voice of a carter or a brewer’s drayman, loud, harsh, carrying the sharp twang of the northern suburbs:

*"Read it again, Gielis! Read the part about the corn-ships at Rupelmonde!"*

*"He cannot read!"* another voice roared, followed by a chorus of rough, barking laughter that had an edge of iron beneath it. *"Let the schoolmaster read it! Up on the mounting-block, master! Up with you!"*

Katharina dropped the wet paper into the lye-tub.

It sank into the vinegar water, drifting down past the submerged type-cases to rest upon the iron signet ring in the sludge at the bottom.

"Lucas," she said softly.

"Yes, Mother?"

"Put on your shoes. Take the basket. Go to Master Claes’s kitchen and ask his woman for three jugs of small beer and two loaves of the brown bread from yesterday’s baking. If there are soldiers in the court, do not look at them. If anyone asks you whose boy you are, tell them your father was a lighterman who was drowned at the breach of the Kowenstyn."

"I know," the boy said. He reached for his ragged wool cap, pulled it down over his ears until only his dark eyes showed beneath the brim, and took the wicker basket from the corner.

He climbed the ladder, his small feet sure on the elm rungs.

Katharina waited by the foot of the stairs until she heard the cooper’s wicket open and click shut.

Then she walked to the press.

She did not look at the bed. She took the iron bodkin, went to the three cases of type that sat upon the trestles, and began to distribute the letter.

It was the slowest work of the craft, the work that apprentices hated most, but she did it with a steady, mechanical rhythm that steadied her racing blood.

*Click. Click. Click.*

She lifted a line of lead from the galley with her composing rule: forty letters, five words. Her right hand moved across the case like a bird hopping among branches: the *e* into its great central box, the *t* into its narrow slot, the *a*, the *o*, the capitals into the upper case behind the frame.

Each letter had to go to its own house. If an *n* fell into the *u*-box, the next book would be fouled with blind errors; if a space were dropped among the hyphens, the line would jam in the stick.

She distributed the first page.

*...A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

The capital *A* fell into its box with a dull, leaden tap. The *D*, the *E*, the *C*.

The title was gone. It was no longer a treasonable broadsheet that could hang three people from the beam of the Town Hall; it was thirty pounds of gray metal, innocent and dumb, waiting for the Latin grammar or the factor’s receipts.

By the time she had reached the fifth line of the second page, the cooper’s hatch opened again.

It was not Lucas.

The boots that came down the ladder were heavy, clumsy, with the dragging, irregular tread of an old man whose knees were swollen with the dropsy.

Master Claes.

The cooper stood on the third step, his round, red face peering into the dimness of the vault. He wore his leather work-apron over his wool doublet, his sleeves rolled up to the shoulders, his hairy forearms gray with the dust of split oak. In his right hand he carried an adze, its curved steel blade shining where he had honed it on the oilstone that morning.

He looked at Katharina. He looked at the press. He looked at the empty drying-poles where the sheets had hung.

"Mistress," he said.

His voice was hoarse, smelling of stale gin and salt bacon.

"Master Claes," she replied, her fingers remaining poised over the nonpareil case.

The old man came down two more steps, until his head was level with the joists. He did not look at the type; he kept his eyes on the floor between his shoes, his thumb rubbing the greasy hickory handle of the adze.

"There are twelve horsemen from the Citadel at the Red Gate," he said.

Katharina did not move. "The horsemen are always at the Red Gate."

"These are not Walloons," he said. "They are the Provost’s own archers, with the black feathers in their hats and the iron collars. They have two carts with them. One has a forge and an anvil; the other has four smiths with twelve-pound sledges."

Katharina felt the cold water from the floor soak through the soles of her slippers.

"Who are they seeking?" she asked.

"Everyone," Claes said. He spat into the shavings behind the ladder. "They have seized the house of Master Willem, the bookseller by the Cathedral. They threw his stock into the gutter and set it afire with a pitch-barrel. They are searching every cellar between the Hopland and the river."

He looked up then. His eyes were small, watery, surrounded by red rings like an owl’s, but they were not unkind.

"Your rent is paid until Saint Catherine’s," he said.

"Yes."

"The four daalders were good silver," he said. "The smith at the bridge weighed them for me. They had the mark of the old mint."

He paused, his adze swinging an inch above the dirt floor.

"If the archers come into my yard, Katharina, I cannot stop them. A cooper does not fight twelve men with carbines. If they find the elm timber under my floorboards, they will burn my stave-sheds, and my boys will be on the road to Ghent with their packs on their backs."

"I know," she said.

The old man looked at the press.

"Pieter was a good lad," he muttered. "He never brought bad women into the yard. He paid his wood-money on the day. But Pieter is dead, mistress. The dead do not buy rye-meal."

"The press will not be here when they come," she said.

Claes stared at her. "It is five hundredweight of seasoned elm. You cannot carry it down the lane on your hip like a basket of sprats."

"I have the wedges," she said. "I have the mallet. If you give me two hours, Master Claes, there will be nothing in this vault except six bundles of split willow and two tubs of old lye."

The old man stood motionless on the ladder for the space of ten breaths.

Then he reached into his leather apron and drew out a heavy wooden beetle—a two-handed mallet of ironwood, bound around the heads with thick hoops of forged brass. He laid it upon the second step of the ladder.

"Take the beetle," he said. "The cheeks are pinned with oak trunnels. If you strike them from the north side, the pins will shear cleanly. Do not burn the chips in the cellar. The smoke has a smell like no other wood, and the archers have noses like hounds."

He turned and climbed the ladder.

At the top, he paused, his gray head outlined against the daylight.

"Lucas is in my kitchen," he said without looking down. "My woman gave him a bowl of porridge with three spoons of honey. Let him stay there until the bells ring for none."

The hatch closed.

Katharina stood alone in the silence.

She did not wait. She did not look at the cases of type, nor at the tin canister behind the chimney.

She walked to the press.

She picked up the heavy ironwood beetle that the cooper had left on the ladder. It was heavy, nearly twelve pounds of dense, dark wood, its handle slick with the sweat of three generations of coopers.

She walked to the side of the elm frame.

There, where the great cross-beam—the winter-beam that carried the whole pressure of the iron screw—was mortised into the upright cheek, the two oak trunnels stood out from the grain like two gray warts.

Pieter had driven those pins.

She remembered the sound of his hammer on the autumn evening two years ago: *thump, thump, thump*, regular and proud, while she had held the tallow candle and Lucas had sat in the shavings with his first wooden hornbook.

Katharina swung the beetle over her shoulder.

She did not close her eyes. She did not weep.

She brought the ironwood down with the full weight of her body, her shoulders, and her thirty years of life behind the blow.

*CRACK.*

The oak pin sheared with a sound like a pistol-shot.

The great elm cheek groaned, shivered through all its fibers, and parted from the cross-beam by the width of a knife-blade.

She swung the hammer again.

*CRACK.*

The timber fell.

The iron screw slipped from its brass nut, crashing down upon the pine bed with a dull, monstrous roar that shook the cellar walls, sending a cloud of ancient, ink-black dust rising into the cold air like smoke from an altar.

Katharina dropped the beetle into the shavings.

She stood in the rising dust, her chest heaving, her hands bleeding where the rough wood of the handle had torn the skin from her palms.

The press was broken.

The lead was safe. The children were fed. The names were in the street.

She reached down, took a handful of the dirty sawdust from the floor, and pressed it into her bleeding hands until the red stopped.

Then she knelt in the dirt, took her iron bodkin from her apron, and began to gather the lead types into the willow basket, one letter at a time.
### Chapter 10

The lead was heavy, but it was not the dead, clumsy weight of river-gravel or pig-iron. It had the dense, oily balance of metal that had been handled ten thousand times by human thumbs, smoothed by the lye and rounded at the corners where the type-casters’ molds had chilled.

Katharina did not dump the letters into the willow basket in blind shovelfuls.

If a case of pica was tossed into a heap without order, the faces of the delicate letters—the kerned *f*s, the tall ascenders of the *b*s and *d*s, the tiny serifs that Granjon had cut with his diamond-pointed gravers—would strike each other like flint against steel, chipping the metal until the letters printed with blind cheeks and broken shoulders. A chipped letter was worse than a lost letter; a lost letter could be replaced by a wooden quadrat, but a blind letter deceived the eye and fouled the sense of every sentence it entered.

She laid the type into the bottom of the basket in flat, tightly wedged rows, separated by strips of pasteboard cut from an old ledger cover.

*Lucas’s pica.*

*Pieter’s italic.*

*The great double-canon capitals that had come from the shop of Ameet Tavernier twenty years ago, each letter as big as a walnut, its face shining like dull pewter in the tallow-light.*

When the third layer of lead was laid, the wicker groaned under the burden. It was nearly eighty pounds of metal, concentrated in a space no larger than a bread-box. She could not lift it by the hemp handles alone; she had to slide her forearms beneath the wicker bottom, bracing her knees against the dirt floor, and heave the basket toward the low brick archway that led to the sewer-vault.

In the dark recess beneath the foundation wall, where the seepage from the town ditch gathered in a shallow pool of black grease, sat the four salt-herring casks that Master Claes had abandoned after the great frost of eighty-five.

The wood of the casks was black, saturated with old brine and fish-oil that had soaked into the oak staves until no worm would touch them. The smell was ancient and dreadful—a mixture of rotten salt-pork, stagnant ditch-water, and the sulfurous slime that grew upon stone that had never seen the sky.

Katharina took the iron draw-knife that lay behind the casks.

She pried the top hoop from the nearest cask. The rusted iron band creaked, shedding a shower of red scales, but the oak staves held together, bound by the dried salt crust in the joints. She tilted the cask forward.

Inside, half-covered by a layer of gray, dried salt-crystals that crackled like gravel under her knuckles, lay the empty hollow.

She did not put the lead directly into the salt. The brine would pit the antimony and eat the fine edges of the type within three weeks. She took the three oiled pigs’ bladders that Pieter had prepared—supple, yellow, smelling of lard—slipped the packages of type inside, and tied the necks with three turns of tarred sail-twine.

She lowered the bladders into the belly of the cask.

Then she shovelled the gray salt back over them with the wooden lye-paddle until the metal was buried twelve inches deep under the bitter crust. Over the salt she laid three dried cod-tails, yellow and shriveled like old leather, and a layer of wet straw that she had taken from the bedding under Clara’s pallet.

If an archer from the Citadel thrust his halberd into that cask, the iron point would strike the salted straw, grate against the cod-skins, and come out smelling of rotten fish. No soldier would plunge his bare arm into eight inches of ancient herring-brine to see what lay at the bottom of the oak.

"Mother."

It was Lucas.

The boy was standing at the bottom of the ladder, his face white, the basket of small beer held against his ribs with both arms. His wool cap was missing, his dark hair plastered to his forehead with melting sleet.

"The cooper says they are at the well," he whispered.

Katharina dropped the lye-paddle into the sawdust. She did not wipe her hands; she wiped her sleeves across her apron, smoothing down the rough gray hemp over the seventy-five silver daalders that were pinned into the lining of her skirt.

"How many?" she asked.

"Six," the boy said. His chest was heaving, his words coming between short, dry gasps that rattled in his throat. "Two horsemen with carbines at the mouth of the alley, and four archers on foot with the Provost’s smith. They have Master Willem from the bookseller’s shop. He was walking between the horses with an iron chain around his neck. His hands were tied behind his back, and he had no shoes on his feet."

"Did they see you?"

"No," Lucas said. "Master Claes pushed me through the wicket into the stave-shed. He threw three bundles of willow-hoops over me and told me to stay like a dead dog until the horses passed."

Katharina walked to the press-bed.

The great oak winter-beam lay across the dirt floor where it had fallen, its sheared pins sticking up from the grain like broken bones. The iron screw lay beside it, its coarse thread shining with black lard.

It looked like timber.

To an alderman who knew the geometry of typography, it was the skeleton of a printing machine; to an archer who had spent three years drinking beer in the camps of the Brabant plain, it was two baulks of old elm and a rusty iron jack from a dray-wagon.

She picked up the two sheepskin inking balls.

The pelt was still wet with the fresh vine-black, sticky and smelling of varnish. She could not hide them; if the Provost’s men found two balls of uncarded sheep’s wool soaked in printing-ink, they would need no press to prove their business.

She carried them to the brazier.

The coals were nearly dead, but a core of dull red heat still pulsed in the center of the iron pan. She took the iron bodkin, punctured the sheepskin in twelve places to let the wool out, and laid the balls upon the fire.

*Sssss-h.*

The grease caught with a sudden, greasy hiss.

A thick, yellowish-white smoke puffed from the wool, smelling foully of burning horn and scorched fat. She took a handful of dry birch-shavings from the cooper’s floor and threw them over the flame. The shavings flared up in a bright, yellow crackle, drowning the smell of the ink in the clean, sweet reek of burning birch-bark.

"Up," she said to the boy.

"The cellar?"

"We do not stay in the cellar," she said. Her voice was low and hard. "If they find us in the cellar with the timber, they will question the floorboards. We sit in the kitchen with Master Claes’s woman. We are sewing shirts for the cooper’s boys. Move, Lucas."

She caught Clara from the straw.

The child was limp, heavy with sleep, her head dropping against Katharina’s shoulder with a soft, moist sigh. Katharina wrapped the heavy frieze shawl around them both, pinning the edges with an old brass bodkin so that only the child’s gray wool cap showed above the cloth.

She did not take the tin canister from the crevice behind the stairs.

To carry the warrant on her body was death; to leave it in the wall was a wager against the stupidity of six men who were cold, wet, and looking for silver rather than old paper.

She climbed the ladder.

Lucas followed her, his small fingers gripping her skirts as they rose through the hatch into the cooper’s drying-shed.

The shed was vast, drafty, smelling of split green timber, sawdust, and the sharp, sour breath of seasoned oak. Master Claes was standing by the great wooden sawing-horse, an adze in his hand, his apprentice boy—a lad of sixteen with a great goiter on his throat—pulling the long frame-saw through a six-inch balk of ash.

*Zzz-sh, zzz-sh, zzz-sh.*

The sound of the saw was loud, regular, filling the shed with a spray of yellow dust that danced in the gray light from the open doorway.

Claes did not stop his work. He looked at Katharina over the top of his iron spectacles, his eyebrows bristling with white sawdust.

"The kitchen," he jerked his chin toward the low brick passage that connected the shed to his dwelling-house. "My woman has the cabbage in the kettle. Sit by the hearth. If they ask who the child belongs to, she is my sister’s girl from Herentals whose mother died of the sweat in the harvest."

Katharina did not thank him. Words were a hazard that neither of them could afford to spend.

She slipped through the low door into the kitchen.

It was a small, square room with a floor of red quarry-tiles, scrubbed until they shone like glazed earthenware, and a great fireplace of Dutch brick where a black iron pot was bubbling over a peat-fire. On the high mantelpiece stood two pewter plates, polished bright as mirrors, and a wooden figure of Saint Joseph with a lily in his hand, carved by some craftsman from Mechelen before the troubles.

By the hearth sat Mother Betje, the cooper’s wife.

She was seventy years old, round and heavy as a beer-barrel, with three chins that rested upon the stiff linen of her neck-ruff like bags of white lard. Her eyes were small, watery, almost lost among the wrinkles of her fat cheeks, but her fingers were nimble, flying over the bone bobbins of a lace-pillow that rested upon her knees.

*Clack-clack, clack-clack-clack.*

The bobbins made a dry, cheerful rattle that sounded like summer crickets in a dry meadow.

She did not look up when Katharina entered.

"Sit," the old woman said in a voice like a heavy drawer being pushed into a counter. "Take the hemp from the basket. The three shirts for Jan need the collars felled. Use the coarse thread. My eyes are too thick for the fine linen today."

Katharina sat on the low rush-bottomed bench by the chimney-corner.

She laid Clara upon the sheepskin rug that lay before the hearth. The child did not wake; the warmth of the peat and the steady, rhythmic *clack-clack* of the bobbins seemed to hold her in a deep, painless stupor.

Lucas sat at her feet, his fingers already busy with the ball of hemp twine, untangling the knots with the swift, nervous dexterity he had learned at the type-case.

Outside in the yard, the sawing stopped.

A horse sneezed—a loud, wet snort that sounded right outside the leaded window-panes of the kitchen.

Then came the sound of an iron scabbard striking the oak door-post of the stave-shed, and a voice that was neither Flemish nor French, but the flat, haughty Castilian of the officers from the Citadel:

*"¿Dónde está el dueño de este corral?"*

Katharina’s needle went into the gray hemp.

*Push. Pull.*

She did not look at the window. She did not look at the door. Her hands moved with the slow, mindless regularity of a woman who had felled five hundred collars for five hundred coopers’ boys, her stitches small, straight, and even as the teeth of a brass comb.

### Chapter 11

The man who entered the kitchen five minutes later was not the Spanish officer.

It was the Provost’s smith.

He was an enormous, round-shouldered Fleming named Roeland, who had a shop near the Crane on the quays where he repaired the ironwork for the municipal dredgers and forged the heavy chains for the prisoners in the Steen. He was dressed in a sleeveless jerkin of ox-hide, black with the grease of the anvil, with an apron of stiff horse-leather tucked up into his belt. In his right hand he carried an iron pry-bar with a curved claw, four feet long and as thick as a man’s wrist.

Behind him came two archers of the Amman, their iron morions wet with sleet, their matchlocks held across their chests with the smoldering cords tucked into their belts to keep the powder dry.

Roeland stopped in the doorway.

The kitchen was so small that his great bulk seemed to fill it from wall to wall, his head almost touching the dried hams that hung from the oak ceiling-beams. He smelled of sea-coal, sulfur, and the sour, sharp reek of cheap gin.

His small, piggish eyes traveled slowly around the room: from the pewter plates on the shelf to the wooden Saint Joseph, to Mother Betje with her lace-bobbins, and finally to Katharina sitting by the hearth with the gray shirt across her knees.

"Old woman," the smith said, his voice coming from deep within his belly, like the rumble of an empty cask on a wharf. "Where is the cooper?"

Mother Betje did not stop her bobbins.

*Clack-clack, clack-clack.*

"Where would he be, Roeland?" she said, not even turning her head. "He is in the shed with the timber. He is making thirty hogsheads for the wine-house at the Red Lion. If the wine-house does not get its casks before the frost locks the canal, Master Claes will lose his contract, and you will not get your four florins for the iron hoops you made for him at Michaelmas."

The smith grunted. He leaned his pry-bar against the door-jamb, reached into his pouch, and drew out a crumpled piece of white paper.

Katharina’s needle did not pause.

*In. Out. Pull.*

It was the *Declaration*.

The page was smeared with black grease from the smith’s thumb, torn across the corner where it had been ripped from some parish door, but the large black-letter capitals were unmistakable even from across the room:

*A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

"You know this craft, mistress?" Roeland said, looking straight at Katharina.

Katharina looked up.

Her face was as smooth and empty as a bowl of cold milk. She had practiced this stillness before the broken mirror in the Cammerpoort while Pieter was dying; she had learned that the human face was only a piece of leather that could be pulled tight over the skull until no thought could show through the grain.

"I know how to sew hemp, master," she said.

"This is not hemp," the smith said, thrusting the paper toward her with his thick, scarred fingers. "This is lead-work. This was printed in the Cammerpoort last night. Three hundred sheets of it were found scattered through the Grote Markt like peas thrown to pigeons. One was nailed to the door of Master Joost van Valckenisse with three copper tacks, right through the eyes of the King’s arms."

"Master Joost is a great lord," Mother Betje said from her pillow. "The great lords have many enemies. When the grain-ships were stopped at Rupelmonde, my cousin had to pay six groats for a loaf of rye that was half bean-meal and dock-weed. If a man sells the town’s bread to the Dutch, he must not wonder if someone writes his name on a wall."

"Hold your tongue, old fool," one of the archers snapped from the passage. He was a thin, nervous man with a patchy beard and a squint that made his left eye wander toward the rafters. "The Provost has ordered twelve men to the gallows for this paper before nightfall. The Lord of Champagney is in the Town Hall with the Spanish colonels. They are mad as wasps in a jug. They say the man who wrote this paper had the accounts from the secret chest in the Council Chamber."

"Then go to the Council Chamber and find him," Mother Betje muttered. "There are no councilors in my kitchen. Only two women, a sick child, and three shirts that must be finished before vespers."

The smith did not look at Mother Betje.

He took three slow, heavy steps across the red tiles and stopped before Katharina.

He smelled of the forge—a hot, dry smell that seemed to suck the moisture from the air. He reached down with his left hand, caught Katharina’s right wrist, and jerked her arm upward into the light of the window.

Lucas moved on the floor. His hand went into his pocket, his small shoulder tensing like a cat’s before a spring.

Katharina did not look at the boy, but she turned her foot slightly, pressing the toe of her wooden shoe down upon his ankle with all the weight she could muster.

*Stay.*

The smith turned her hand over.

His thumb—broad as an ox-tongue, the nail split and black with coal-dust—rubbed the inside of her forefinger.

The gray, calloused notch of the composing stick was there.

It was distinct, unmistakable to any man who had ever worked among printers. The lead and the brass left a mark that stayed in the flesh for twenty years—a small, triangular dent where the corner of the rule pressed against the skin, bordered by a ridge of yellow, thickened horn that was as hard as shoe-leather.

The smith looked at the notch.

He looked at her fingers, at the fine black line beneath the nails that no soap had ever cleaned.

The kitchen was so silent that the bubbling of the cabbage in the iron pot sounded like a horse drinking from a bucket.

Outside, the sleet beat against the leaded glass: *tap, tap, tap, tap.*

"Your man was Pieter Huysman," the smith said.

It was not a question. It was a statement of fact, spoken with the dull, mechanical certainty of an appraiser valuing an old iron kettle.

"Pieter is in the lime-pit," Katharina said. She did not try to pull her hand away. She let her wrist lie limp in his great, greasy grip, her pulse beating against his calloused skin with a slow, even thud. "He has been dead since Lent. You forged the iron collar that was around his neck in the Steen, Roeland. You know he is dead."

The smith’s thumb did not leave the notch.

"Pieter had a half-press," he said. "He had two baulks of elm that he bought from the ship-breaker. I made the iron spindle for him. I turned the thread on my own lathe. It had a three-inch pitch."

Katharina looked him in the eyes.

Her pale gray eyes met his small, dark ones. There was no fear in her gaze; there was only the cold, unyielding exhaustion of a woman who had already seen the bottom of the well and knew that the mud there was no deeper than her knees.

"The elm is in the cellar," she said.

The archer with the squint stepped into the room, his hand on the hilt of his short-sword. "What is in the cellar?"

The smith did not answer him immediately.

He looked down at Clara, who had turned her face toward the light, her small, white forehead exposed beneath the frieze shawl, her thin mouth open as she breathed the warm cabbage-air. He looked at Lucas, whose dark eyes were fixed on the iron pry-bar by the door with a desperate, feral hatred that was older than the war itself.

Then he looked at Katharina’s apron.

Beneath the rough gray hemp, the outline of the seventy-five silver daalders was invisible, but the weight of the metal made the skirt hang stiff, dragging the wool down on the left side until the hem touched the tiles.

"The elm is broken," the smith said.

He released her wrist.

His great hand dropped to his side, his fingers curling into a fist that smelled of grease.

"What?" the archer said, peering past his shoulder.

"The press is broken," Roeland said, turning his back on Katharina and walking toward the passage. "I saw the timber when I came through the stave-shed. Old Claes split the cheeks with his beetle this morning. The spindle is sheared and the bed is in three pieces. A man cannot print treason on an armful of firewood."

"We ought to search the vault," the archer insisted, his voice rising with the querulous suspicion of a small official whose authority was being bypassed. "The sergeant said every hole—"

"The vault is full of split staves and rotten herring-salt," the smith roared, turning on the soldier with such sudden, monstrous ferocity that the man backed into the passage until his scabbard struck the wall. "Do you want to carry four hundredweight of wet elm up that ladder in the sleet, you half-starved cabbage-eater? The press that printed this paper was a double-press with a six-foot coffin. It came from some shop with twelve men and a drying-loft, not from a cooper’s pit under three tubs of lye. Get out into the lane. The horses are freezing, and the corporal will be at the Red Lion before we have seen our beer."

He caught his iron pry-bar from the wall.

He did not look back at Katharina. He did not look at Mother Betje.

He strode down the brick passage, his heavy boots shaking the floor-tiles with every step, the two archers trailing behind him like dogs behind a butcher’s dray.

The outer door of the stave-shed slammed shut.

Then came the clatter of the horses’ hooves in the alleyway—first two, then four, turning toward the High Street and the river.

Katharina sat motionless on the bench.

Her needle was still between her fingers, the coarse hemp thread trailing across her lap like a gray snake.

Mother Betje’s bobbins began to move again.

*Clack-clack, clack-clack-clack.*

"Roeland is an honest lad," the old woman said to her lace. "His mother was a laundress at the Béguinage. She always gave four groats to the poor-box at Saint Catherine’s, even when the flour was ten stuivers the peck."

Katharina leaned her head back against the whitewashed brick of the chimney.

A single tear—hot, salty, stinging like lye—slipped from the corner of her left eye, ran down the hollow of her cheek, and fell upon the collar of the cooper’s boy’s shirt.

She did not wipe it away.

She took the needle, pushed the point through the coarse cloth, and pulled the thread home.

### Chapter 12

By dusk, the wind had veered into the northwest, bringing the real frost down from the Zuyderzee.

The mist that had hung over the river for seven days was swept away by a dry, roaring gale that set the weathervanes on the Cathedral screaming like gulls and turned the mud of the Grote Markt into a pavement of black iron.

Katharina walked through the Fish Market.

She had left the children with Mother Betje by the kitchen fire. Clara was eating a bowl of boiled barley with lard, her small cheeks pink with the heat, while Lucas was helping the cooper’s apprentice plane the ash-staves for the wine-casks, his composing stick tucked away in the lining of his coat like an amulet.

In her market-basket, under a clean cloth, Katharina carried twenty of the silver daalders.

She did not go to the great shops on the Meir, where the merchants from Genoa and Augsburg sold their fine cloths and their spices under the eyes of the Spanish guards; she went to the narrow cellars of the Veemarkt, where the northern skippers sold their goods directly from the river-barges before the port-officers could set their seals upon the hatches.

The quays were deserted, save for the sentries at the water-gates who stood huddled over iron baskets of burning sea-coal, their pikes tucked under their arms, their cloaks wrapped twice around their faces to keep out the biting salt wind.

The Scheldt was running black and wild, flecked with white crests that smashed against the stone piers of the Crane with a dull, hollow boom. Out in the stream, three hundred yards from the bank, the black hulls of the Spanish guard-ketches lay at their anchors, their lanterns tossing against the dark sky like yellow fireflies.

Beyond them, four miles to the north, where the river curved toward the drowned lands of Saaftinge, a thin red flare rose into the night.

One. A pause. Then two more, close together.

*Lillo.*

The Dutch garrison at the forts was burning its signal-fires.

Katharina stopped in the lee of an old salt-warehouse, her shawl held tight beneath her chin.

She knew what those three red flares meant. Every lighterman on the quays knew them: a boat had come through the Spanish lines from the south. A skiff, or a small rowing-barge with a muffled rowlock, had slipped past the guard-ketches in the dark and reached the safety of the Prince’s guns.

*Balthasar van Cloet.*

He was at Lillo now.

He was sitting in the warm guard-room of the fort, his wet cloak drying before a fire of turf, drinking English beer with the Dutch captains while his three hundred printed sheets were turning the Town Hall of Antwerp into a slaughter-house of terrified aldermen.

He had won his wager.

He had lost his house on the Meir, his three hundred volumes of civil law, and his notary’s seat in the Council Chamber; but he had saved his neck, and he had left an iron spear planted between the ribs of the men who had sought to destroy him.

And she?

She had seventy-five silver daalders.

She had a broken press in a damp cellar, two children whose father was in the lime, and a pair of hands that would carry the mark of the type-case until the worms ate them.

She turned away from the river.

She walked down the narrow lane that led to the cellar of Master Hendrik, the peat-merchant from the Kempen.

The merchant was sitting in his low vault, surrounded by mountains of dark, dried turf that smelled of the bog and dead heather. He was an old Calvinist from Turnhout who wore an iron medal of the Prince of Orange hidden beneath his wool waistcoat, his fingers yellow from tobacco-leaf.

"Mistress Huysman," he said, squinting through the smoke of his pipe.

"Master Hendrik," she said. She did not waste words. She set five of the silver daalders upon the wooden scale-board where he weighed his turf.

The silver clicked against the brass.

The old man’s pipe stayed in his mouth, the smoke curling around his gray nose like a wreath of wool. He looked at the five coins, then at her face.

"Brabant silver," he murmured. "Old mint. Where did you find these, girl?"

"My sister’s man is a drayman at Mechelen," she said, using the lie that had become her skin. "I want three loads of the black Kempen turf. Not the brown moss that burns to white ash in an hour, but the heavy, greasy sods from the bottom of the bog that hold the heat until morning."

"The black turf is dear," he said. "The soldiers have burned the bogs at Herentals, and the carts must come by night through the woods."

"I have the silver," she said.

"And meal?" he asked. "You have no meal in that cellar. I saw Lucas at the baker’s yesterday, buying two pennyworth of bran that a dray-horse would turn his nose at."

"I want two sacks of sound rye," she said. "Sound grain, dry, free of the black-rot. And a tub of Zeeland butter."

The old man picked up one of the daalders. He weighed it in his palm, feeling the thick, unclipped rim.

"It will be at the cooper’s wicket before midnight," he said. "My boy will bring it on the hand-cart. If the patrol asks, it is stave-grease and willow-bark for Master Claes."

He looked at her through the smoke, his small, dark eyes suddenly sharp beneath his bushy brows.

"They are hanging men at the Grote Markt tomorrow," he said softly.

Katharina’s fingers remained on the rim of the basket. "Who?"

"Two clerks from the Chancellery," he said. "And the little bookseller from the Cathedral porch. They found forty copies of the *Declaration* in his hay-loft. He swore by all the saints he had never seen them before, that someone had dropped them through his roof-shutter in the night; but Master Joost van Valckenisse was on the bench, and Master Joost would hang his own mother if she had a piece of clean paper in her pocket."

Katharina felt the cold wind from the lane bite into the back of her neck.

"And Van Valckenisse?" she asked. "Is he still in his seat?"

The old man spat into the turf-dust.

"The Spanish Colonel gave him an escort of twenty horsemen to ride to Brussels at noon," he said. "They say the Duke of Parma wants to see his account-books. A man does not take twenty horsemen with him to Brussels unless the King is preparing a chamber for him in the Castle of Vilvoorde. The paper did its work, mistress. Whoever pressed those letters had an iron thumb."

Katharina turned toward the door.

"Midnight," she said. "Do not forget the butter."

She stepped out into the frozen dark.

### Chapter 13

The winter of 1587 was the longest that the old men of Brabant could remember.

By Christmas, the Scheldt was frozen from bank to bank, an expanse of gray, hummocked ice across which the wolves came down from the woods of Waas to scavenge the carcasses of horses that had died in the camps outside the walls.

In the cellar beneath the cooper’s shed, the frost stayed on the north wall until the middle of February, a thick, white crust that glittered like salt in the light of the peat-fire.

But the fire did not go out.

Day and night, the black Kempen turf glowed in the iron brazier, throwing a deep, steady red heat into the vault that dried the stone floor and kept the water from freezing in the wash-tubs.

In the corner behind the chimney, the two sacks of sound rye meal stood upon two blocks of oak to keep them from the damp, smelling of clean summer fields and dry flour. A side of salt bacon hung from the iron hook where Pieter had once dried his paper, and on the shelf above the bed sat a stone jar of Zeeland butter, yellow and sweet, with three loaves of white wheat-bread that Katharina had baked in Mother Betje’s oven on the eve of the Epiphany.

Clara’s cough had gone.

She was five years old now, her cheeks round and red as winter apples, her legs straight beneath a new petticoat of heavy red frieze that Katharina had sewn for her with wool bought from the English factors. She sat on the sheepskin rug before the brazier, holding a small wooden cat that the cooper’s apprentice had carved for her from an ash-shaving, singing a nonsense-song about a miller who had three gray geese.

At the composing frame, Lucas was working.

He did not have the elm press.

The broken baulks had been carried up the ladder two weeks after the raid and sawed into split staves for the wine-casks; the iron screw had been sold to a blacksmith in the Klapdorp for four groats and an old iron kettle.

To the world, to the Amman, to the Provost’s clerks who walked the Kammenstraat with their registers, the widow of Pieter Huysman was only a seamstress who felled collars for the cooper’s household and earned two stuivers a day by washing linen in the public troughs by the bridge.

But beneath the four herring-casks, the three cases of type remained.

Once every week, when the cooper’s yard was silent and the snow was falling thick upon the thatch, Katharina would lift the oiled pigs’ bladders from the salt.

She did not print pamphlets.

She had no press to pull an impression; but she had the composing stick, she had the brass rules, and she had the boxwood galleys that Pieter had planed with his own hands.

Every night, for two hours before the Cathedral bell struck nine, she taught the boy his letters.

Not the letters of the hornbook, which were blurred and rude like marks made with a charred stick; but the true letters of the art: the proportions of the roman capital, the balance of the italic lower-case, the reason why the cross-bar of the *e* must never be higher than one-third of the body, and how a line of Latin verse must be spaced so that the white paper between the words was like light falling through an open colonnade.

"Set the third line of the *Aeneid*," she would say to him, her fingers guiding his small wrist.

*Click. Click. Click.*

The boy’s hand would fly to the case.

*I - t - a - l - i - a - m*

*space.*

*f - a - t - o*

*space.*

*p - r - o - f - u - g - u - s*

He was quick. His eye was as true as his father’s had been; he could tell a turned letter by the touch of his thumb without looking at the face, and he never dropped a quadrat into the space-box.

When the line was set, she would not ink it.

She would take a scrap of damp paper, lay it upon the face of the lead, and press it down with the flat of her palm, rubbing the back of the rag with the smooth ivory handle of the notary’s scraper until the dry letters left an embossed, colorless impression upon the linen—a blind print, white upon white, that could be read only by holding the paper toward the red glow of the peat.

*Arma virumque cano...*

"Will we have a press again, Mother?" Lucas asked one night, looking up from the stone with his composing stick in his hand.

Katharina looked at the empty space where the elm timber had stood.

The floor there was swept clean, the red brick showing through the dirt, surrounded by three bundles of dry willow-hoops that Master Claes had given them for kindling.

"We will have a press," she said.

"Where?"

"In Leiden," she said.

The boy looked at her, his dark eyes widening in the firelight.

"Leiden is in Holland," he said. "The heretics are there. The soldiers say that in Leiden they eat dogs and worship an image of an ox made of brass."

"In Leiden," Katharina said, her voice dropping into the quiet, steady tone she used when she spoke of Pieter, "there are sixteen printing-houses that have their doors open to the street. Christopher Plantin’s son-in-law is there, with eight presses that work by daylight. There are no warrants signed by Balthasar van Cloet. There are no archers who break the iron screws with sledges."

She reached into her bodice.

She drew out the small leather purse that hung between her breasts by a silk cord.

Inside the purse were forty-two silver daalders.

The rest of the winter was paid for; the bread was in the chest; the children were warm. The forty-two coins were the boat-money.

In April, when the ice broke in the Scheldt and the green water began to run swift and deep toward the sea, the Dutch lighters from Zeeland would come up to the forts at Lillo under the cover of the spring fogs.

A woman with two children and three bags of type-metal could find a place beneath the ballast-stones of an onion-boat for twelve silver florins.

"When do we go?" Lucas whispered.

"When the storks come back to the roof of Saint Walburga," she said.

She stood up from the bench.

She walked to the crevice behind the stairs, reached in, and took down the tin canister for the last time.

She did not open it.

She carried it to the brazier, set it down upon the glowing coals of the black Kempen turf, and watched the red lacquer blister and peel from the tin.

Inside the box, the Genoa paper—the warrant with the six lines of Latin, the red wax seal with the broken lion’s paw, the name of Balthasar van Cloet, and the black roman letters she had written with the iron point—began to smoke.

A thin, blue curl of vapor rose from the seam of the lid, smelling of scorched linen and ancient ink.

She did not take it off the fire.

She stood with her hands folded in her apron, her face lit by the red glow of the peat, watching the tin turn black, then dull red, until the letters within were reduced to a handful of white ash that drifted through the cracks like snow.

The record was gone.

The man who had signed it was a fugitive in the marshes of Holland; the man who had ordered it was in an iron cell in the Castle of Vilvoorde; the man who had suffered it was in the lime-pit by the Kronenburg Gate.

Only the craft remained.

She turned back to the table.

"Lucas," she said, her voice clear and untroubled as the morning bell.

The boy lifted his head. "Yes, Mother?"

"Distribute the line," she said. "The case must be clean before we sleep. Tomorrow we begin the fourth book of the *Aeneid*."

The boy smiled—a quick, proud flash of white teeth in the firelight—and reached his fingers toward the lead.

*Click.*

The *A* fell into its box.

*Click.*

The *r*.

*Click.*

The *m*.

In the silent cellar beneath the frozen city, the metal found its home, waiting for the spring, for the river, and for the press that stood beyond the sea.

### Chapter 14

The thaw came in the first week of March, not with gentle rain, but with a sudden, violent wind from the south that smelled of the French meadows and drove the sea-ice back upon the Zeeland sandbanks in great, groaning ridges.

The Scheldt rose four cubits in two nights.

The muddy brown torrent came down from the hills of Hainaut, carrying with it the trunks of drowned alders, the carcasses of drowned sheep, and the wreckage of the temporary bridges the Spanish engineers had built across the tributaries at Rupelmonde.

In the cellar of the cooper’s shed, the water seeped through the mortar between the bottom two courses of brick, forming a shallow, yellowish pool around the lye-tub.

Katharina did not wait for the water to reach the bedding.

She had packed their goods into three baskets of stout willow-wicker, woven by Master Claes’s own hands from the finest peeled osiers of the river-islands.

The first basket held their clothes: the two frieze cloaks, the mended wool shifts, the children’s wooden shoes, and the three pewter spoons Pieter had given her on their marriage morning.

The second held the food: twenty pounds of dried rye bread, hard as ship’s biscuit, three pounds of salt bacon wrapped in clean linen, and a small wooden firkin of Zeeland butter.

The third basket was small, no larger than an apothecary’s chest, but it required both Katharina and Lucas to lift it by its hemp-wrapped handles.

Inside lay the metal.

The three cases of type had been distributed to the last letter; the pica, the italic, the double-canon capitals, and the brass rules were packed into small, square boxes of thin beech-wood, wedged tight with dry sawdust so that they could not rattle or shift when the basket was carried down a slippery gangplank.

On the top of the metal lay the tools: Pieter’s two brass composing sticks, the boxwood galleys, the iron bodkin, the bone folder, and the small, flat steel scraper that had once belonged to Balthasar van Cloet.

Master Claes came down the ladder for the last time.

He wore his best cloak of brown Ghent cloth, stiff with age, and a pair of high leather sea-boots that came to his hips, grease-blackened to keep out the river-water.

He looked at the three baskets. He looked at the empty vault, where the red brick was wet with the spring seepage.

"The onion-boat is at the wharf of the Blue Tower," he said.

His voice was lower than usual, muffled by the wool scarf he had wrapped around his throat against the river-fog.

"He is my sister’s second boy," the old man said. "He has three hogsheads of Ghent beer for the garrison at Lillo, and sixty sacks of dry onions from the polders. He reached into his pocket and drew out an iron key.

"This is the key to the cooper’s shop at Middelburg," he said, laying the iron in her hand. "My brother Jan was master there until he died in the year of the pacification. The shop is behind the fish-market, at the sign of the *Hoop*. It has a dry loft that will hold two beds, and an undercroft where a small press could stand without troubling the street."

Katharina looked at the key.

It was long, cold, its bit cut into five complex wards that had been greased with mutton-fat to keep them from the sea-rust.

"I have no more silver for you, Master Claes," she said.

The old man spat into the pool of water that lay around his boots.

"Did I ask for silver, woman?" he muttered. "Pieter gave me four silver guilders when the Spaniards burned my yard in seventy-six, and he never asked for a receipt on stamped paper. Take the key. When Lucas is a master printer with six boys at the balls, let him print a Latin Bible for the church at Saint Andrew’s and leave my name in the margin."

He turned toward the ladder, his boots splashing in the shallow water.

"Bring the boy," he said. "The tide turns at midnight. If we miss the ebb, the wind will pin the barge against the wharf, and the Provost’s searchers will be on the deck with their iron spits before the sun is up."

### Chapter 15

The night was very dark, with a thin, driving drizzle that blurred the lanterns of the Citadel into faint, yellow smudges against the black wall of the sky.

The wharf of the Blue Tower smelled of rotting cabbage, bilge-water, and the wet hemp of twenty moored barges that groaned against their oak fenders as the tide began to suck the river toward the sea.

Katharina walked between Master Claes and the boy, her hand holding Clara’s small fingers inside her cloak.

Lucas carried the small wicker basket between his hands, his head down, his breath coming in short, regular puffs that were swallowed by the mist. He did not look at the Spanish sentry who stood twenty paces away beneath the stone arch of the water-gate, an iron halberd resting against his shoulder, his back turned to the river to catch the shelter of the masonry.

The onion-boat was a broad, flat-bottomed hoy of fifty tons, its single mast lowered upon the deck-crutches to clear the bridge-piers.

The deck was an expanse of wet, slippery fir-planks, piled high with sacks of yellow onions that filled the cold air with a sharp, stinging smell that made Clara sneeze twice beneath her shawl.

A young man in a red wool bonnet and a sleeveless leather coat appeared from the dark of the steerage hatch.

He did not speak. He took the three baskets from their hands, swinging the heavy wicker of the type-metal into the hold with an effortless heave of his thick shoulders that showed he had handled salt-casks since he was twelve.

"Down," Master Claes whispered behind them.

Katharina stepped onto the narrow elm ladder that led into the forward hold.

The space beneath the deck was tiny, dark, smelling of bilge-water, old cheese, and the sweet, suffocating reek of five hundred bushels of dry onions. In the narrow space between the mast-step and the forward bulkhead, the young skipper had made a low cave among the sacks, lined with two bundles of dry rye-straw.

"Lie flat," the young man said in the quick, clipped dialect of the Zeeland islands. "Do not cough. Do not speak. We have three miles of Spanish water before we reach the reach of Doel. If the guard-boat hails, my name is Gielis from Tholen, and I have six kegs of small ale for the sergeant at the fort."

The deck-hatch closed above their heads.

The darkness became total.

Katharina lay in the straw, her back against the curved fir ribs of the hull, her arms holding Clara and Lucas against her ribs.

Beneath them, through two inches of oak and pine, she could feel the river.

It was no longer the sluggish ditch-water of the town canals; it was the great, living muscle of the Scheldt, cold, swift, swollen with the spring melt, rushing toward the west with a deep, gurgling roar that vibrated through the timber into the bones of her skull.

*Creak.*

The heavy hemp mooring-line was cast off above.

The barge lurched once, her bow swinging into the dark stream, then leaned gently to the south as the current caught her flat bottom.

There was no sound of oars.

The skipper was running under the bare poles, using the five-knot ebb of the spring tide to slip past the Spanish ketches without a splash of wood or a squeak of leather.

Above their heads, through the seams of the deck-planks, came the distant, bronze stroke of the Cathedral bell.

One.

The bell of Saint Mary of Antwerp.

The sound traveled out across the black water, thin, sweet, and infinitely sad, fading slowly into the rush of the river and the wind that blew from the sea.

Lucas shifted in the straw.

His hand reached out in the dark, found his mother’s fingers, and pressed into them a small, hard object.

Katharina felt it with her thumb.

It was not a silver coin.

It was a piece of lead—a single type of the great double-canon capital *A*, its face sharp and clear, its metal cold from the river damp.

"For the first page, Mother," the boy whispered into her ear.

Katharina closed her fingers over the letter.

The sharp corners of the lead bit into her palm, exactly as the composing stick had bitten into Pieter’s hand, into her hand, and would bite into the boy’s hand in the years that were to come.

The barge surged forward into the dark.

Ahead of them, six miles down the widening reach, where the river opened into the salt marshes and the great estuary of the North, the guns of the Dutch forts stood waiting in the mist; and beyond the forts, across fifty miles of gray water, lay the city of Leiden, with its open shops, its sixteen presses, and its clean, white paper waiting for the light.

Katharina laid her head against the onion-sacks, drew the children close against her heart, and listened to the river run.
### Chapter 16

The reach of Doel was where the Scheldt ceased to be a river and became a salt-drowned marsh.

Through the gaps in the hoy’s deck-planks, Katharina watched the light change from the absolute black of the Antwerp reach to a thin, watery gray that had no sun in it, only the cold glare of wet mud. The air in the hold grew colder, tasting no longer of ditch-cabbage and sewer-grease, but of sea-wrack, dead cockles, and the vast, bitter emptiness of the Zeeland flats.

The hoy gave a sudden, sickening roll to larboard.

Her flat bottom struck a mud-shelf with a dull, sucking thud that sent three sacks of onions tumbling across the straw against Katharina’s shins. The timber groaned; then the current caught the stern, the rudder gave a heavy, vibrating kick that sounded through the hull like a muffled drum, and the boat slid off the silt into deep water again.

Above them, bare feet ran across the planks.

A rope slapped against the deck. The creak of a block—greaseless ash-wood turning on an iron pin—whined as the skipper hoisted the small foresail to catch the wind that was drawing down from the East Scheldt.

Then came the voice.

It was not from the hoy. It came from across the water, thin and metallic through the dawn mist, bouncing off the surface of the river with a hollow, echoing clink:

*"¿Quién es? ¡Alza la vela!"*

Katharina’s hand clamped over Clara’s mouth.

The child did not struggle. Her little body went rigid in the straw, her eyes turning up toward the gray seams of the deck with the ancient, instinctive obedience of a creature that had been hunted since the day it was weaned.

Beside them, Lucas did not move. He lay with his cheek pressed against the beech-wood box that held Pieter’s pica, his hand clutching the single double-canon *A* inside his doublet like a shield against his ribs.

The hoy’s deck shuddered as the skipper let the tiller fall into the comb.

His boots clattered forward, and his voice came through the hatch—rough, flat, thick with the drawling vowels of the Tholen islanders:

*"Gielis of Tholen! Lighter for the guard-post at the red beacon! I have sixty bags of winter onions and four tubs of small beer for Master Sergeant Diego!"*

A pause.

The sound of oars—two heavy ash sweeps working in iron tholes, dipping into the water with a slow, mechanical *chunk-hiss, chunk-hiss*.

A boat was coming alongside.

Katharina could hear the water slapping against the clinker-built sides of a Spanish row-barge, the iron gudgeons squeaking as the coxswain put the helm over. Through a chink in the forward bulkhead, two inches above her forehead, she saw the gray surface of the river.

Twenty yards away, half-swallowed by the yellow mist, sat the patrol-boat.

It was a long, black cutter with six benches of oars, manned by Walloon mercenaries in dirty buff jerkins and iron skull-caps. In the sternsheets stood a Spanish corporal, his yellow leather coat dark with river-spray, an iron-stocked arquebus held across his chest in the crook of his left arm.

The corporal looked at the hoy’s deck.

His eyes were small, red-rimmed from the night-watch, his mustache stiff with salt-rime. He did not look like a man who cared about the King’s customs or the decrees of the Council of Troubles; he looked like a man whose feet were wet, whose belly was empty, and who had spent four hours in an open boat listening to the gulls scream over the mud.

*"¿Dónde está la cerveza?"* he barked.

"On the cabin-head, master!" Gielis shouted back, leaning over the low bulwark with a wooden firkin in his arms. "Two tubs of the double-ale from Mechelen, sweet as honey and brown as an old cowl. The cooper said it would keep the rot out of the soldiers’ lungs until the easterly winds break."

The Spanish boat bumped against the hoy’s side.

The oak rubbed against the fir with a harsh, splintering screech that made the hair on the back of Katharina’s neck stand up like bristles. An iron boat-hook caught the hoy’s gunwale, the hook biting into the timber two feet above where the basket of type-metal lay in the bilge.

The corporal reached up.

He did not come aboard. He caught the firkin by its hemp handle, swung it down into the cutter’s bottom with an effortless, practiced twitch of his shoulder, and tapped the bung with his dagger-pommel.

A smell of sour malt drifted into the mist.

The corporal nodded once—a short, contemptuous jerk of his chin.

*"Boga,"* he muttered to his oarsmen. *"Row."*

The iron hook let go of the wood with a dull scrape.

The sweeps dipped: *chunk-hiss, chunk-hiss*. The long, black cutter slid away into the fog, its stern-lantern a pale red eye that flickered once, twice, and vanished into the gray expanse of the river.

Gielis did not move for five minutes.

He stood by the mast-step, holding the halyard in his hand, listening to the tide.

Then he reached down and kicked the deck-hatch three times with his heel.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

"Up," his voice came through the wood, thin and shaking with a strange, high tremor that had not been there before. "Up, woman. We are past the beacon."

Katharina pushed the hatch back.

The cold air hit her face like a wet sail.

The mist was tearing open before the northwest breeze, ragged gray curtains flying back to reveal the great, yellow reach of the Honte. The river here was two miles wide, running wild and salt toward the horizon, its water the color of lead, flecked with dirty yellow foam where the tide fought the shallows of the Saaftinge banks.

Ahead of them, a mile to the northwest, a low, green bank of earth rose out of the marshes like the back of a sleeping whale.

On the highest point of the earthwork stood a tall mast of peeled fir, stayed with four hemp shrouds.

From its truck, snapping in the salt gale with a sound like whip-lashes, flew a long, narrow banner of three colors:

*Orange. White. Blue.*

The banner of the Prince.

On the rampart beneath it, two iron demi-culverins stood pointed toward the southern reach, their long, black barrels wet with sea-rime, behind them the low, sod-covered roofs of the Dutch guard-house.

Katharina stood on the wet deck, holding Clara against her hip, her hair whipped across her mouth by the wind.

Lucas climbed through the hatch behind her. He did not look at the water, nor at the green bank of earth; his eyes were fixed on the three-colored flag that was dancing against the gray sky like a flame.

"Mother," he said, his small hand catching her apron. "Are those the heretics?"

Katharina looked down at him.

Her face was stiff with cold, her eyes burning with salt, but her mouth softened into the first smile the boy had seen upon her lips since the night the sergeant had broken the shop in the Cammerpoort.

"Those are the men who have the paper, Lucas," she said.

### Chapter 17

The town of Middelburg sat in the middle of its green island like a great spider in a web of salt-ditches.

It was not like Antwerp. It had no palaces of blue Tournai stone, no four-story warehouses with gilded pediments where the merchants of Lisbon and Genoa kept their cloves and their silk; its houses were low, broad-beamed, built of dark red brick from the Zeeland kilns, with high, steep roofs of black pantiles that looked like the scales of an eel.

The streets smelled of the sea.

Not the sweet, greasy rot of the inland river-docks, but the sharp, clean reek of dried cod, Stockholm tar, pickled herrings, and the salt-crust that the southwest gales blew off the sand-dunes of Westkapelle until every window-pane on the market-place was frosted like winter ice.

Katharina walked down the Lange Delft.

She did not carry her goods in an old sprat-basket now.

The Zeeland skipper had lent them his two-wheeled hand-cart—a stout little dray of ash-wood, with high willow-hurdles and broad wooden wheels that rolled easily over the round pebbles of the street. Lucas walked between the shafts, his head held high, his shoulder braced against the cross-bar with the proud, serious importance of a boy who had been given a horse’s work.

On the cart lay the three willow baskets, covered with a clean piece of sail-cloth that Master Claes’s nephew had given them when they parted at the wharf.

Behind them walked Clara, holding Katharina’s skirts with one hand and a round, dry gingerbread-cake with the other—a cake that an old woman in a starched linen cap had thrust into her fingers at the water-gate without asking whether her father had died a Catholic or a Calvinist.

The town was roaring with life.

In the market-place before the great Abbey of Saint Norbert, where the Prince’s governors now kept their council, four hundred men were gathered around the butter-cross. They were not the lean, terrified burghers of Antwerp, who walked with their eyes on their shoe-buckles and stopped speaking whenever a man in an iron cap rode by; they were loud, heavy-shouldered men in short coats of blue frieze, with broad felt hats cocked over their eyes and gold hoops in their ears, shouting in the broad, guttural dialects of Holland, Friesland, and the English ports.

Every third shop had a sign of typography swinging from its iron crane.

*In the Sign of the English Bible.*

*The Crowned Pelican.*

*The Golden Key of David.*

Through the open ground-floor windows, Katharina could hear the rhythmic, double-beat of the presses at work: *clack-thump, clack-thump, clack-thump*.

The sound was everywhere.

It echoed off the brick gables; it mingled with the screaming of the gulls and the clattering of the wooden pattens on the cobbles; it was the pulse of the island, steady, indifferent, and free.

She turned into the narrow lane behind the Fish Market.

The lane was called the *Kromme Weele*—the Crooked Pool. It ran along the edge of the old abbey moat, where the willows were just beginning to show their yellow catkins above the dark water.

At the end of the lane, where the brick wall turned toward the potteries, stood the shop of the *Hoop*.

It was a small, narrow house of two stories, built of timber and brick, with a high, pointed gable that leaned three inches toward the street like an old scholar listening to a tale. Over the door hung a wooden hoop of shaved birch, weathered gray by the sea-salt, with an adze and a draw-knife carved into the oak lintel beneath it.

The key was heavy, cold, smelling of Master Claes’s mutton-fat. She slipped it into the lock.

*Clack.*

The bolt shot back with a dull, hollow report.

She pushed the door open.

The smell that came out of the house was not the sour, bitter rot of the Antwerp cellar.

In the center of the room stood a heavy workbench of beech-planks, four inches thick, supported by four stout oak posts that were mortised into the ground-sills of the house.

Behind the bench, where the light fell from a high window that looked out over the abbey gardens, was an open space of red floor-tiles, ten feet by twelve, clean, dry, and level as a pond.

A press could stand there.

A double-screw press of good Zeeland elm, with a six-foot coffin and an iron platen that would take a whole sheet of royal paper without a groan.

Katharina walked to the center of the tiles.

She set down her basket of clothes. She took the second basket from the cart, carried it to the workbench, and set it upon the wood.

"Lucas," she said.

The boy stood in the doorway, the light from the street falling across his ink-stained face, his dark eyes looking from the high oak rafters down to the red tiles of the floor.

"Bring the lead," she said.

The beech-wood boxes sat in their nests of sawdust, untouched, the oil-bladders unbroken.

Katharina reached in, took out the first box, and slid the lid back.

The pica lay there in its rows, gray, cold, and beautiful, sixty letters of Granjon’s roman shining in the northern daylight like silver coin fresh from the die.

She reached into her pocket.

She took out the iron signet ring that she had fished from the lye-tub on the night of the flight—the ring with the two severed hands of Antwerp that she had carried through the Spanish lines in the toe of her stocking.

She did not drop it into a tub.

She laid it upon the beech-wood bench, took the heavy iron adze that hung from a nail behind the door, and struck the iron ring with the flat of the steel head.

*Clang.*

The iron cracked.

The two hands of Antwerp parted from the shield, the broken pieces jumping across the wood to fall into the heap of clean ash-shavings beneath the bench.

"There are no hands cut off in this town," she said to the boy.

She picked up the brass composing stick.

She set the slide to eighteen pica ems, tightened the steel thumb-screw until the brass groaned, and held the stick out toward the light.

"Take the letters, Lucas," she said, her voice steady as the tide that was running past the sea-dykes of Westkapelle. "We have forty quires of Dutch paper to set before the packet sails for London."

The boy reached his fingers into the box.

*Click.*

The first letter fell against the brass rule.

*A.*

Outside in the lane, the gulls screamed over the fish-stalls, the bells of the Abbey struck the third hour of the afternoon, and from the great water-gate by the haven came the deep, bronze boom of a ship’s gun, saluting the new flag that was standing out to sea.

### Chapter 18

The guild-book of Saint Luke in Middelburg did not have thirty-two red crosses drawn through its margins.

It was a small, square volume of Dutch parchment, bound in pigskin that had been dyed the color of a wet slate, held shut by two brass clasps shaped like open books. It lay upon the oak lectern in the small room above the Fish-Packers’ Hall, where the four elders of the craft met every second Tuesday after the noon-meal to hear disputes over apprentices’ indentures, to inspect the weight of paper-stocks brought from the French mills, and to collect the two-stuiver fee for every title entered upon the town register.

Katharina stood before the four elders.

She had not worn her old frieze cloak; she had bought three yards of plain black wool from an English cloth-merchant at the haven, sewn it into a decent gown with a narrow linen collar that lay flat upon her collarbones, and covered her hair with a widow’s cap of white lawn, stiffened with a little starch from the wheat-flour.

Beside her stood Lucas.

His hair was combed back with a bone comb, his ears scrubbed until they were red, his hands clean of ink for the first time in six months; but his fingers stayed curved in the shape of the composing stick, his right thumb twitching slightly against his seam as if he were feeling for the nick on a letter.

The dean of the guild was an old man named Aernout van Geluwe.

He had been a printer in Ghent until the Duke of Parma had retaken the town in eighty-four; he had lost his house, his two presses, and his right eye to a Spanish halberd in the Vrijdagmarkt, but he had carried his punch-cutters’ matrices across the drowned polders in a leather knapsack tied between his shoulder-blades. Now he ran three presses behind the Meat Market, printing maps for the admiralty of Zeeland and Latin grammars for the new school at Franeker.

He peered at Katharina through a pair of spectacles with heavy horn rims, his single gray eye watery and sharp as a gull’s.

"You are Pieter Huysman’s widow," he said.

His voice had the dry, whistling rattle of the Ghent canals, slow and measured.

"I am," Katharina said.

"Pieter was a good workman," the old man muttered, turning the pages of the pigskin register with a yellow forefinger. "He was admitted to the freedom at Antwerp in the year seventy-eight. I remember his trial-piece: an octavo of the *Sentences* of Master Peter Lombard, set in Granjon’s pica with the italic notes in the margin. The register was as clean as an altar-cloth. He had an eye for the white space. Most of these German botchers set their lines as tight as turnips in a cart; Pieter let the paper breathe."

He looked at the boy.

"Whose boy is this?"

"Pieter’s," she said. "He is seven years old. He knows his boxes. He can set forty lines of Latin in the hour without a turned letter."

The other three elders looked at the boy with the cold, assessing eyes of horse-dealers inspecting a colt.

One was a bookseller from Dordrecht who sold Dutch translations of the Geneva psalms; the second was a type-founder from Mechelen who cast letters for the whole province from a cellar in the Pottenbakkerssingel; the third was a notary named Joos van Berchem, a cousin to that Van Berchem who had been hanged from the crane at Antwerp during the great plunder.

The notary leaned forward, his hands resting upon a bundle of papers tied with black hemp tape.

"There is a difficulty, mistress," he said.

Katharina’s fingers did not tighten on her apron. "What difficulty, master?"

"The freedom of the guild is personal," the notary said, his voice dropping into the thin, dry cadence of the courts. "It descends to the son when he is of age, provided he has served seven years to an acknowledged master and produced his piece to the satisfaction of the bench. It does not descend to the widow."

"The custom of Antwerp—" Katharina began.

"This is not Antwerp," the notary cut her short, tapping the table with his ivory rule. "This is the province of Zeeland. In this town, a widow may keep the shop open for twelve months after her husband’s death, provided she employs a sworn journeyman to direct the press and sign the declarations. After the twelve months, she must either marry a master of the craft or sell her timber to the highest bidder."

"My husband has been dead sixteen months," she said.

"Precisely," the notary said. "And you have no journeyman."

The room was silent, save for the screaming of the terns over the fish-slabs in the street below and the slow, rhythmic *tick-tick* of an iron clock that hung behind the dean’s chair.

Old Aernout van Geluwe reached into his waistcoat.

He took out a small piece of printed paper, no larger than a man’s hand, folded twice across the center. He did not open it; he held it between his two fingers, feeling the grain of the paper.

"This came from the packet-boat yesterday," the old man said.

Katharina looked at the paper.

She knew the fold. She knew the faint, greenish cast of the linen rag, which had been washed in the river-water at Troyes.

It was a leaf of the *Declaration*.

"Where did you get that, master?" she asked, her voice dropping an octave, steady and cold.

"A customer brought it from Flushing," Aernout said. He did not look at the notary; he looked at Katharina with his single, sharp eye. "He said the Duke of Parma had sent three regiments of Spanish foot to Antwerp to quarter upon the burghers because the Town Council could not find the press that struck it. He said Master Joost van Valckenisse has been carried to the Castle of Vilvoorde in an iron wagon, with his legs chained beneath the belly of his mule."

The type-founder from Mechelen leaned back in his chair, a slow, grim grin spreading across his leathery face.

"Van Valckenisse was a thief," the founder said. "He stole three tons of type-metal from my brother’s shop in seventy-six. If the Spaniards hang him, they will only be doing the work the hangman should have done ten years ago."

Old Aernout unfolded the leaf upon the oak lectern.

He took off his horn spectacles, wiped them on the sleeve of his black gown, and set them back upon his nose. He brought his eye down to within two inches of the paper, his finger tracing the capital letters of the fifth line:

*...A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

"The *M*," he whispered.

Katharina did not breathe.

"The capital *M* has a broken left shoulder," the old man said, his voice so low that the notary had to lean forward to hear it. "A tiny notch, no wider than a horsehair, where the punch-cutter’s graver slipped when Hendrik van den Keere was cutting the face for Plantin in the year sixty-nine. There were only four cases of that letter cast. One was sold to the King’s printer in Paris; one was burned at Tournai; one is in Plantin’s private press; and the fourth..."

He stopped.

He looked at Lucas.

The boy did not flinch. His dark eyes met the old man’s single eye with the calm, terrifying certainty of a child who had seen a press smashed with sledges and knew that words were things that could kill men.

"The fourth belonged to Pieter Huysman," old Aernout said.

He folded the paper again.

He did not give it to the notary. He slipped it into the breast of his gown, buttoning the black cloth over it with three slow turns of his yellow fingers.

"The law of Zeeland is the law," the old man said, turning his face toward the notary. "A widow may not keep a press without a master. But the law does not say that the master must have two legs, nor that he must be forty years old. Lucas Huysman is the son of an acknowledged master of the art. He is enrolled upon the books of Antwerp. If he serves his time in this town, under the inspection of the elders, the press at the *Hoop* may work under his father’s sign until his pieces are perfected."

The notary opened his mouth to speak, but Aernout lifted his hand—a hand that had lost two joints from the middle finger, scarred by old lye-burns and punch-cuts.

"I am the dean of this guild, Master Joos," the old man said with a sudden, harsh snap in his voice that silenced the clerk like a blow. "I knew Pieter Huysman when we were boys together in the shop of Willem Silvius, sleeping under the paper-loft and stealing candle-ends to read the *Colloquies* of Erasmus. ### Chapter 19

The press that came to the *Hoop* in the second week of April was not an elm half-press.

It was an old two-pull press of seasoned Dutch oak, built at Gouda thirty years before, that had belonged to a printer who had died of the dropsy in the hospital of the Holy Ghost.

It had a long, six-foot coffin lined with polished blue slate from the quarries of Namur, a brass nut that had been cast in one piece with the winter-beam, and an iron screw five inches thick that had been turned by a smith who knew how to cut a true three-inch pitch. The cheeks were six inches square, bound with iron straps at the joints, and the platen—twenty inches by fourteen—was forged from a single slab of charcoal-iron, ground so flat that when it came down upon the slate, you could not pass a hair between the metal and the stone.

It took four draymen with two stout cart-horses to haul the timber through the narrow passage of the *Kromme Weele*.

Master Claes’s nephew, the young skipper Gielis, had brought his two deck-hands from the onion-boat to help them raise the cheeks.

They worked by the light of the high window that looked out over the abbey gardens.

The great oak posts rose into the air, their tenons sliding into the heavy ground-sills with a deep, wooden *chunk* that shook the cooper’s shavings on the floor. Gielis drove the oak trunnels with a six-pound ironwood mallet—*thump, thump, thump*—while Lucas held the tallow candle and Clara sat upon an empty barrel-head, her hands wrapped in her red frieze apron, watching the great iron screw rise toward the ceiling like a black spiral tower.

When the last pin was home, Katharina laid her palm against the oak cheek.

The timber did not groan. It did not tremble.

It was four hundredweight of dead, unyielding heart of oak, rooted in the red tiles of the floor as if it had grown from the clay beneath the house.

"The bed is true, mistress," Gielis said, wiping the sweat from his forehead with his sleeve. He laid a mason’s level across the blue slate. The air-bubble sat precisely between the two black marks on the glass tube. "You could strike off the King of Spain’s head on that stone and never chip the corners."

Katharina walked to the workbench.

The three cases of type were in their frames now, sitting upon the birch trestles that the cooper’s apprentices had planed for them.

Lucas stood at the case.

He was not wearing his ragged adult doublet now; he wore a neat working-apron of brown linen that Katharina had sewn for him, with a leather pocket on the right hip to hold his composing rule and a horn comb to clean the type.

He had the composing stick in his hand.

"The English text, Mother?" he asked.

"The English text," she said.

She laid the copy before him upon the wooden visor.

It was a bill of lading for thirty bags of white sheep’s wool, shipped by Master Richard Hakluyt of London aboard the hoy *The Good Intent* of Harwich, to be delivered to the merchant factors at Middelburg:

*SHIPPED by the Grace of God, in good Order and well Conditioned...*

Lucas’s hand flew across the case.

*Click. Click. Click.*

The brass rule clicked against the lead.

The capitals were Plantin’s double-canon; the body was Granjon’s pica; the spaces were the thin brass quadrats that Pieter had filed in the cellar of the Cammerpoort two years ago.

Katharina did not help him.

She walked to the press.

She took the two sheepskin balls that she had stuffed with fresh Zeeland wool and covered with pelt from an uncastrated ram bought from the butchers of the Veemarkt. She had worked them on the ink-block for an hour that morning, using ink made from boiled linseed oil and vine-black that old Aernout had given her from his own cellar—clean, rich black that had no turpentine in it, smelling of old vineyards and summer earth.

"The paper," she said.

Clara slipped down from her barrel.

The child was six years old now, quick on her feet, her hands nimble and clean. She took the first sheet of royal crown-paper from between the wet pine boards—thick, cream-colored Dutch rag from the new mills at Alkmaar, smelling of fresh water and clean linen—and laid it upon the tympan.

Lucas brought the galley.

He slid the thirty lines of lead into the iron chase that sat upon the Namur slate.

Katharina wedged the beech quoins with the wooden shooting-stick.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The chase rang true.

She took the sheepskin balls.

*Pat-pat-pat, roll-roll-roll.*

The leather kissed the lead. The black letters shone in the daylight like jet.

She folded down the frisket. She folded down the tympan.

"Pull, Lucas," she said.

The boy stepped onto the oak platform.

He caught the ash-wood bar with both hands. He did not need to strain his belly against the wood; the five-inch screw turned with the smooth, oily ease of a well-balanced water-wheel, the iron platen descending upon the damp paper with an even, monstrous pressure that seemed to draw the breath from the room.

*Pull.*

The bar came home against the stop.

The boy threw it back. The counter-weight lifted the iron with a clear, bell-like ring.

Katharina turned the rounce.

The carriage rolled out from the shadow of the coffin into the yellow sunlight that was pouring through the garden window.

She lifted the tympan. She lifted the frisket.

She took the sheet by the corners with her fingernails and held it up before the boy’s eyes.

The black was absolute.

The lines were straight as a plumb-line, the margins white and clean as new-fallen snow, the impression biting into the linen fibers with the deep, crisp authority of an art that had survived the fire, the sleet, and the sledges of the King.

*SHIPPED by the Grace of God...*

At the bottom of the sheet, in the small, clean italic that had belonged to Pieter Huysman, ran the imprint:

*Printed at Middelburg, at the Sign of the Cold Tympan, by the Widow and Son of Pieter Huysman. 1588.*

Lucas looked at the words.

He did not speak. His small, ink-stained thumb reached out, touched the wet black line where his father’s name stood in the lead, and then drew back, leaving no mark upon the paper.

"Four hundred and ninety-nine," Katharina said, her voice clear and sweet in the quiet room.

### Chapter 20

The summer of 1588 did not smell of flowers or hay; it smelled of gunpowder, sulfur, and the sour wool of five thousand English soldiers billeted in the barns along the canal from Flushing to Middelburg.

In the narrow sea between the North Foreland and the shoals of Flanders, the King of Spain’s great fleet was coming up the Channel: one hundred and thirty galleons, naos, and galleasses, their gilded stern-castles towering sixty feet above the water, carrying thirty thousand soldiers and two thousand brass cannon to conquer England.

Along the coast of Zeeland, the Prince’s flyboats and flat-bottomed gun-ketches lay behind the sandbanks of Dunkirk and Sluis like a pack of sea-wolves. They were small craft, manned by rough, barefoot Beggars from the islands whose ears had been cropped by Spanish executioners, their captains sworn to drown every barge that the Duke of Parma tried to launch from the Flemish canals.

The town of Middelburg was in a frenzy of printing.

Every ship that arrived from Dover brought dispatches from Lord Howard of Effingham and Sir Francis Drake, scribbled on greasy leaves of paper that were hurried from the haven to the *Hoop* by boys running through the mud. The English factors needed proclamations; the Admiralty of Zeeland needed orders of battle; the town council needed broadsheets to warn the farmers against spies who were said to be poisoning the wells with arsenic.

The press behind the Fish Market did not stop.

From five in the morning, when the sun rose red and hot out of the East Scheldt marshes, until ten at night, when the curfew-gun boomed from the Abbey tower, the ash-wood bar came home against the oak cheeks with a steady, rhythmic cadence:

*Pull. Clack. Release. Crank.*

Lucas was nine years old now. He had grown two inches since the winter, his arms hardening until the muscles of his shoulders stood out beneath his linen shirt like cords. He worked at the rounce with the effortless, swinging rhythm of an experienced pressman, his left hand feeding the damp Dutch paper onto the tympan while his right caught the handle of the spit.

Clara sat by the window in the afternoon light.

She was six, her yellow hair bound in a neat cap of Dutch lawn, her small fingers sorting the space-boxes. ### Chapter 21

The man who stood in the doorway did not look like an alderman of Antwerp.

He did not wear a cloak of Flemish broadcloth trimmed with marten-fur; he wore an old doublet of stained frieze, torn at the armpits and patched with greasy canvas, that hung upon his shoulders like a shroud upon a dead thorn-bush. His beaver hat was gone; in its place was a soiled linen night-cap, tied beneath his chin with a piece of hemp string, from beneath which his gray hair hung in wet, tangled rat-tails across his forehead.

His shoes were not Spanish cordovan; they were clogs of cracked willow-wood, stuffed with straw, tied to his swollen ankles with leather thongs that had cut into the gray skin until the sores were crusted with yellow pus.

He stood with his hands pressed against the oak door-post, his body shaking with a violent, rhythmic ague that made his teeth chatter together with a dry, clicking rattle: *t-t-t-t-t*.

Katharina did not rise from the bench.

She sat motionless in the tallow-light, her hands remaining folded upon her black wool apron, her eyes taking in the hollow temples, the yellowish skin stretched tight over the sharp cheekbones, the thin spade-beard that had turned entirely white and was crusted around the mouth with dried salt-foam.

"Balthasar van Cloet," she said.

Her voice was not loud. It had the quiet, level weight of a stone dropping into a deep cistern.

The man let his head fall forward against the wood of the door-post.

A long, shuddering sigh came from his throat—a breath that smelled of marsh-fever, bad beer, and the sour rot of clothes that had not been dried by a fire for three months.

"The... the imprint," he whispered.

His voice was a broken rasp, stripped of every trace of the chancellery Latin, thin as the rustle of dead rushes in a winter ditch.

"The imprint was upon the wool-bills," he said. He did not look at her face; he looked at the great oak press that stood in the center of the room, its iron screw shining with clean oil. "At the haven. A merchant from Flushing had a bill of lading for thirty bales of sheep's wool. At the bottom of the sheet... *The Cold Tympan*... set in Pieter Huysman's italic. I knew the capital *T*. It was the only case in the Low Countries that had the cross-bar with the double serif."

He took two stumbling steps into the shop.

His wooden clogs clicked upon the red tiles—a hollow, clumsy sound that made Lucas stir among the shavings by the wall.

"They did not welcome you at The Hague," Katharina said.

Van Cloet gave a dry, wheezing cough that brought a fleck of brownish blood to his lower lip.

"The Hague," he muttered, his hand reaching out to steady himself against the corner of the workbench. "The ministers at The Hague are priests with different collars. They do not want the account-books of Antwerp. They want money to pay the English mercenaries. When I gave them the names of the five aldermen who stole the salt-duties, the Grand Pensionary looked at the parchment, folded it twice, and locked it in his iron chest. He told me that two of the aldermen had sons who were merchant factors in Amsterdam, and that the Republic could not afford to trouble the credit of Amsterdam while the Spanish fleet was at sea."

He sank down onto the bench beside the press.

His knees seemed to fold beneath him like dry reeds; his head dropped between his shoulders, his thin chest heaving beneath the ragged frieze.

"They gave me six silver guilders," he said. "And an order of expulsion from the province of Holland. They said an inquisitor who had signed twenty warrants of execution in the Cammerpoort was not a neighbor that the Calvinist burghers would care to meet upon the Lange Vijverberg."

"They were right," Katharina said.

Van Cloet looked up then.

His pale, watery gray eyes met hers. The arrogance of the magistrate was gone; the cold, bureaucratic precision that had weighed Pieter’s life against an empty house on the Meir was gone; there was only the naked, animal terror of a man who had heard the hounds behind him in the swamp and knew that the water was too deep to cross.

"The Provost's men are in Middelburg," he whispered.

Katharina did not move. "There are no Spanish provosts in this town."

"They are not Spanish," he said. "They are Walloons. Two men from the regiment of Mondragón who deserted after the siege of Sluis. They have taken service with the secret commission in Brussels. He reached inside the breast of his ragged doublet.

"What is that?" Katharina asked.

"It is the register," he said.

Two men were standing in the shadow of the brick wall that bounded the potteries.

They wore long, dark cloaks that reached to their heels, with high-crowned hats of black felt pulled low over their faces. They were looking at the sign of the *Hoop*.

She walked to the press-bed, took the tarred package from the slate, and dropped it into the deep leather pocket of her apron.

"Lucas," she said, her voice clear and hard as iron.

"Get under the paper-loft," she said.

Van Cloet did not move. He stared at her with his watery, terrified eyes, his hands clutching his knees.

"Move," she said, her voice dropping into a whisper that had the edge of an adze. "If they kill you in my shop, the blood will ruin three reams of Alkmaar paper, and I have five hundred copies of Drake's victory to print before the morning tide."

### Chapter 22

The two men came into the shop five minutes after the children had gone.

They did not knock. They stepped through the open doorway together, their boots making no sound upon the red tiles because they had bound their soles with strips of thick wool felt—the trick of city bravoes who worked in the dark alleys of the river-ports.

They were tall, lean-jawed men with the pale, sickly skin of men who had spent three years in the garrisons of the Flemish coast.

The first was a Walloon with a great red scar across his upper lip that had drawn the mouth up into a permanent, snarling grin, showing four blackened teeth like broken pegs. He carried a heavy oak cudgel in his right hand, loaded with lead at the head, and a long, double-edged dagger tucked into his sash.

The second was a Fleming from the suburbs of Ghent, younger, with greasy yellow hair that hung over his ears and small, restless eyes that darted into every corner of the shop like a ferret’s in a rabbit-warren. He had a wheel-lock pistol in his belt, its brass spanner hanging from his neck by a leather cord, the cock down upon the pyrites, ready to strike.

The Walloon looked at Katharina.

She was standing at the inking table, her back to the press, holding the two sheepskin balls in her hands. She did not look up when they entered; she was rocking the balls across the ink-block with a slow, mechanical rhythm: *pat-pat-pat, roll-roll-roll*.

The smell of boiled linseed oil and vine-black was strong in the room, warm and heavy, covering the odor of the wet street.

"Where is the notary, mistress?" the Walloon asked.

He spoke Dutch, but his tongue was thick, clumsy with the mangled vowels of the southern border.

Katharina stopped the balls.

She laid them down upon the stone, wiped her ink-stained fingers upon her apron, and turned her face toward the door.

"The notary?" she said. Her voice had the flat, indifferent rasp of a shopkeeper who had been interrupted while counting her receipts. "Master Joos van Berchem has his house in the Lange Delft, behind the Cloth Hall. If you want a deed witnessed, you are four hours late. His clerks lock the chambers at the second drum."

The Fleming with the yellow hair laughed—a short, ugly bark that had no mirth in it.

"Not Van Berchem," he said, taking two steps forward until his boots were within four feet of her bench. "The old rat from Antwerp. Balthasar van Cloet. He was seen coming down the canal from Flushing three hours ago. A boatman said he asked for the shop that had Pieter Huysman's letters."

Katharina looked him in the eyes.

Her pale gray gaze was as steady and cold as the water in the abbey moat.

"Pieter Huysman was my man," she said. "He died in the Steen eighteen months ago. The man who signed his death-warrant was Balthasar van Cloet. If that dog came into this lane, master, I would not give him an ash-shaving to wipe his boots on. I would call the cooper’s apprentices with their draw-knives and have his skin nailed to the town pump before the watch went off."

The Walloon stared at her.

His small, piggish eyes traveled from her white widow’s coif down to her black wool gown, then to the great oak press that stood in the light of the tallow dips.

"The press is new," he said, pointing with his leaded cudgel toward the long coffin.

"The press is twenty years old," she said. "It belonged to Master Dirck of Gouda. I bought the timber from his executors with thirty silver florins that I earned from the English factors. The Fleming pointed with his dagger-point.

"The mud is fresh, mistress," he whispered.

Her fingers did not find a silver coin. They found the heavy iron bodkin—four inches of forged steel, ground to a three-sided point that Pieter had used to lift jammed letters from the chase. He looked down at the yellow-haired man, who lay upon his back with his eyes rolled up into his skull, his mouth open, a thin trickle of dark blood running from his ear into the sawdust.

The cooper turned the man over with the toe of his heavy sea-boot.

"He will live until morning," Claes muttered, spitting into the shavings. "Take them to the haven, boys. Put them into the bottom of the salt-ketch from Zierikzee. Tell the skipper to drop them into the deep water off the Roompot when he clears the sandbanks. The crabs must eat as well as the burghers."

The two deck-hands did not speak.

They caught the Walloon by his heels and dragged him through the door, his broken shoulder bumping over the stone threshold with a dull, heavy *thump-thump*, while Gielis heaved the unconscious Fleming over his shoulder like a sack of oats and followed them into the dark lane.

The street was silent again, save for the dripping of the rain from the eaves and the distant crying of the gulls.

Master Claes walked to the press.

He picked up the wheel-lock pistol from the floor, weighed it in his hand, and laid it upon the Namur slate beside Pieter’s italic cases.

"You have a guest in the paper-loft, Katharina," the old man said softly.

Katharina did not look up from the inking block.

"He has the register, Master Claes," she said.

The cooper looked at the ceiling-joists, where a single board had been shifted aside to show a white, terrified face peering down through the gap.

"The register of the Steen?" the old man asked.

"Yes."

Claes reached into his belt, drew out his horn tobacco-box, and took a pinch of the dried leaf between his yellow fingers.

"The wind is from the west," he said, looking through the window toward the abbey tower. "The packet for London sails with the morning tide. If that paper is put into type tonight, mistress, it will be in the hands of the Queen's councilors at Greenwich before the Spanish fleet has rounded the Orkneys."

He walked to the door, his heavy boots clicking on the red tiles.

At the threshold, he paused, his broad, round back outlined against the blue night.

"Lucas is in my kitchen," he said. "He has set the title-page. He told my woman that the capitals were too small by half a line, and that he needed the double-canon from the red box."

The old man grinned—a slow, warm wrinkle of his red cheeks—and stepped out into the lane.

Katharina turned toward the ladder.

"Van Cloet," she said.

The boards creaked above.

The former commissioner of the town of Antwerp came down the rungs one by one, his willow clogs clattering against the elm poles, his thin body shaking so hard that he had to catch the upright with both arms to keep from falling into the shavings.

He reached the floor.

He stood before her, his head bowed, his hands empty.

Katharina took the tarred package from her apron pocket and laid it upon the composing stone.

She took the small steel bodkin, cut the three seals of black resin, and threw the sheepskin back.

Inside lay sixty leaves of Genoa paper, closely written in the dry, beautiful hand of the chancellery, bearing the broad red seal of the town of Antwerp.

Across the top of the first page ran the title:

*ACTA ET CONFESSIONES IN CAUSA TYPOGRAPHICA...*

"Light the four dips," Katharina said.

Van Cloet blinked at her through his tears. "What?"

"The candles," she said, her voice clear and sweet as the Cathedral bells across the Scheldt. "Light the four dips. Take the ivory scraper. If there is a word in this Latin that is false, Van Cloet, scrape it out before the boy sets the line. We print the truth in this house, or we do not print at all."

She picked up the brass composing stick.

She set the slide to twenty pica ems, tightened the steel screw, and held the tool out into the yellow light of the tallow.

The former inquisitor took the tinder-box from the shelf with hands that shook no longer.

*Strike. Strike.*

The spark caught the charred linen. The pale blue flame stood up, straight and clear in the salt air.

In the shop behind the Fish Market, the mother and the customer stood together over the lead, waiting for the boy to bring the letters.

### Chapter 23

The Latin was not the loose, colloquial speech of the market-stalls; it was the cold, unyielding language of the Roman law, where every verb had the weight of an iron fetter and every noun was an account of goods seized into the King’s hand.

Van Cloet sat upon the low three-legged stool by the composing frame, the four tallow dips burning in a cluster before him on a slab of clean Namur slate.

In his left hand he held the ivory-handled scraper; in his right, a goose-quill that he had trimmed to a needle-point with Pieter’s bodkin. As Katharina set the lines, the former commissioner would read the text aloud, his voice dropping into the rhythmic, chanting cadence of a notary reading a will before the aldermen:

*“...Die decima septima mensis Maii, anno Domini millesimo quingentesimo octogesimo sexto. Interrogatus Petrus Huysman, typographus, civis Antverpiensis...”*

Lucas stood beside his mother.

The boy did not look at Van Cloet. He looked at the manuscript leaf that was held in the wooden copy-clamp above the case. His fingers moved with a mechanical, unhesitating swiftness that seemed to anticipate the Latin words before the notary’s tongue had finished the syllables:

*P - e - t - r - u - s*

*space.*

*H - u - y - s - m - a - n*

Every letter fell into the brass stick with a small, sharp *click*.

Katharina did not look at the manuscript. She stood at the second frame, setting the Dutch translation that Van Cloet had written in the margins—the common tongue that the merchants of London and the burghers of Middelburg could read without the aid of a schoolmaster:

*“...Being asked by the Lord Amman whether he had received from the hands of the factor of Frankfurt certain quires of paper containing the prohibited works of the heretics: The said Pieter Huysman answered that he had received no paper from Frankfurt, save only six reams of white printing-paper for the use of the Latin school of Saint James...”*

She set the line.

She reached for the next handful of lead.

Her fingers knew the boxes without her eyes directing them: the *a*, the *n*, the *s*, the *w*, the *e*, the *r*.

*“...Being shown the iron lever found in his press-room, and asked why the screw of the said press was of a smaller diameter than the ordinance of the Guild of Saint Luke permitteth: The said Pieter answered that the timber was his own, bought with his own money from the ship-breaker, and that an honest workman was not bound to measure his screws by the rule of the aldermen’s clerks...”*

Van Cloet paused.

The tallow dip sputtered, throwing a small, hot bead of sheep’s grease across the Latin text. He did not scrape it away. He looked up at Katharina, his eyes shadowed by the brim of his linen night-cap.

"He was stubborn," the old man said softly. "The Amman told him twice that if he would give the names of the two journeymen who pulled the sheets, he would be dismissed with a fine of twenty florins. He looked at the floor. He said that if a printer betrayed his pressmen, the ink would turn to poison upon his bread."

Katharina did not pause.

*P - o - i - s - o - n.*

*space.*

*u - p - o - n.*

*space.*

*h - i - s.*

*space.*

*b - r - e - a - d.*

"And the warrant?" she asked. Her voice was level, completely flat, without anger or sorrow. "The warrant was signed by your hand, Van Cloet."

Van Cloet turned the leaf of the register.

On page twenty-four, the Latin script ceased to be the neat cursive of the clerk; it became a series of short, disjointed notes, written with an ink that had faded to a pale, yellowish-gray:

*“...Adhibitis tormentis... The question being applied by the cord and the water... The prisoner remained silent for the space of two hours, calling only upon the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and naming no citizen...”*

The notary laid his finger upon the margin.

"Look," he whispered.

Katharina leaned over the frame.

Beneath the record of the torture, written in a tiny, cramped hand that had been squeezed between the Latin text and the stitch-holes of the binding, were four lines in Dutch:

*“Let the wife have the clothes and the bedding. Let no search be made of the cooper's stave-shed behind the Cammerpoort, forasmuch as the cooper is an ancient burgher of good report and hath no part in this matter.”*

The signature beneath the note was *B. v. C.*

Katharina looked from the faded ink up to the yellow face of the old man.

"You wrote that," she said.

"I wrote it on the night he died," Van Cloet said. He did not look at her; he looked at his own swollen fingers on the table. "Van Valckenisse wanted the house. He wanted the press broken in the street so that the neighborhood would see the King’s justice and be afraid. But he did not care about the cellar. I knew Pieter had a second press. Every master who had his letters from Plantin kept a half-press in his cellar against the bad months. If I had written the stave-shed into the warrant, Katharina, the Provost’s archers would have found you and the boy before the morning watch was called."

"Why?" she asked.

The old man was silent for the space of ten breaths.

The wind from the sea sighed through the garden willows outside, and from the haven came the hollow, metallic strike of the three-o'clock bell from the guard-hoy.

"Because Pieter was a craftsman," Van Cloet said at last, his voice dropping so low that the sound was lost in the rustle of the paper. "I was a notary for thirty years, mistress. I have written five thousand deeds, two hundred wills, and forty sentences of the cord. Most men scream like hogs when the iron touches them; they name their brothers, their mothers, their priests, anything to have the cord loosened by the width of a finger. Pieter did not scream. When the turnkey threw the vinegar into his face, he looked at me through his swollen eyes and said: *‘The paper will outlive the King, Master Balthasar.’* I went home to my house on the Meir, and I looked at my three hundred volumes of civil law, and I saw that they were only dead sheepskin."

He picked up his goose-quill once more.

"Turn the leaf," he said. "We have twenty pages of the accounts to set before the sun touches the abbey spire."

Katharina took the composing rule.

She lifted the finished galley of lead, carried it to the press, and slid the type into the royal chase.

Lucas was already at the second case, his small hands flying among the italic boxes, his face flushed with the pride of a boy who was setting his father’s monument into metal that would never perish.

*“The paper will outlive the King.”*

She tightened the quoins.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

The iron frame rang clear as a church bell in the dawn.

### Chapter 24

The morning broke over the island of Walcheren with a sky of pale, washed turquoise, clear and brilliant after the three days of gale.

At six o'clock, the haven of Middelburg was awake.

The tide was at the full, lapping against the green oak piles of the quays with a crisp, slapping sound that carried the smell of salt and cold mud into every alley of the town.

Along the wharves, sixty ships were preparing for sea.

The English transports were hoisting their yards, their crews singing wild, rhythmic chanties as they hauled the anchor-cables home; the Dutch flyboats were running out their guns to salute the Admiral’s ketch; and at the Blue Tower, where the deep-water ships lay, the English packet *The Elizabeth* of Rye was loosing her topsails to catch the morning land-breeze.

Katharina walked down the Lange Delft.

She did not walk alone.

Beside her walked Lucas, carrying under his arm a flat, square package wrapped in clean, dry sail-cloth, tied with blue linen tape.

The package contained forty copies of the *Acta et Confessiones*.

The ink was dry; the paper was stiff and crisp, smelling of fresh Dutch rag and the clean vine-black from Troyes. On the title-page, beneath the names of the seventy-four citizens of Antwerp, ran the imprint that would be read that week in London, in The Hague, and in the secret assemblies of the churches across the drowned lands:

*PRINTED AT MIDDELBURG, BY THE DIRECTION OF BALTHASAR VAN CLOET, LATELY COMMISSIONER OF ANTWERP, AT THE PRESS OF THE COLD TYMPAN. 1588.*

Behind them walked Master Claes and his nephew Gielis.

The young skipper carried an iron-shod boat-hook across his shoulder; the cooper wore his Sunday cloak of brown Ghent cloth, his face clean-shaven, his eye sharp as he looked at the foreign sailors who lounged before the tavern doors.

Between them walked Van Cloet.

The former magistrate was transformed.

He wore an old, but clean, doublet of black English wool that Mother Betje had altered for him with her shears; his linen night-cap was gone, replaced by a decent felt hat with a broad brim that shaded his pale, yellow face; his swollen feet were enclosed in a pair of stout leather shoes with brass buckles that Gielis had bought for him from a sea-tailor by the crane.

He walked with an ash-stick, his steps slow and painful, but his head was held erect.

He did not look like a fugitive who had been hunted through the salt-ditches; he looked like an ambassador from a ruined country, carrying the record of his people’s death to the only court that remained open to receive it.

They reached the wharf where *The Elizabeth* lay.

The master of the packet was an Englishman named Captain Thomas, a thick-set man with a gray beard and a face like a weathered salt-block, who stood by the gangplank with his register in his hand.

The captain looked at Van Cloet.

He looked at the black wool doublet, at the beaver hat, and at the seal-ring with the three water-lilies that the notary had taken from his pocket and placed once more upon his thumb.

"Master Van Cloet?" the captain asked in his blunt, nautical French.

"I am he," Van Cloet replied.

"I have an order from Sir Francis Walsingham," the captain said, tapping the paper on his clipboard. "A cabin in the steerage, two pots of beer a day, and a safe-conduct from the Lord Mayor of London from the Tower stairs to the court at Greenwich."

He looked at Katharina.

"Are these the papers?"

Lucas stepped forward.

The boy did not wait for his mother to speak. He laid the package of printed sheets into the captain’s great, hairy hands with the grave, deliberate dignity of an apprentice delivering a finished work to the dean of his guild.

"Forty copies, master," Lucas said in his neat, schoolboy Dutch. "The register is true to the letter. There is not a turned letter in the text, and the margins are an inch and a half on every leaf."

The English captain looked down at the boy.

A slow, broad grin spread across his leathery cheeks, wrinkling the gray hair around his ears. He tapped the boy’s cheek with his thick forefinger.

"You have your father’s eye, lad," he said. He tucked the package beneath his arm, turned to Van Cloet, and pointed toward the gangplank. "Aboard, master. The wind will not wait for the King of Spain, and we must clear the shoals of Walcheren before the ebb begins."

Van Cloet did not move immediately.

He turned back to the wharf.

He looked at old Master Claes, who nodded once, a short, stiff jerk of his gray chin. He looked at Gielis, who touched the brim of his red wool bonnet with his thumb.

Then he looked at Katharina.

He reached into his breast, drew out the ivory-handled scraper—the little tool that had erased fifty false words from the warrants of the Steen—and held it out to her.

"Take it," he said.

Katharina did not reach for the ivory.

"I have no false words to scrape, Master Balthasar," she said.

The old man looked at the little tool in his hand.

A strange, quiet smile touched the corners of his thin, white beard—the smile of a man who had reached the end of a long, dark corridor and found that the door at the far end opened into the sunlight.

He turned toward the edge of the wharf.

With a sudden, quick flick of his wrist, he threw the ivory scraper out over the water.

The tool turned once in the bright morning air, its steel point catching the sun like a needle of fire, and fell into the green, rushing depth of the Scheldt.

*Plop.*

The water closed over the ivory. A circle of white bubbles rose to the surface, drifted three yards toward the sea, and was swallowed by the tide.

Van Cloet stepped onto the gangplank.

He did not look back. He climbed to the deck of the packet, his black cloak billowing in the west wind, his hand resting upon the carved teak rail as the ship began to swing her bow toward the channel.

The mooring-lines were cast off.

*The Elizabeth* slipped away from the wharf, her white sails swelling to the breeze, her wake trailing behind her like a broad ribbon of green glass across the yellow waters of the haven.

Katharina stood upon the stone quay, holding Lucas’s hand in hers.

The sun was high now, pouring down upon the red roofs of Middelburg, upon the great spire of the Abbey, upon the sixty ships that were standing out to sea under their white banners of freedom.

"Mother," Lucas said, looking up into her face.

"Yes, heart?"

"The English wool-bills," the boy said, his eyes bright in the morning light. "We have two hundred sheets to pull before the noon-meal."

Katharina looked down at him.

She felt the cool wind off the sea lift the lawn of her widow’s cap; she felt the warm, living blood pulse in the small hand that held her fingers; she heard the great bronze bells of the town begin to ring for the third hour.

"Come," she said.

She turned her back upon the water and walked toward the *Hoop*, where the press stood waiting in the light.

### Chapter 25

The autumn of 1589 was the golden autumn of the province of Zeeland.

The great Spanish fleet was gone, its bones rotting on the black rocks of the Hebrides and the wild coast of Ireland; the Duke of Parma had drawn his tired regiments back from the Flemish coast into the inland towns; and the Scheldt, though still closed by the Dutch forts to the trade of Antwerp, was white with the sails of eight hundred lighters carrying the wool, the corn, and the books of the northern republic to the markets of the world.

In the shop of the *Hoop*, behind the Fish Market, the light fell through four new windows of clear French glass that Master Claes had set into the brickwork with his own hands.

The floor was no longer red tiles covered with ash-shavings; it was paved with broad slabs of blue stone from Namur, scrubbed every Saturday with vinegar and dry sand until you could eat your bread from the floor without a plate.

In the center of the room stood two presses.

The old Gouda press sat on the south side, its oak cheeks dark and smooth with three generations of oil, working off the town receipts and the bills of lading for the haven.

On the north side, where the morning sun was brightest, stood the new press.

It had been built by the carpenters of the Dutch Admiralty from four beams of seasoned English elm, its screw turned by Roeland the smith—who had slipped across the river from Antwerp six months before with his tools in a beer-barrel—its platen ground from a slab of Swedish iron that was true to the thickness of a silk thread.

Above the door, swinging from a new bracket of forged iron that was shaped like two open hands holding an olive branch, hung the new sign:

*IN THE SIGN OF THE COLD TYMPAN.*

*LUCAS HUYSMAN AND SISTER, TYPOGRAPHERS.*

Lucas stood at the frame.

He was eleven years old now, tall for his years, his face bearing the quiet, thoughtful gravity of his father, his sleeves rolled up to show forearms that had been hardened by two hundred thousand pulls of the ash-wood bar.

He was setting the title-page of a great work:

*HISTORIA ET ACTA MARTYRUM BELGICORUM...*

*The History and Acts of the Martyrs of the Netherlands, gathered from the authentic registers of the towns, with the warrants, sentences, and testimonies of the faithful.*

It was an octavo of eight hundred pages, printed on fine Dutch rag paper from the mills of Zaan, set in Robert Granjon’s pica, with the marginal notes in the delicate italic that Pieter Huysman had carried out of the ruined city in the bladders of salt.

Clara stood at the second case.

She was seven, her hair braided into two neat coils beneath her Zeeland cap, her fingers setting the small brevier type of the index with a swift, laughing ease that made the lead dance against the brass rule like water over pebbles.

"Lucas," she said, holding up a letter between her two fingers.

"What is it, Clara?"

"The *M*," she said. "The capital *M* with the broken left shoulder."

The boy stopped his composing stick.

He took the lead from his sister’s hand. He brought it close to his eyes, his thumb feeling the tiny notch where the graver had slipped twenty years before in the workshop of Hendrik van den Keere.

"Where did you find it?" he asked.

"In the bottom of the old beech box," she said. "Under the sawdust."

Lucas walked to the new press.

He did not set the broken letter into the title-page.

He carried it to the high oak lectern that stood beside the window, where the great pigskin register of the shop lay open to the day.

On the first leaf of the book, beneath the date of their entry into Middelburg, sat the single black impression that they had pulled on the morning after the flight:

*A.*

Beneath the capital *A*, Lucas laid the broken *M*.

Beside it, upon the white vellum, lay the single silver daalder that remained of the seventy-five coins Balthasar van Cloet had paid for the children’s winter—the coin with the head of the old Emperor, worn smooth by thirty years of human thumbs, its silver shining in the autumn sun like a piece of the sea.

Katharina stood by the inking stone.

She did not wear her widow’s coif today; she wore a simple cap of white lawn that left her hair free, her gown of clean gray wool caught at the waist by a silver clasp that the elders of the guild had given her on the feast of Saint Luke.

Her hands were still stained with the ink—the indelible, honest black that no water could ever wash from the skin of those who served the letters.

She walked to the window.

Outside in the lane, the cooper’s apprentices were rolling a great tun of Rhine wine toward the haven, their voices rising in a cheerful, roaring chorus that mingled with the screaming of the gulls and the deep, bronze chiming of the Abbey bells.

Beyond the town gables, across the green marshes of the island, the great reach of the Scheldt ran blue and wide toward the sea, carrying forty ships out into the west under their swelling sails.

The river was free.

The press was standing.

The children were fed.

Katharina laid her hand upon the oak cheek of the new press, felt the deep, steady pulse of the timber beneath her palm, and turned back to the stone to ink the forme for the morning run.
### Chapter 26

The paper did not come from Troyes that autumn.

In France, the King, Henry of Valois, had been stabbed to death in his camp at Saint-Cloud by a Jacobin friar with a butcher's knife, and the crown had fallen to Henry of Navarre—the Huguenot prince whose white plume had danced through forty battles of the religious wars. The roads from Champagne to the sea were stopped by the cavalry of the Catholic League; the great paper-mills along the Seine and the Marne stood idle, their water-wheels choked with weeds, their vat-men conscripted to drag the culverins through the mud of Normandy.

In the haven of Middelburg, the price of a ream of royal crown-paper jumped from thirty-two stuivers to three silver florins in a single week.

Katharina stood on the stone quay by the crane, her hands tucked inside her wool sleeves against the raw salt drizzle that was blowing off the East Scheldt.

Beside her stood Master Aernout van Geluwe, his single gray eye peering down into the open hold of a ketch that had just come through the channels from Dordrecht.

The hold was empty of French linen.

In its place lay four hundred bundles of a strange, rough paper, tied with willow-twigs, its color not the creamy white of Troyes, but a pale, greenish-gray like the belly of a pike. It smelled of river-silt, peat-smoke, and boiled hemp.

"It is from the new mill at Alkmaar," old Aernout said, spitting into the yellow foam that slapped against the dock-timbers. "A windmill. A miller from the polders who had two sawmills on the Zaan set up eight wooden stampers to crush old sails and fisherman's nets. He has no lye from beech-ashes; he washes the pulp with salt-water and vinegar from the beer-dregs."

He reached down, took a sheet from an open bundle, and held it up to the gray sky.

The paper was thick, stiff as sheepskin, with little specks of uncarded hemp-cord showing through the grain like brown freckles. The watermark was not the anchor of Genoa or the three lilies of France; it was a rough, wooden windmill with four sails, set between the two letters *Z* and *M*.

*Zaan-Molen.*

"It is like cardboard," Katharina said, feeling the rough texture between her thumb and forefinger. "If you strike that with Granjon's pica, the serifs will catch in the knots and the ink will pool around the ascenders like puddle-water."

"Then you must boil the oil longer, mistress," the old dean muttered. "You must double the rosin and grind the soot until your shoulders crack. Henry of Navarre will not have his peace this year, nor next. If we wait for the French water-wheels to turn again, the presses of Holland will rot on their ground-sills, and the English books will be printed in London on German refuse."

He looked at her through his horn spectacles.

"The English minister, Master Cartwright, is at the Golden Compass inn," he said. "He has two hundred pages of a commentary on the Gospel of Saint Luke, written in the English tongue. The bishops in London have burned his first book at Paul's Cross, and the Queen's pursuivants are searching the ships at Gravesend for his person. He has fifty gold Jacobus sovereigns in his pouch, and he wants twelve hundred copies in small octavo before the winter freezes the canal to Flushing."

"Twelve hundred," Katharina said.

"It will take sixty reams of this green hemp," Aernout said, tapping the bundle with his ash-stick. "Can the boy set the English without a tutor?"

Katharina looked across the haven toward the high brick gables of the *Kromme Weele*.

Through the rain, the thin plume of blue peat-smoke from the chimney of the *Hoop* was rising straight into the salt air—clean, sweet, fueled by the turf she had bought with honest earnings.

"Lucas knows the English words," she said. "He knows *sin*, and *righteousness*, and *judgment*. The English words have fewer letters than the Dutch, but they have more *w*s than the case can hold. I must cast twenty pounds of the double-*w* from the Mechelen matrices before we can set the fifth chapter."

"Then buy the hemp," the old man said. "The English sovereigns are full weight. Thirty-eight shillings to the piece on the exchange. With fifty Jacobus pieces, Katharina, you can buy the timber for a third press and send the girl to the French school at Saint Peter's."

Katharina nodded once.

She turned to the skipper of the ketch, drew her leather purse from her apron, and laid twelve silver florins upon the damp wood of the hatch-coaming.

The work was expanding.

It was no longer the desperate, hunted craft of the cellar, where thirty lines of lead were set by stealth between the patrols of the Amman; it was the steady, merciless industry of the North, where the great river of words flowed out to supply the hunger of an entire continent in arms.

### Chapter 27

The boy who brought the iron bodkin to the *Hoop* in the second week of November was named Maeyken.

He was seventeen years old, but his body was stunted, his left shoulder drawn up two inches higher than his right by five years of carrying water-buckets up the stone stairs of the Steen, his feet deformed by the cold damp of the dungeon floors. He had no shoes; his ankles were wrapped in strips of dirty sail-cloth tied with osier-twigs, and across his forehead ran a pale, hairless scar where a turnkey's iron bunch of keys had split the flesh to the bone.

He did not come to the front door of the shop.

He sat on the mounting-block by the cooper's stave-shed, his head resting against the wet brick, shivering in a thin doublet of gray hemp that had lost every button, watching the press through the open window with the hollow, ravenous eyes of a dog outside a butcher's stall.

Lucas saw him first.

The boy had come out to empty the lye-tub into the kennel. He stopped with the wooden bucket in his hands, looking at the ragged figure in the rain.

"Who are you, lad?" Lucas asked in the broad, easy speech of the haven.

The boy did not answer immediately. His tongue worked in his dry mouth, his yellow teeth clicking together with the ague.

"Is... is this the house of Pieter Huysman?" he whispered.

Lucas set the bucket down upon the cobbles. His hand went instinctively to the composing rule in his pocket, his small body tensing with the old, inherited caution of the Cammerpoort.

"My father was Pieter Huysman," Lucas said. "He has been in the pit at Antwerp these three years. Who sent you from the river?"

The ragged boy reached into the lining of his torn doublet.

His fingers were blue with the cold, the nails broken and festering around the quicks. He drew out a small object wrapped in a piece of oiled sheep-bladder, greasy and black with old coal-dust.

He did not give it to Lucas.

"I promised the master," the boy whispered, his eyes filling with tears that ran down the dirt of his hollow cheeks. "In the lower pit. Under the water-gate. He gave me his bread for five days when the fever took me. He said: *‘If you ever cross the drowned lands to the North, Maeyken, find the woman who has the mark of the composing stick on her thumb. Give her the iron, and tell her that the letters did not break.’*"

Katharina came to the doorway.

She had heard the voice from the stone. She wiped her hands upon her apron—the rough linen apron that smelled of fresh vine-oil and turpentine—and stepped out into the November mist.

She looked at the boy.

She looked at the stunted shoulder, at the scar across the brow, at the trembling hand that held the yellow bladder.

"Maeyken," she said softly.

The boy looked up at her.

"You were the child who carried the small beer," she said. Her voice was not louder than the whisper of the rain in the willows. "Pieter wrote of you in the margin of his grammar. He said there was a turnkey's boy named Maeyken who had the dropsy in his feet, who brought him two clean quills from the clerk's desk in the night."

The boy sank to his knees upon the wet cobbles of the lane.

He held up the little package with both hands, his head bowed until his forehead touched the stone threshold of the shop.

Katharina took the bladder.

She cut the tarred string with her thumbnail.

Inside lay an iron punch.

It was three inches of forged steel, half an inch square, its shank dark with the brown rust of the dungeon air. On the end of the steel, cut into the hard metal with a diamond graver twenty years before, stood the face of a letter:

The capital *M*.

Not a common type cast from lead and antimony, which could be melted down in a kitchen pipkin or battered flat with a cobbler's hammer.

This was the punch—the father of the letter.

The steel master-die that Hendrik van den Keere had hardened in goat's milk and charcoal fire, from which five hundred copper matrices could be struck, and from those matrices five hundred thousand letters of lead could be poured.

On the left shoulder of the *M*, tiny as the eye of an ant, was the notch where the master's hand had slipped.

Pieter had hidden it in the sole of his shoe when the archers entered the shop in the Cammerpoort. He had carried it through the examinations, through the cord, through the seven weeks of the Steen; and when the cold had reached his heart, he had pressed it into the hands of a scullion-boy whose life he had bought with five crusts of black rye-bread.

Katharina held the steel punch in the palm of her hand.

The metal was cold—colder than the rain, colder than the stones of Middelburg—but as her fingers closed over the shank, she felt the great, unyielding hardness of the steel against her bones.

They had broken the walnut press.

They had melted the six thousand weight of lead in the cannon-foundry at the South Gate.

They had buried Pieter in the quicklime outside the walls, where the grass would not grow for twenty years.

*And the steel had come across the river.*

She reached down with her left hand, caught the ragged boy by the collar of his doublet, and lifted him from the stones.

"Come inside, Maeyken," she said, her voice clear and sweet as the bells of the Abbey. "There is hot barley in the pot, and the bed under the paper-loft is dry. Lucas, take the boy's clogs. Clara, bring the stone jug of double-ale from the cellar."

The boy stood upon the red floor-tiles, his bare feet sinking into the clean ash-shavings, looking at the two great presses that stood in the light of the French windows like two black towers of strength.

Katharina walked to the frame.

She laid the steel punch upon the clean vellum of the register, beside the silver daalder and the single printed *A*.

The master had come home.

### Chapter 28

The book grew through the winter of 1589 like an oak tree pushing its roots through frozen clay.

It was no longer twelve leaves of octavo, printed in haste by the light of a single tallow dip while the patrol passed in the alley; it was the great *Martyrology* of the southern towns, a monument in two folio volumes that would stand upon the library shelves of Oxford, Geneva, and Heidelberg when the houses of Antwerp were dust.

From every port of the northern sea, the depositions arrived.

Ministers who had fled from Valenciennes brought the lists of the forty weavers hanged in the market-place; merchants from Tournai brought the account-books of the confiscated cloth-halls; widows from Bruges brought the letters their husbands had written with iron-gall ink upon strips of linen torn from their shirts on the night before the fire.

Maeyken worked at the ink-block.

The boy's left shoulder was still crooked, but the good food and the dry air of the cooper's loft had filled his hollow cheeks; his skin had lost its dungeon-yellow, turning to the healthy, weather-beaten brown of the Zeelanders. He worked the great sheepskin balls with a slow, powerful swing of his body, his crooked shoulder giving him a strange, rhythmic leverage that pressed the ink into the grain of the hemp paper with an evenness that no journeyman in the town could match.

Lucas was at the second press.

He was twelve now, his voice breaking into the deep, resonant baritone of a man, his eyes steady behind his brass spectacles. He could cast fifty lines of pica in the hour from the new iron mold that Roeland the smith had made for them; he could plane a forme until the Namur slate rang like a silver basin; and he had begun to cut his own initials in wood—great, bold capitals surrounded by borders of ivy-leaves and corn-ears that were cleaner than anything coming out of the shops of Delft.

Clara sat by the great window.

She had her own composing frame now, built low to the floor from seasoned ash-wood, where she set the indices and the tables of errata.

She was eight years old, her fingers so light and swift that they looked like two white butterflies among the lead subdivisions. She knew every ligature in the Greek alphabet; she knew the four forms of the Hebrew point; and when a proof-sheet was laid before her on the white paper, her sharp eyes would find a turned comma or a double space before the ink was dry.

In January of 1590, the packet from London brought a letter.

It was written on fine English vellum, sealed with the broad seal of the Court of Chancery, and addressed to *Katharina Huysman, Master-Printer at the Sign of the Cold Tympan, Middelburg*:

*“Mistress:*

*These are to certify you that Master Balthasar van Cloet, formerly of the town of Antwerp, departed this life in peace at the parish of Saint Dunstan-in-the-West, in the city of London, on the third day of December last past.*

*Before his death, the said Master Balthasar did deliver into the hands of the Right Honorable Sir Francis Walsingham, Her Majesty’s Principal Secretary, the complete registers and papers of the Council of Troubles, which papers have been presented before the Queen’s Council at Greenwich to the great enlightenment of Her Majesty’s ministers.*

*By his last will and testament, written with his own hand upon the twenty-eighth day of November, the said Master Balthasar hath bequeathed to Lucas Huysman, son of Pieter Huysman of Antwerp, his library of the civil law, now lying in seven chests at the Dutch church of Austin Friars in London, together with sixty gold Jacobus sovereigns to be employed in the purchase of three sets of Greek and Hebrew matrices from the foundry of Frankfurt.*

*Item, to Clara Huysman, his silver signet-ring with the three water-lilies.*

*Item, to Katharina Huysman, the assurance that in the presence of the Almighty God, before whom he was straightway to appear, he did hold her name to be that of an honest workman who had preserved the truth alive when the magistrates had fled.*

*Written by me, Thomas Phelippes, Secretary to the Council.”*

Katharina read the letter to the end.

She did not weep.

She carried the vellum to the high lectern, folded it once, and laid it inside the great register beside the steel punch and the silver coin.

"Mother," Lucas said from the press.

He was holding the ash-wood bar in his hand, his body braced for the pull, the royal chase waiting upon the Namur slate.

"What is it, Lucas?"

"The last sheet of the second volume," the boy said.

His voice was deep, steady, filled with the solemn pride of a craftsman who had completed his master-piece.

"The imprint," he said. "How shall we set the imprint?"

Katharina walked to the frame.

She picked up the brass composing stick that Pieter had filed in the Cammerpoort twelve years before.

She looked at the lead letters that lay in the box—Granjon’s pica, Hendrik van den Keere’s double-canon, the italic that had come through the sleet in the oiled bladders.

She did not set the names of the aldermen.

She did not set the names of the commissioners who had died in the Castle of Vilvoorde, nor the names of the Walloon archers who had drowned in the mud of the Scheldt.

She set four lines only:

*FINISHED AT MIDDELBURG IN ZEELAND,*

*AT THE EXPENSE OF THE TRUTH,*

*BY KATHARINA, WIDOW, AND LUCAS AND CLARA, ORPHANS OF PIETER HUYSMAN.*

*THE TIMBER BREAKS; THE LETTER REMAINS.*

*1590.*

She handed the stick to the boy.

Lucas set the lines into the chase.

He planed the metal with the wooden planer; he drove the quoins home with the shooting-stick until the iron frame was solid as a rock.

Maeyken swung the sheepskin balls.

*Pat-pat-pat, roll-roll-roll.*

The leather kissed the lead. The black letters shone in the winter light.

Clara laid the damp sheet of green Alkmaar hemp upon the tympan, her fingers true to the brass pins.

Katharina stepped onto the platform.

She laid her hand upon the ash-wood bar beside the hand of her son.

Together, the widow and the boy leaned their weight against the wood.

*Pull.*

The great iron screw turned in its brass nut.

The Swedish platen came down upon the paper with the smooth, monstrous, irreversible power of an art that had conquered the King, the winter, and the grave.

*Clack.*

The bar hit the stop.

Outside in the lane, the bells of the Abbey of Saint Norbert began to strike the twelve hours of noon, their bronze notes rolling out over the red roofs of the town, over the green dikes of Walcheren, over the gray, unconquered waters of the North Sea, toward the day that had no night.

Katharina lifted the tympan.

The paper came away from the lead with that clean, tearing hiss that is the music of the press.

The sheet was white.

The black was absolute.

The press was standing in the light.
### Chapter 29

The seven chests of Austin Friars came down the gangplank of the Harwich ketch in the first week of April 1590, when the marsh-marigolds were opening in the ditches of the abbey gardens and the storks were clattering their beaks upon the brick chimneys of the Lange Delft.

They were great chests of English oak, bound with straps of three-inch hoop-iron, their corners capped with heavy shoes of beaten copper that had turned green from the salt air of the Thames.

Each chest was sealed with three cakes of vermilion wax bearing the stamp of the Dutch church in London—the open book with the seven seals—and labeled in the neat secretary hand of Master Thomas Phelippes:

*FOR THE HOUSE OF THE COLD TYMPAN, AT MIDDELBURG IN ZEELAND.*

It took six porters from the Fish-Packers’ Guild, with three pairs of stout ash-poles and hemp carrying-slings, to heave the timber across the cobbles of the haven to the shop behind the Fish Market.

Katharina did not open the chests in the street.

She had the doors of the *Hoop* barred with their oak drop-bolts; she had the shutters drawn across the French windows so that the curious apprentices from the cooper’s stave-shed could not peer through the leaded glass.

Lucas stood with a crowbar of charcoal-iron that Roeland the smith had forged from a Spanish gun-carriage.

The boy—nearly thirteen now, his shoulders as wide as a young drayman's, his hands hard and brown from the lye—set the claw of the bar beneath the iron strap of the first chest.

*Creak. Crack.*

The seasoned English oak groaned. The forged nails drew from the wood with a dry, screeching shudder, shedding flakes of red rust upon the Namur floor-slabs.

He threw back the lid.

The smell that came from the chest was not the sour brine of the river-lighters; it was the ancient, dry perfume of calfskin, sheepskin parchment, dried rue, and the aromatic dust of old Venice paper that had been kept in cedar-lined presses for fifty years.

Clara knelt upon the stones.

Her eight-year-old hands plunged into the clean oat-chaff that had been packed around the books to keep them from the sea-damp. She drew out the first volume:

A great folio of seven hundred pages, bound in dark brown calf over wooden boards, stamped on the covers with the gilded arms of the University of Louvain:

*CORPUS JURIS CIVILIS, CUM GLOSSIS ACCURSII.*

*Paris, 1548. Ex officina Roberti Stephani.*

She opened the cover.

On the pastedown, written in an ink that had faded to the color of dry tobacco, was the inscription in a sharp, beautiful notary’s hand:

*Ex libris Balthasaris van Cloet, I.C. Antverpiensis. Emptus quatuor florenis, anno 1558.*

*Bought for four florins, in the year 1558.*

Beneath the signature, added in the same hand thirty-one years later, in the parish of Saint Dunstan in London, were three lines written with a trembling pen:

*“To Lucas Huysman, who knoweth how to press the law into metal that does not lie. Let him read the Digest touching the recovery of stolen goods, and remember that an honest craftsman is the only true magistrate.”*

Lucas took the book from his sister's hands.

He did not turn the leaves; he held the heavy volume against his chest, his thumb feeling the raised bands of the spine—five stout cords of hemp, sewn with waxed linen thread, as firm and true as the day Robert Estienne’s binder had pulled them taut across the sewing-frame.

"They did not burn them," the boy whispered.

Katharina stood over the chest.

She reached her hand down into the oat-chaff, past the folios of the canon law, past the great commentaries of Bartolus and Baldus, until her fingers touched something small, heavy, and cold that lay in the bottom corner of the timber.

It was an iron box, six inches square, fastened with a brass padlock that had been wrapped in an oiled leather rag.

Beside the padlock lay a small key of polished steel.

She turned the key.

The lock clicked back with the smooth, oily whisper of a clockwork mechanism.

Inside the iron lay three small wooden caskets of walnut-wood, each no larger than a prayer-book, and a heavy linen bag that gave forth the dull, dead clink of thick gold.

She lifted the lid of the first walnut casket.

The sunlight, filtering through the heart-shaped hole in the window-shutter, fell across sixty small bars of polished copper, an inch long and a quarter of an inch square, packed in rows between strips of green baize.

On the end of each copper bar, stamped into the metal by a steel punch with a pressure of three tons, sat the strike:

A matrix.

The Greek alphabet.

Not the rude, clumsy Greek of the German foundries, which looked like marks made in wet clay with a stick; but the magnificent *Grecs du Roi*, cut for King Francis the First by Claude Garamond—the flowing, cursive script with its forty-two ligatures, its breathing-marks like delicate bird-wings, its capital letters that stood like the columns of an Athenian temple.

Lucas caught his breath.

His fingers hovered over the copper bars, trembling, not daring to touch the pristine strikes that had never known the molten lead.

"Garamond's Greek," he breathed.

"The second casket," Clara said, her voice high and breathless.

She opened it herself.

The Hebrew matrices.

Thirty-six letters and vowel-points, cut from copper that had been burnished until it shone like red gold, carrying the square, majestic characters that Daniel Bomberg had used for his great Venetian Bible—letters that had been forbidden in Antwerp since the Duke of Alba had quartered his horsemen in the printers' lofts.

And in the third casket:

The matrices of Robert Granjon’s *Philosophie Italic*, complete with the double-vowels, the swash-capitals, and the long *s* that curled like a willow-bough over the vowels.

Beside the caskets, the linen bag held sixty gold Jacobus pieces: thick, heavy disks of twenty-two-carat gold, bearing the profile of the Scottish king who was now the heir to the English throne, their rims sharp, their surfaces bearing the royal motto that had survived the Armada:

*FACIAM EOS IN GENTEM UNAM.*

*I will make them one nation.*

Katharina closed the iron box.

She sat upon the edge of the open chest, her hands resting upon the dark calfskin of the Roman law.

The shop was silent, save for the rhythmic, distant *chunk-chunk* of old Master Claes's adze in the stave-shed next door and the singing of a linnet that Clara had hung in a willow cage by the garden door.

Three years ago, in the frost of the Cammerpoort, twenty-five silver daalders had been the price of bread, peat, and life until the spring thaw.

Now, on the red tiles of Middelburg, sat the library of an alderman, the matrices of three languages, sixty pieces of English gold, and two printing-presses that had their names entered upon the books of the States of Zeeland.

"We must build the foundry, Lucas," she said.

The boy lifted his head from the Greek letters.

His eyes were bright, clear, filled with the vision that had kept his father alive in the cellar of the Steen.

"Roeland has the charcoal from the Kempen," he said. "He has the iron furnace ready in the cellar of the potteries. He says we can pour forty pounds of lead before the bells ring for prime."

### Chapter 30

The casting of the Greek was an art that had never been seen in the province of Zeeland.

In the vaulted cellar beneath the *Hoop*, where Master Claes had once kept his split willow-poles, Roeland the smith had built an iron furnace of Dutch sea-coal, with a chimney of curved earthenware pipes that carried the yellow sulfur-smoke into the main flue of the kitchen chimney.

Over the coals sat an iron melting-pot that held eighty pounds of metal.

It was not common lead.

Common lead was soft; it would mash like butter beneath the pressure of the platen after two hundred pulls. The metal for the Greek letters had to be a cunning alloy, calculated by weight like an apothecary's prescription:

Sixty pounds of clean English lead from the mines of the Peak;

Twenty pounds of fine tin from the stream-works of Cornwall, to make the metal run fluid as milk into the hair-lines of the ligatures;

And eight pounds of black antimony from the mountains of Hungary, to give the face the diamond hardness that would resist the iron screw.

Roeland stood by the furnace, his leather apron stiff with charcoal-dust, his sleeves rolled up to reveal forearms covered with red scars from old lead-spits.

In his left hand he held the casting-mold—an instrument of hardened steel and polished brass, built in two halves that slid together upon iron guides, lined with strips of seasoned wood to protect the founder's palms from the heat.

In the bottom of the mold sat the copper matrix of the Greek letter *Theta*.

Lucas stood beside him.

The boy held the iron ladle—a tiny cup no larger than a walnut, fitted with a narrow pouring-lip and a wooden handle two feet long.

He dipped the ladle into the silvery pool.

The molten alloy was covered with a thin, iridescent scum of yellow dross that smelled of sulfur and heated tin. He skimmed the dross with an iron scraper, drew up a spoonful of the clear, shimmering metal, and held it over the mouth of the mold.

"Now, lad," Roeland grunted. "A quick drop, and toss the mold upward with your wrist as the lead bites. If you do not give it the jerk, the air will stay in the counter, and your *Theta* will have a hole in its belly like a blind eye."

Lucas poured.

*Spat.*

His right wrist snapped upward with a quick, nervous twitch of the muscles.

The steel mold gave a sharp, metallic *clink*.

Roeland touched the spring with his thumb.

The two halves of the mold flew apart upon their hinges.

Out dropped the lead type.

It fell onto the table of green baize, smoking slightly, its shank four-sided and true, its jet—the superfluous sprue of metal at the base—breaking off with a clean, crystalline snap as Roeland touched it with his pliers.

Clara caught it with her brass tweezers.

She brought it to the light of the tallow dip.

The letter stood up from the lead shoulder, sharp, black, and perfect:

*Θ.*

The circle was an unbroken ellipse of hair-line steel; the cross-bar was a delicate needle that did not touch the sides by the width of a spider's web.

"It is sound," she whispered.

"It is sound," Roeland roared, clapping Lucas on the shoulder with a hand that left five black soot-marks upon the boy’s shirt. "We will cast twelve hundred in the day! The ministers in Leiden will have to take their hats off when the boys from Middelburg come down the canal!"

Katharina sat by the cellar stairs, her apron full of the cold jets that she was breaking from the shanks with a small pair of nippers.

The work went on with the rhythmic, relentless speed of a clock:

*Dip. Pour. Toss. Click.*

*Drop.*

*Dip. Pour. Toss. Click.*

*Drop.*

The pile of Greek types grew upon the baize cloth like a heap of silver wheat: the *Alphas*, the *Betas*, the intricate ligatures where the *Sigma* and the *Tau* intertwined like two wrestling vines.

By noon, the case was full.

By dusk, the three thousand letters of the first font were sitting in their wooden galley on the workbench above, planed, rubbed with the pumice-stone to remove the burrs from the shanks, and dressed with the iron dressing-hook until every letter was of the exact height of the royal paper.

Lucas stood before the galley.

He took his brass composing stick.

He set the slide to twenty-two pica ems.

He did not set a decree of the States-General; he did not set a bill of lading for the wool-ships; he did not set an indictment of the Duke of Alba.

He reached his fingers into the new Greek box.

*Click. Click. Click.*

The strange, ancient letters fell into their line:

*Ἐ - ν*

*space.*

*ἀ - ρ - χ - ῇ*

*space.*

*ἦ - ν*

*space.*

*ὁ*

*space.*

*Λ - ό - γ - ο - ς*

*In the beginning was the Word.*

He carried the stick to the press.

He laid it upon the Namur slate, inked the lead with a touch of the fresh vine-black, laid a scrap of clean linen paper over the face, and pressed it down with the flat of his hand.

He lifted the sheet.

The Greek letters stood upon the white rag, dark, graceful, and indestructible, shining in the evening light that was pouring through the open door of the *Hoop*.

Katharina stood behind him, her hands resting upon his shoulders.

"Pieter would have wept to see that, Lucas," she said softly.

The boy did not answer. He laid the paper upon the open lectern, beside the steel punch and the silver coin, and smiled.

### Chapter 31

The trade of the town was changing with the seasons.

In the autumn of 1591, a great ship arrived at the haven from the island of Texel, her sides painted with yellow ochre, her high stern carved with the figure of an old man with a pair of brass dividers in his hand:

*THE MIRROR OF THE SEA.*

She had come from the Baltic, loaded with six hundred lasts of Prussian wheat, twelve hundred bundles of Russian flax, and fifty logs of seasoned Polish oak for the shipbuilders of Veere.

Aboard her was a man named Lucas Janszoon Waghenaer.

He was a pilot of Enkhuizen, sixty years old, with a face like an old piece of salt-beef that had been dried in the rigging of a fishing-smack, his blue eyes small and watery from forty years of looking for sandbanks in the fog of the North Sea.

He wore a pilot’s coat of coarse blue frieze, with horn buttons as big as walnuts, and carried under his arm an enormous roll of heavy parchment, four feet long, tied with a greasy cod-line.

He came into the shop of the *Hoop* on a Tuesday afternoon, when the tide was low and the smell of the mud was strong through the open windows.

He did not look at the books of civil law; he did not look at the Greek matrices; he walked straight to the new press, laid his great, calloused hand upon the elm cheek, and struck the timber three times with his knuckle.

"Good wood," the pilot said in his broad, whistling North Holland dialect. "Seasoned with sea-water. A press that has English elm in its cheeks will not spring when you pull a four-foot map across the slate."

He unrolled the parchment upon the workbench.

Lucas and Clara came to the table to hold down the corners with four iron weights.

It was a chart of the Scheldt channels, drawn with Indian ink and water-colors, showing the soundings from the sandbanks of Westkapelle to the wharves of Antwerp.

Every buoy was marked with a red cross; every beacon had its flag; the shallow flats where the Spanish ketches lay were shaded with gray wash; and in the center of the river, between the drowned lands of Saaftinge and the fort of Lillo, were the soundings in fathoms:

*Four. Three and a half. Five. Six.*

"Can you cut that into copper, boy?" the pilot asked, looking at Lucas over the top of his iron spectacles.

Lucas looked at the lines.

He had never cut copper. He had cut initials in pear-wood and boxwood; he had engraved borders of ivy-leaves for the Latin titles; but copper was a different craft—an art where the graver had to bite into the cold plate with the precision of a surgeon's scalpel, where an error of a hair's breadth would send a fifty-ton ketch upon a hidden sand-reef in the dark.

"I can cut it," Lucas said.

"It must have the soundings in small italic," the pilot said. "The pilots of Zeeland cannot read the German black-letter when the gale is blowing out the binnacle-lamp. It must be clean, open, and large enough to read by the light of a tallow dip in an open cabin."

"I have the italic," the boy said. "Granjon's. The face that Pieter Huysman had from Paris."

The pilot looked at Katharina.

"It will take sixty copper plates," he said. "The States of Holland have granted me a privilege for ten years. Every ketch that sails from the Texel to the Spanish coast must carry the book in her steerage. If you print twelve hundred copies, mistress, you will need forty bales of the royal paper from Alkmaar, and the Admiralty of Zeeland will pay you four hundred gold guilders upon the delivery of the first three quires."

Katharina looked at the chart.

Her eye caught the narrow reach between Doel and the beacon—the place where the Spanish corporal had hailed the hoy in the dawn mist three years ago, where she had held Clara's mouth in the onion-straw while the iron boat-hook scratched the fir-planks of the hull.

The beacon was marked upon the chart:

*The Red Beacon of Doel. Two fathoms at low water. Mud bottom.*

The place of terror had become an entry in a mariner's manual.

The mud where Pieter's pressmen had drowned their letters had become a safe channel, measured by fathoms, guarded by the Prince's guns, open to every ship that carried the flag of the Republic.

"We will take the work, Master Waghenaer," she said.

The pilot drew from his pocket a leather bag of silver daalders.

He did not count them; he set the bag upon the chart with a heavy, satisfying clink.

"For the copper," he said. "Get the plates from the coppersmiths of Dinant who are living in the Pottenbakkerssingel. Tell them Lucas Waghenaer wants the red copper from the Meuse, beaten cold until the grain is tight as an eel-skin."

He turned toward the door, his heavy pilot's boots clicking on the Namur slabs.

At the threshold, he stopped and looked back at the boy.

"Do not spare the oil on the plate, lad," he said. "A chart that has faint lines is a chart that drowns honest men. Make the black bite into the paper until a blind man can feel the channel with his thumb."

### Chapter 32

The copper-work took eighteen months.

Through the winter of 1591 and the long summer of 1592, the sound of the shop changed from the double-beat of the wooden press to the high, dry, rhythmic scraping of the steel burin upon the red metal:

*Scritch. Scritch. Scritch.*

Lucas sat by the north window, where the light was purest, his eye shielded by a green silk shade, a magnifying glass of German crystal mounted on an iron stand before his face.

He worked with three gravers: a lozenge-pointed tool to cut the deep channels for the coast-lines; a square-pointed tool to shade the sandbanks with delicate cross-hatching; and a fine diamond-point to engrave the soundings and the names of the parish churches that served as marks for the pilots.

Clara stood by the polishing bench.

She had learned to prepare the plates with fine charcoal and water, rubbing the copper until the surface was a mirror of dark, red fire in which she could see her own laughing eyes and the white lawn of her cap.

When a plate was engraved, it could not be printed upon the common press.

The wooden screw and the iron platen were made for relief-printing—for type that stood up from the chase and gave up its ink to a gentle kiss of the paper.

A copper plate was intaglio.

The lines were sunk into the metal; the surface had to be wiped clean with the palm of the hand until not a speck of black remained upon the bright copper, while the ink remained trapped in the tiny, microscopic furrows that the graver had cut.

Roeland the smith had built them a rolling-press—a new machine of seasoned ash-wood and polished iron, with two heavy rollers three feet long that turned upon iron axles by means of a great wooden cross with four arms.

The bed was a slab of Swedish iron that slid between the rollers with a slow, monstrous pressure that would crush an oak plank into matchwood.

Katharina worked the press herself.

She would take the copper plate, warm it over a small brazier of clean beech-coals until the metal was lukewarm to the touch, and rub the thick, greasy ink into the lines with a dauber of folded wool.

Then came the delicate work—the work that no boy or apprentice could do, which required the soft, sensitive skin of a woman's hand:

The wiping.

She would wipe the plate first with a rag of coarse canvas, to remove the bulk of the ink; then with a piece of soft calico; and finally with the ball of her bare right thumb, lightly dusted with fine white whiting from the chalk-cliffs of England.

She did it with a swift, skimming touch that left the polished copper as bright as silver, while every line, every sounding, every red cross of a buoy remained black and full of ink.

She laid the damp French rag upon the plate.

Over the paper she laid four blankets of fine wool cloth from the mills of Leiden.

Lucas took the arms of the cross.

He threw his weight into the wood.

*Crank. Creak.*

The iron rollers turned.

The bed slid between the cylinders with a low, deep groan that made the floor-slabs vibrate beneath their feet. The blankets were squeezed into the furrows of the copper with a pressure of eight thousand pounds, sucking the ink out of the metal into the very fibers of the damp rag.

Lucas lifted the blankets.

Katharina took the sheet by the corners.

She lifted it from the copper.

The chart came away with a sharp, crisp *hiss*.

It was magnificent.

The sea was white; the coast-lines were black, clear, and sharp as iron wire; the sandbanks stood out from the water in delicate, golden-brown stippling; and in the corner, surrounded by a border of sea-monsters and tritons holding compasses, ran the title:

*THE MIRROR OF NAVIGATION FOR THE RIVERS AND CHANNELS OF ZEELAND AND BRABANT.*

*Engraved and printed at Middelburg, at the Sign of the Cold Tympan, by Lucas Huysman. 1592.*

Old Aernout van Geluwe came to see the first proof.

The old dean was eighty-four now, his single eye dimmed by cataracts, his hand shaking upon his ash-stick as he leaned over the bench.

He did not use his spectacles.

He brought his face down to within an inch of the paper, his thumb feeling the raised black ridges where the ink had been pulled out of the copper by the rolling-press.

"It has the bite," the old man whispered.

A tear formed in the corner of his single eye, hung for a moment upon his white cheek, and fell upon the border of the triton's tail.

"I have seen the work of Antwerp in its glory," he said. "I have seen the books that Christopher Plantin printed for the King of Spain, with the plates of the brothers Wierix and the borders of Pieter van der Borcht. They were great books, mistress. They were magnificent. But they were printed for priests and courtiers; they were printed with the King's gold, to be locked in the closets of the Escorial."

He looked at Lucas.

"This is printed for the men who hold the tiller," he said. "This is printed for the skipper of the onion-boat, for the lighterman in the reach of Doel, for the boy who must find the channel in the snow-storm when the lights are out. This paper will save five thousand lives upon the salt-banks."

He reached into his pocket and drew out his horn signet-box.

He took out the gold seal of the Guild of Saint Luke—the seal that had been carried out of Ghent in the leather knapsack eight years before.

He did not wait for the meeting of the elders.

He dipped the seal into a dish of red sealing-wax that Clara held for him, and pressed the mark of the open book into the margin of the chart:

*Admissum per seniores artis typographicae.*

"You are a master, Lucas," the old man said, his voice breaking with the ancient pride of the craft. "You need no more years of service. You have cut the sea upon copper, and the sea has acknowledged your hand."

### Chapter 33

In the spring of 1594, Katharina turned thirty-seven years old.

She sat in the small garden behind the *Hoop*, where the peach trees that Master Claes had planted against the brick abbey wall were in their first pink blossom.

The afternoon was warm, filled with the lazy, golden light of the early summer, the air smelling of clean earth, blooming clover, and the salt tide that was running up the canal from Flushing.

Through the open door of the shop came the familiar, triple-music of the house:

The steady, heavy *clack-thump* of the Gouda press, where Maeyken was striking off the town proclamations;

The high, metallic *click-click* of Clara’s composing stick at the Greek frame;

And the deep, resonant rumble of the rolling-press, where Lucas was printing the third edition of the *Mirror of the Sea*.

Katharina had not touched the composing rule that week.

Her hands were still hard, her thumbs still bore the gray, indelible notch of the craft, but she had laid the bodkin aside.

She wore a gown of fine black cloth from Douai—the same cloth that Balthasar van Cloet had worn on the morning he had come down the cellar ladder in the Cammerpoort—with a broad collar of white Flanders lace that Mother Betje had made for her on her lace-pillow.

Around her neck, on a thin chain of English gold, hung the silver signet-ring with the three water-lilies that Van Cloet had bequeathed to her daughter.

A step sounded upon the gravel path.

It was Master Claes.

The cooper was nearly eighty-five now, his back bent like an old willow-tree, his white hair hanging to his shoulders in thin, silky locks; but his knees were sound, and he still carried his two-handled ironwood beetle across his shoulder whenever he walked through the stave-sheds.

He sat down upon the bench beside her, his breath whistling softly in his dry throat.

"The packet from London came in with the noon-tide," he said.

"Did it bring letters?" she asked.

"Two," the old man said, drawing them from his leather pouch. "One from the Dutch church at Austin Friars, with forty gold sovereigns for the second volume of the *Martyrology*. The ministers say the Queen's council has ordered three copies to be placed in the library of the Archbishop of Canterbury at Lambeth."

"And the second?"

The old man looked at her with his small, watery eyes.

"The second is from Antwerp," he said.

Katharina went very still.

The pink blossoms of the peach tree seemed to freeze against the blue sky; the singing of the linnet in the window was suddenly loud, sharp, and painful in her ears.

"From Antwerp," she whispered.

"The young skipper Gielis brought it," Claes said. "He slipped through the lines at Lillo under a load of salt-herring. He said the Duke of Parma is dead in France, and the King of Spain has sent his nephew, the Archduke Ernest of Austria, to be governor of the Netherlands."

He laid the letter into her lap.

It was not written on Dutch hemp paper, nor on the clean linen of Troyes.

It was written on a scrap of coarse, gray wrapping-paper, yellowed with damp, torn from the margin of an old invoice-book. The ink was brown, watery, made from soot and vinegar—the ink of a city that had run out of oil.

The handwriting was that of old Gillis, the paper-merchant of the Hopland:

*“To the Widow of Pieter Huysman, at Middelburg:*

*These are to let you know that the house in the Cammerpoort has been cleared of the rubble by the order of the new Governor.*

*Master Joost van Valckenisse died in the Castle of Vilvoorde in the month of December, having confessed upon his deathbed that he had falsely accused four honest printers to obtain their goods.*

*The bench of aldermen hath declared the memory of Pieter Huysman to be clear of all crime of treason or heresy, and hath ordered his name to be restored to the guild-book of Saint Luke with the mark of honor.*

*If your son desireth to return to this town, the shop at the sign of the Golden Compass is vacant, and the Council will grant him thirty gold florins to re-establish the timber.*

*Gillis, formerly merchant.”*

Katharina read the words twice.

She did not move. She did not speak.

She looked down at her hands—the hands that had broken the lye-ice in the cellar tub, that had swung the twelve-pound ironwood beetle against the elm cheeks, that had held the child's mouth in the onion-straw while the Spanish cutter bumped against the hoy.

Lucas came through the garden door.

He had stripped off his paper-cap; his face was flushed with the heat of the rolling-press, his shirt open at the throat to show the strong, brown column of his neck.

"Mother," he said, holding a freshly pulled sheet in his hands. "The map of the river is finished. Look."

Katharina did not look at the map.

She handed him the scrap of gray paper from Antwerp.

The boy read it standing in the gravel path, the pink peach-blossoms drifting down upon his shoulders in the warm breeze.

His face did not change.

His dark eyes, so like his father's, remained calm, steady, and profound as the reach of the Scheldt at the turn of the ebb.

"Will we go back, Mother?" he asked softly.

Katharina looked around the garden.

She looked at the high brick gables of the *Hoop*, where the two presses were singing their rhythmic, eternal song of labor; she looked at Clara, who was leaning through the window with a proof-sheet in her hand, laughing with Maeyken over some jest of the shop; she looked at old Master Claes, whose hand was resting upon the ironwood mallet that had sheared the pins in the Cammerpoort seven years ago.

"Antwerp is the past, Lucas," she said.

Her voice had no bitterness in it; it was clear, sweet, and final as the sound of an iron bell falling through the sea-fog.

"The King is in Antwerp," she said. "The archers are in Antwerp. The priests are in Antwerp. They will restore your father's name upon a piece of dead sheepskin in the Town Hall, and they will want you to print the Latin masses for the bishop's choir."

She stood up from the bench.

She walked to her son, reached up, and laid her palm against his cheek.

"Our town is here, Lucas," she said, looking out over the roofs toward the great blue reach of the North Sea. "Our town is wherever the paper is clean, wherever the press is standing in the light, and wherever a child can set the truth into metal without looking for the bailiff's horses in the lane."

The boy looked down at the gray scrap of paper in his hand.

He did not tear it.

He folded it once, then twice, with the neat, deliberate fold of a printer, and slipped it into the leather pocket of his apron beside his composing rule.

"We have forty quires of the *Mirror* to pull before vespers, Mother," he said.

"Then go to the press, master," she said with a smile.

Lucas turned and walked back into the shop, his steps firm and proud upon the gravel.

Katharina stood in the garden, listening to the music of the house.

*Clack-thump.*

*Click-click.*

*Creak-rumble.*

The sound was not the sound of one family, nor of one shop; it was the voice of a free nation, speaking to the world through the mouth of the iron screw, through the bite of the lead, through the clean, white linen that would outlive the crowns of Spain and the stone of all its castles.

She looked up at the sky.

The great spire of the Abbey of Saint Norbert stood up against the blue summer air, its bells beginning to chime the four quarters of the hour, rolling out their bronze music over the island, over the river, and over the unconquered sea.

The press was standing.

The children were masters.

The letters were free.

### Chapter 34

The autumn of 1595 brought the great frost once more, but it was not the frost of the Cammerpoort.

It did not find Katharina Huysman huddled in a wet cellar beneath four salt-herring casks, breaking the green lye-ice with the head of a wooden brush while her children coughed in the damp straw.

It found her in the great front parlor of the *Hoop*, where the fire of black Kempen turf glowed red and clear in a fireplace of blue Delft tiles, throwing its warmth across a floor of polished oak planks that were covered with thick wool rugs from the English looms.

On the table of carved walnut sat two silver flagons of Rhine wine, three loaves of white wheaten bread, and a dish of dried figs that a captain from the Levant trade had brought to the shop in exchange for twenty charts of the Mediterranean channels.

Around the table sat the guild of the town.

Old Aernout van Geluwe was gone; he had died in his sleep in the winter of ninety-three, his single eye closing upon a world that had become too large for his ancient spectacles, leaving his house and his three presses to the town hospital.

In the dean’s chair sat Lucas Huysman.

He was eighteen years old.

He wore the black velvet doublet of a master-printer of the province of Zeeland, with a narrow ruff of white pleated lawn and an iron signet-ring upon his thumb—the ring with the two severed hands of Antwerp that Roeland the smith had welded together again, setting between the palms a tiny, golden olive-branch that caught the firelight whenever he moved his hand.

Beside him sat Clara.

She was ten, her yellow hair bound in a net of silver wire, her fingers holding the feather-end of a Dutch goose-quill with which she was entering the receipts of the week into the great pigskin ledger.

Across the hearth sat old Master Claes, his feet wrapped in a warm sheepskin bag, sipping his hot beer with ginger, his head nodding in time with the rhythmic, distant *chunk-thump* of the press that was still working in the undercroft below.

The door from the shop opened.

Roeland the smith came in, his hair brushed, his leather apron replaced by a coat of clean blue cloth, followed by Maeyken, who carried in his arms a square package wrapped in white silk.

They laid the package upon the walnut table before the young dean.

"It is the piece, Lucas," the smith said, his deep voice filling the room like an organ-pipe. "The master's piece. The examiners from The Hague and the elders of Leiden have signed the docket."

Lucas untied the silk ribbons.

He drew out the book:

A single volume in quarto, printed on vellum from the calves of the Betuwe, bound in white pigskin that had been blind-stamped with the arms of the seven provinces:

*THE GOSPEL ACCORDING TO SAINT JOHN.*

*In four tongues: Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Dutch.*

*Set in the types of Claude Garamond, Robert Granjon, Daniel Bomberg, and Hendrik van den Keere.*

*Printed at Middelburg, at the Sign of the Cold Tympan, by Lucas Huysman and Clara Huysman. 1595.*

Lucas opened the book.

The four columns of text stood upon the vellum side by side, straight, true, perfectly justified, the four alphabets harmonizing with each other like four voices in a cathedral choir:

*In the beginning was the Word...*

*Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ Λόγος...*

*In principio erat Verbum...*

*בְּרֵאשִׁית הָיָה הַדָּבָר...*

The margins were three inches wide, clean, white, without a single spot or blemish. The black was the deep, velvety jet of the Champagne vines; the red capitals were the vermilion of native cinnabar, ground in nut-oil until they shone like rubies upon the vellum.

At the bottom of the last page, beneath the printer’s device—the wooden press with the open frisket and the motto *The Timber Breaks; the Letter Remains*—ran the dedication:

*“To the Memory of Pieter Huysman, Typographer of Antwerp, who preserved the punches in the darkness of the Steen;*

*And to Katharina, his Wife, who pulled the bread from the iron screw when the city was destroyed.”*

Lucas took the book in both hands.

He rose from the dean's chair.

He did not show it to the elders of the guild; he did not show it to the smith, nor to the cooper.

He walked around the table to where his mother sat by the hearth.

He knelt upon the wool rug before her, laid the vellum volume upon her knees, and bowed his head until his forehead rested against her hands.

"It is finished, Mother," the boy said.

Katharina looked down at the book.

She did not turn the leaves.

Her thumb, with its hard, gray notch that thirty years of the composing stick had carved into the flesh, reached out and touched the gold olive-branch on the ring that had returned from the mud of the Cammerpoort.

She felt the warmth of the fire upon her face; she felt the steady, regular pulse of the young man’s forehead beneath her fingers; she heard the great bells of the Abbey of Saint Norbert strike the nine hours of the night, their bronze voices carrying across the snow-covered dikes of the island to the open sea.

She looked at Clara, who was smiling at her through her tears; she looked at old Claes, who had raised his wooden mug of beer in the shadow of the chimney; she looked at the dark windows where the northern stars were glittering like diamonds over the free water.

"Pieter is content, Lucas," she said.

Her voice was low, sweet, filled with the vast, untroubled silence that comes after a long storm when the morning tide begins to turn toward the light.

She laid her hands upon the white pigskin of the master’s piece, closed her eyes, and listened to the river of the world run on.

### Chapter 35

In the spring of the year 1600, when the new century was opening its eyes upon the world, a small boy sat upon the three-legged stool in the undercroft of the *Hoop*.

He was six years old, his hair as yellow as corn-straw, his knees bare beneath a small working-apron of brown linen that had been cut down from an older brother’s doublet.

In his left hand he held an iron composing stick with a brass rule.

Beside him stood Clara Huysman, twenty years old now, her face bright and beautiful beneath her Zeeland cap, her hand guiding the child’s small fingers toward the subdivisions of the lower case.

"The *e*, Pieter," she said, her voice laughing and patient in the morning light. "The *e* is in the great central box, right beneath your thumb. It has the little cross-bar, see? If you turn it upside-down, it looks like an old boot."

The little boy reached his fingers into the box.

*Click.*

The tiny lead letter fell against the brass rule.

*e.*

Across the room, at the great elm press that had been built eight years before, Lucas Huysman was working the rounce.

He was twenty-three now, tall, broad-shouldered, an elder of the church and a magistrate of the town, his velvet doublet replaced by his working-shirt, his sleeves rolled up to the shoulders as he swung the carriage beneath the platen with the effortless grace of ten years of daily labor.

Beside him stood Maeyken, his crooked shoulder forgotten, his face brown and healthy as he worked the sheepskin balls on the ink-block:

*Pat-pat-pat, roll-roll-roll.*

The clean vine-black shone upon the leather like jet.

"Damp the sheet, lad," Lucas called to his apprentice.

The paper was laid upon the tympan.

The frisket fell with its soft, leathery slap.

The ash-wood bar came home.

*Pull.*

*Clack.*

The iron screw hit the stop; the counter-weight lifted the platen with its clear, musical chime; the carriage rolled out into the yellow sunlight that was pouring through the garden windows.

Katharina sat by the window in her high wicker chair.

She was forty-three years old.

Her hair was entirely white now, smooth and silver beneath the white lawn of her widow's cap; her face was marked by the fine, gentle lines that time and the sea-winds carve upon the faces of those who have seen the worst that the world can do and have not turned their eyes away.

She did not work.

Her hands lay resting in her lap, folded upon the clean black wool of her apron, holding an old horn spectacle-case that had belonged to Pieter Huysman in the days when they had lived above the shop in the Cammerpoort.

Through the window, she could see the peach trees in the abbey garden.

They were in full bloom, a sea of pink and white blossoms that tossed in the fresh spring breeze off the Scheldt. Beyond the garden walls, the red roofs of Middelburg stretched away toward the haven, where fifty ships were drying their sails in the morning sun, their masts like a forest of peeled fir against the blue horizon.

A sparrow flew down to the window-sill, hopped twice upon the stone, cocked its eye at the white-haired woman in the chair, and chirped—a sharp, cheerful note that was answered by the linnet in its willow cage by the door.

The little boy at the case looked up from his stick.

"Grandmother," he said in his clear, treble voice.

Katharina turned her head.

"Yes, little Pieter?"

The child held up his composing stick toward the light.

He had set three words—the first line he had ever composed with his own fingers, chosen from the great Dutch Bible that lay open upon the lectern:

*D - E*

*space.*

*L - I - C - H - T*

*space.*

*S - C - H - Y - N - T*

*The light shineth.*

The letters were not straight; the second *i* had a thick space where a thin space belonged, and the *t* was leaning slightly toward its neighbor; but the line was held true by the brass rule, and the lead shone in the sun like clean silver.

Katharina looked at the line.

She looked at the boy’s small, ink-stained thumb—the thumb that was already beginning to show the faint, white pressure-mark where the brass was biting into the tender flesh.

She looked at Lucas, who stood by the press-bar, his hand resting upon the timber, his face turned toward his son with the same quiet, proud smile that Pieter had worn when the first sheet of the Latin grammar had come off the pins twelve years ago in the ruined town across the river.

She looked at Clara, whose hands were already reaching for the next quire of paper; at Maeyken, who was humming an old song of the river-barges as he smoothed the sheepskin; at the great oak presses that stood like two silent, unshakeable rocks in the center of the room.

The lye-tub was dry.

The four salt-herring casks had rotted into dust in the cellar of the Cammerpoort.

The warrant of Balthasar van Cloet had been white ash in the fire of the black turf for ten years.

The King of Spain who had ordered the presses smashed had died in his dark palace of the Escorial, his body eaten by worms, his great Armada scattered to the winds of the northern sea.

*And the line was set.*

Katharina leaned her head back against the willow wicker of her chair.

A deep, sweet, infinite peace descended upon her heart—the peace of the workman who has finished the long day’s run, who has cleaned the ink from the stone, who has distributed the letter into its true houses, and who hears the night-watch called through the silent streets while the bread is safe in the chest.

She closed her eyes.

The bells of the Abbey of Saint Norbert began to chime for noon, their bronze notes falling through the spring air like golden drops, filling the room, the garden, and the town with their great, triumphant music.

Beneath the sound of the bells, steady, continuous, and eternal as the pulse of the living earth, the ash-wood bar came home against the oak:

*Pull.*

*Clack.*

*Release.*

*The press stood in the light.*
### Chapter 36

The letter from Leiden arrived in the wet spring of 1601, sealed with the green wax of the University and carried in the leather saddle-bag of the post-rider from Haarlem.

It was addressed not to the dean of the guild, but to *Mistress Katharina Huysman, at the Sign of the Cold Tympan, behind the Fish Market in Middelburg*:

*“Honored Mistress:*

*We have here in our academy Master Carolus Clusius, formerly prefect of the imperial gardens to the Emperor Maximilian at Vienna, who hath brought with him from the Levant and the borders of Hungary certain rare plants, bulbs, and herbs hitherto unknown to the physicians of the West.*

*The said Master Carolus hath composed a work entitled RARIORUM PLANTARUM HISTORIA, containing the descriptions, virtues, and true figures of more than seven hundred strange herbs, including that foreign flower which the Turks call TULIPAN, whose roots he hath planted in the sandy earth of our Hortus.*

*Forasmuch as the printers of Leiden have their hands full with the disputations of the theologians touching the divine decrees, and Christopher Plantin’s heirs at the Golden Compasses in Antwerp are restrained by the Archduke’s censors from printing any work that hath not passed the examination of the Spanish Jesuits:*

*The Curators of the University do earnestly request that your house, which hath shown itself so excellent in the cutting of figures upon copper and the even pulling of large folios, should undertake the impression of this history, to the glory of the Republic and the advancement of natural philosophy.*

*We send with this messenger fifty-four blocks of pear-wood, cut by the hand of Master Pieter van der Borcht, together with sixty reams of royal paper from the mill of the White Cross at Alkmaar.*

*Jan van Hout, Secretary to the University.”*

Katharina stood by the great walnut table in the front parlor, the letter open before her, the green seal resting upon the polished wood.

Beside the letter lay the first of the pear-wood blocks:

A block four inches by six, three-quarters of an inch thick, its surface planed so smooth that it felt like silk beneath the fingers. On the wood, cut with delicate knives and fine gouges across the grain of the pear-tree, stood the figure of the tulip:

The bulb, with its brown, paper-like tunic and its cluster of white root-threads; the two broad, fleshy leaves curling around the stem with their parallel veins; and at the top, opening its six pointed petals to an invisible sun, the great cup of the flower, shaded with minute, microscopic lines that showed the flame-like streaks of red and yellow upon the white ground.

Lucas came into the parlor from the undercroft.

He had heard the post-rider’s horn at the haven gate. He looked at the woodblock, his dark eyes widening as he brought his face close to the pear-wood.

"It is Van der Borcht's work," he said, his voice hushed. "The master who cut the plates for Plantin's great *Herbal* thirty years ago. Look at the root, Mother. He has cut the fibers with a single stroke of the graver, so clean that you can see where the dry skin peels from the flesh."

"It is relief-work," Katharina said. "Wood, not copper. It must be locked into the chase beside the pica, so that the letter and the flower are struck by the platen at a single pull of the bar."

"The height," Lucas said, reaching for his brass calipers.

He measured the thickness of the block.

The pear-wood was three lines lower than the lead type.

"It is cut for the French presses," he said. "The French presses have a thinner tympan. If we lock this into the chase with Granjon's pica, the platen will strike the lead before it touches the wood, and the flower will print with a faint, blind face like a ghost in the grass."

"Then we must underlay the block," Katharina said.

She did not hesitate.

She walked to the small pine chest that stood beneath the window—the chest where she kept the remnants of their long flight from the south.

She lifted the lid.

She reached down past the old linen night-cap that had belonged to Balthasar van Cloet, past the three brass marriage-spoons, until her fingers found a bundle of small pasteboard cards, tied with blue linen tape.

They were the pasteboard strips she had cut from the ledger-cover in the cellar of the Cammerpoort seven years ago—the cards that had separated the cases of lead in the willow basket when they had carried the type across the frozen cobbles of Antwerp under the noses of the Amman's archers.

The pasteboard was old, yellowed at the edges by the salt-damp of the Scheldt, but it was three-ply rag, even, hard as horn, compressed by forty years of pressure in the chancellery files.

She laid a strip upon the table.

She measured it with Lucas's calipers.

It was precisely the thickness of two sheets of royal paper—the exact difference between the French pear-wood and the Dutch lead.

"Cut the pasteboard to the size of the block," she said to her son. "Paste it to the back of the pear-wood with a little flour-paste and vinegar. When the platen comes down, the wood and the metal will meet the tympan at the same hair's breadth, and the tulip will stand out upon the Alkmaar rag as bold and true as if it were growing in the sand-dunes of Katwijk."

Lucas looked at the old pasteboard.

He looked at the dark ink-stains on the edges—stains that had come from the lye-tub where the iron signet-ring had fallen into the sludge.

"The old paper," he whispered.

"The old paper," she said. "The paper that was written to condemn honest men shall lift the flower to the light."

### Chapter 37

The printing of the *Rariorum Plantarum Historia* occupied the house of the *Hoop* for three hundred days.

It was a work that tested every craft of the art: the composition of the Latin descriptions, where five hundred names of herbs had to be set in Granjon’s pica with the Greek synonyms in Garamond’s cursive; the casting of sixty thousand new letters from the Mechelen matrices; the grinding of a special black ink that had no lampblack in it, made entirely from the soot of burned walnut-husks to give the wood-cuts a soft, velvety depth that matched the living sap of the plants.

Clara sat at the great window every morning from prime until tierce, reading the proofs against Master Carolus’s own manuscript.

She was twenty-one now, her fingers sure upon the pen, her knowledge of the tongues so profound that the professors from the Latin school at Franeker sent their young scholars to the shop behind the Fish Market to learn how a Greek diphthong should be pointed in the margin.

One morning in the month of August, when the heat was heavy upon the haven and the smell of the dried stockfish was thick in the lane, a young man stepped through the open door of the *Hoop*.

He was twenty-four or five, tall, slender, with the fair hair and clear blue eyes of the northern provinces, dressed in a scholar’s doublet of dark green wool with cuffs of clean white linen.

Over his shoulder he carried an apothecary’s botanical drum of green japanned tin, strapped with leather, from the lid of which projected the green leaves of a wild sea-holly.

He took off his broad felt hat, bowing low to Clara where she sat at the composing frame.

"Is this the house of the *Cold Tympan*?" he asked.

His voice was clear, musical, carrying the soft, rounded vowels of the province of Utrecht.

Clara looked up from her proof-sheet.

Her eye caught the blue veins on the back of his hand, the long, delicate fingers that were stained around the nails with the yellow juice of celandine and the green of crushed elder-leaves.

"It is the house of the *Cold Tympan*, master," she said.

"I am Dirck van Foreest," the young man said. "I have come from the gardens of Master Clusius at Leiden. The master hath sent me with the descriptions of twelve new species of the sea-starwort and the horned poppy that grow upon the salt-marshes of the island of Walcheren."

He set his tin drum upon the corner of the workbench.

He reached inside, drew out a fresh plant that was wrapped in damp river-moss, and laid it upon the Namur slate:

A flower with five petals of pale violet, its center a cluster of bright orange threads, its fleshy, spoon-shaped leaves beaded with droplets of fresh salt-water that caught the morning sun like pearls.

Clara leaned over the stone.

She smelled the plant—the sharp, pungent scent of salt-earth, iodine, and cold sea-spray that was the breath of the Zeeland flats.

"It is the *Aster Tripolium*," she said softly, reading the Latin name from her memory of the fifth book. "The starwort of the salt-ditches. It opens its eye when the sea runs up the channels at the third quarter, and shuts its petals when the ebb leaves the mud bare."

The young scholar stared at her.

His blue eyes widened with a sudden, delighted wonder that brought a flush of red into his pale cheeks.

"You know the flower, mistress?" he asked.

"I know its face," Clara said, smiling up at him from her low stool. "I have set its name twenty times in Granjon’s italic. I know that it hath five petals in the wild marsh, but when Master Carolus planted the seed in his garden at Leiden, the petals doubled into ten and the violet turned to the color of an old plum."

The young man laughed—a clear, ringing laugh that made the linnet in its willow cage answer with three quick, joyful whistles.

"Master Carolus told me that the printers of Middelburg were scholars," he said. "He told me that there was a woman in the *Kromme Weele* who knew more Greek than the burgomaster of Amsterdam. But he did not tell me that she had the eyes of a falcon and the speech of a garden-flower."

Katharina came through the garden door.

She carried a basket of ripe plums from the abbey trees, their blue skins bloomed with white dust.

She stopped upon the threshold, her gray eyes resting upon the young scholar in his green doublet, then upon Clara, whose cheeks were the color of the peach-blossoms in May.

She did not speak.

She had seen that look twenty-five years before in the dark aisle of Saint Andrew’s church in Antwerp, when Pieter Huysman had dropped his composing rule into her lap during the Mass of the Nativity.

The world was not made of lead alone.

The world was not only the iron screw, the warrants of the dead magistrates, and the bitter lye-tub in the dark.

The world was the seed that fell into the salt-marsh, that pushed its pale green shoot through the mud of the drowned polders, and opened its violet eye to the sun when the tide ran home to the sea.

She set her basket of plums upon the workbench beside the *Aster Tripolium*.

"Welcome to the *Hoop*, Master Dirck," she said, her voice warm and deep as the voice of an old bell. "The tea is hot in the kitchen, and my son hath just pulled the proof of the fourth quire. Come and eat with us, and tell us how the tulips are thriving in the sand of Leiden."

### Chapter 38

In the winter of 1603, the great frost came down once more from the north, locking the haven of Middelburg in three feet of solid ice that stayed unbroken until the feast of Saint Matthew.

The ships lay at their berths like wooden houses upon a lake of blue glass, their rigging white with hoar-frost, their crews sitting around iron fire-baskets on the ice, playing at the game of ninepins with frozen turnips.

In the shop of the *Hoop*, the work did not pause.

The two presses ran from morning until dark, their iron screws turning with that smooth, muffled groan that was the breath of the house.

Around the great fireplace in the front parlor, a new company was gathered.

Dirck van Foreest sat at the side of the hearth, his green coat replaced by the dark wool doublet of a physician of the province of Zeeland, his fingers trimming a reed-pen with an ivory knife.

Beside him sat Clara, her head leaning upon his shoulder, her hand resting upon the wooden cradle that Master Claes had carved from a single block of English elm two summers before.

Inside the cradle lay the child:

A boy of four months, his skin pink and sweet, his yellow hair curling around his small ears like the wool of a new-shorn lamb, sleeping peacefully beneath a quilt of red English frieze.

His name was Pieter.

*Pieter van Foreest.*

He had been baptized in the great church of the Abbey of Saint Norbert on the first Sunday of Advent, his sponsors being Lucas Huysman, Master-Printer, and the old cooper Master Claes—who had been carried to the church in a wooden sedan-chair by four of his apprentices, his two-handed ironwood beetle resting across his knees like a marshal’s baton.

Katharina sat by the window in her high wicker chair.

She held upon her lap the great pigskin register of the shop.

It was nearly full now: sixty leaves of thick Dutch parchment, covered with the entries of fourteen years of labor.

On the last page, written in Lucas’s bold, clean hand, were the titles of the books that had come off their presses in the year that was closing:

*The Plant-History of Master Carolus Clusius, in folio, with five hundred woodcuts;*

*The Maritime Atlas of Master Waghenaer, in royal folio, with sixty copper plates;*

*The New Testament in the Greek and Dutch tongues, for the use of the churches of Zeeland;*

*And twelve thousand bills of lading for the ships of the East India Company, which had that summer sent its first fleet of fourteen great vessels from the Texel to the islands of the spices.*

Katharina turned the leaves back.

Back past the Greek Gospels; back past the Latin *Martyrology*; back past the English bills of lading for the wool-factors; back to the very first leaf, where the single black impression stood alone in the center of the vellum:

*A.*

Beneath the letter lay the single silver daalder with the head of the old Emperor Charles, and beside it, in its little nest of baize, the steel punch of the capital *M* with the broken left shoulder.

She picked up the steel punch.

She felt the cold shank between her fingers—the metal that Pieter had held in his shoe when the iron chains were put upon him in the Cammerpoort.

The steel was not rusted.

Lucas had kept it polished with olive-oil and fine emery-powder; the face of the *M* was as sharp, as clear, and as unyielding as on the day Hendrik van den Keere had taken it from the charcoal furnace thirty-five years before.

She looked at the cradle.

The child stirred in his sleep, his little hand reaching out from beneath the wool quilt, his fingers curling into a tiny, perfect fist that caught the warm light of the peat-fire.

"Clara," Katharina said softly.

The young mother lifted her head. "Yes, Mother?"

"Do you remember the night at the beacon of Doel?"

Clara looked at the child in the cradle, then at the frost on the leaded window-panes.

"I remember the smell of the onions, Mother," she said, her voice dropping into a tender, solemn whisper. "I remember the cold wood of the hoy's ribs against my shins, and the sound of the iron boat-hook scratching the timber over our heads. And I remember your hand upon my mouth."

"I was afraid," Katharina said.

It was the first time she had spoken that word in fourteen years.

The room went very quiet.

The fire of black Kempen turf pulsed with its deep, scarlet glow, throwing the shadow of the great elm press across the blue Delft tiles like an enormous, outstretched arm that covered the mother, the daughter, and the child.

"I was thirty years old," Katharina said, looking at her ink-stained fingers. "I had twenty-five silver daalders in my skirt, two sick children on the straw, and six pounds of iron in my hands. When I looked at the soldiers in the lane, I thought that the world was an iron shoe, and that we were only the beetles that were crushed beneath the heel."

She stood up from her chair.

She walked to the cradle.

She took the steel punch—the master-die of the broken *M*—and laid it gently into the bottom of the elm cradle, right beneath the straw mattress where the child’s feet rested.

"The shoe is broken," she said.

Lucas came through the door from the shop.

He was wearing his velvet doublet, his hair brushed back from his broad forehead, his signet-ring shining on his thumb. In his hands he held a newly bound volume in octavo, covered in clean white vellum with green silk ties:

*AN ACCOUNT AND DEFENSE OF THE FREEDOM OF THE SEAS.*

*By Hugo Grotius, Advocate of Holland.*

*Printed at Middelburg, at the Sign of the Cold Tympan. 1603.*

He laid the book upon the table beside his father’s register.

"The packet sails tomorrow, Mother," he said. "The ice has broken in the middle reach. The Dutch ships are going out to the Indies, and they carry our books in every cabin from the Texel to Java."

Katharina laid her hands upon the shoulders of her two children—the master-printer and the scholar’s wife.

She looked through the clear French glass of the window into the night.

The storm had ceased.

The winter sky above the island of Walcheren was high, vast, brilliant with ten thousand sharp, glittering stars that stood over the sea like the lanterns of an infinite fleet.

From the Abbey of Saint Norbert, the great bronze bells began to ring for the midnight watch, their deep, majestic voices falling through the cold air, carrying their message across the dikes, across the marshes, across the drowned lands of the Scheldt, toward the dawn that was rising over the free world.

Katharina drew her breath—a long, sweet, untroubled breath that filled her lungs with the clean, salt air of the sea.

The lye was clean.

The press was standing.

The letter was home.

### Chapter 39

The testament of Katharina Huysman was written in the month of September in the year of our Lord 1605, being the forty-eighth year of her age, at her house of the *Hoop* in Middelburg.

It was not drawn by a notary of the chancellery; it was not sealed with the red wax of the Amman, nor witnessed by the archers with their carbines.

It was written with her own hand, with a goose-quill dipped in the black vine-ink of her own shop, upon three leaves of royal linen paper from the mill of the White Cross at Alkmaar, and set into type by her grandson Pieter when he had reached his twelfth year:

*“In the Name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.*

*I, Katharina, widow of Pieter Huysman, typographer of Antwerp, being of sound mind and memory, though somewhat stiffened in my joints by the cellar-damp of my youth, do make this my last declaration and testament touching the craft that hath preserved my children alive:*

*First, I commit my soul into the hands of Almighty God, my Creator, who delivered me out of the hand of the hunter and brought my feet into a large place; and my body to the earth in the churchyard of Saint Peter in this town, near to the grave of Master Aernout van Geluwe, desiring that no Latin oration be made over my dust, but only that the boys of the Latin school may sing the twenty-third Psalm in the Dutch tongue, and that twelve poor widows of printers or coopers may each receive a cloak of sound black frieze and two silver florins to buy them peat for their winter hearths.*

*Item, to my son Lucas, I leave the two presses of oak and elm that stand in the undercroft of the Hoop, with the matrices of Claude Garamond, Robert Granjon, and Hendrik van den Keere, charging him upon my blessing that he shall never suffer a line to be struck upon those beds that hath been bought with the money of a tyrant, nor alter the truth of a text for the favor of any magistrate or prince on earth.*

*Let him remember that the timber of a press is only wood, which may be split with an ironwood beetle in an hour; but the letter is cast from antimony and tin, which will outlast the stone of the Town Hall and the crowns of all the kings who have sought to drown the speech of honest men in their own blood.*

*Item, to my daughter Clara, wife of Master Dirck van Foreest, I leave the silver signet-ring with the three water-lilies, which was the property of Balthasar van Cloet, lately commissioner of Antwerp; together with the great register of the secret trials of the Steen, charging her that she shall preserve the vellum clean from moth and mildew, so that the children who come after us may read the names of those who stood faithful in the fire, and know what price was paid for the bread they eat in peace.*

*Item, to the boy Maeyken, who carried the steel punch across the river in the oiled sheep-bladder, I leave twenty gold Jacobus sovereigns and the freedom of the second press, with the right to cast his own letters in the foundry of the potteries and to enter his name as a sworn master upon the book of the Guild of Saint Luke.*

*Item, touching the art and mystery of typography, which I have practiced with my hands for thirty years, I leave these three rules to all who shall come after me in this house:*

*First: Let the paper be sound. A book printed upon bad rag, though it be set in the letters of the King’s printer, is like an house built upon sea-sand; the ink sinketh through the pores, the margins rot with the damp of thirty years, and the wisdom of the author is lost in a yellow blur. Better is forty quires of clean Dutch linen than three hundred bales of German refuse.*

*Second: Let the ink be ground with the stone. There is no true black that can be made in haste with lamp-black from the chimney-flue. The oil must be boiled with the bread-crust until the fat is gone; the vine-charcoal must be ground upon the porphyry until the grit is as fine as flower-dust; and the sheepskin balls must be worked with the palm until the pelt is as soft as the cheek of a child. If the ink is greasy, the paper will weep; if it is dry, the lead will tear.*

*Third: Let the compositor remember that the white space is the light of the page. A page crowded with letters, without room for the margin to breathe, is like an alley in a plague-town, where the houses lean together until the sky is shut out. Keep the measure true; set the spaces with an even thumb; and let the capital letters stand upon their lines like honest burghers walking to the church.*

*Written at Middelburg, this twenty-fourth day of September, 1605.*

*KATHARINA HUYSMAN.”*

### Chapter 40

The Cathedral of Our Lady in Antwerp stood five leagues to the south, beyond the drowned reaches of the Saaftinge and the green earthworks of Fort Lillo.

On clear winter evenings, when the north wind blew the sea-fog from the flats and the frost was sharp upon the marshes, you could see its great stone spire from the high dunes of Westkapelle—a pale, gray needle of Brabant stone rising out of the mist forty miles away, catching the last red ray of the setting sun like an iron spear held up against the twilight.

The river that ran past its feet was empty.

No barges came down from Hainaut with the white flour; no English ketches lay at the quays with their bales of sheep's wool; no Portuguese caravels swung their high, gilded lanterns before the water-gates.

The grass grew three inches high between the cobbles of the Meir; the great Cloth Hall was an empty shell where the owls nested in the carved oak rafters; and in the courtyard of the Town Hall, where the five commissioners had once divided the stolen quires in the back room of the Golden Fleece, an old soldier in a rusted morion sat upon an overturned cannon, mending his breeches with a piece of sail-twine.

Across the water, in the province of Zeeland, the world was awake.

In the haven of Middelburg, two hundred ships were drying their white canvas in the morning sun, their topmasts dressed with the three-colored banners of the Prince, their decks ringing with the shouting of twelve languages.

The great iron crane by the haven turned upon its pivot from prime until vespers, swinging the bales of paper, the chests of Greek matrices, the tuns of Rhine wine, and the barrels of spice into the holds of the ships that were standing out for England, for Muscovy, for the Americas, and for the great islands of the southern ocean.

Behind the Fish Market, in the lane of the *Kromme Weele*, the door of the *Hoop* stood wide to the street.

The smell of the shop came out into the lane—the clean, sharp, magnificent perfume of dried Dutch rag, boiled walnut oil, fresh ink, and the sawdust of seasoned elm.

Through the windows came the music:

*Clack-thump.*

*Pull.*

*Crank.*

*Release.*

The sound that had begun in the dark cellar beneath the cooper's stave-shed, that had been whispered over the vinegar water while the Spanish horses passed in the night, that had been struck from the broken elm timber with the twelve-pound beetle, had become the voice of an empire.

Lucas Huysman stood at the carriage.

His hair was touched with gray at the temples now, his face lined with the honorable furrows of thirty years of the press; but his hands were steady upon the ash-wood bar, his eye true to the register-pins as he laid the damp sheet upon the tympan.

Beside him stood his son Pieter, sixteen years old, tall, strong, his working-apron clean, holding the composing stick with the easy, natural grace of the third generation of the craft.

At the second frame sat little Katharina—Clara’s daughter—seven years old, her yellow hair bound in a white lawn cap, her small fingers sorting the nonpareil spaces with a quick, laughing rhythm that sounded like rain upon the leaded panes.

The boy lifted the newly pulled sheet.

He did not look at the text; he brought the paper to the light, held it up before his father's eyes, and smiled.

The impression was absolute.

The letters stood up from the creamy linen like jet carved upon an altar of ivory, clean, sharp, without a blur, without a turned letter, without a single spot of grease upon the wide, generous margins.

At the bottom of the page, where the printer’s mark stood in the center of the white space, ran the words that had survived the cord, the lime-pit, and the sea:

*THE TIMBER BREAKS;*

*THE LETTER REMAINS.*

Lucas laid his hand upon his son’s shoulder.

Together, the father and the son looked out through the open door into the sunlit street, where the gulls were turning against the deep blue sky and the bells of the free town were ringing for the noon.

The work was done.

The line was true.

The press stood in the light.
### Chapter 41

The binding of the *Book of the Foundations* was undertaken in the winter of 1606, when the town of Middelburg celebrated the twentieth year of its municipal freedom from the Spanish crown.

It was not a book intended for sale at the book-fairs of Frankfurt, nor to be hawked under the cloaks of peddlers along the canals of Flanders.

It was a single, unique codex, commissioned by the Burgomaster and the Council of the town, to be chained to the iron reading-desk in the Great Council Chamber of the Town Hall, beside the charters of the ancient counts of Zeeland and the great treaty of the Union of Utrecht.

Lucas Huysman bound the volume with his own hands.

He had learned the craft of the binder from old Master Dirck of Gouda, who had believed that a book was an house built of leather and hemp, and that if the foundation-cords were not true to the plumb-line, the whole structure would tumble into the gutters within fifty years.

The boards were cut from a plank of heart-of-elm—the very timber that had formed the cheek of the press Pieter had built in the cellar of the Cammerpoort, which Master Claes had kept seasoned under his willow-racks for nineteen years.

The wood was dark, dense as iron, seasoned by twenty winters of salt-air and lye-smoke until no beetle could drive a tooth into its grain.

The sewing was done upon five heavy bands of triple-twisted hemp, soaked in hot mutton-tallow and beeswax.

On these bands, Lucas gathered the original leaves:

The single sheet of Genoa paper with the six lines of Latin, signed with the iron-gall ink of Balthasar van Cloet, bearing the broken red wax seal with the Spanish lion's paw;

The gray wrapping-paper from the Hopland, with old Gillis’s receipt for the two reams of French crown-paper, marked with the three greasy daalders’ rings;

The manuscript of the *Declaration*, written in Van Cloet’s chancery hand, with the blood-smear from the smith’s thumb on page four;

The sixty leaves of the secret register of the Steen, with the names of the seventy-four citizens of Antwerp;

And the final proof-sheet of the *Martyrology*, pulled on the green Alkmaar hemp on the morning the Armada drifted into the northern sea, bearing the imprint: *The timber breaks; the letter remains.*

The covering was a single skin of white calfskin, alum-tawed in the pits of Dinant, supple as silk and tough as parchment.

Lucas did not stamp the leather with gold.

Gold was the metal of courts and emperors, of the men who had paid sixty thousand florins to have their titles stamped upon the missals of Madrid.

He used the cold iron tools that his father had filed from old rasps in the cellar of Antwerp:

A double fillet around the margins, straight, clean, and deep;

At the four corners, four stamps of the open hand, holding between its palms a single wheat-ear;

And in the center of the front board, enclosed within a circle of triple twisted cords, the device of the house:

The wooden screw, the open frisket, and beneath them, cut into the calfskin with an iron gouge that left a sharp, colorless impression upon the white leather:

*MDLXXXVII — MDCVI.*

*From the pit to the light.*

When the book was sewn, pressed, and finished, Katharina stood beside her son in the undercroft.

She reached into her apron pocket and drew out the two brass clasps.

They were not new clasps bought from the brass-founders of the haven; they were the two hinges from the door of the lye-tub in the Cammerpoort—the brass plates that Pieter had salvaged from the broken press in their courtyard nineteen years before.

Lucas riveted the brass to the elm boards with four small pins of soft copper, tapping them home with his little chasing-hammer until the heads sat flush with the metal.

*Click.*

The clasps met their staples with a firm, sharp snap.

The book was shut.

The memory was bound.

The record of what had happened to Pieter Huysman, of the twenty-five silver daalders, of the two sick children on the straw, and of the man who had signed the warrant and died in the parish of Saint Dunstan, was enclosed within five hundredweight of seasoned elm and white calfskin, sealed against the dust, the worm, and the forgetting of men.

### Chapter 42

In the summer of 1608, a company of English strangers arrived at the haven of Middelburg aboard a pink from Hull.

They were not merchants of the Merchant Adventurers, with their velvet hats and their gold chains; they were poor folk from the county of Nottingham, dressed in homespun coats of gray wool, their shoes tied with leather thongs, their women carrying their infants in baskets of unpeeled willow.

They had fled from their villages near Scrooby and Babworth, because the bishops of the English King had threatened to harry them out of the land if they would not conform to the ceremonies of the common prayer.

Their elder was a grave, gentle man of forty years named William Brewster.

He had been a secretary to Sir William Davison, Queen Elizabeth’s ambassador in the Low Countries twenty years before; he had seen the Dutch towns in their struggle; he knew the speech of Zeeland and the ways of the maritime folk.

He came to the house of the *Hoop* on a rainy Saturday in the month of August, when the wind was blowing the salt-spray across the roofs of the *Kromme Weele*.

He did not come to buy Dutch bibles; he came to see the woman who had kept the press in the cellar of the Cammerpoort.

Katharina sat in her wicker chair by the parlor hearth, a cup of warm small beer on the table beside her, her hands resting upon her knees.

The English elder knelt upon one knee upon the wool rug before her, holding his felt hat between his hands, his gray eyes looking into hers with the profound, quiet reverence of a pilgrim who had reached a holy well.

"Mistress Huysman," he said in his slow, careful Dutch, which had the dignified cadence of the court of Greenwich.

"Master Brewster," she replied.

"We are going to Leiden," the Englishman said. "We have seventy souls in our company—weavers, plowmen, and their wives. We have no gold. We have no houses. The King’s officers seized our goods at the haven of Boston, and we have only our two hands and our faith in the promises of God."

He looked at the great press that stood visible through the open parlor door—the new press of English elm and Swedish iron that was shining in the gray light.

"We desire to print," he said. "In the town of Leiden, in the narrow alley called the *Stinksteeg*, behind the church of Saint Peter, we have hired a house with a dry cellar. We would set forth the reasons for our separation from the bishops, and the true order of the churches of the New Testament."

He looked down at his hands—hands that had held the diplomatic dispatches of Walsingham, but were now rough from the oars and the rope-work of the North Sea passage.

"I have the Latin," he said. "I have the Greek. But I do not know the balance of the iron screw. I do not know how the lye is boiled, nor how the leather of the inking balls is preserved against the salt-air. The printers of London say that a man must serve seven years to an acknowledged master before he can justify an octavo line."

Katharina looked at him.

She saw in his gray, patient eyes the same quiet, unshakeable stubbornness that she had seen in Pieter’s eyes on the night they had hidden the elm timber under the salt-turnips in Antwerp.

It was the same lineage.

It did not belong to one town; it did not belong to one language; it was the ancient, secret brotherhood of the men who had rather sleep upon damp straw with an alphabet in their pockets than sit at the tables of kings with an iron collar around their throats.

She turned to Lucas, who was standing by the mantelpiece.

"Lucas," she said.

The master-printer stepped forward.

"Mother?"

"Take the elder down into the undercroft," she said. "Show him the small half-press—the press of Gouda oak that we brought from the hospital nineteen years ago. Give him the two cases of Robert Granjon's pica that were cast from the Mechelen matrices. Give him the iron composing stick that his father filed from the carriage-spring."

She turned back to the English pilgrim.

"Take the timber to Leiden, Master William," she said, her voice clear and sweet as the bells of the Abbey across the lane. "Set it up behind the church of Saint Peter. When the King’s pursuivants come to search your lofts, do not run into the street. Put the lead into the salt-barrels. Put the pasteboard behind the chimney. And remember that if you have only three lines of the truth locked in an iron chase, the whole navy of England cannot batter it out of the minds of honest men."

The Englishman took her hand.

He did not kiss the ring; he brought the back of her calloused, ink-stained thumb to his lips, his tears falling warm upon the gray notch where the brass rule had bitten into the flesh.

"The seed will cross the great ocean, mistress," he whispered.

"Let it cross," Katharina said. "The water is wide, but the paper is light. It will float where the galleons drown."

### Chapter 43

The autumn of 1610 was the quietest season that the island of Walcheren had known since the Spaniards had broken the dykes thirty-eight years before.

The Twelve Years’ Truce had been signed at Antwerp in the previous year; the great war that had devoured two generations of men was suspended; the rivers were open; the gates of the towns were left unbarred from sunrise until the evening gun.

Katharina was fifty-three years old.

She could no longer walk the two leagues from Middelburg to the sea-dyke of Westkapelle; but on a golden afternoon in the month of October, her son Lucas had her carried in an open wicker wagon, lined with soft sheepskins and cushions of green English wool, through the villages of Koudekerke and Zoutelande to the great western rampart of the island.

The dyke of Westkapelle rose forty feet above the salt-marshes—a monstrous wall of beaten clay, granite boulders from the Norwegian fjords, and millions of oak piles driven into the sand, holding back the whole weight of the Atlantic Ocean from the hollow fields of Zeeland.

The wagon stopped upon the crest of the rampart.

The day was very still.

The North Sea lay before them like an infinite floor of blue glass, stretching away to the western horizon without a wave, without a foam-streak, its surface rising and falling in a slow, majestic swell that made the seaweed upon the granite rocks heave with a gentle, breathing sigh.

Beside the wagon stood Lucas, his hand resting upon the wooden wheel-rim, his hair silver at the temples, his eyes looking out toward the English coast.

Beside him stood Clara, holding her six-year-old boy Pieter by the hand, while her husband Master Dirck pointed with his cane toward three great East-Indiamen that were coming up the channel from the south under a cloud of white canvas, their gilded stern-castles flashing in the autumn sun.

Katharina sat propped upon her pillows.

She did not look at the ships.

She looked to the southeast, where the low, blue line of the Flemish coast lay upon the edge of the water like a cloud of smoke.

Somewhere out there, beyond the sandbanks of the Drooge Zand, beyond the drowned reaches of the Saaftinge, lay the city of Antwerp.

The city of her youth.

The city where the lye-ice had cracked in the tub; where the sergeant had ground Pieter’s spectacles into the mud; where the man with the beaver hat had thrown down the twenty-five silver daalders upon the clean pine wood of the press.

She closed her eyes.

She did not hear the screaming of the gulls that turned against the blue sky above the dunes; she did not hear the laughing voices of the children who were gathering cockle-shells upon the wet sand below the dyke.

She heard the sound of the cellar.

The small, dry, regular click:

*Click.*

*Click.*

*Click.*

The tiny lead letters falling into the brass composing stick in the dark.

The *a*.

The *e*.

The *m*.

The *A DECLARATION AND MANIFEST...*

The names of the five aldermen.

The names of the men who had died in the lime.

The name of Pieter Huysman, citizen and master of the art.

She opened her eyes.

The sun was sinking toward the sea, a great, round shield of crimson fire that threw a broad road of molten gold across the quiet water, reaching from the foot of the dyke straight to the western sky.

She looked down at her hands—her small, wrinkled hands that lay upon the green wool of the blanket.

The skin was thin now, transparent as vellum, showing the blue veins that ran across the back like the rivers upon Waghenaer's charts; the knuckles were swollen with the old aches of the winter nights; but the gray notch on the inside of the forefinger remained—deep, clear, indelible, the seal of her life and of her house.

"Lucas," she whispered.

The master-printer leaned over the side of the wagon, his face close to hers.

"Yes, Mother?"

"The forme is true," she said.

Her voice was very faint, thin as the rustle of a dry leaf in the autumn grass, but it had no tremor in it.

"The lines are justified," she said. "The quoins are tight. The tympan is clean. There is no turned letter in the text."

Lucas took her cold hand into his two warm, strong palms.

He did not speak. His throat was full; his eyes were bright with tears that caught the crimson light of the setting sun.

"Pull the bar, Lucas," she murmured, her eyelids falling slowly over her gray eyes. "The sheet is dry. The light is come."

Her head sank back into the green cushions.

The breath went out from her lips—a long, gentle, peaceful sigh that was swallowed by the great salt wind that was rising from the western ocean.

The little boy Pieter looked up at his mother.

"Mother," he asked in his clear, treble voice. "Is grandmother asleep?"

Clara knelt beside the wagon.

She took her mother’s cold fingers, brought them to her lips, and kissed the gray notch upon the thumb.

"Yes, heart," she said through her tears. "Grandmother is asleep. The work is finished."

The sun touched the water.

A great flare of gold and scarlet broke across the sea, filling the sky, the dunes, and the island with an unearthly, transfiguring glory that turned the white sails of the ships into wings of fire.

Out on the salt-flats, the tide turned.

The great waters of the North Sea began to run up the reaches of the Scheldt, deep, swift, and free, washing the sandbanks of Doel, flowing past the green earthworks of Lillo, rolling past the ruined wharves of the ancient city, carrying the memory of the letter out into the infinite light.

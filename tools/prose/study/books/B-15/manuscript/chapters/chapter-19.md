# Chapter 19: The Sutton Homestead

The driveway to the Sutton homestead was three-quarters of a mile of unformed red sand between two lines of dying peppercorn trees. The trees had dropped their dry berries, carpeting the ruts in a pink and gray crust that crackled under the tires.

The house sat on a slight rise of limestone ground above the dry creek bed. It was a classic federation homestead: wide verandas on four sides, green corrugated iron roof with decorative ridge capping, and exterior walls of horizontal cypress weatherboards painted cream forty years ago and now flaking in long strips.

On the veranda stood a woman in faded denim jeans, leather boots, and a broad-brimmed straw hat. A heavy twelve-gauge shotgun leaned against the veranda rail beside a wooden water barrel.

Evelyn stopped the utility forty feet from the gate. She switched off the engine, stepped out with her hands visible at her sides, and reached into her vest pocket for her credential wallet.

"You're on private land," the woman said. Her voice was clear, sharp, carrying easily across the red dirt. She did not touch the shotgun, but her hand rested three inches from the breech.

"My name is Evelyn Brand," Evelyn called out. "I'm a forensic fire investigator with National Mutual."

The woman's eyes narrowed under the hat brim. "National Mutual canceled my structural insurance three weeks ago. Sent a letter saying the property was an uninsurable fire risk due to fuel loads in the river corridor."

"I know," Evelyn said, walking slowly to the gate. "They canceled Frank Miller's policy twenty-four hours before his homestead burned."

Claire Sutton went completely still.

She was fifty, perhaps fifty-five, with graying brown hair pulled back in a tight knot. Her face was lined with deep sun creases around the eyes and mouth, her forearms corded with muscle from hard physical work.

"Come onto the veranda," Claire said, unlatching the wire gate. "Get out of the heat."

The veranda floor was red-gum planking, worn smooth by generations of boots. In the corner by the kitchen door sat an old Coolgardie safe with burlap sides, its water tray dry and filled with dust.

Claire pulled out two canvas director's chairs. "You want water? It's from the rainwater tank. Might taste of pine needles."

"Water is fine," Evelyn said.

Claire went inside and returned with two heavy glass tumblers. The water was cool and tasted of zinc and dry leaves.

Evelyn sat down. She looked out across the paddocks. To the east, the almond groves of Apex Agro-Holdings began precisely at the property fence: thousands of trees in neat, mechanical rows, vibrant green against the dead red clay of the surrounding plains.

"They run four pumps night and day," Claire said, following her gaze. "You can hear the diesel engines through the floorboards when you're trying to sleep. A low thump-thump-thump that never stops. They took forty thousand megalitres out of the deep aquifer last year. Lowered the water table fifty feet. All my bore wells went dry in October."

"Where is your 1912 deed, Claire?"

Claire turned her head slowly. Her gray eyes were direct, unwavering. "Who told you about the deed?"

"Frank Miller," Evelyn said. "And the Land Titles Office in Sydney."

Claire stood up, walked into the house, and returned carrying a heavy volume bound in cracked pigskin leather. She laid it across Evelyn's knees.

The volume was eighteen inches high, fastened with two brass clasps. On the front cover, gold lettering was embossed into the leather: *PARISH OF REDGATE: ALLOTMENT AND WATER REGISTER, 1912.*

Evelyn unclasped the brass buckles. The pages were heavy parchment, edged in gilt that had tarnished to dark bronze. The copperplate script recorded the original Crown survey of the anabranch, signed by the Minister for Public Works in Sydney on August 14, 1912.

*...granting unto Sergeant Thomas William Sutton, his heirs, successors, and assigns, perpetual and indefeasible entitlement to divert, impound, and distribute four hundred and fifty acre-feet of surface water annually from the Redgate weir...*

Evelyn ran her fingertips over the embossed wax seal.

She remembered swimming in the Yanco canal during the nineteen-eighties while her father cleaned the silt screens with an iron rake. The water had been deep and cold, running green from the mountain dams, smelling of sweet mud and wild watercress. The world had seemed permanent then, governed by legal covenants that neighbors respected because their grandfathers had cleared the scrub together.

"Has a man named Raymond Kroll visited this farm?" Evelyn asked.

Claire's mouth hardened. "Last Tuesday. Drove a white Toyota ute with commercial mining plates. Said he was assessing the property for a regional infrastructure valuation."

"Did he look under the house?"

"He asked to check the foundation blocks," Claire said. "Claimed he needed to inspect for termite damage and dry rot before the valuer-general's office could sign off on my debt restructuring. I took him around the back. He spent twenty minutes on his knees by the western crawlspace hatch with a flashlight."

"He was measuring the bearers," Evelyn said. "He was calculating the cubic volume of the crawlspace to determine how many chemical blocks he needs to bring this house to flashover in under three minutes."

Claire stared at her. The color drained from her tanned face, leaving her skin a pale, sallow gray.

"They're going to burn my home?"

"They are going to try," Evelyn said. "There is a severe weather warning for Friday night. Thirty-knot northwesterly winds and dry lightning. If the house catches during a dust storm, the volunteer brigades won't even see the smoke until the roof falls in."

Claire looked down at the pigskin register on Evelyn's lap.

"My grandfather survived three years in the trenches at the Somme," Claire said, her voice dropping into a rough whisper. "He came home with a shrapnel wound in his hip and fifty pounds of army kit. The government gave him this block of saltbush and a shovel. He lived in a hessian tent for two years while he dug the boundary channel with a team of draught horses."

She touched the brass clasp of the book.

"He built this house board by board. He drove the cypress stumps into the clay with a manual sledgehammer. He used to say that as long as we held the Crown grant, no banker in Sydney could take the river away from us."

"The bankers found an engineer who works with thermite," Evelyn said.

Claire stood up. She walked to the railing and pointed toward the low ridge that rose two miles to the west, above the dried creek.

In the center of the kitchen table lay a cloth-bound volume titled *Record of Irrigation Divergences, Farm 419*. The pages were ruled in blue ink, recording eighty years of seasonal river flow in faded copperplate writing.

Evelyn opened the book to January nineteen ninety-three. The entries for that summer were written in a hurried, trembling hand: *Canal flow restricted to twenty percent. Temperature forty-four degrees for six consecutive days. Crop loss estimated at seventy percent. Commercial bank issued notice of demand on mortgage debt.*

"My mother kept that record until the day she died," Claire said, setting down a heavy porcelain teapot on the oilcloth. "In nineteen ninety-three, the receivers arrived in three white sedans while we were picking fruit. They didn't care about the orchard. They walked straight into this kitchen, demanded the irrigation share certificates, and told my father that his family tenure was an obsolete commercial model."

Evelyn looked at the neat cursive columns. The memory of her own father's kitchen in Yanco rushed back with physical intensity: the kitchen table covered in foreclosure notices from the State Bank, the brown legal folders with red ribbons, the smell of burnt toast and cold tea. Her father had sat in his wooden armchair by the window, staring at the dry citrus trees until the sheriff's officer knocked on the screen door with the eviction warrant.

"My father gave up," Evelyn said, her voice dropping into a flat, steady register. "He signed the deed of surrender, packed two suitcases into his utility, and moved into a rented flat in Melbourne. He died four years later in a hospital ward that smelled of carbolic acid and floor polish. He never looked at an orange tree again."

Claire poured two mugs of dark tea. "I am not moving to Melbourne, Evelyn. I am staying on this land."

Claire turned the fragile pages of the farm record volume, pointing to an entry dated October nineteenth, nineteen seventy-four: *Completed construction of southern drainage canal. Flow verified by Department of Public Works inspector. Irrigation share certificates deposited with Commonwealth Bank for safe custody.*

"My father worked two jobs to pay for that concrete flume," Claire said, her voice steady and proud. "He drove grain trucks for the wheat board in the winter and picked fruit all summer. When the state government proposed the first water market reforms in the nineties, he stood up at the community meeting in the town hall and told the politicians that water was the lifeblood of the community, not a gambling chip for merchant bankers."

Evelyn looked at the sepia photograph pinned to the inside cover of the volume. It showed Claire's grandfather standing beside a steam-powered tractor in nineteen twenty, his face shadowed beneath a battered felt hat, his hand resting on the iron steering wheel.

"My father had that same look in his eyes," Evelyn murmured, staring out through the kitchen flyscreen at the dusty orchard rows. "The look of a man who believed that if he kept his boundary fences straight and paid his taxes on time, the law would protect his labor. He died before he understood that the law had been rewritten behind closed doors by corporate underwriters and property lawyers."

Claire Sutton closed the cloth-bound record book, tying the cotton ribbon around the blue boards with steady, sun-browned fingers.

"When the fire brigade arrived at Frank Miller's farm," Claire said, looking out through the flyscreen door into the yard, "the tanker crew tried to draft water from Frank's irrigation sump. But the sump was bone dry because Apex had padlocked the feeder gate three days earlier. Frank had to stand in the red dirt and watch sixty years of family memories burn down because there wasn't enough water in the ditch to fill a five-gallon knapsack."

Evelyn watched a pair of galahs settle on the dry windmill blades behind the sheep yard. "We are going to prove that the dry sumps and the inverted draft vectors were part of the same operation, Claire. The law moves slowly in regional districts, but physical evidence does not disappear."

Claire placed her grandfather's brass compass on the table beside the irrigation volume. The compass case was scratched with seventy years of field use, its magnetic needle pointing true north across the yellowed survey maps.

"My grandfather told me that land can forgive a bad crop, but it never forgives a bad steward," Claire said. "The corporate managers in Griffith think land is just a financial asset to be leveraged on an electronic exchange. But when the topsoil blows away in the westerlies, no balance sheet can hold the desert back."

The grandfather clock in the corner of the kitchen struck twelve with a slow, mechanical chime that echoed through the dark house. Evelyn closed her field notebook, listening to the dry wind rattle the galvanized iron roofing sheets overhead.

Claire refilled the kettle at the cast-iron sink pump, pumping the brass handle until clear groundwater spilled over the spout. The pump leather was worn thin, groaning with every stroke against the cylinder wall.

"My grandfather installed that pump in 1934," Claire said, setting the enamel kettle onto the gas flame. "The well goes down sixty feet into the river gravels. The water board tried to install an electronic flow meter on the riser pipe two months ago, but I ran the contractor off with the tractor. If they meter the well, they will tax every gallon I pump for the cattle."

A thin plume of white dust was rising against the brown scrub. Under the shimmering heat haze, a white utility truck stood motionless on the crest of the hill, its windshield flashing like a signal mirror in the afternoon sun.

# Chapter 1: The Mercer Slab

The heat arrived before the sun had cleared the top wire of the boundary sheep fence. At seven in the morning the digital thermometer on Evelyn Brand's dashboard registered forty-one degrees Celsius, the dry air coming through the open truck vent smelling of parched saltbush, dead ironbark, and the sharp chemical sting of smoldering polyvinyl chloride.

She parked the Toyota Land Cruiser forty feet back from the police tape, turned the ignition key, and sat listening to the metal ticking as the exhaust manifold cooled. Out across the red clay plain, where the irrigation ditches of the lower Murrumbidgee had cracked into dry hexagonal plates three months ago, the Mercer homestead looked like an unroofed furnace. Corrugated galvanized iron sheets lay buckled across the footprint, their silver skin scorched to the color of tarnished pewter, the roof trusses having dropped straight down into the floor space without bowing the outer masonry walls outward.

A white police sedan sat fifty yards down the track under the shade of a dead box tree. Senior Constable Tim Ryan stood by the open driver's door, his blue uniform shirt dark with sweat across his shoulders, holding a clipboard and a thermos cup.

Evelyn reached for her inspection kit on the passenger seat. The heavy canvas bag held her brass calipers, the steel awl, a stiff wire brush, four dozen borosilicate glass sample jars with Teflon-lined caps, and her diamond-point marking pen.

Before she stepped out into the dust, she unlatched her vacuum flask. The water was lukewarm, flat, and tasted faintly of stainless steel. She took three slow swallows, counting three seconds between each, letting the liquid settle behind her sternum before screwing the cup down tight.

She stepped out into the glare. The red dust was ankle-deep along the track, rising in fine clouds that settled on the leather of her lace-up boots and clung to the damp hair at her temples. Two magpies sat on the blackened stump of an ornamental kurrajong tree beside the mailbox, silent, their beaks parted against the heat.

"Morning, Evelyn," Ryan said as she approached the tape. He wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist. "Thought you'd be here yesterday afternoon."

"I was finishing the Miller paperwork in Wagga," Evelyn said. She stopped at the yellow barrier. "Who held the perimeter overnight?"

"Local reserve constable from Corowa sat in his car until four," Ryan replied. He flipped a page on his clipboard. "No foot traffic. No vehicles. Just kangaroos coming down from the scrub looking for water in the cattle troughs. The house was cold by midnight, but the bricks are still radiating like a pizza oven."

"Where is Thomas Mercer?"

"Jerilderie," Ryan said. "Sitting on a canvas camp chair in his sister's back garden. The doctor gave him two valium yesterday morning. The man is sixty-one years old, Evelyn. He lost his wife to cancer two years back, his canal allocation was cut to zero in August, and now his grandfather's homestead is five tons of scrap iron and charcoal. He hasn't said ten words since the brigade pulled him out of the driveway."

"Did he have any livestock left on the lease?"

"Thirty head of crossbred ewes in the back holding paddock," Ryan said. "Council stock inspector came out at dawn to load them onto a transport float. Half of them were blind from dust cataracts. The country is finished, Evelyn. If it doesn't rain by February, the whole shire will be blowing across the Victorian border."

Ryan unclipped a padlock on the farm gate, looped the chain over the post, and stepped back to let her pass. "Take your time in there. Don Driscoll left his brigade log in the mailbox if you need the water gallonage. I'll be in the car with the air conditioner running."

Evelyn ducked beneath the yellow crime-scene tape.

The site smelled wrong. A standard rural structural blaze in drought country carried the heavy, sickening stench of wet sheep manure, scorched wool carpets, and damp tallow from burning kitchen larders. The Mercer house had none of that. It smelled clean. It smelled of calcined lime, spalled portland cement, and a faint, sweetish undertone that reminded her of industrial floor polish.

She walked the perimeter first, clockwise, following the procedure laid down in chapter four of NFPA 921 standards. Her boots made no sound in the ash.

The house had been a four-room brick-veneer cottage, built in the late nineteen-fifties on an unreinforced concrete slab. The outer brick leaves were still standing, but the interior timber partition studs had been consumed down to the bottom plates. What caught her eye was the external ground cover. The dry spear grass grew right up to the concrete footing along the eastern wall. In a natural grassfire or an accidental structure fire fanned by thirty-knot summer winds, the burning embers would have ignited the dry paddock grass for five hundred yards downwind.

Here, the grass was yellow, brittle, and completely unburned. Not a single scorched leaf.

The fire had not traveled out. It had been pulled inward, drawing its oxygen from the open doors and windows with the concentrated draft of a foundry cupola.

She unclipped her pocket thermometer and pressed the stainless probe into the ash near the collapsed front veranda. Fifty-six degrees. The embers were sixteen hours cold, yet the deep masonry retained the thermal load of a blast furnace.

She knelt on the concrete edge, set her canvas kit beside her knees, and pulled the steel awl from its leather sheath. A small piece of river gravel had wedged itself into the chevron tread of her left boot. She worked the sharp point of the awl into the rubber groove, levering the stone out with a firm, practiced twist. It dropped into the gray powder with a dry patter.

She moved along the southern foundation line toward the meter box. The galvanized steel enclosure was still attached to the brick wall beside the laundry door. She opened the latch with the tip of her screwdriver. Inside, the ceramic cartridge fuses had shattered from thermal expansion, but the copper busbars showed no arc pitting. No electrical beaded copper. No signs of an overcurrent fault traveling back to the transformer pole on the boundary track.

Beside the meter box, a small garden bed had once held geraniums. The soil had pulled away from the concrete footing in a continuous three-inch gap, dried to the consistency of powdered brick.

She inserted her soil moisture probe into the fissure. The digital readout flashed two point one percent. Dry enough to catch fire from a dropped match, yet the dead roots in the fissure showed zero charring.

She stood up, adjusted the strap of her tool kit, and stepped over the fallen lintel into the center of the ruins. The blackened subfloor lay buried beneath a three-inch carpet of crushed ceiling plaster and soot.

She stopped at the threshold of what had been the corner study. The timber wall studs here had burned differently from those in the kitchen. The charring was not uniform. It climbed the corner framing in two narrow, diagonal streaks that met at an acute angle near the ceiling line, forming an inverted cone of destruction.

Evelyn knelt again. With her gloved fingers, she brushed aside the top layer of sodden plasterboard. Beneath the white chalk lay the bare concrete slab.

The concrete was not smooth. Across an area three feet square, the surface aggregate had popped, fractured, and cratered into jagged pockets half an inch deep. Spalling. It took temperatures exceeding six hundred degrees within the first eight minutes of ignition to shatter concrete aggregate through internal steam pressure. An ordinary electrical fire, smoldering in wall insulation, took forty minutes to reach that threshold.

She took her brass caliper from the kit and measured the widest crater. Forty-four millimeters across. The edges were razor-sharp, free of soot.

That meant the extreme heat had occurred before the smoke filled the room. The heat had come first; the soot had come later, deposited when the oxygen starved and the flames choked.

She looked toward the brick chimney stack at the back of the study. A blackened cast-iron floor vent sat flush with the hearth, its damper rusted wide open.

Evelyn shifted her weight onto her heels. Her steel-toed boot crushes a brittle piece of charred skirting board with a sharp, dry crack.

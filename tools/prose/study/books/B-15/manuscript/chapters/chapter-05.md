# Chapter 5: The Geometry of Draft

The Corowa Motel smelled of synthetic carpet cleaner, old refrigerants, and dry heat. Evelyn pushed the vinyl-topped desk against the wall beneath the fluorescent tube, kicked her boots into the corner, and laid out the photographic contact sheets on the acrylic bedspread.

There were three sets of photographs, each printed on heavy eight-by-ten matte paper from the forensic laboratory in Wagga Wagga.

Set one: The Oakhaven Winery cellar, February 2021.
Set two: The Miller homestead in Merriwa, October 2026.
Set three: The Mercer homestead, taken six hours ago under the blinding noon sun.

She pulled the brass desk lamp closer, angling the shade so the glare did not bounce off the emulsion.

From twenty feet away, the three fires looked like ordinary rural catastrophes: heaps of blackened timber, collapsed corrugated iron roofs, twisted copper water pipes sticking out of the gray ash like broken bones. But Evelyn did not look at the roofs. Roofs were gravity and physics; they fell where the supporting timbers failed.

She looked at the bottom six inches of the walls.

In the Oakhaven cellar, the inverted V-pattern against the southern masonry wall had an apex angle of forty-two point five degrees. The char depth on the surviving yellow box studs was twelve millimeters. The concrete slab beneath the server rack showed elliptical spalling along a five-foot linear axis.

In the Merriwa homestead, the inverted V-pattern against the chimney breast had an apex angle of forty-two degrees flat. The char depth on the mountain ash joists was eleven point nine millimeters. The spalling on the concrete slab was identical in depth, shape, and aggregate fracture.

And on the Mercer slab this morning: forty-two degrees. Eleven point eight millimeters.

She picked up her steel drafting ruler and laid it across the contact sheets.

Arsonists were creatures of habit. The amateur with a petrol can left puddles: irregular, amoeba-shaped burn marks on the floorboards where the fuel pooled in low spots, with heavy, velvety soot choking the upper walls and light bulbs coated in unburned hydrocarbons. Amateurs always used too much fuel and too little air. They blew out the windows, starved the room of oxygen, and left half their accelerant behind for the police laboratory's gas chromatograph.

This was not an amateur.

This was someone who treated thermal oxidation as a branch of precision mechanical engineering.

To create an inverted thermal cone of forty-two degrees on a concrete slab without blowing the room windows, an ignition source required three distinct elements. First, a fuel that burned at temperatures exceeding one thousand degrees without generating heavy particulate carbon. Second, a shaped containment vessel that directed the thermal plume laterally across the subfloor before allowing it to rise. And third, a calibrated low-level draft that supplied exactly enough air to support combustion without cooling the flame front.

She took her loupe from her shirt pocket and leaned over the Mercer study photograph.

Beside the spalled depression on the slab lay the rectangular opening of the subfloor ventilation flue. In standard nineteen-fifties rural construction, those flues were cast-iron grates set into the external brickwork to prevent dry rot in timber joists. But in the photograph, the internal mortar lining of the flue was scorched clean. The soot did not blow out of the house; the air had been pulled through the brickwork from the outside paddock, drawn through the hearth, and vented up through the ceiling joists.

Someone had cut an opening in the external foundation wall behind the rose bushes.

They had inserted an auxiliary air conduit, connected it to the underfloor crawlspace, and turned the Mercer study into a forced-draft blast furnace.

The same flue modification had been present at Merriwa. She remembered the loose bricks beneath Frank Miller's back veranda, which the local volunteer captain had dismissed as settling cracks caused by the drought.

She pulled her field notebook toward her and turned to a blank grid page.

With her sharp graphite pencil, she sketched the schematic. Air intake at ground level. Fuel block placed four inches above the slab on an insulated ceramic or titanium spacer to prevent heat loss into the ground. Ignition triggered by an electronic thermal delay or a chemical fuse that left zero metallic residue. The air enters, feeds the flame front, drives the heat upward into the ceiling cavity, and flashes over the roof timbers in less than eight minutes.

By the time the volunteer brigade arrives from twelve miles away, the roof has caved in, crushing the fuel apparatus under three tons of burning timber and red-hot corrugated iron. The fire consumes its own machinery.

She calculated the chemical stoichiometry in the margin of her notebook. To generate a twelve-hundred-degree plume across a three-foot slab for twelve minutes required approximately two point two kilograms of high-density synthetic methyl ester gel. The reaction was exothermic, oxygen-balanced, and produced minimal free carbon. That explained why the photoionization detector had registered zero volatile organic compounds.

The phone on her bedside table rang.

The shrill, mechanical warble startled her. She stood up, walked to the nightstand, and picked up the receiver.

"Brand."

"Evelyn." It was Warren Holst's voice. Smooth, calm, unhurried, carrying the faint, hollow resonance of his executive speakerphone in Wagga Wagga. "I'm still at the desk. The Mercer claim just hit the electronic queue from the Griffith agency. Total loss certificate requested."

Evelyn held the receiver against her ear, listening to the faint rustle of papers on Holst's mahogany desk seventy miles to the east.

"I'm still working the scene, Warren."

"The preliminary reports from the shire captain say it's clean, Evelyn. Electrical fault in the study wall. The regional fire officer signed the clearance at two o'clock."

"The regional fire officer didn't scrape the slab," she said. Her voice was flat, measured, stripped of inflection.

"We have an obligation to our client, Evelyn. Murrumbidgee Rural Finance holds the paper on that property. They've already filed their formal loss notice. If we hold up the settlement past thirty days, we're liable for commercial penalty interest under the underwriting treaty."

"Murrumbidgee Finance is Apex, Warren."

A brief pause hung on the line. She could hear the faint rhythmic tap of Holst's heavy gold pen against his desk blotter.

"Apex Agro-Holdings is our largest regional commercial policyholder, Evelyn. They represent forty million dollars in annual premium across the southern basin. They employ twelve hundred people in their processing sheds in Griffith and Hillston. We do not insult their commercial financing entities by treating a tragic drought loss as an insurance fraud investigation without incontrovertible physical proof."

"I haven't sent the samples to the Sydney lab yet," she said.

"Then send them," Holst replied. His voice dropped half an octave, smooth, paternal, and cold. "Send them through the standard courier tomorrow. But don't start seeing ghosts from five years ago, Evelyn. Oakhaven was settled. The board was satisfied. Let Mercer have his closure."

"Goodnight, Warren."

She hung up the receiver before he could answer.

She took out her magnifying loupe to study the photographic prints of the Merriwa chimney foundation. Under ten-times magnification, the salmon-colored brickwork showed tiny spiderweb fractures radiating outward from the mortar joints. Those microscopic fissures were caused by rapid thermal shock, occurring when masonry temperatures spike from ambient levels to six hundred degrees in less than three minutes.

Beside the Merriwa print, she placed the high-resolution scan of the Oakhaven bottling room footing. The fissure density per square inch was identical. The fractures possessed the same depth, the same radial orientation, and the same complete absence of soot penetration within the cracks. The heat had arrived so fast that the masonry fractured before smoke particles could enter the voids.

She checked the timestamp on the digital photo metadata. Every image carried calibrated coordinate tags, verifying that the photographer had recorded the foundation ruins before any salvage machinery entered the site. The evidentiary chain was pristine.

She walked back to the desk, pulled the three contact sheets into the center of the blotter, and adjusted them under the lamp. She aligns the three photographs until the apex angles match down to the millimeter.

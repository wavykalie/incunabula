# Chapter 22: The Veranda at Griffith

The Riverina Country Club sat behind a hedge of manicured oleanders on the outskirts of Griffith, isolated from the dust of the surrounding plains by eighty acres of irrigated fairway. The grass was an unnatural, vivid emerald, kept lush by two million gallons of recycled municipal water pumped every week from the town treatment plant.

Evelyn walked onto the rear veranda at twelve-thirty on Wednesday.

The veranda was crowded with men in linen shirts, tailored moleskin trousers, and leather boots. They were board members of irrigation corporations, corporate agribusiness executives, water brokers, and commercial bank managers attending the annual Murrumbidgee Water Allocation Summit.

A white banner hung above the bar: *AUSTRALIAN WATER FUTURES — MAXIMIZING BASIN EFFICIENCY.*

Warren Holst stood near the western balustrade with a glass of gin and tonic in his hand. He was talking to a tall, silver-haired man wearing an Apex Agro-Holdings lapel pin. Both men were laughing at something written on a printed spreadsheet.

Evelyn stepped through the open French doors.

Her boots were scuffed and gray with red dust. Her canvas vest was salt-stained around the collar. As she crossed the slate floor, the conversation near the railing dropped away.

Holst's smile vanished. His face stiffened into a smooth, cold mask.

He murmured an excuse to the Apex director, turned, and walked toward the quiet corner of the veranda near the potted palm trees.

"You are violating a formal corporate directive, Evelyn," Holst said, keeping his voice low, his words tight and controlled. "There are four uniformed police officers from the Griffith highway patrol standing by the front foyer. I can have you removed in handcuffs in thirty seconds."

"Call them," Evelyn said. She stopped two feet from him. "Have them arrest me. I will spend the weekend in the cells at the police station, and on Monday morning my solicitor will tender the Lidcombe mass spectrometry logs to the magistrate in open court."

Holst took a sip of his drink. The ice cubes clinked against the crystal glass.

"No magistrate in this district will admit an unverified laboratory draft," he said.

"The draft is signed by Marcus Chen," she said. "And I have the physical scrap of the 1912 deed recovered from the Merriwa boundary fence. With the seal of the Water Conservation and Irrigation Commission intact."

Holst's eyes shifted toward the room behind her. Several executives were watching them with polite curiosity.

"What is it you think you are achieving, Evelyn?" he asked, his voice softening into that reasonable, paternal tone he used when settling contested death benefits. "Suppose you are right. Suppose a third-party consultant employed aggressive methods to clear legal encumbrances on distressed titles. What does exposing it do?"

"It stops a murder," she said. "Frank Miller was nearly inside that house when it blew. Claire Sutton is sixty years old and lives alone. If Kroll ignites that crawlspace on Friday night, she will burn alive in her bed."

"Nobody is going to burn alive," Holst whispered sharply. "The properties are monitored. The occupants are always away or accounted for."

Evelyn felt a cold shock travel down her spine.

"You coordinate the timing," she said.

Holst did not answer. He turned his glass slowly in his fingers, watching the condensation run down the clear glass onto his thumb.

"Look around this room, Evelyn," he said, gesturing with his head toward the executives at the bar. "These men represent twelve billion dollars in agricultural export capital. They supply food to forty countries. They employ eight thousand people across this valley. If Apex collapses because its water rights are tied up in ten years of High Court litigation, every business from Hay to Narrandera goes under. The banks will fail. The regional economy will turn into a dust bowl."

"And that justifies arson?"

"It justifies recognizing reality," Holst said. "The 1912 grants are legal relics. They were drafted when farmers plowed with bullock teams and three hundred thousand people lived in the whole state. You cannot run a modern industrial agricultural export economy on nineteenth-century common law water deeds kept in cardboard boxes."

"Then change the legislation," Evelyn said. "Compensate the families. Do not burn their homes in the middle of the night."

"The state government cannot afford the compensation," Holst said coldly. "The buyback cost of those four perpetual deeds is one hundred and ten million dollars. The Treasury does not have the funds. So the market clears the problem."

He looked at her, his pale blue eyes completely devoid of warmth.

"Walk away, Evelyn. I will withdraw the police report. I will accept your resignation with full six-figure severance and guarantee your professional reference for private consulting work in Victoria. If you stay in Redgate on Friday night, you will lose everything. Your pension. Your license. Your freedom."

"I took an oath when I received my investigator's badge," she said.

Holst set his untouched drink on the wide wooden railing.

"An oath to an insurance company, Evelyn. We are underwriters, not the Salvation Army."

Jack Callan, a licensed livestock agent who had worked the Riverina for forty years, pushed a glass of draft beer across the small round table on the hotel veranda. His face was lined with deep sun wrinkles, his hands rough and stained with copper sulphate dip.

"Apex moved into this district five years ago with checkbooks open and four solicitors in tow," Callan said, lowering his voice as three men in business attire walked past toward the dining room. "They didn't start with the good farms along the river. They targeted the families on the branch canals where the government pumps were forty years old. They waited until the second year of drought, when the seasonal water allocation dropped below ten percent."

Evelyn rested her notebook on her knee. "And when the farmers ran out of working capital?"

"The banks wouldn't touch them," Callan said. "The major trading banks had directives from Sydney to reduce their regional agricultural loan exposure. That was when Holst's finance outfit appeared. They offered five-year mortgages with interest deferred until crop harvest. But the fine print carried a clause: if the borrower suffered a total loss of primary dwelling by fire, flood, or tempest, the entire mortgage balance became due and payable within twenty-eight business days."

"A manufactured acceleration trigger," Evelyn noted.

"Exactly," Callan said. "Old Frank Miller didn't understand the contract. He thought he was borrowing two hundred thousand dollars to keep his cattle alive until the rain broke. When his house burned down, Holst's lawyers served him with a bankruptcy notice before Driscoll had even rolled up the fire hoses."

Jack Callan drained his beer and signaled to the barmaid for another round. "You have to understand the mentality of these corporate funds, Evelyn. They don't think in terms of seasons, crops, or families. They think in terms of asset yield per unit of capital. If a megaliter of water produces two hundred dollars of Valencia oranges on a family farm, but twelve hundred dollars of export almonds on an automated corporate plantation, the market treats the family farm as a structural inefficiency that must be eliminated."

"And Warren Holst facilitates the elimination through insurance underwriting," Evelyn said.

"Holst is just the administrative clerk who signs the papers," Callan replied with a tired shrug. "The real engine is the capital flowing in from offshore pension funds that demand a twelve percent return in a zero-interest world."

Callan set his empty beer glass on the coaster and wiped his mustache with a paper napkin. "When the state parliament created the water market, they promised it would save regional communities. What they really did was turn water into gold, and whenever you create gold in a dry country, the vultures arrive with their fountain pens."

Through the open French doors of the club dining room, Evelyn heard the clink of crystal glasses and the low laughter of agricultural bankers discussing cotton futures. The men at the center table wore tailored linen jackets and open collars, their mobile phones lined up beside their bread plates like miniature instruments of commerce.

Holst sat at the head of the long table, leaning toward a state member of parliament. He tapped his pen against a leather-bound folder, nodding with the easy confidence of an executive whose balance sheets were protected by political influence.

He turned on his polished heel and walked back into the crowded room, his chalk-stripe jacket disappearing among the linen shirts and corporate suits.

# Chapter 30: The Tactical Beam

"Step back from the opening, Raymond."

The Cree LED emitter erupted with twelve hundred lumens of blinding white light.

The beam struck Kroll directly across the eyes.

He recoiled with a violent jerk, throwing his left forearm up to shield his face. The sudden optical shock stripped his night vision in a microsecond, turning his world into a searing white flare surrounded by black shadows.

"Do not move your right hand," Evelyn said. Her voice was flat, carrying above the roar of the wind.

Kroll froze on his knees. In his right hand, three inches above the red dirt, he held the eight-pound chemical block. The digital timer on the casing was dark, its toggle switch set to the neutral safety position.

Through the harsh white beam, his face showed stark and pale. The gray stubble on his jaw glistened with sweat.

"Evelyn," he said, blinking rapidly against the light. His voice was hoarse, shaken, but he forced a thin, mechanical smile. "You shouldn't be out here in this dust."

"Set the block on the ground, Raymond. Slide it two feet to your left."

Kroll lowered the chemical package slowly. His fingers lingered on the aluminum casing for a second before he pushed it across the gravel.

"You're making a catastrophic professional mistake," Kroll said, slowly straightening his back. He kept his hands raised at shoulder height, palms forward. "You have no jurisdiction here. You're trespassing on private agricultural land."

"I have two trail cameras forty feet behind you," Evelyn said. "They recorded your vehicle in the creek bed. They recorded your approach across the fence line. And this inspection lamp has a high-definition video recorder mounted to the barrel."

Kroll's eyes darted toward the shadows behind her.

"Whatever Holst told you," Kroll said, speaking rapidly, his voice dropping into a persuasive, reasonable pitch, "this isn't what you think. It's a controlled soil remediation test. The property has severe subterranean hydrocarbon contamination from an old farm dump."

"It has an uncurtailable 1912 riparian water allocation," Evelyn said. "Worth thirty million dollars on the permanent transfer exchange. Just like Frank Miller's homestead. Just like Oakhaven."

Kroll's mouth set into a hard, bitter line. The professional facade cracked, exposing the cold, calculating engineer beneath.

"Miller is an illiterate dryland cocky who couldn't grow wheat if God watered it for him," Kroll spat. "He's sitting on twelve thousand megalitres of high-security water that belongs in productive commercial export agriculture. Those almond trees keep this whole shire alive, Evelyn. What does Claire Sutton produce? Fifty bales of dirty wool a year?"

"She owns the land," Evelyn said. "And you are an arsonist."

"National Mutual will settle this in twenty-four hours," Kroll said, taking a half-step toward the corner of the house. "You know how the board works. There are eighty thousand dollars in escrow for your consulting fee if you turn that camera off and walk to your truck."

"Keep your hands where I can see them, Raymond."

"Step back from the foundation wall, Raymond," Evelyn said. Her voice cut through the dry wind like a razor.

She switched on her high-intensity searchlight, bathing Kroll in three thousand lumens of white beam. At the same moment, Claire Sutton stepped out from the shadow of the back veranda, holding the twelve-gauge shotgun at port arms.

Kroll froze for a fraction of a second, his hand hovering two inches above the digital timer. Then he slowly stood up, squinting into the glare of the light. His expression was not one of panic; it was the cold, calculating irritation of an executive interrupted during a routine maintenance procedure.

"You are trespassing on private property, assessor," Kroll said smoothly. He did not raise his hands. He adjusted his wire-rimmed glasses with a steady finger. "I am conducting an authorized structural risk audit on behalf of the primary mortgagee."

"With an engineered siloxane accelerant and an inverted draft blower?" Evelyn walked forward two paces, keeping the beam fixed on the plastic drum at his feet. "You are caught red-handed, Raymond. The camera on the water tank recorded the entire placement. The state police arson task force is forty minutes out on Highway 18."

Kroll smiled thinly, his eyes cold and defiant. "An interesting hypothesis, Evelyn. But a video recording in the dark proves nothing. In five minutes, this entire district will experience a high-voltage transmission surge from the regional substation. If this dry timber catches fire, it is an unavoidable natural catastrophe in a drought zone. A jury in Wagga Wagga will never convict a registered consulting engineer on circumstantial telemetry."

"The chemical analysis is already logged with the forensic laboratory," Evelyn said. "Dr. Chen matched the molecular structure to your patent filing from twenty-nineteen. The game is over, Raymond."

Kroll did not move toward his tool bag. His eyes darted to the dark outline of Claire Sutton standing on the veranda with the shotgun leveled at his chest, then back to Evelyn's searchlight.

"You have no jurisdiction here, Evelyn," Kroll said, his voice dropping into an icy, professional cadence. "You are an insurance claims assessor employed by National Mutual. You have no statutory powers of arrest or evidence seizure under the Police Service Act."

"Under Section 352 of the Crimes Act, any person may apprehend an offender caught in the act of committing a felony," Evelyn answered without lowering the searchlight. "And setting an incendiary device beneath an occupied dwelling is aggravated arson under Section 196, carrying life imprisonment."

Kroll gave a soft, dismissive chuckle, though the muscles along his jaw were corded tight. "Aggravated arson? This is a structural test of fire-retardant floor coatings, authorized under an environmental risk assessment commissioned by the mortgage holder. My consulting contract explicitly permits non-destructive thermal evaluation of rural foundation materials."

"With a synthetic siloxane gelling agent and an inverted draft blower?" Claire Sutton stepped down from the veranda, her boots crunching into the dry gravel. "You burned Frank Miller's house while he was in Singleton, you coward. You burned Tom Mercer's house and left him in a caravan with nothing but a kettle and two blankets."

Kroll stared at Claire, his face tightening with sudden contempt. "Those men were broke, Sutton. Their farms were dead twenty years ago. The state spent millions subsidizing water to properties that couldn't produce enough fruit to pay their fertilizer bills. We brought thirty million dollars of capital into this district."

"You brought kerosene and corruption," Evelyn said. "And now you are going to explain it to a jury."

Kroll took a slow step backward, his boots sliding half an inch in the loose gravel. "You are making a catastrophic professional mistake, Evelyn. In twenty-four hours, the executive board of National Mutual will announce a comprehensive corporate restructuring. Your division will be disbanded, your active files will be archived, and your professional standing in the insurance industry will be permanently destroyed."

"My professional standing does not rely on corporate cover-ups, Raymond," Evelyn replied, her searchlight beam unwavering on his chest. "I spent twenty years investigating fires for National Mutual. I watched honest families lose everything because assessors like you wrote technical reports designed to protect underwriter profit margins. That ends tonight."

Claire Sutton raised the shotgun slightly, the twin steel barrels gleaming under the stars. "Don't move your hands toward your pockets, Kroll. We have enough evidence here to put you in Goulburn jail for the rest of your life."

Kroll looked from the barrels of the shotgun to Evelyn's searchlight, his face hardening into a bitter, venomous glare. He realized that the time for negotiation had passed.

"Step away from the truck, Raymond," Evelyn commanded, taking another step forward into the clear gravel.

Kroll lowered his hands slowly, looking at the two women in the beam of the light. "You have no idea what forces you are interfering with, Brand. National Mutual has forty million dollars invested in regional water assets through offshore holding vehicles. If this investigation goes public, the share price will collapse and the board will face a royal commission."

"Then the board will have to answer to the royal commission," Evelyn said.

"Drop the timer module onto the rubber mat, Raymond," Evelyn said in a clear, authoritative voice.

Kroll hesitated for two seconds, his eyes darting toward the open door of his truck. Then he saw Claire Sutton raise the shotgun to her shoulder, her finger resting against the front trigger. He slowly opened his fingers, letting the electronic timer drop onto the rubber matting with a dull click.

"Do not move your right hand, Raymond," Evelyn repeated. She took two paces forward into the open yard. "The perimeter is secured. Detective Inspector Vance is at the cattle grid with armed officers."

Kroll's face remained rigid, the skin pulled tight across his cheekbones under the pale wash of the yard light. His eyes flicked from Evelyn's service revolver to Claire Sutton's shotgun. He calculated the ballistic angles with the cold arithmetic of a thermal engineer.

"You have no authority to detain me on private land, Evelyn," Kroll said, his voice flat and controlled. "I am an independent consulting engineer conducting an authorized environmental audit on behalf of the registered mortgagee."

"The registered mortgagee does not authorize five hundred milliliters of nitromethane inside a foundation crawl space," Evelyn said. "Nor does an environmental audit require a delay timer wired to an internal heater coil."

Beside the corrugated iron tank, Donald Vance emerged from the darkness, his tactical vest buttoned to the neck and his Glock pistol held at the low ready position. Two uniformed state troopers moved into position behind the timber fence, their heavy tactical torches cutting through the dust.

"Raymond Kroll, you are under arrest for attempted arson with intent to endanger life," Vance announced. "You are not obliged to say anything, but anything you do say may be used in evidence."

Claire Sutton stepped forward until she stood three paces from Kroll. She held the double-barrel shotgun broken over her forearm, her eyes fixed on the man who had spent three years calculating the destruction of her family's land.

"My father built this homestead with his own hands in 1948," Claire said, her voice dropping to a low, gravelly register. "He hauled the foundation stones from the quarry in an open wagon. You thought you could burn seventy years of sweat with five dollars worth of chemicals and an insurance check."

Kroll did not meet her gaze. He kept his eyes fixed on the gravel between his boots, his jaw clenched tight as the handcuffs clicked into the final ratchet notch around his wrists.

Vance signaled to the two uniformed troopers. "Escort Mr. Kroll to the patrol car. Secure his vehicle and impound all tools, electronics, and documentation on site."

The headlights of the tactical support wagon illuminated the boundary fence, casting long shadows across the dry irrigation ditch. The two tactical officers placed Kroll into the rear cage of the patrol vehicle, locking the security mesh with a heavy steel key.

The tactical radio crackled as the command unit at the highway junction confirmed the roadblock was operational. No private traffic would enter the Redgate road until the crime scene was fully secured by the state forensic technicians.

Across the yard, the wind caught the rusted iron vane of the windmill, swinging the rudder around with a harsh metallic screech. A spatter of cold raindrops struck the dusty windscreen of the police wagon, leaving dark circular rings on the glass.

Kroll's gaze flicked to the dirt. With a sudden, explosive motion, he kicked a shower of sharp limestone gravel directly into the beam of her torch, lunged sideways toward the low shadows of the peppercorn trees, and scrambled at full sprint toward the dry creek bed.

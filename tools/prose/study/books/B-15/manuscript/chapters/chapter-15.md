# Chapter 15: The Anatomy of Drought

The track along the river levee was two ruts of powdery gray silt that rose in a choking cloud behind the utility truck. Evelyn pulled off the track fifty yards from the dry bed of the Lachlan, cut the engine, and stepped out into the furnace heat of the afternoon.

The thermometer on the rearview mirror read forty-six degrees.

The river was dead. Where ten feet of brown water had flowed during her childhood, there was only a trench of cracked mud that had dried into hexagonal slabs like gray ceramic tiles. A rusted iron irrigation pipe stuck out of the riverbank, its foot-valve choked with dead river weeds. On the opposite bank, fifty red river gums stood bare and bleached, their leaves dropped months ago in a desperate bid to survive the heat.

Evelyn climbed onto the tailgate of the utility. She took a swallow from her insulated steel flask. The water was lukewarm and tasted of metal, but it cut the dust in her throat.

She opened her field briefcase on her knees.

She laid out the three property maps: Oakhaven, Coram Creek, Merriwa. Beside them, she placed the cadastral overlay from the Murray-Darling Basin Authority that she had printed in the Wagga basement.

The pattern was not geographical. It was hydrological.

All three properties sat directly upstream of the major diversion weirs that fed the private canal networks of Apex Agro-Holdings.

Under the Water Management Act, every megalitre of water in the basin was classified by priority. High security water went to permanent plantings: citrus, grapes, almonds. General security water went to annual crops: cotton, rice, wheat. And when drought dropped river storage below twenty percent, general security allocations were cut to zero.

Right now, the Burrinjuck and Blowering dams were sitting at fourteen percent capacity.

For two years, family farmers had received zero water allocations. Their paddocks were dust bowls. Their bank accounts were overdrawn.

Except for the holders of the 1912 Crown grants.

Those original grants had been drafted before the concept of state water quotas existed. They were modeled on English common law riparian rights: absolute, uncurtailable access to river flows for agricultural production. Under federal High Court precedent, statutory water curtailment orders could not extinguish pre-Federation and early state covenants without payment of full constitutional compensation.

One acre-foot per day. Guaranteed.

Across the three properties, those senior covenants tied up twelve thousand megalitres of clean, uncurtailable water every season. Water that flowed straight past Apex's pumping stations because the law said it belonged to Frank Miller, Nancy Vance, and the heirs of Bartoli.

Twelve thousand megalitres.

On the open water trading exchange in Griffith, temporary high-security water was trading at eleven hundred dollars a megalitre. On the permanent transfer market, an uncurtailable high-security entitlement was valued at eight thousand dollars per unit share.

The three paper titles were worth nearly one hundred million dollars on the private water market.

And the only thing keeping that water from being converted into tradeable Apex investment units was the physical existence of the parchment grants.

Evelyn set the maps down. A hot gust of wind lifted the edges of the paper, rattling them against the steel tailgate.

This was why the fires were so clean.

This was why there were no pour patterns, no amateur accelerants, no soot trails. If a property was investigated as an ordinary arson fraud, the police would look for financially distressed owners burning their own homes to collect structural insurance. The insurance companies would litigate, the banks would freeze the assets, and the title deeds would be subpoenaed into court records where lawyers would catalogue them.

If the fire was ruled an accidental structural fire, an electrical failure, an unfortunate consequence of extreme drought, the file closed in thirty days.

The owners took their insurance cheques and left the district. The mortgagees foreclosed on the bare land. When the liquidator applied for a replacement title from the Land Titles Office in Sydney, the registrar would issue a standard Torrens title, stripped of the un-digitized riparian covenants because the original paper had been destroyed in the fire.

The water vanished from the property. It flowed straight into the general pool.

Apex's corporate water brokers were waiting with open purchase orders to buy every single drop at government auction.

It was an industrial slaughter of property rights, engineered with laboratory precision.

Evelyn looked at her hands. The red dirt was ground deep into her skin, around the cuticles and across the scars on her knuckles where she had slipped with the crowbar at Oakhaven five years ago.

For five years, she had carried the weight of Oakhaven. She had believed she had missed something, that her preliminary report had let an arsonist walk free because she had been too slow, too cautious, too bound by the standard procedural checkboxes of NFPA 921.

She hadn't missed an amateur. She had been outmaneuvered by an industrial chemist with corporate protection.

She opened her field notes and turned to the section marked *Hydrological Indices*.

The Murray-Darling Basin water register had recorded two hundred and forty thousand transactions over the past twelve months. Ninety percent of those trades were temporary seasonal transfers: fruit growers paying panic prices to keep citrus groves alive for another six weeks. But the permanent transfers told a different story.

Five corporate trusts, all registered to commercial nominee addresses in Melbourne and Saint Helier, had acquired fourteen percent of all high-security river entitlements in the Lachlan valley.

They did not grow crops. They did not own tractors or employ harvesters.

They were water banks.

They bought senior water entitlements, leased the seasonal allocations back to desperate farmers at five hundred dollars a megalitre during peak summer heatwaves, and borrowed against the capital appreciation of the underlying deeds to purchase the next parcel.

And when a senior entitlement was tied to a perpetual 1912 Crown grant, it was the gold bullion of the water market.

A 1912 grant could not be curtailed by the Minister. It could not be rationed by the Murray-Darling Basin Authority. If the river dropped to a muddy trickle, the holder of a 1912 deed was entitled to take their four hundred acre-feet before a single corporate almond tree received a drop.

That was why Apex could not simply outbid Frank Miller on the open market.

Miller had refused to sell. Nancy Vance had refused to sell. Old Bartoli had run the corporate agents off his vineyard with a pruning hook.

So the corporate agents had stopped offering money.

They had brought in an engineer with twenty-five years of process safety experience, a specialist who knew how to turn a hundred-year-old timber homestead into a blast furnace without leaving a drop of kerosene on the floorboards.

Evelyn looked out across the dry riverbed toward the southern ridge.

The wind had shifted five degrees to the north, carrying the dry, dusty heat of the central desert. The cicadas in the dying red gums had stopped screaming, silenced by the suffocating temperature that pressed down on the basin like an iron lid.

She took a photoionization detector from her equipment bag. She calibrated the sensor with a zero-gas filter, watching the digital display settle at zero-point-zero parts per million.

She walked twenty paces down the dry levee bank to where the drainage pipe from the Merriwa boundary ditch emptied into the riverbed.

She held the probe six inches above the cracked silt.

The digital readout flickered. *0.4 ppm. 0.8 ppm. 1.2 ppm.*

Trace volatile organic compounds. Not petroleum. The faint molecular signature of synthetic polyol ester carried down the drainage channel by the wind after the Merriwa fire.

The evidence was in the dirt itself.

She traced the flow lines on the cadastral chart. The Lachlan riverbed curved five miles south toward Redgate weir.

If Kroll had executed three fires across sixty miles of basin, he had not stopped. The corporate investment timeline demanded continuous water security before the summer harvest closed.

She checked the fourth property on the cadastral index: Lot 304, Parish of Redgate.

*Claire Sutton.*

The property bordered Apex Orchard Block Nine. The mortgage was in default. The grant dated back to 1912.

Evelyn stood up on the truck tailgate. The heat radiated off the steel roof of the cab like a hot plate.

She capped her flask. She gathered the maps and slipped them back into her briefcase.

Kroll led the way into the corporate demonstration pavilion, a vast steel-framed hangar cooled by six industrial chillers that kept the indoor temperature at twenty-two degrees. Inside, row upon row of computerized fertigation skids stood on epoxy-coated concrete, their digital flow meters humming with steady mechanical power.

"The public clings to a sentimental mythology of the family farm, Evelyn," Kroll said, gesturing toward the gleaming stainless steel piping. "They romanticize two hundred acres of dry red dirt and thirty sheep starving along a fence line. But thermodynamics does not respect nostalgia. The Murray-Darling basin is an arid watershed. Distributing water in open earthen canals to unviable tenures results in a sixty percent loss through evaporation and seepage. It is an economic absurdity."

"So your corporate employers solve the absurdity by burning down the homesteads," Evelyn said coldly.

Kroll stopped beside a multi-valve manifold and turned to face her, his mouth curling into a sharp, clinical smile. "My corporate clients invest capital in high-efficiency closed-loop irrigation infrastructure. We produce four times the crop yield per megaliter compared to the historical leaseholders. When an unviable enterprise reaches the end of its economic lifecycle, capital reallocation is necessary. Whether that reallocation occurs through mortgage foreclosure or structural retirement is merely an operational detail."

"The Mercer fire was not an operational detail," Evelyn said. "It was felony arson with an engineered chemical accelerant."

"Arson is a legal conclusion, assessor," Kroll replied, his voice dropping to a low, mocking pitch. "In thermal physics, there is only heat flux, oxidation rate, and boundary layer dissipation. If a structural envelope experiences rapid thermal degradation, an assessor must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that an exogenous fuel was introduced. And your standard forensic laboratory cannot prove that which it cannot detect."

Kroll pointed his stainless steel pointer at a computerized schematic on the pavilion wall. The display showed the hydraulic network of the Apex demonstration orchards, tracking water pressure across forty thousand drip emitters in real time.

"Look at the telemetry data, Brand," Kroll said, his tone icy and condescending. "We maintain an average application efficiency of ninety-two percent. Not a single liter of water is lost to evaporation or deep percolation. Meanwhile, your friend Claire Sutton is running flood irrigation down open dirt ditches, losing sixty percent of her Crown allocation before the water even reaches the root zone. In an arid continent, that is not agriculture; that is environmental vandalism."

"And that justified burning Frank Miller's house to the ground?" Evelyn asked.

Kroll lowered the pointer and looked at her through his clean safety glasses. "An engineer deals with energy balances and systemic outcomes. When an archaic enterprise fails to adapt to resource constraints, structural turnover is the inevitable thermodynamic result."

Kroll adjusted his watch, turning his back on the irrigation manifold. "You are an investigator of ruins, Evelyn. You spend your life looking at ash, scorched iron, and melted glass, searching for someone to hold responsible for the decay of rural communities. But capital does not operate on moral accountability; capital moves toward efficiency. In ten years, every tree in this valley will be watered by automated telemetry, and nobody will remember who held the Crown leases in nineteen twelve."

The sun was sinking into a bank of purple dust on the western horizon. The sky burned a fierce, bruised orange, the exact color of cypress timber at flashover.

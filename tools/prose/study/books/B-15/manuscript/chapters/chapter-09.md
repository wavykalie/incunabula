# Chapter 09: The Corporate Air

The plate glass windows of National Mutual's regional headquarters did not open. They were sealed against the dust of the Riverina by thick rubber gaskets that had baked hard and cracked during the dry spell. Behind them, two industrial chillers on the roof hummed continuously, circulating air that tasted of damp filters, photocopy toner, and chicory syrup.

Evelyn Brand stood by the glass on the fourth floor with a paper cup in her hand. The chicory coffee was lukewarm and sour. Down below, the Murrumbidgee River crawled between steep banks of dry gray mud. A line of dead willows leaned over stagnant pools that showed green scum under the midday sun. Out past the levee bank, the wheat silos rose like concrete teeth against the haze.

Her boots felt heavy on the commercial carpet. The wool pile had been treated with flame retardant that smelled faintly of ammonia when the humidity dropped below twenty percent. She set the cup on the laminated sill and wiped her fingers on her trousers. Red clay from the Merriwa ruins was still embedded under her fingernails.

"You look like you slept in a ditch, Evelyn."

Warren Holst did not look up from his desk. He was signing letters with a broad-nibbed fountain pen, his movements rhythmic and steady. A gold signet ring on his pinky caught the cold glare of the fluorescent tubes. On the corner of his mahogany credenza sat a framed photograph of a yacht moored in Sydney Harbour.

"I slept in the back of the utility," Evelyn said. "Merriwa is sixty miles out. The motel in town burned down three months ago."

"I am aware of the local lodging situation," Holst murmured. He capped the pen with a satisfying click. "Sit down. Please. You make the upholstery nervous."

She pulled out a low leather chair opposite the desk. The leather was cold against the back of her legs. She dropped her field notebook onto the blotter. The corners were dog-eared, the binding gray with ash.

Holst glanced at the notebook without touching it. His eyes were small, pale blue, framed by rimless titanium spectacles. He wore a gray chalk-stripe suit that showed no creases, even in the middle of a forty-degree heatwave.

"Frank Miller filed his loss statement this morning," Holst said, leaning back. His chair tilted with a gentle hydraulic hiss. "Total structural loss. One million two hundred thousand on the homestead, plus six hundred thousand on plant and equipment. An unfortunate incident. Very sad for the district."

"It was not an accident," Evelyn said.

Holst smiled, though his mouth barely widened. "Every claimant who watches forty years of dryland wheat go to dust thinks God has a personal grievance against him. It is human nature, Evelyn. Grief seeks intent."

"I don't look for intent. I measure char depth." She leaned forward, resting her forearms on her knees. "The fire started in the crawlspace beneath the northern joists. Temperature exceeded nine hundred degrees within four minutes. Iron roofing sheets melted through along the ridge beam. That does not happen from a dropped cigarette or dry wiring."

"Old cypress pine burns hot."

"Cypress pine does not melt corrugated iron down to slag," Evelyn said. Her voice was flat, clipped, the register she used when giving sworn testimony in the Land and Environment Court. "Standard residential fuel load tops out at seven hundred degrees. To reach eleven hundred, you need an accelerator with high calorific density. And there was no pour pattern. No hydrocarbon wash."

Holst picked up a bone paper knife and balanced it across two fingers. "You mentioned that in your preliminary note on the Oakhaven vineyard. And on the Coram Creek property."

"Three properties," Evelyn said. "Separated by sixty miles of scrub. All three built on cypress piles. All three burned between two and four on Friday mornings. All three totally consumed before the local volunteer brigades could draft water from dry tanks."

"A cluster," Holst said smoothly. "Clusters happen in prolonged drought. You know the statistical variance better than anyone in this branch. Aridity lowers ignition thresholds across the board. Vegetation cures faster. Radiated heat carries further."

"A cluster is three hay barns catching from lightning," Evelyn said. "This is precision demolition."

Holst placed the paper knife flat on the leather blotter. He picked up his gold pen again, turned it over once in his palm, and set it down.

"National Mutual pays claims, Evelyn. We do not write crime novels. If the regional police sergeant at Merriwa writes 'accidental due to electrical short circuit in subfloor junction box,' we do not hold up a million-dollar settlement to search for ghosts in the cellar."

"The sergeant at Merriwa is seventy years old and hasn't climbed into a crawlspace since the flood of seventy-four," she said.

Holst tilted his head. His spectacles reflected two blank white squares of ceiling light. "We have commercial obligations. Apex Agro-Holdings holds the mortgage on the Miller title. They are our largest institutional policyholder in the Riverina. They do not appreciate their debt recovery being delayed by academic theories."

"Apex owns the mortgage?" Evelyn asked.

"Apex owns many things," Holst said softly. "Including the patience of our Sydney board. Take the weekend, Evelyn. Write up the Merriwa file as inconclusive. Clear the backlog on the domestic hail claims."

He picked up a fresh document from his out-tray. The fountain pen scratched across the parchment paper with a dry, decisive scrape. Evelyn watched the wet black ink absorb into the cotton weave.

"I need three days," she said.

Holst did not look up from the page. "You have forty-eight hours to submit the standard form. After that, the file closes by administrative order."

She stood up. Her boots left two faint circles of red dust on the gray carpet. Holst did not glance at them as she turned and walked toward the heavy glass door.

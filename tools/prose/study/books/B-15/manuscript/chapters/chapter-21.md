# Chapter 21: The Thermal Perch

The scrub along the western ridge was dense mallee eucalyptus and dry needlewood, growing out of red limestone gravel that clattered underfoot like broken pottery. Evelyn had left her truck two miles back in a dry gully, concealed under a brown camouflage tarp lashed down with bungee cords.

She carried her private field pack: forty pounds of forensic gear, two gallons of water, and a pair of military-grade thermal imaging binoculars she had purchased five years ago with her own savings after the Oakhaven inquest.

It was eleven o'clock on Tuesday night.

The temperature had dropped to thirty-two degrees, but the breeze had picked up from the northwest, blowing steady at fifteen knots. The dry branches of the mallee scraped together with a hollow, wooden clicking that sounded like bones.

She lay prone on a flat shelf of limestone fifty feet above the valley floor. A thin ground mat insulated her chest from the sharp stones.

Through the rubber eyecups of the thermal binoculars, the world appeared in false-color shades of violet, emerald, and hot gold.

Below her, eight hundred yards to the east, the Sutton homestead was a cluster of cool purple shapes. The iron roof had cooled quickly after sunset, showing as dark indigo against the warm amber ground of the yard. Inside the house, a single yellow window showed where Claire Sutton sat in the kitchen, keeping watch with her shotgun across the table.

Evelyn panned the binoculars slowly across the dry creek bed.

The creek was choked with dead river oaks and dry roly-poly bushes that registered as dull green mounds. Nothing moved. A pair of feral goats walked along the gravel bed, their warm bodies glowing bright magenta against the dark earth, their movements slow and cautious.

She checked the wind speed with her handheld anemometer. Seventeen knots, gusting to twenty-one.

If an arsonist wanted to destroy the house without being seen, he would not approach from the road. The road was open red dirt, visible from the front veranda for half a mile. He would come up through the creek bed, using the dead timber for cover, until he reached the dry wash sixty yards behind the western foundation wall.

She raised the binoculars again.

At eleven-forty, a faint thermal bloom appeared on the far side of the creek, two miles to the north.

It was a vehicle, driving without headlights along the dry boundary fence of Apex Orchard Block Nine. The heat of its exhaust pipe and catalytic converter glowed white-hot against the metal of the undercarriage.

The vehicle stopped at the culvert where the irrigation canal crossed under the gravel road.

A silhouette stepped out from the driver's door.

In the green phosphor display of the high-magnification mode, the figure resolved with striking clarity. The man was tall, broad-shouldered, wearing a dark canvas field jacket and heavy work boots. He moved with the practiced, economical movements of an engineer, carrying a rectangular case in one hand and a surveyor's carbon-fiber tripod in the other.

He set up the tripod on the edge of the culvert.

He mounted an instrument on top of the tripod. Through the binoculars, Evelyn could see the cylindrical lens of an infrared radiometer or laser rangefinder. The man aimed the beam directly at the Sutton house, sighting along the western gable.

He was calculating the line of sight and the thermal plume dispersion angle.

He stood by the tripod for eight minutes, taking notes on a digital tablet that cast a faint green glow across his chin and forehead. His face was square, heavy-jawed, with short graying hair cropped close to the skull.

It was Raymond Kroll.

She had seen his photograph in the 2019 conference proceedings of the Australasian Fire and Emergency Service Authorities Council. He had presented a technical paper on "Accelerated Pyrolysis Patterns in High-Aridity Structural Environments."

Now he was applying his research to a weatherboard homestead in the middle of a drought basin.

Kroll finished his notes. He packed the radiometer back into its padded case, collapsed the tripod with three swift clicks, and loaded both pieces of equipment into the rear tray of his utility.

He walked around the vehicle to inspect the canal drainage culvert.

Through the thermal lens, Evelyn watched him check the steel flow gate with an engineer's inspection mirror. He was verifying that the culvert was dry and free of brush that could catch fire prematurely and alert the orchard security patrols. Everything was measured, calculated, executed to procedural precision.

He climbed back into the driver's seat, started the engine, and backed slowly along the fence line, disappearing into the dark shadows of the almond orchard.

Evelyn lowered the binoculars. Her eyes ached from the green phosphor glare.

He had not come to burn the house tonight. The wind was wrong; seventeen knots was enough to spread a brush fire, but it was coming from the west-northwest, which would drive the smoke toward the highway where passing grain trucks could spot it.

He was waiting for the Friday front. The forecast had called for northwesterly gales exceeding thirty-five knots, accompanied by severe dry lightning.

On Friday night, the sky would give him total cover.

From the crest of the limestone ridge, three hundred feet above the valley floor, the landscape appeared as an endless grid of dark geometric blocks under the starlight. To the north, the bright perimeter floodlights of the Apex almond processing plant glowed like an industrial city, the hum of its sorting machinery carrying three miles across the cool night air.

Evelyn adjusted her night-vision monocular and scanned the access road running along the main irrigation canal. At eleven-forty, a pair of headlights appeared at the five-mile gate. The vehicle was moving slowly, traveling at less than twenty kilometers an hour with its amber clearance lights extinguished.

Through the green phosphorus tube of the monocular, she tracked the utility truck as it reached the pump station. The vehicle stopped beside the concrete flume. A figure stepped out into the beam of the vehicle headlights, wearing dark work overalls and a hard hat. The man carried a long cylindrical object from the rear tray, setting it down carefully on the concrete platform above the sluice gate.

She pulled out her stopwatch and timed the operation. For seven minutes, the figure worked over the sluice mechanism with a wrench, adjusting the mechanical limit stops on the gate hoist. When he finished, he climbed back into the cab, extinguished his headlights completely, and drove south along the dirt track leading directly toward the Sutton boundary.

The wind shifted slightly to the west, bringing with it the sharp scent of burning stubble from a distant paddock burn twenty miles to the north. Evelyn adjusted her collar against the night chill that was settling over the limestone ridge.

Through her monocular, she watched the vehicle drive along the eastern canal levee. The utility truck paused at every culvert crossing, the driver illuminating the ditch banks with a handheld spotlight. He was not checking for water leaks; he was verifying that the dry grass along the boundary fences formed a continuous fuel bed leading directly from the access road to the homestead outbuildings.

She memorized the route. The vehicle was establishing the approach corridor for the night of the fire to ensure that an operational team could reach the Sutton perimeter without passing through any populated rural intersections.

From the limestone outcrop, she observed the distant lights of the regional highway. A long livestock road train rumbled past to the west, its twin exhaust stacks barking in the night air. The valley was an enormous chessboard of water titles, debt covenants, and dry orchards, waiting for the rain that never came.

Evelyn calibrated her digital anemometer on the flat limestone outcrop, holding the sensor head into the southerly breeze. The wind speed registered twelve knots with gusts to eighteen.

The thermal gradient across the valley floor followed a classic nocturnal inversion pattern. Cold air drained down the dry creek beds toward the river, while a layer of compressed warm air remained trapped between the hillcrests. A surface fire ignited under these specific microclimatic conditions would travel along the low-lying drainage lines with exceptional velocity.

She folded the binoculars into their case, checked her watch, and lay back against the limestone rock to wait for dawn.

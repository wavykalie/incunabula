# Chapter 25: The Attic Watch

The attic of the Sutton homestead smelled of dry pine rosin, roasted zinc, and dead wasps. Decades of summer heat had baked the unlined corrugated iron roof until the rafters wept amber beads of sap that had hardened into brittle rosin tears.

Evelyn sat on a wool bale near the western louvers. Her knees were drawn up, her boots wedged against the ceiling joists. Through the narrow wooden slats, the entire western horizon was visible across four miles of saltbush and dry lignum.

The sky at five in the afternoon had turned the color of bruised zinc.

A low-pressure trough from the Great Australian Bight was colliding with the tropical heat dome over the interior. The wind screamed through the radio mast on the chimney, vibrating the iron sheets with a steady, deep drone.

The regional weather broadcast on her battery receiver had been issued at four o'clock: *Severe Fire Danger Warning for the Northern Riverina and Lower Lachlan. Northwesterly winds thirty to forty knots, maximum temperature forty-six degrees, with isolated dry thunderstorms producing severe dry lightning.*

It was the arsonist's weather window.

If a fire started in dry timber under thirty-knot gales, the flame front moved faster than a galloping horse. Radiated heat from the crowning canopy ignited spot fires a quarter-mile downwind before the primary structure had even collapsed.

Downstairs, the house was silent except for the groaning of the timber framing.

Claire Sutton was in the kitchen, sealing the brass-bound 1912 parish water register inside a heavy polyethylene container lined with closed-cell neoprene foam. Beside the container sat three boxes of high-velocity twelve-gauge birdshot and her father's double-barreled shotgun.

Evelyn checked the radio scanner clipped to her webbing.

The scanner was monitoring three frequencies: the New South Wales Rural Fire Service command channel for Sector Four, the local police dispatch from Griffith, and the private UHF channel used by Apex Agro-Holdings orchard security patrols.

The RFS channel was chattering with reports of tree branches falling across power lines near Leeton. Volunteer brigades were already stretched thin, standing by tankers in village sheds forty miles to the south.

She uncapped her digital anemometer. The small plastic cups spun in a blur through the open louver.

*Wind speed: 32 knots. Gusting: 41 knots. Ambient humidity: 8 percent.*

Eight percent relative humidity meant that any cured cypress pine exposed to heat would drop below four percent internal moisture within sixty seconds. At four percent moisture, timber did not require a sustained pilot flame to propagate combustion; radiative flux above twenty kilowatts per square meter caused instantaneous surface ignition.

She looked toward the eastern boundary fence.

The two hundred thousand almond trees of Apex Orchard Block Nine stood in absolute geometric rows, their dark foliage thrashing in the gales. The corporate drip lines were pumping thousands of gallons of deep bore water into the root zones, keeping the orchard soil black and damp while the surrounding public pastures turned to blowing sand.

Between that damp orchard and the dry anabranch lay the Sutton homestead: fifty tons of seasoned river timber waiting for a spark.

A sudden gust of wind slammed against the western gable.

The air under the corrugated iron roof was fifty-four degrees Celsius, smelling of scorched dust, dry bat droppings, and the resinous sap of seventy-year-old cypress pine rafters. Evelyn crouched on a plank laid across the ceiling joists, her forehead glistening with sweat in the darkness. Through a ventilation louver in the eastern gable, she had an unobstructed view of the farm entrance and the dry canal bridge three hundred yards away.

She held the thermal imaging binoculars to her eyes, adjusting the objective focus ring until the distant fence posts stood out as sharp yellow lines against the dark purple of the cooling paddocks. Every twenty minutes, she swept the northern horizon where the regional highway ran past the almond plantations. The heat stored in the bitumen radiated upward in undulating bands, distorting the headlights of distant transport trucks into elongated yellow smudges.

Below her, through the tongue-and-groove ceiling boards, she could hear the occasional metallic creak of the house settling in the night air. The drought had dried the foundation clays until the entire timber frame shifted on its brick stumps, doors jamming in their casings and plaster cracks opening along the hallway cornices. In an environment this dry, a structure was already eighty percent of the way toward ignition. It needed only a three-minute thermal pulse to initiate self-sustaining combustion.

She checked the digital display on her environmental monitor. The ambient wind speed was twenty-eight knots from the northwest, perfectly aligned with the orientation of the Sutton roofline. The relative humidity had fallen to six percent. If someone ignited the subfloor crawlspace under these conditions, the vertical fire spread through the wall cavities would outpace any domestic garden hose within two minutes.

From her vantage point among the roof collar ties, Evelyn studied the western fence line with her handheld infrared thermometer. The soil surface along the boundary ditch registered forty-seven degrees Celsius even after midnight, holding daytime radiant heat in the packed red clay.

She noticed a series of irregular thermal blooms along the dry canal levee two hundred yards to the north. Through the twenty-power spotting scope, the blooms resolved into small solar-powered telemetry units mounted on star pickets. Each unit carried a passive infrared motion sensor wired to a radio transmitter.

Apex had established a clandestine electronic perimeter around the Sutton property. The sensors were positioned to detect any vehicle entering or leaving the farm to ensure that their field team would know the exact moment the homestead was unoccupied.

She copied the radio frequency displayed on her portable frequency scanner: four hundred and thirty-three megahertz, operating on a low-power digital pulse format. She programmed her field transceiver to monitor the channel, setting the audio alert to a single click in her earpiece whenever a sensor tripped.

Beside her in the dust, a brown huntsman spider crawled across a fallen rafter, its legs moving with silent precision over the rough timber grain. The attic was a dead space, an oven of trapped heat and dry cypress dust that smelled of ancient drought and neglected labor.

She checked the focal adjustment on her optical pyrometer. Through the small peep-sight, the timber joists of the homestead ceiling showed a uniform surface temperature of thirty-nine degrees. The wood had baked in the summer heat for seventy days, reducing its moisture content to less than five percent. In that condition, the timber would ignite with the speed of dry paper.

Through the attic ventilation louver, Evelyn watched a solitary wedge-tailed eagle circle low over the dead citrus blocks on Portion Forty-Three. The bird banked on a thermal of rising desert air, its primary feathers splayed against the pale horizon.

She checked her field watch. Two hours remained until midnight. The desert temperature was dropping slowly, the hot westerlies giving way to gusting dry air from the northwest. Everything was aligned for combustion.

Through the night-vision monocular, Evelyn observed the distant silhouette of the grain silos against the starlight. The massive concrete towers stood like silent monuments on the horizon, their floodlights casting long white fingers across the dry railway sidings.

From the attic louvers, Evelyn surveyed the four quadrants of the homestead perimeter with her thermal scope. The eastern approach along the irrigation levee was completely clear, the dry clay bank registering ambient temperature.

To the north, the sheep paddocks sloped gently toward the gravel road. A mob of thirty merino ewes stood clustered under a dead red gum tree, their body heat showing as small white clusters on the liquid crystal display. Nothing moved along the tree line. The night was vast, silent, and cold, wrapped in the dry dust of three rainless years.

A loose sheet of corrugated iron on the shearing shed roof tore free with a violent shriek of rusted screws. The sheet flipped three times in the red dust, cartwheeled sixty yards across the sheep yards, and crashed into the wire boundary fence with a deafening rattle.

# Chapter 29: The Shadow at the Hatch

The night was pitch black. The dust cloud had swallowed the stars, turning the sky into a low ceiling of oppressive red gloom. The wind came in long, howling surges that whipped the branches of the peppercorn trees against the iron shed.

Evelyn crouched in the three-foot gap between the kitchen water tank and the galvanized corrugated wall of the laundry.

The tank was three thousand gallons of corrugated steel, vibrating like a bass drum as the wind slammed into its curved surface. The smell of dry red silt was so thick she had to breathe through her handkerchief.

Her eyes were adjusted to the dark.

At eleven-fifteen, the wooden boundary gate sixty yards away swung open without a sound.

A dark silhouette drifted across the red dirt of the yard.

Kroll moved with absolute confidence. He wore no flashlight. He carried his heavy rucksack in his left hand, walking along the line of the laundry wall where the shadows were deepest.

He stopped ten feet from Evelyn's position.

He knelt in the dirt beside the western crawlspace hatch. The hatch was a simple wooden panel of vertical cypress boards, held closed by an old iron swivel latch.

Kroll set his rucksack down on the red gravel. He pulled a pair of thin leather driving gloves from his pocket and slipped them over his fingers.

He unzipped the main compartment of the pack.

With steady, deliberate movements, he lifted out an eight-pound brick of grayish-white chemical compound wrapped in clear vacuum-sealed plastic. Attached to the side of the brick was an aluminum casing containing a digital timer display and two red insulated lead wires.

He rotated the iron latch on the hatch.

The dry wooden hinges groaned with a faint, high-pitched squeak as he lifted the hatch panel and leaned it back against the weatherboards.

The dark crawlspace opened before him, smelling of dry earth and cold masonry.

Kroll picked up the chemical block. He reached into his vest pocket, produced a small magnetic spirit level, and checked the surface of the timber bearer to ensure his adhesive bracket would seat flush against the grain.

Every motion was practiced, methodical, the calm procedure of an industrial technician installing a valve in a refinery turnaround.

He leaned forward onto his knees, ducking his head beneath the foundation bearer to slide the charge into the darkness.

At two-fifteen in the morning, the sound of an approaching vehicle cut through the steady rush of the wind. Evelyn pressed herself against the timber siding of the rainwater tank, her boots resting on the concrete apron.

A white utility truck turned off the canal access road without lights, its diesel engine idling at low revolutions. The vehicle rolled to a stop behind the machinery shed, sixty yards from the house. The driver's door opened with a soft click. A tall man in dark coveralls and heavy work boots stepped out into the dust, carrying a canvas tool bag and a plastic fuel drum.

Through her digital night-vision monocular, Evelyn watched the man's face. The harsh ambient light of the stars illuminated his high cheekbones, his close-cropped gray hair, and his wire-rimmed safety glasses. It was Raymond Kroll.

Kroll moved with the disciplined economy of an industrial engineer. He did not look around or hesitate. He walked directly to the eastern foundation wall of the Sutton homestead, where the crawlspace ventilation grate had been removed. He set the canvas bag on the ground, knelt in the dirt, and unrolled a twelve-foot length of flexible metallic ducting.

He reached into the bag and extracted a portable high-output blower fan powered by a rechargeable lithium battery pack. He connected the fan to the ducting, inserting the delivery nozzle six feet into the subfloor void beneath the kitchen joists. From his vest pocket, he pulled a glass jar containing a thick, amber-colored gel and an electronic timer assembly with a ceramic heating element.

He set the jar inside the foundation crawlspace, uncapped the lid, and inserted the glow plug into the chemical matrix. He reached for the timer button to initiate the arming sequence.

From her position behind the concrete water tank, Evelyn observed Raymond Kroll through her thermal monocular. The engineer was kneeling on a square of rubber matting beside the foundation wall, working with steady, unhurried precision.

Kroll opened an aluminum equipment case resting on the dirt. Inside the foam-padded interior were four precision metering valves, a digital tachometer, and a set of stainless steel delivery nozzles. He selected a two-inch nozzle, connected it to the flexible metallic ducting, and tightened the hose clamp with a small ratchet wrench.

He reached for a digital manometer and inserted the sensing tube into the subfloor crawlspace. The instrument measured air pressure differentials across the foundation vents. Kroll adjusted the speed control dial on the electric blower until the manometer registered a negative pressure of twelve pascals inside the subfloor void.

By creating negative pressure within the crawlspace, the blower would draw fresh combustion air inward through the foundation grates while forcing superheated exhaust gases upward through the hollow wall cavities. It was an industrial furnace design scaled to fit beneath a weatherboard cottage.

He reached into his canvas bag and removed a two-liter amber glass bottle sealed with a threaded Teflon cap. He set the bottle in an insulated holder, uncapped the seal, and poured three hundred milliliters of viscous liquid into a stainless steel delivery tray. The chemical had no smell in the open air, but through the thermal imager, Evelyn could see a faint evaporative cooling halo forming around the rim of the tray.

He connected the electric glow plug leads to a solid-state timer module, setting the digital countdown display to forty-five minutes.

From her position behind the rainwater tank, Evelyn monitored the timer display through her telephoto lens. The red light-emitting diodes flickered steadily in the dark: *00:44:59 ... 00:44:58 ... 00:44:57*.

Kroll stood up, brushed the red dust from the knees of his coveralls, and walked back to the utility truck. He reached into the open cab and pulled out an insulated thermal flask, pouring himself a cup of coffee. He leaned against the front fender, sipping the hot liquid while watching the dark silhouette of the homestead.

His posture was completely relaxed. He showed none of the nervous apprehension typical of amateur arsonists who look repeatedly over their shoulders or rush their movements. Kroll had performed this operation three times before without detection. He had complete confidence in his engineering calculations, his chemical accelerant, and the corporate lawyers who protected his syndicate in Sydney.

Through her earpiece, Evelyn heard the faint crackle of Claire Sutton's radio link. Two clicks. The signal confirming that the approach road had been blocked by the tractor and the heavy chain.

Kroll finished his coffee, tossed the dregs into the dust, and screwed the plastic cup back onto the flask. He reached for his leather gloves on the truck seat, preparing to conduct a final inspection of the ventilation ducting before departing.

Evelyn watched through her spotting scope as Kroll attached the delivery hose to the subfloor ventilation grate. His movements were methodical, guided by a checklist clipped to his left sleeve.

He connected the electric blower to the twelve-volt power supply, testing the motor with a momentary toggle switch. The fan whined for two seconds with a high-pitched mechanical drone, then cut off.

He pulled a small electronic manometer from his pouch, verifying the draft velocity inside the foundation crawlspace. The air velocity was three point eight meters per second, creating an optimal convective draft toward the central chimney flue.

From her position behind the water tank, Evelyn watched Kroll connect the electrical leads from the timer module to the ceramic glow plug. His hands were covered in thin leather work gloves, moving with practiced efficiency as he verified the polarity of the connections with a handheld circuit tester.

He pulled a small aerosol canister from his pocket and sprayed a fine coating of conductive graphite powder onto the electrical terminals to prevent arcing. Every detail of the preparation revealed the work of a professional combustion specialist who understood the physics of forensic detection.

Kroll knelt beside the foundation vent, opening his canvas tool roll on the dry gravel. His movements were steady, practiced, and unhurried. He selected an insulated ratchet driver and checked the socket head against the brass flare nut on the copper supply tube.

From her vantage behind the water tank, Evelyn observed the installation sequence through her optical monocular. Kroll connected the copper tube to the brass manifold of the timer unit, and tightened the compression fitting with two precise turns of the wrench.

He checked the connection with a digital manometer as he pumped two bars of test pressure into the line to verify that the joint was gas-tight. A professional arsonist using compressed accelerants could not afford a leak that might trigger a premature flash before the electronic timer reached zero.

Once the pressure held steady on the gauge, he reached into his leather vest and retrieved a small sealed glass ampoule that contained five milliliters of yellow liquid. It was the same concentrated nitromethane blend that Evelyn had identified in the laboratory mass spectrometer.

He inserted the ampoule into the chamber above the ceramic glow plug. When the electrical timer discharged its capacitor bank, the heating element would shatter the glass ampoule. This would vaporize the liquid into an explosive aerosol within the enclosed crawl space. The expanding fireball would blow through the timber floorboards directly below the kitchen stove, designed to mimic a catastrophic liquefied petroleum gas cylinder failure.

The design was brilliant, undetectable by standard municipal fire inspectors, and completely fatal to the structure.

Kroll reached into his leather tool roll and produced an electronic digital timer encased in black anodized aluminum. The unit was compact, roughly the size of a handheld multimeter, with a four-digit red display and two brass binding posts protruding from the base.

He adjusted the rotary selector switch on the side of the housing. The display flickered, then began a silent countdown from forty-five minutes.

From her vantage behind the water tank, Evelyn tracked every movement through her monocular. Kroll's actions followed the rigorous procedural protocol of an explosives technician preparing a controlled blast. He secured the unit to the foundation pillar with two strips of reinforced duct tape, checking the clearance between the heating element and the dry subfloor joists with a steel ruler.

Every physical measurement was calibrated to maximize the thermal transfer into the cypress floorboards above. The fire would ignite in complete darkness, consume the structural timbers within seven minutes, and appear to any post-incident investigation as an accidental electrical fire caused by overloaded subfloor wiring.

Evelyn noted the precise placement of the timer housing relative to the prevailing floor joists. Kroll had chosen the central bearer beam of the house, where the ancient red gum timber was sixty years dry and saturated with household oil vapors from decades of country cooking. A blast at this exact focal point would bring down the central chimney stack, crushing the kitchen before the occupants could reach the veranda doors.

The wind shifted three degrees to the south, carrying the scent of distant rain across the dry grass. Evelyn checked the phosphorescent compass dial on her wrist, noting that the atmospheric pressure continued its steady plunge toward nine hundred and ninety millibars.

Evelyn stepped out from behind the water tank.

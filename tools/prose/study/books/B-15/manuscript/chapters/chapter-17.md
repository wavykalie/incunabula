# Chapter 17: The Forty-Five Degree Line

The black asphalt of the Mid-Western Highway had softened under forty-five degrees of heat. The tires of Evelyn's utility truck made a rhythmic, sticky hiss as they peeled bitumen from the expansion joints. Outside the closed cabin, the air was a blast furnace of red dust and eucalyptus vapors, carrying the dry, high-pitched scream of cicadas from the dead timber along the roadside.

She passed the Shire boundary sign for Redgate at one o'clock. The green metal sign was pitted with shotgun pellets and faded to the color of dry moss.

To her left, the main irrigation channel of the Northern Lachlan Scheme ran parallel to the road. The canal was seventy feet wide, an engineered trough of gray reinforced concrete built during the Depression. Today it held four inches of red sludge and five hundred dead carp that had belly-flopped into the shallows when the water stopped flowing in November. Their dried scales shone like silver coins under the sun.

Evelyn checked the coolant gauge on the instrument panel. The needle stood at one hundred and five degrees, two needle-widths below the red warning sector.

She pulled off the road onto a gravel clearing where an abandoned roadhouse stood behind two rusted fuel bowsers. The roof had blown off the garage in the windstorms of eighteen, leaving bare timber rafters that looked like the ribcage of a sheep.

She left the engine idling to keep the cabin air conditioner blowing its thin stream of cool air. She unrolled her cadastral map across the passenger seat.

The map covered forty square miles of the Redgate alluvial plain.

There were three hundred and forty registered rural titles in the parish. But when she cross-referenced the state irrigation registry with the historical Crown grant indices, only four properties had retained their original 1912 water allocations.

Three of those titles were already marked with red grease-pencil crosses in her notebook:

*Oakhaven. Destroyed 2021. Title transferred to Southern Basin Capital.*
*Coram Creek. Destroyed November 2025. Title transferred to Murrumbidgee Development Corporation.*
*Merriwa. Destroyed March 2026. Loss under settlement review.*

That left one.

Her finger traced the blue line of the Lachlan's old anabranch down to Lot 304, Parish of Redgate.

*Sutton. Claire Margaret Sutton.*

The property was twelve hundred acres of red clay loam and saltbush, wedged between the western levee of the government channel and the eastern boundary of Apex Agro-Holdings Orchard Block Nine.

Apex's almond trees were visible from where she sat: a square grid of two hundred thousand green trees stretching to the southern horizon, kept alive by high-pressure drip lines that pumped thirty thousand gallons an hour from deep artesian bores.

Between those green trees and the dry anabranch sat the Sutton homestead.

The title search she had pulled in Wagga showed that Claire Sutton's grandfather had received the grant in 1912 under the Returned Soldiers Settlement Scheme. The grant carried an uncurtailable allocation of five hundred acre-feet per year from the anabranch weir.

And the title was encumbered by a second mortgage to the Commonwealth Bank, with an outstanding balance of four hundred and eighty thousand dollars due for renewal in thirty days.

Evelyn reached for her water bottle. The plastic was warm to the touch.

If the Sutton house burned before the renewal date, the bank would appoint a receiver. The receiver would auction the land without the un-digitized paper title. Apex would buy the parcel at liquidation rates, merge the riparian allocation into Block Nine, and secure enough water to expand their third processing mill.

She stepped out into the blinding white light to inspect the undercarriage of the utility.

The ground temperature radiated through the soles of her heavy leather work boots. A thin film of red limestone dust coated the wheel wells, the brake calipers, and the oil sump guard. She checked the transmission fluid with the dipstick: clear amber, smelling of friction plates but showing no sign of overheating.

In the drainage swale behind the abandoned fuel pumps, three dead river gums stood with their bark hanging in long, dry strips. The leaves had turned brittle as potato crisps, ready to ignite from a single stray spark from an exhaust pipe.

She looked north toward the irrigation pumping station at the weir.

Two large commercial turbines were humming behind chain-link security fences topped with razor wire. The pumps were pulling seventy cubic feet per second from the river pool, forcing the water through three-foot steel pipelines directly into the private holding dams of Apex Block Nine.

A wooden sign was chained to the security gate: *MURRUMBIDGEE BASIN WATER SECURITY INFRASTRUCTURE — AUTHORIZED APEX CONTRACTORS ONLY.*

Public river water converted into private corporate equity behind razor wire.

A sudden mechanical chirp cut through the drone of the cicadas.

Evelyn walked around the eastern perimeter of the Sutton farmyard, where the red earth was baked into hexagonal tiles by three years of unremitting heat. Beside the corrugated equipment shed, a row of mature cypress pines leaned thirty degrees to the southeast, their needles dried to brittle brown tinder by the prevailing desert westerlies. If an ignition occurred along the boundary ditch, a forty-knot wind would drive crown fire through those trees in ninety seconds, throwing firebrands straight onto the weatherboard roof of the main house.

She pulled her mechanical anemometer from her utility vest and held it above her head. The light aluminum cups spun with a dry rattling sound. The digital display registered thirty-two kilometers per hour, with gusts reaching forty-eight. The ambient air temperature sat at forty-one degrees Celsius, while relative humidity hovered at seven percent. Under the McArthur forest fire danger index, those parameters yielded an extreme rating of seventy-eight, well past the threshold where structural suppression becomes impossible once a ceiling collapses.

She examined the foundation ventilation grates under the north veranda. The rusted wire mesh had pulled away from the jarrah baseboards, leaving three open voids eighteen inches wide. If someone placed a mechanical draft box against those openings, a positive-pressure blower would force hot gas through the subfloor cavity within four minutes, pre-heating the bearer timbers to pyrolysis temperature before any flame broke through the external cladding.

Evelyn walked along the western timber wall of the Sutton machinery shed, checking the weatherboards with her inspection knife. The seasoned jarrah was dry and cracked, with resin droplets boiled to the surface by decades of summer sun. A spark from a grinding wheel or a grass fire would ignite that timber in seconds.

Beside the tractor bay, she found an old corrugated iron rainwater tank that had split along its lower seam. She peered inside the rusted hatch. The tank was dry, containing only three inches of red silt and dried eucalyptus leaves.

"We lost the pump two months ago," Claire said, walking up behind her with a pair of fencing pliers in her pocket. "The local repair shop in Griffith told us the replacement parts were on backorder from Melbourne. But when I checked with the distributor, they said Murrumbidgee Rural Finance had placed an account hold on our customer credit."

Beside the orchard boundary, the concrete irrigation flume was cracked along its southern expansion joint. Evelyn bent down to examine the dry bed. A dead hare lay in the dust, its bones bleached white by three months of sun. Under normal operating conditions, that channel carried six cubic feet of water per second, feeding forty family plots between the railway siding and the river.

On the dashboard, the coolant temperature warning light flashed red. The engine management alarm sounded three sharp, piercing beeps as steam hissed from beneath the radiator cap.

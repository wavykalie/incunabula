# Chapter 2: Char and Spall

The wire brush cut through the calcined debris with a harsh rasp. Evelyn worked from the outside edge of the spalled patch toward the center, clearing a circle two feet wide around the floor vent.

The concrete beneath the study hearth told a technical story that no insurance report could soften. When concrete heats slowly, the moisture inside the porous matrix evaporates through microscopic capillaries, escaping as vapor into the room without rupturing the binder. But when an accelerant is applied directly to the slab and ignited under forced ventilation, the pore water flashes to superheated steam in seconds. The steam cannot escape. The vapor pressure exceeds the tensile strength of the concrete, and the surface blows off in violent shards.

She reached for her inspection mirror and the small LED penlight.

The craters in the Mercer slab were clean. The exposed aggregate consisted of crushed river gravel, quartz pebbles, and gray basalt chips. Every basalt fracture showed a fresh, glassy cleavage plane. Basalt was a dense volcanic rock that resisted thermal shock until roughly five hundred and fifty degrees. Above six hundred degrees, the differential expansion between the feldspar and pyroxene crystals tore the rock apart from within.

There was no soot on those cleavage faces. The surface was as clean as if struck by a quarryman's sledgehammer.

She took her photoionization detector from its padded pouch in the kit. The handheld instrument had an ultraviolet lamp rated at ten point six electron volts, calibrated to detect volatile organic vapors in parts per million. She turned the rotary dial and held the Teflon sampling probe half an inch above the spalled crater.

The digital display blinked zero point one parts per million. Then zero point zero.

No petroleum distillates. No kerosene. No diesel fuel. No light aromatic hydrocarbons.

She leaned closer, resting her left hand on the cold brick of the chimney. The smell she had noticed at the perimeter was stronger here. It was thin, sweet, and faintly oily, like the scent of drying vegetable varnish or synthetic lubricants used in aerospace hydraulic lines. It was not petrol. Petrol left a heavy, aliphatic reek that hung in low pockets of brickwork for weeks. It was not diesel. Diesel left thick, greasy smears that refused to dry.

She set the penlight between her teeth and picked up her small hardened-steel cold chisel and brass mallet.

Holding the chisel at a thirty-degree angle against the lip of the largest crater, she struck the head with three light taps. A flake of scorched mortar and concrete aggregate detached cleanly. She caught it in a clean sheet of folded greaseproof paper, slid the paper into a four-ounce glass jar, and pressed the Teflon lid home until the threads locked.

With her diamond-point scribe, she scratched the code onto the glass: *C-01 / MERCER / STUDY SLAB / SPALL*.

The inverted V-pattern against the brick chimney rose exactly five feet from the hearth before terminating at the header joist. She examined the brickwork. The mortar joints were sandy, crumbly, and pinkish-white. Standard brick mortar turns pink only when sustained temperatures surpass eight hundred degrees Celsius.

She measured the apex angle of the burn pattern with her folding protractor. Exactly forty-two degrees.

She sat back on her heels and looked through the gap where the northern wall had stood. Two hundred yards beyond the fence line, the dry irrigation canal stretched toward the horizon like an empty concrete trough, its bed covered in yellow tumbleweeds. Beyond the canal stood the boundary fences of the Riverina Almond Consortium, thousands of acres of young trees standing in neat, drip-fed rows behind three-meter security fencing. Their foliage was deep, glossy green, watered by deep artesian bores and diverted river rights, while the Mercer pasture beside them was bare dirt.

Thomas Mercer had farmed this section for thirty-four years. He had run two hundred head of merino ewes and forty acres of late-season plums until the river allocations were cut to zero four seasons ago.

Evelyn unclasped the fiber-optic inspection probe from her leather technician case. The flexible neck was three feet long, tipped with a micro-camera and twin white LEDs. She slid the probe into the throat of the floor vent, watching the small LCD monitor on her wrist strap.

The subfloor cavity beneath the hearth was dry. The camera picked up the rusted remains of a steel strap, bolted to the underside of the floor joist directly beneath the hearth. The strap did not belong to the nineteen-fifties house framing. It was modern galvanized strapping, eighteen-gauge, secured with hex-head tek screws that still showed bright zinc plating where the heat had not direct contact.

Suspended from that strap had been an apparatus.

The joist above the strap was gouged clean, the wood turned to white ash, while the joists two feet away were only blackened.

She pulled the probe back, coiled the braided cable, and set the tool in her canvas kit.

She picked up her steel inspection hammer. The head was forged carbon steel, two hundred grams, with a tapered pick on the reverse. She swung the flat face against the concrete slab three inches outside the spalled perimeter.

The sound was high, bright, and solid.

She moved the hammer two inches to the left, directly over the spalled depression. She angled the strike to test the subsurface density. If the concrete had been poured over an empty cellar void, the sound would ring hollow across the entire bay. But if an intentional cavity had been excavated under the vent pipe, only the hearth stone would echo.

She brought the face of the hammer down with deliberate force.

She taps the spalled concrete with her inspection hammer, listening to the hollow thud.

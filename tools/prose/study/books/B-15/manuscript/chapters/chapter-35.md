# Chapter 35: The Television Screen

The television in the front parlor was an old cathode-ray tube set with a wood-grain plastic casing, sitting on a corner cabinet beside Claire's family photo albums. The antenna on the roof had been aligned toward the transmitter in Griffith after the wind died down.

At midday, the ABC News special bulletin broke into the regular broadcast.

The studio anchor was seated before a blue digital map of New South Wales.

*Police have arrested prominent corporate risk consultant Raymond Kroll following an overnight operation in the Riverina district,* the anchor said. *Kroll has been charged with four counts of aggravated arson, commercial fraud, and conspiracy to commit murder in connection with a series of suspicious fires across the Murrumbidgee irrigation area.*

The screen cut to video footage recorded an hour earlier outside the Griffith police station.

Kroll was walking between two plainclothes detectives. His head was down, his gray hair uncombed, his dark jacket pulled up around his neck. When a reporter thrust a microphone toward his face, Kroll did not look up. He was placed in the back of an unmarked police wagon, the steel mesh door slamming shut with a heavy mechanical clunk.

*Simultaneous search warrants are currently being executed at the Melbourne headquarters of Apex Agro-Holdings,* the reporter continued over footage of corporate glass towers on Collins Street. *Federal police and investigators from the Australian Securities and Investments Commission have seized servers and financial records as part of a national probe into fraudulent water market speculation.*

Claire Sutton sat on the arm of the sofa, watching the screen in silence.

Evelyn stood by the parlor window.

She looked out across the western paddock toward the ridge where she had camped on Tuesday night. The heat haze was gone. The sky was a pale, clean gray, scoured clear by the southwesterly wind.

For five years, the fire at Oakhaven had been an unresolved calculation in the back of her mind. She had gone over her notebooks a hundred times, wondering whether her tools had failed her, whether her eyes had missed the evidence of human malice in the ashes of the old Italian's cellar.

She had not failed.

The system had simply been designed to swallow the truth and print a settlement receipt.

Claire switched off the television with the plastic power knob. The screen shrank to a small white point of light in the center of the tube, lingered for three seconds, and vanished into darkness.

"They won't get the water now," Claire said.

"No," Evelyn said. "The Supreme Court will place the titles under protective judicial custody tomorrow morning."

Claire looked down at her hands, her knuckles rough and red from fifty years of farm work.

"Thank you, Evelyn."

By midday, three satellite broadcast vans from the national television networks were parked outside the police command building on Railway Street. Microwave transmission dishes rose twenty feet into the white sky, their reflective surfaces shimmering in the desert heat.

Evelyn watched the press conference through the plate glass window of the second-floor detective office. Below on the asphalt, the state police assistant commissioner stood at a bank of microphones beside the state water minister.

"This morning, detectives from the State Crime Command Arson Squad executed twenty-two search warrants across the Riverina and Melbourne," the commissioner announced, his voice amplified through the television speakers. "We have arrested Raymond Kroll, a registered consulting engineer, and filed criminal charges alleging three counts of aggravated arson, one count of attempted arson, and eleven counts of obtaining financial advantage by deception."

The reporters shouted questions in a sharp, overlapping barrage.

"Is it true that senior executives of National Mutual Insurance and Apex Agro-Holdings are under investigation?" an investigative reporter asked from the front row.

"The investigation is ongoing," the commissioner replied. "Search warrants are currently being executed at corporate headquarters in Collins Street, Melbourne, and regional offices in Wagga Wagga. The joint task force has frozen all water entitlement transfers associated with the disputed agricultural holdings."

Evelyn leaned her forehead against the cool glass. Outside on the pavement, the red dust blew across the gutters, dry and relentless. Thirty years after her father had packed his suitcases in Yanco, the financial machinery that destroyed family farms across the basin was finally laid bare under the television floodlights.

Across the street from the courthouse, local farmers gathered in small clusters beneath the dusty peppercorn trees, listening to the radio broadcasts from their truck cabs. Many of them had driven two hours from outlying irrigation settlements along the canal branches, their faces weathered and drawn from three years of drought.

Tom Mercer was among them, standing beside his rusted utility with a copy of the midday newspaper in his hands. The front-page headline was printed in bold black type: *CORPORATE ARSON RING SMASHED IN RIVERINA WATER GRAB*.

Evelyn walked down the stone courthouse steps, carrying her leather briefcase. When Mercer saw her, he folded the newspaper and stepped forward, his eyes bright behind his wire-rimmed glasses.

"They told us on the radio that the insurance claims were being reopened, Miss Brand," Mercer said, his voice trembling slightly.

"Every file is being reassessed under independent oversight, Tom," Evelyn said, resting a hand on his forearm. "The mortgage foreclosures are frozen by Supreme Court injunction. Your land title and your grandfather's water rights are safe."

Mercer looked away across the sunlit street toward the river gums, swallowing hard against a surge of emotion. "My grandfather told me that a man can survive a dry season, but he can't survive a dishonest neighbor. It turns out the neighbor wasn't a farmer down the road; it was a bank in a glass tower."

The television news broadcast showed live helicopter footage of the Apex almond plantations in Griffith, where state police and corporate regulators were serving search warrants on the corporate headquarters. Across the Riverina, regional radio stations were broadcasting continuous updates, reading the names of the family farmers whose water rights had been restored.

Tom Mercer folded his newspaper and looked up at the stone portico of the Wagga Wagga courthouse. "My wife passed away two years ago, Miss Brand. She died believing that we had lost the farm because of bad luck and drought. I wish she was here today to see the truth written on the front page."

Outside the courthouse, two television crews set up their tripod cameras beside the war memorial. The local news presenter adjusted his microphone, preparing for a live cross to the evening bulletin in Sydney. The story had broken into the national news cycle.

"Keep the register locked in the safe," Evelyn said, picking up her field pack from the hall floor. "I have to be in Wagga before the banks close."

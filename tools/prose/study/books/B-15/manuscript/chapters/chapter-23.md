# Chapter 23: The Midnight Choice

The room at the Redgate Railway Hotel smelled of fly spray, old linoleum, and thirty years of cheap tobacco trapped in the horsehair mattress. The single window looked out onto the railway siding where six rusted wheat hoppers sat on dead grass under a yellow sodium vapor streetlamp.

Evelyn sat on the edge of the iron bedstead with her elbows on her knees.

On the small laminate table beside her sat her service credentials, her investigator's leather wallet, her bank passbook, and her field notebook.

The ceiling fan turned slowly overhead, wobbling on its brass rod with a rhythmic, squeaking complaint that sounded like a wounded bird.

She checked her bank balance on her mobile phone. Her savings account held fourteen thousand six hundred dollars. Her superannuation balance was the accumulation of twenty-one years of crawling through burnt basements, breathing asbestos dust, and writing expert reports for the Supreme Court. It stood at four hundred and twelve thousand dollars.

If Holst filed the criminal complaint on Monday, that money would be frozen under the Proceeds of Crime Act.

She would be fifty-two years old, unemployed, stripped of her professional accreditation, facing trial in Sydney with commercial lawyers whose hourly rates exceeded her monthly salary. She would end her life living in a caravan like Frank Miller, drinking warm beer on a gravel stand, waiting for a pension check that barely covered the rent.

And for what?

To save a weatherboard house belonging to a woman she had met twenty-four hours ago? A woman who would likely lose the property to the bank anyway within sixty days when the mortgage defaulted?

She stood up and walked to the window.

Outside, the northwesterly wind was rising. The corrugated iron roof of the railway goods shed rattled in the dark. A gust of hot air blew through the loose window frame, carrying the sharp, alkaline smell of dry clay and distant dust storms.

She remembered her father's hands.

They had been rough, scarred by orange thorns, with calluses so thick he couldn't feel the heat of a tea mug. When the bank took the orchard, he hadn't shouted. He hadn't fought the bailiff. He had walked into the packing shed, set his pruning shears on the workbench, and sat down on an empty wooden crate. He had sat there for three hours in the dark, watching the sun go down over the dead trees.

He had died four years later in a public hospital in Wagga, his heart worn out by sixty years of unpayable debt.

"It is just arithmetic," Holst had said.

Evelyn turned away from the window.

She looked at her field notebook. The blue ink lines connected the three dead homesteads to Apex Agro-Holdings.

If she walked away, Kroll would arrive at the Sutton farm on Friday night. He would place his chemical gel beneath the floor joists. The house would burn to the stumps in four minutes. The paper deed would turn to ash. Claire Sutton would move into a rental unit in Griffith. Holst would get his underwriting bonus. Apex would plant another fifty thousand almond trees.

And the district would pretend, as it had pretended for five years, that drought and bad luck were the only things killing the family farms.

She checked her field pack inventory on the bed.

Two rolls of tamper-evident forensic tape. Twelve sterile sample jars with Teflon-lined caps. A digital thermometer with a surface contact thermocouple. Two rolls of black electrical tape. A handheld UHF transceiver tuned to the emergency band.

She picked up her forensic inspection torch.

It was a heavy, machined aluminum cylinder with a Cree LED emitter that threw twelve hundred lumens of pure white light. She unscrewed the base cap, checked the gold-plated contacts, and dropped four fresh lithium batteries into the tube. She screwed the cap tight.

She pressed the switch.

A brilliant circle of white light splashed across the cracked plaster of the opposite wall, illuminating every fracture, every water stain, every grain of dust with forensic clarity.

She switched off the torch.

She packed her notebook, her evidence bags, and her ultraviolet lamp into her canvas rucksack. She took her car keys from the table.

She left her corporate credit card and her National Mutual identification badge lying flat on the laminate dresser beside the telephone.

The abandoned tractor shed on the boundary of Portion Thirty-Seven had collapsed at one end, its rusted corrugated roof sheets flapping in the night breeze with a hollow metallic clang. Evelyn entered through a gap in the timber cladding, keeping the red filter on her inspection torch turned down to minimum intensity.

Inside, the air smelled of stale diesel, sheep manure, and a sharp, sweet chemical vapor that made her sinuses tingle. In the far corner, half-concealed beneath a rotten canvas tarp, were four twenty-liter plastic drums with tamper-proof dispensing spouts.

She knelt beside the drums and read the manufacturer label stamped on the blue polyethylene casing: *Polydimethylsiloxane Industrial Intermediate, Formulation 88-X*. Beside the drums stood a portable positive-pressure ventilation fan powered by a four-stroke Honda engine, its flexible high-temperature ducting coiled neatly on a steel frame.

She pulled out her digital camera and photographed the equipment from four angles, including a six-inch steel rule in each frame for forensic scale. On the wooden workbench beside the fan lay an electronic timer wired to a ceramic glow plug, identical to the component recovered from the Mercer foundation slab.

The physical chain of evidence was complete. This was the staging cache for the next operation. Kroll had pre-positioned the equipment within three miles of the Sutton homestead, waiting for the weather forecast to confirm forty-knot northwesterly winds and single-digit humidity before initiating the ignition cycle.

Evelyn moved deeper into the shadows of the abandoned tractor shed, her rubber-soled boots stepping over old fan belts and rusted iron cogs. On a wooden shelf behind the tractor battery charger, she found an insulated cooler box taped with yellow packing tape.

She pulled on a fresh pair of nitrile gloves and slit the tape with her pocket knife. Inside the cooler, packed in dry vermiculite granules, were six glass ampoules containing a viscous amber fluid. Each ampoule was labeled with a handwritten identification code: *Catalyst Compound B-7 / High Oxidation Rate / Formulation 2026*.

Beside the ampoules lay an electronic test meter and a technical binder with the title *Thermal Degradation Modeling for Timber Structural Assemblies*. The binder was stamped with the corporate emblem of Kroll Risk Engineering Consultants.

She turned the pages under the red glow of her torch. The manual contained twenty-four pages of finite element calculations, detailing the rate of thermal penetration through seasoned hardwood timbers when exposed to synthetic siloxane combustion. A handwritten table on page eighteen listed the critical ventilation parameters required to achieve total structural collapse in domestic dwellings: *Optimal subfloor draft velocity: four point two meters per second. Ambient humidity window: under ten percent. Estimated time to roof truss compromise: seven minutes.*

Every aspect of the fires was documented in the binder. The calculations proved that the fires were not spontaneous accidents or random grassfires; they were precision thermodynamic demolitions planned with mathematical rigor.

She pulled her camera from her vest and photographed every page of the notebook, including the handwritten chemical formulas and the signature block at the bottom of the final sheet: *Approved by R. Kroll, FIEAust, Registered Forensic Engineer*.

She packed the ampoules and the binder into her evidence pouch, zipped the nylon compartment shut, and backed out of the shed toward the darkness of the surrounding paddocks.

Beside the wooden workbench in the abandoned tractor shed, Evelyn inspected a heavy plastic toolbox containing electrical wiring components. Inside were six high-temperature silicone spark plug leads, a digital multimeter, and three precision solid-state relays rated for twenty amperes.

She examined the manufacturer label on the relays: *Omron Industrial Automation / Component 88-Delta*. The batch numbers matched the electronic invoices discovered in the corporate registry files for Murrumbidgee Rural Finance. Kroll had purchased the components in bulk from an electrical wholesale warehouse in Wagga Wagga, charging the expenses directly to the project stabilization retainer.

She photographed the toolbox and its contents in situ, recording the serial numbers of the relays and the multimeter in her notebook. She dusted the latches of the box with magnetic black powder, lifting two clear friction ridge impressions on clear lifter tape. The ridge detail was sharp and unbroken, showing a distinctive whorl pattern on the thumb impression that would permit direct comparison against Kroll's biometric records on the national criminal database.

She packed the lifted latent impressions into a protective card sleeve, sealed the sleeve in an evidence envelope, and logged the time and location in her register.

Under the floorboards of the abandoned tractor shed, Evelyn discovered an inspection pit that had been covered with old jarrah sleepers. She pried two sleepers aside with her pry bar, shining her inspection torch into the dark brick-lined trench.

Inside the trench sat a portable electric fuel transfer pump connected to a ten-meter length of braided chemical hose. A digital flow meter was mounted on the discharge line, its totalizer display reading three hundred and forty-two liters.

She examined the intake strainer at the end of the hose. The mesh was coated with a thin film of the amber siloxane gel. She collected a swab of the residue using a sterile cotton applicator, sealing the applicator inside a labeled glass test tube.

The discovery of the transfer pump in the inspection pit established that Kroll had prepared multiple fuel charges on site, decanting the bulk chemical drums into portable containers for rapid deployment.

On the wooden workbench, Evelyn found an invoice from an industrial gas supplier in Griffith for three cylinders of compressed nitrogen. Nitrogen gas was commonly employed in high-temperature chemical systems to purge oxygen from fuel lines prior to ignition, preventing premature flashback into the storage containers.

She photographed the cylinder serial numbers and logged the delivery address, which named an agricultural equipment repair shop managed by Kroll's brother-in-law. Every physical transaction was linked by an unbroken paper trail.

Evelyn photographed the shipping manifest taped to the underside of the workbench drawer. The document listed four crates of high-pressure brass solenoid valves imported from a manufacturer in Switzerland, consigned directly to Raymond Kroll at the workshop address.

Solenoid valves of this specification were rated for operation at two hundred degrees Celsius, far exceeding the temperature limits of standard agricultural irrigation equipment. They were built for industrial gas furnaces and petrochemical burner assemblies.

Beside the manifest, she discovered a small digital scale and five empty glass vials with PTFE septa. The vials showed faint yellow residue along the base. She swabbed the inner glass rim with a sterile cotton applicator, sealing the tip in an airtight glass vial for mass spectrometry analysis.

The forensic trail of physical components held together without a single missing link. Every chemical component, every electrical circuit, and every mechanical fitting had been purchased through legitimate commercial channels using accounts funded by National Mutual's underwriting reserves.

Under the second work table, Evelyn discovered three empty cardboard boxes labeled with the manufacturer name of an electrical distributor in Melbourne. The packing slips confirmed the purchase of six solid-state digital relay boards, matching the specific switching components recovered from the Frank Miller scene.

She closed the door behind her without locking it, walked down the creaking wooden stairs to the rear gravel yard, and climbed into the truck.

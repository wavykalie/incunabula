# Chapter 6: Captain Driscoll's Watch

The shed of the Corowa Rural Fire Service stood on the southern edge of town, beside the grain silos and the rusted rail sidings. It was a three-bay prefabricated steel building, painted faded green, its corrugated walls vibrating with the drone of a heavy evaporative cooler mounted over the side door.

Captain Don Driscoll was fifty-eight, with forearms like cured ham and pale blue eyes rimmed with red dust. He was sitting on an upturned twenty-liter foam drum beside the brigade's main tanker, cleaning the grease nipples on a hose reel with an oily rag.

"You're the assessor from National Mutual," Driscoll said without looking up. He didn't offer his hand. His fingers were black with chassis grease.

"Evelyn Brand."

"I already gave the sergeant my report. House was gone when we turned the corner at the three-mile gate. Nothing to save but the diesel tank and the hay shed."

Evelyn leaned against the front bumper of the tanker. The steel was cool in the shade of the bay. "What time did the pager drop?"

"Ten past eleven Monday night." Driscoll tossed the rag into an open steel drum. "Truck rolled at eleven-eighteen. We were on scene at eleven-thirty-two."

"Twenty-two minutes from ignition to arrival," Evelyn said.

"That's about right."

"And when you arrived?"

Driscoll pulled a battered tin of rolling tobacco from his shirt pocket. He extracted a paper, pinched a shred of dark leaf, and rolled a cigarette with one hand. "The roof was already in the dirt. No flames in the grass. No spot fires across the road. The whole place was just burning down into the floorboards like a charcoal pit."

"Was there any wind?"

"Dead calm," Driscoll said. He struck a wooden match on the side of the drum, cupped the flame against the draft of the swamp cooler, and drew smoke into his lungs. "That's what was strange about it. We had thirty knots blowing out of the north all afternoon. But by ten o'clock, the wind died completely. The air was sitting there like warm treacle. Then Mercer's place went up like a furnace."

"Did you check the power lines?"

"Service drop was intact. Fuse at the pole hadn't blown until the roof dropped on the switchboard." Driscoll looked at her through the gray tobacco haze. "You think Tom Mercer burned it?"

"I'm asking what you saw, Don."

"Tom Mercer didn't burn nothing," Driscoll said harshly. He tapped the ash from his cigarette onto the concrete floor. "Tom's sixty-one. His hips are shot. He spent thirty years breaking his back on that red dirt trying to raise sheep on two hundred acres of dry scrub. Two years ago, the water trust shut off his canal gate because the big syndicates down the river bought up all the high-security licenses. Tom didn't have five hundred dollars to buy diesel for his tractor, let alone hire somebody to torch his house."

"He had a new mortgage," Evelyn said in an even tone.

Driscoll snorted. "Yeah. With them operators from Griffith. Some bloke in a silver Land Cruiser spent three weeks parked outside Tom's gate back in July. Kept showing him brochures about luxury retirement units in Albury. Tom told me about it over at the bowling club. Said they offered to pay out all his farm debt if he transferred his nineteen fourteen irrigation title."

"What did Tom say?"

"Told him to go to hell. Said the water belonged to the farm. His grandfather dug that canal with a team of six bullocks in nineteen twelve." Driscoll paused, his pale eyes narrowing as he looked past Evelyn toward the shimmering heat on the road. "Next thing you know, the house is flat and Tom's living in a caravan behind his sister's place in Jerilderie."

Driscoll stood up, walked to the large framed topographic map hanging on the timber wall of the shed, and tapped a grease-blackened finger against the grid coordinates of the parish boundary. "Look at the red circles. Seven properties in this valley have sold out to the Melbourne trusts in the last three years. Every one of them had their water entitlements carved out and piped into the corporate almond blocks. Tom was the last family leaseholder between the main canal weir and the state forest."

He wiped his hands on his overalls. "Tom knew what was coming. He told me three weeks ago that someone had cut his boundary fence and driven a vehicle along his dry canal bank at night. He thought it was pig hunters. But there weren't any dogs, Evelyn. Just a white utility truck that parked by his weir and turned its lights off."

He turned back to face her, his expression grim and exhausted.

Driscoll opened the metal door of an old filing cabinet beside the bench and pulled out a manila folder labeled with the parish fire district numbers. He thumbed through yellowed incident printouts until he found the incident log for the night of the fire.

"Look at the telemetry," Driscoll muttered, tapping a blunt pencil against the column marked Dispatch Code. "Automatic weather station at the grain silos logged relative humidity at eight percent at twenty-two hundred hours. Wind gust speed was zero point two knots. Yet the thermal imaging sensor on Tanker One detected an interior temperature of seven hundred and fifty degrees Celsius through the master bedroom window before the roof truss system failed."

He tossed the incident printout onto the table. "You don't get seven hundred and fifty degrees out of pine furniture in eight minutes without help. I told the police sergeant down at the station, but he just wrote down electrical fault and filed it away."

Driscoll drops his cigarette into an empty oil tin and warns her that big money is buying up the valley.

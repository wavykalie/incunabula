# Chapter 40: The Open Highway

The Mid-Western Highway on Sunday morning was a clean, black ribbon stretching through greening grass. The rain had washed the dust from the bitumen, leaving the road slick and reflective under a sky of brilliant, washed blue.

Evelyn Brand drove west at fifty-five miles an hour in her private Toyota utility.

The truck had no corporate decals on the doors, no company tokens plugged into the dashboard. On the passenger seat sat her tool kit, her NFPA 921 reference volume, her camera case, and a box of fresh inspection notebooks with blank white pages.

In her glove compartment was a formal letter of appointment from the New South Wales Department of Planning and Environment, retaining her as an independent forensic consultant to review historical water entitlement disputes in the lower Darling basin.

She rolled the driver's side window down two inches.

The air that rushed into the cabin was cold, sharp, smelling of wet red loam, damp sheep wool, and wild river mint growing in the ditches along the road.

To her right, the northern canal was running four feet deep with fresh, brown runoff from the catchment hills, swirling past the concrete culverts with a steady, quiet rush.

A flock of white cockatoos erupted from a dead river gum beside the road, wheeling across the blue sky in a wide, screaming circle before diving down toward the greening paddocks.

Evelyn reached forward to the dashboard.

The Toyota Land Cruiser cruised south along the Hume Highway at one hundred kilometers an hour, the windshield wipers sweeping a steady arc against the gray road spray.

On either side of the dual carriageway, the drought-stricken hills of the southern Riverina were already changing color. The washed red soil had darkened to deep terracotta, and in the road verges, tiny green shoots of spear grass were pushing up through the damp silt. Every creek along the boundary was running bank-full, the brown floodwater swirling past the weathered bridge pilings.

Evelyn switched off the two-way communications radio on the dashboard. The digital display blinked once and went dark, leaving the cab in the steady mechanical drone of the diesel engine and the rhythmic hiss of tires on wet bitumen.

In the passenger footwell, her canvas field kit sat upright, packed with clean sample vials, calibrated calipers, and three fresh field notebooks with blank white pages.

She adjusted her rearview mirror. The rain clouds stretched north across the horizon for two hundred miles, covering the valley from Wagga Wagga to the Victorian border.

She pressed down lightly on the accelerator, watching the road ahead open and clear under the morning sun.

The Hume Highway crossed the Murray River on a high concrete bridge outside Albury. Below, the river was running fast and wide, swollen with storm runoff from the Snowy Mountains and the western slopes.

Evelyn rolled down the driver's side window of the Land Cruiser, letting the cool, damp river air fill the cab. The smell of coal smoke and burned gypsum plaster was completely gone, replaced by the clean scent of wet river gums, pine plantations, and green clover.

On the passenger seat lay a white envelope from the State Crime Command, containing her formal appointment as senior technical consultant to the joint arson task force. The inquiry would take months, covering dozens of disputed commercial files across two states.

She turned the radio dial to a regional station, listening to the weather update. The bureau reported widespread rainfall across the entire southern basin, with eighty millimeters recorded in Wagga Wagga and further storms forecast throughout the week.

She switched the radio off, preferring the quiet rhythm of the road.

She clicked the rotary switch of the windshield wipers, turning off the intermittent sweep as the last drops evaporated from the glass, leaving the road ahead open and clear under the morning sun.

# Chapter 14: The Caravan Stand

The Riverside Caravan Park sat on a bend of the dry Murrumbidgee three miles outside town. A line of dead pepper trees offered no shade to the dozen battered caravans parked on cracked gravel stands. The communal amenities block had two broken shower screens and smelled of municipal chlorine and stale bore water.

Evelyn found Frank Miller sitting on a folding aluminum chair under the faded striped annex of a 1984 Viscount caravan.

He wore gray cotton work trousers held up by suspenders and a faded blue singlet that showed skin tanned to the color of saddle leather. His knuckles were swollen with arthritis, his fingernails split from fifty years of fencing and tractor maintenance. On the plastic table beside him sat a warm six-pack of Carlton Draught and an enamel mug half-filled with cold tea.

"You're the insurance woman," Miller said. His voice was gravelly, worn thin by wind and red dust. He did not look up from the beer can he was holding.

"Evelyn Brand," she said. "National Mutual."

"Your boss called me yesterday," Miller said. "Mr. Holst. He said the claim was being finalized in Sydney. Said I'd have the paperwork to sign on Monday."

"I need to ask you about your water title, Frank."

Miller set the can down with a dull plastic clatter. He looked at her then, his pale gray eyes watery behind red-rimmed lids.

"What about it? It burnt. Everything in the house burnt. My grandfather's First World War medals. My wife's wedding silver. The whole bloody lot."

"Where did you keep the 1912 deed?"

Miller narrowed his eyes. "In the tin box in the bottom of the wardrobe. Where my father kept it. Where his father kept it. What difference does it make? You're paying out on the timber and the tin, aren't you?"

"Did anyone approach you about that deed before the fire?"

Miller picked up the beer can again. He took a long swallow, his Adam's apple bobbing above the stretched collar of his singlet.

"Three times," he said.

"Who?"

"Young blokes in white shirts with company clipboards. Drove out from Griffith in air-conditioned Land Cruisers. Said they represented a regional water trust. Offered me two hundred thousand for the paper."

"Two hundred thousand dollars?"

"Cash," Miller spat. "Told me I was sitting on a dry paddock that hadn't grown a wheat crop since twenty-sixteen. Said the water was going to waste while the almond groves up the river were dying on the branch. Told me I could take the two hundred grand and retire to the coast."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them to get off my property before I got the dog onto them," Miller said. His voice rose, shaking with old anger. "That water belonged to my grandfather. He dug the irrigation ditches with a horse scoop in nineteen-fourteen. The Crown gave him that grant for serving in the Light Horse. It wasn't theirs to buy."

"When was the last visit?"

"Ten days before the fire," Miller said. "A different bloke came that time. Older. Didn't wear a tie. Wore clean work boots and an engineer's vest. Had an iPad in a heavy rubber case."

Evelyn leaned forward against the annex pole. "Did he give a name?"

"Called himself Raymond. Said he was an independent valuation consultant. Didn't talk about buying the title. Just asked a lot of questions about the house."

"What kind of questions?"

"Asked about the subfloor," Miller said, staring out across the dusty caravan park. "Asked if we had cypress stumps or concrete piers. Asked where the main electrical board was. Asked if the floor joists had been treated with creosote back in the fifties. I thought he was from the council doing a bushfire risk survey."

"Did he look under the house?"

"I showed him the crawlspace hatch on the west side," Miller whispered. His knuckles whitened around the aluminum can. "I showed him right where the old well was. God help me, I took him around the back and pointed out the bearers."

He crushed the empty can in his hand. The thin metal crumpled with a sharp, violent crack.

"They burned my home, didn't they?" Miller looked at her, his jaw trembling. "Those corporate bastards burned it down so they could steal the river."

Miller wiped the sweat from his forehead with the back of his hand, leaving a smear of gray soot across his brow.

"My grandfather planted those river red gums along the boundary ditch in nineteen-twenty," Miller said, pointing through the screen door toward the dry river flats. "He carried the seedlings in wet burlap sacks from the nursery in Hay on the back of a packhorse. He built the irrigation intake with red bricks he baked in a pit kiln on the riverbank."

"Did Kroll ask about the intake?" Evelyn asked.

"He asked where the diversion gate connected to the main channel," Miller replied. "He wanted to know if the gate was controlled by an electric motor or an old hand-wheel. I told him it was an iron wheel that hadn't been greased since twenty-two. He wrote that down on his tablet. He asked if the water commission still sent an inspector out to check the weir level."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him no inspector had set foot on Merriwa since the regional office in Griffith was closed by the state government in twenty-four," Miller said. "He smiled when I told him that. He said that was good to know. Said it made the restructuring much simpler."

Miller reached into the cardboard carton beneath the annex table and pulled out a fresh can of beer. The pull-tab snapped with a sharp hiss.

"They're buying up the whole valley, aren't they, miss?"

"They are buying the water," Evelyn said. "The land is just the dirt holding the pipe."

Evelyn took the sealed evidence envelope from her jacket. She did not open it. She held it so the plastic window showed the charred corner of the 1912 vellum and the purple ink seal.

"They tried to burn it," Evelyn said. "They didn't finish the job."

Miller stared at the parchment. His eyes widened in the harsh sunlight that bounced off the caravan's aluminum skin. A single tear tracked through the dust on his cheek, cutting a pale gray line through the red dirt.

"Don't sign Holst's release on Monday, Frank," she said.

"What am I supposed to live on?"

"The police are opening a criminal investigation," she said. "If you sign the release, the title passes to the mortgagee. If the mortgagee takes title, the water allocation dissolves. Hold out for two weeks."

Miller looked down at his crushed beer can. Then he nodded once, a short, stiff drop of his chin.

Instead, we have high-molecular-weight siloxanes combined with a cross-linked polyacrylamide matrix."

If an assessor inspects the scene looking for petroleum residues with a photoionization detector, the instrument reads clean air."

Dr. Chen clicked his computer mouse to rotate the molecular diagram on the screen. The bonds between the silicon and oxygen atoms rotated in three dimensions, rendered in bright green wireframes.

"Notice how the cyclic structure protects the polymer chain from thermal depolymerization until temperature exceeds eight hundred degrees," Chen said.

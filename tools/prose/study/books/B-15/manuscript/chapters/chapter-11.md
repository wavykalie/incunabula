# Chapter 11: The Boardroom Audit

The executive boardroom smelled of beeswax polish and chilled mineral water. The long jarrah table reflected the recessed ceiling spotlights in six concentric ovals. At the far end, two flags stood on polished brass poles: the Australian national flag and the corporate standard of National Mutual, green with a golden sheaf of wheat.

Warren Holst sat with his back to the panoramic window. Behind him, fifty miles of yellow paddocks stretched away toward the Cocoparra Range under a dome of cloudless white sky.

"You are presenting an allegation of criminal conspiracy, Evelyn," Holst said. He turned a page of her preliminary report. The paper made a clean, sharp rustle in the silent room. "Against an entity with a three-hundred-million-dollar credit facility with our primary reinsurance syndicate."

Evelyn Brand stood beside the projection screen. Her laser pointer cast a small red dot onto the enlarged photograph of the foundation piles at Merriwa.

"The physical evidence does not consult credit facilities, Warren," she said. Her mouth was dry. She took a sip of tap water from a tumbler she had brought from the second-floor kitchenette. "Look at the calcination pattern on pile four. The concrete spalled inward toward the central vent. That indicates a radiant heat source positioned directly beneath the bearers, shielded from prevailing winds."

Holst did not look at the screen. He kept his eyes on the bound report.

"The local fire captain signed off on an electrical fault in the hot water service," he said.

"The hot water service was located on the eastern wall, thirty feet away," Evelyn said. "Its copper pipes were intact. The solder had melted at four hundred and fifty degrees, which is normal for a structural fire. But under the crawlspace hatch, the steel reinforcing rod in the stump had sagged. Steel does not deform under dead load until temperature reaches six hundred degrees Celsius. To soften nine-millimeter rebar in four minutes requires an accelerant."

Holst stood up and walked to the credenza behind his chair. He picked up a leather-bound folio stamped with the seal of the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority.

"Our treaty with Swiss Re carries an aggregate retention of forty million dollars," Holst said, resting his hand on the folio. "Under Clause Fourteen, if an underwriting branch issues a formal declinature based on arson without securing an indictment from the state director of public prosecutions within one hundred and eighty days, the primary insurer bears the entire exposure without reinsurance indemnity. Do you comprehend the arithmetic, Evelyn? If we decline the Mercer claim and Apex files suit in the Supreme Court, National Mutual defends the action alone at eighty thousand dollars a week in counsel fees."

"The physical evidence is governed by the National Fire Protection Association Code 921, Warren," Evelyn replied. "Section eighteen point four explicitly defines the inverted cone pattern as a primary indicator of localized accelerant placement. The depth of calcination in the concrete slab at Merriwa reached forty-five millimeters directly beneath the crawlspace bearer. In an accidental electrical fire, concrete calcination rarely exceeds eight millimeters because the thermal plume rises toward the ceiling plane. Here, the heat was driven downward by a mechanical blower."

Holst smiled, though his pale eyes remained completely flat. "A mechanical blower. You intend to stand in the witness box before Justice Carruthers and testify that an international agricultural investment partnership installed fans in a farmer's crawlspace to burn down three hundred thousand dollars of weatherboard and iron?"

"Apex did not want the weatherboard and iron," Evelyn said, keeping her voice completely steady. "Apex wanted the water license attached to the property title. The mortgage contract contains an acceleration clause triggered by structural destruction of the primary residence. When the house burns, the farmer defaults. When the farmer defaults, the mortgage holder takes the land and the senior 1914 irrigation title transfers to their corporate water trading bank."

Holst took off his titanium glasses and laid them beside his crystal water glass. Without the lenses, his eyes appeared larger, paler, the pale gray-blue of stagnant river ice.

"In ninety-three," Holst said, his voice level and cold, "when the State Bank went under, every farmer between Leeton and Narrandera blamed the receivers. They blamed the currency market. They blamed the Asian financial crisis. They said the bank valuers were colluding with corporate syndicates to buy land at forty cents on the dollar."

Evelyn felt a tight pulse in her throat. "My father was one of those farmers."

"I know he was," Holst said. "I read your personnel file when you transferred from the forensic branch in Melbourne. Your father had eighty acres of Valencia oranges on the Yanco canal. He borrowed against his water allocation to install drip lines. Then the interest rate hit seventeen percent. The bank foreclosed. He lost the orchard."

"He didn't lose it," Evelyn said, keeping her voice level. "He was forced out by a statutory margin call three days before the harvest was weighed."

"The market cleared the debt," Holst said, leaning back. "That is what capital does, Evelyn. It moves water and land from operators who cannot service the debt to operators who can. It is not malice. It is arithmetic."

"Arson is not arithmetic," she said.

She remembered the winter of ninety-three. The smell of rotten citrus hanging over the irrigation ditches. Her father sitting at the oilcloth table in the kitchen with a yellow legal pad, calculating cubic meters of ditch water against compounding monthly interest until his pencil lead snapped. Two months later, the bailiff had arrived with a police escort from Griffith. Her father had stood on the gravel driveway holding an iron spanner, watching the auctioneer stick red repossession stickers on the tractor and the spray vat.

"If Apex wants the water," Evelyn said, "they can buy it on the open exchange like every family farm in the district."

"Apex operates on four-year forward contracts," Holst replied smoothly. "They supply sixty percent of the export almond market to Japan and Germany. They have half a million trees that require eighteen megalitres a day during the flowering cycle. If those trees die of drought stress, the corporate loss is seventy million dollars in capital assets alone. Do you think a board in London or Melbourne cares about a ninety-year-old water register in a grazier's linen cupboard?"

"So you know," Evelyn said.

Holst looked at her for three seconds without blinking. The grandfather clock in the corner of the boardroom ticked with a heavy brass clunk.

"I know that an insurance policy is a contract of indemnity," Holst said. "We indemnify against documented financial loss. Frank Miller will receive his payout. He will move into town. He will buy a brick villa with an air conditioner and spend his retirement playing lawn bowls. Apex will acquire the parcel from the liquidator. The district's export volume will remain stable. And our branch will meet its underwriting quota."

"And the three other fires?" Evelyn asked. "Bartoli at Oakhaven. Nancy Vance at Coram Creek. Both took settlements under duress after losing their houses to fires that burned identical fuel profiles."

Holst picked up a second folder from his leather blotter. He slid across a copy of the reinsurance treaty signed between National Mutual and Southern Basin Reinsurance.

"The treaty specifies loss ratios, Evelyn. It does not invite philosophical post-mortems on rural sociology. When an agricultural asset is underperforming and debt coverage falls below one-point-two, the probability of structural ignition increases by three hundred percent across all drought basins. It is an actuarial baseline established thirty years ago by Lloyd's of London."

"You are quoting fraud statistics to justify corporate arson," Evelyn said.

"I am explaining the economic machinery that pays your monthly salary," Holst replied. "You have twenty-one years of accrued defined-benefit superannuation in our staff trust fund. That fund is invested in commercial rural bonds issued by the Murrumbidgee Irrigation Authority. If Apex defaults on its bulk water service fees, that bond yield drops to zero. Your pension pool dissolves along with the farm mortgages."

Evelyn pointed at the projected slide of the foundation stump. "The calcination depth on that concrete pier is eighteen millimeters. According to section nineteen of NFPA 921, that level of thermal dehydration requires eight hundred degrees maintained for thirty continuous minutes. The Miller house was built of dry cypress timber that burned out in twelve minutes flat. The heat source was not the house framing."

"A technical nuance," Holst said, waving his hand toward the ceiling spotlights. "No magistrate in the Riverina understands concrete petrography. If the volunteer captain writes 'accidental electrical fire,' the Coroner will accept the certificate without scheduling a hearing. You are creating procedural friction where none exists."

"The State Crime Command has opened a preliminary assessment file," she said.

Holst's eyes narrowed. The titanium frames gleamed under the overhead lamps.

"Alan Croft in Parramatta is an administrator," Holst said. "He has sixteen unresolved arson homicides in Western Sydney on his desk this morning. He is not going to allocate three detectives and a mobile forensic laboratory to inspect blackened stumps in Merriwa because an underwriting investigator has personal childhood issues with farm foreclosures."

"He will if I provide the gas chromatography spectra," Evelyn said.

Holst leaned his elbows on the table and laced his fingers together. His gold signet ring tapped once against the polished jarrah.

"You have twenty-four hours, Evelyn. If your final report is not logged in the internal claims system by five o'clock tomorrow afternoon with an endorsement of accidental loss, I will exercise my executive discretion under section four of your employment contract. Your access to the Wagga branch will terminate, your security token will be revoked, and your active files will be assigned to external assessors."

"External assessors like Raymond Kroll?" she asked.

Holst did not flinch. His expression remained smooth, polished, unyielding.

"Kroll is an accredited consultant with twenty-five years of forensic practice," Holst said. "He knows how to conclude an inquiry without generating unnecessary commercial liabilities."

He closed the manila folder with a dry, sharp clap.

"Take twenty-four hours," Holst said. "Consult your professional interests."

Holst stood up and poured two fingers of iced water into his crystal tumbler. The ice cubes clinked against the glass with a sharp, brittle sound that echoed through the high-ceilinged room.

"In ninety-one," Holst said, staring out at the Cocoparra Range, "the state government reorganized water allocations under the Murray-Darling Basin Agreement. They told the regional boards that water was no longer a public utility attached to farmland, but a tradeable financial commodity. That single policy decision transformed irrigation rights into speculative assets. Capital syndicates in Sydney and London bought up forty percent of the senior entitlements within five years."

He turned back to face her, his face framed against the blinding glare of the white sky. "The family farmers in this valley were operating on nineteenth-century economics in a twenty-first-century financial market. They held senior water titles worth millions of dollars while drowning in mortgage debt. Apex did not invent that imbalance, Evelyn. Apex simply recognized that the water had to move to commercial agribusiness operators who possessed the capital to build high-efficiency pipelines."

Evelyn stepped toward the boardroom table, looking directly at Holst. "You can cancel my access tokens, Warren. You can terminate my regional authority under clause four of the employment agreement. But the physical samples are already logged in the state crime laboratory, and the chain of custody is filed with the coroner's office. An executive committee cannot vote away a gas chromatograph readout."

Evelyn switched off the projector. The cooling fan in the unit whined to a stop, leaving the boardroom in absolute silence. She gathered her slides, slipped her notebook into her vest pocket, and walked toward the door without looking back.

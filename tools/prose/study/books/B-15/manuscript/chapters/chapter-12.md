# Chapter 12: The Sydney Spectrum

The monitor in the regional laboratory cubicle showed a split screen. On the left was Detective Senior Sergeant Alan Croft from the State Crime Command Arson Squad in Parramatta. On the right was Dr. Marcus Chen, chief forensic chemist at the Lidcombe laboratory.

Behind Dr. Chen, rows of gas chromatography mass spectrometers stood against white tiled walls, their status lights flashing green and amber.

"We ran the core samples from the Merriwa subfloor," Chen said, tapping his keyboard. A line graph replaced his video feed. The graph showed a flat horizontal baseline with two sharp, narrow peaks rising like needle points at eleven and fourteen minutes.

Evelyn leaned toward her camera. "Where are the petroleum hydrocarbons?"

"There are none," Chen said. His voice was tinny through the desk speakers. "No diesel. No unleaded petrol. No kerosene or mineral turpentine. If this had been an amateur job with a jerry can, I would have seen the typical bell curve of weathered n-alkanes between C9 and C22."

"Look at the second peak," Evelyn said.

"Retention time 14.2 minutes," Chen said. "Mass-to-charge ratio 279, followed by 149 and 167. It is a synthetic phthalate ester. But not the plasticizer you find in commercial polyvinyl chloride piping."

Croft's voice cut in from the Parramatta window. "Speak plain English, Marcus. Did someone torch the place or not?"

"Someone used a specialized high-density chemical gel," Chen said. "The carrier is a synthetic polyol ester thickened with aluminum octanoate. It is designed to burn at twelve hundred degrees without producing visible smoke or petroleum soot. When it combusts, it breaks down into carbon dioxide, water vapor, and microscopic aluminum oxide dust that looks identical to wood ash."

Evelyn wrote the technical terms in her field notebook: *polyol ester, aluminum octanoate*.

"Who manufactures it?" she asked.

"Nobody sells it over the counter," Chen said. "It is an industrial thermal compound. It was originally patented in the late nineties for clearing pipeline blockages in oil refineries. You ignite it with a low-voltage nichrome wire or an electronic match. It produces an intense, localized heat lance that burns through steel casing in less than ninety seconds."

"A military-grade thermite substitute," Croft murmured.

"More sophisticated than thermite," Chen replied. "Thermite leaves iron slag and magnetic residue that any first-year arson investigator finds with a handheld magnet. This compound leaves almost nothing. If Evelyn hadn't chipped the mortar out from between the foundation bricks directly below the bearer, we never would have caught the ester trace."

"What about the thermal degradation profile?" Evelyn asked. "Could it be mistaken for an electrical short circuit?"

"Only by an investigator who didn't take microscopic core samples," Chen said. "An electrical arc produces localized copper beading with a sharp boundary zone. This compound creates an expansive radiant flux that anneals surrounding iron reinforcement and dissolves zinc plating without leaving carbonaceous soot."

"How much would it cost to synthesize?" Croft asked.

"The raw chemical precursors are controlled under schedule seven of the poisons standard," Chen said. "You cannot purchase them without a commercial laboratory license and an environmental clearance certificate. Whoever mixed this had access to a certified chemical facility with vacuum distillation columns."

"Have you got names, Evelyn?" Croft asked.

"Not on the police record yet," she said. "The corporate connections run through four layers of proprietary limited companies. But the pattern is identical across three properties: Oakhaven, Coram Creek, and Merriwa."

"Three properties don't make a brief of evidence," Croft said. "I need an identifiable suspect with physical custody of the accelerant, or a paper trail leading directly to a bank account. Without that, the Director of Public Prosecutions won't touch it. Especially if the victim has already accepted the insurance payout."

"Frank Miller hasn't signed the release yet," Evelyn said.

"He will," Croft replied. "A farmer with no house and five years of drought debt does not fight the insurance company. He takes the settlement so the bank doesn't take his utility truck."

Dr. Chen displayed a second comparative graph.

"Here is the sample from the 2021 Oakhaven fire," Chen said, zooming into the chromatogram baseline. "Five years old. We pulled the retained paraffin wax block from the state archives in Lidcombe. Notice the retention peak at 14.2 minutes. The identical phthalate ester profile. The identical absence of petroleum aromatics."

Evelyn wrote down the archive accession number: *LC-2021-VAR-892*.

"Two scenes separated by five years and sixty miles," Croft said on the Parramatta line. "That eliminates random amateur arson. That is a signature."

"A signature that costs forty thousand dollars a shot to formulate," Chen added. "You don't buy polyol ester gel at a farm supply depot in Griffith."

"Keep the spectral raw data on the server, Marcus," Evelyn told the chemist. "Do not archive the file."

"The data is logged," Chen said. "Chain of custody number 88-Delta-401. But be careful, Evelyn. A compound like that doesn't come out of a farm shed. Whoever is making it knows exactly what the forensic standards look for, and exactly how to beat them."

Claire Sutton traced the edge of her coffee mug with a calloused thumb. "When the state parliament decoupled water rights from farm titles under the National Water Initiative back in 2004, the politicians promised it would create market liquidity. They told us farmers could sell surplus water in dry seasons to pay down bank loans. What actually happened was that corporate syndicates bought up thousands of megaliters of high-security water and parked it in private accounts, waiting for drought years to lease it back to us at twelve hundred dollars a megaliter."

Evelyn watched the dust swirl past the cafe window. "And Murrumbidgee Rural Finance offered mortgages secured against those historical allocations."

"They offered survival," Claire said with bitter precision. "When the commercial banks refused to extend our seasonal credit lines three years ago, Apex sent their field agents down every dirt track between Griffith and Deniliquin with pre-approved loan packages."

The video call disconnected. The screen returned to the gray desktop. Evelyn closed her notebook, clicked the ballpoint pen shut, and slid it into her vest pocket.

# Chapter 38: The Courthouse Steps

The Supreme Court building in Wagga Wagga was built of golden sandstone blocks quarried from the river hills in 1888. A broad flight of twelve stone steps led down to Fitzmaurice Street, flanked by two iron gas lamps that had been converted to low-pressure sodium lights.

The autumn air on Friday afternoon was crisp and sharp. The temperature was sixteen degrees, the wind blowing from the southwest with the fresh, clean scent of alpine rain.

Evelyn walked out through the heavy cedar doors after four hours of committal testimony.

Warren Holst was standing on the bottom landing beside the stone balustrade. He wore a dark navy overcoat of heavy wool, buttoned to the throat against the breeze. His legal counsel, a junior barrister in a horsehair wig and black silk gown, was fifty feet away near the curb, talking into a mobile phone.

Holst turned as Evelyn reached the lower steps.

"The magistrate committed the matter for trial in Sydney," Holst said. His voice was quiet, lacking the resonant authority it had carried in the boardroom.

"I was in the gallery," Evelyn said.

"They won't offer bail on Monday," Holst said, looking down at his polished leather shoes. "The prosecutor raised flight risk because of the Singapore accounts. I will spend eighteen months in a correctional center at Junee waiting for a trial date."

"You chose the accounts, Warren."

Holst looked at her, his pale blue eyes clear behind his titanium spectacles.

"I knew the fires were too clean, Evelyn. Right from the first file at Oakhaven."

"Why did you sign the release?"

"Because the firm needed the reinsurance treaty," Holst said, pulling his coat collar higher against the wind. "Apex had five hundred million dollars in capital under management. If National Mutual lost their account, our regional branch would have been closed by head office in twenty-twenty-two. Every underwriter, every clerk, every claims assessor in this building would have been redundant."

"So you burned the farmers to protect the branch."

"I protected the institution," Holst said. "That is what managers do. We absorb the moral deficit so the organization survives. You never understood that, Evelyn. You always thought the law was an instrument of justice. The law is simply an operating manual for the distribution of property."

"It distributed you to Junee," she said.

Holst smiled, a small, sad grimace that barely moved his lips.

"For now," he whispered. "In five years, I will be out on parole. Apex will have reorganized under chapter eleven, the water rights will have been auctioned by the receiver, and the almond trees will still be drinking forty thousand megalitres from the canal. Nothing stops the arithmetic."

His barrister waved from the curb. An unmarked silver sedan pulled up to the stone steps, its hazard lights flashing.

At the Merriwa agricultural showgrounds, the afternoon sun cast long golden shadows across the dry sheep pavilions and rusted corrugated iron exhibition sheds. Frank Miller sat on the timber tailgate of his stock crate, holding a white paper envelope containing a formal letter from the New South Wales Department of Planning and Environment.

"They cancelled the mortgage foreclosure," Miller said, looking up at Evelyn with eyes that were wet with tears. "The minister signed an executive order nullifying the transfer of Portion Thirty-Two. The water allocation is restored to the original Crown title."

Claire Sutton leaned against the timber rail beside him, holding a thermos of black coffee. "The regional legal aid service is filing a class action against Apex in the Supreme Court on Monday. We are seeking damages to rebuild Frank's house and replant the boundary shelterbelts."

Evelyn accepted a tin mug of coffee from Claire. The liquid was hot, strong, and bitter with the taste of chicory.

"It will take three seasons to bring the citrus back into bearing, Frank," Evelyn said, looking out over the parched arena where dry thistles blew against the wire boundary fences.

"I have the time, miss," Miller said, folding the government letter and placing it carefully in his shirt pocket. "A man who was born on this dirt has all the time in the world."

Behind the Merriwa show pavilion, Claire Sutton unrolled a blue surveyor map across the timber bench. The map displayed the proposed community irrigation pipeline, a cooperative project designed to connect six family holdings directly to the main river weir.

"We formed a growers cooperative on Wednesday," Claire said, her eyes shining with determination. "Instead of selling our allocations to offshore investment funds, we are pooling our water rights under a common trust owned by the farmers. The state infrastructure fund has agreed to provide matching grants for pressurized pipeline construction."

Frank Miller nodded in agreement, leaning forward on his cane. "No more open dirt ditches. No more water lost to the sand. We are going to install drip lines under every orange tree between here and the creek."

Evelyn looked at the cooperative charter. The signatures at the bottom were those of the families who had worked the valley for three generations: Sutton, Miller, Mercer, Callan, Henderson.

"It will be hard work, Claire," Evelyn noted with a faint smile.

"We know all about hard work, Evelyn," Claire replied, folding the survey plan. "What we couldn't survive was being robbed in the dark by men in polished boots."

Claire Sutton walked to the edge of the show ring, looking out at the low hills to the west where the first storm clouds were gathering. "The banks thought we were helpless because we didn't have lawyers in Collins Street. But they forgot that people who work the land know how to stand together when the storm hits."

Frank Miller folded his hands across the top of his cane, watching a pair of dust devils spin across the dry showground arena. "For two years, people in town looked at me like I was a failed man who couldn't manage his farm. Today they know the truth. That is worth more to me than any insurance check."

Holst buttoned his top overcoat button with a steady hand, turned away from her, and walked down to the waiting car without looking back.

# Chapter 18: The Filtering Matrix

Evelyn shut down the engine before the head gasket could blow. She stepped out into the heat, popped the hood latch, and wedged the steel prop rod into place. A cloud of sweet-smelling ethylene glycol vapor rose from the overflow tank, condensing on the dusty front grille.

She let the radiator hiss into the stillness while she set up her mobile workspace on the truck's tailgate.

She opened her Panasonic Toughbook. The ruggedized laptop was heavy, housed in a thick magnesium-alloy case designed to withstand mine blasts and desert heat. The battery indicator showed three hours of runtime remaining.

She did not connect to the cellular network; Holst's audit memo had warned her that her corporate credentials would be flagged by the IT security operations center in Melbourne. Instead, she opened her local cache of the Riverina Cadastral Geographic Information System database, downloaded during her last forensic review in Griffith.

She opened an offline query window.

She built a structured filter using four parameters:

*Param 1: Senior riparian right registered prior to January 1, 1914.*
*Param 2: Physical residence constructed with timber or cypress stump subfloor.*
*Param 3: Distance to Apex commercial holdings less than five kilometers.*
*Param 4: Current mortgage status classified as non-performing or in default review.*

She pressed Enter.

The hard drive clicked rhythmically as the query searched sixty-five thousand parcel records across four river basins.

The screen refreshed.

*Records matched: 4.*

*Record 1: Lot 82, Parish of Oakhaven (Bartoli). Structural destruction verified, title canceled.*
*Record 2: Lot 211, Parish of Coram (Vance). Homestead burned, liquidation completed.*
*Record 3: Lot 14, Parish of Merriwa (Miller). Fire loss logged, settlement pending review.*
*Record 4: Lot 304, Parish of Redgate (Sutton). Physical structure intact, mortgage in default.*

Evelyn leaned her hip against the tailgate.

There was no ambiguity. The selection was an operational checklist.

Kroll had not chosen these properties by chance or convenience. He was executing a target list derived from corporate debt audits. Someone inside the regional banks or the underwriting syndicate was feeding credit default data to Apex's legal representatives. When a senior titleholder entered the ninety-day default window, the property was flagged.

If the owner refused to sell the riparian deed voluntarily, the technical consultant conducted a preliminary site survey.

He checked the joists. He checked the crawlspace vents. He identified the dead-air pockets beneath the weatherboards where a high-density polyol ester charge could ignite without leaving an exterior scorch mark before flashover.

She pulled up the technical schematics for the Sutton property from the state valuation archive.

The homestead had been built in 1928 by Sergeant Thomas Sutton using local red river gum stumps and dressed Murray pine weatherboards. The central hallway measured eight feet across, creating a natural chimney effect that connected the front veranda to the rear laundry. Underneath the timber floorboards, the subfloor clearance varied from fourteen inches at the front steps to thirty-six inches beneath the rear kitchen.

That thirty-six-inch void was the vulnerability.

It provided ample space for an intruder to slide an eight-pound chemical block beneath the main floor bearers without lifting floorboards inside the residence.

She reviewed the fire behavior calculations in her spreadsheet.

Ambient air temperature: forty-six degrees Celsius. Fuel moisture content of fifty-year-old Murray pine: four-point-two percent. Wind speed: thirty knots from the northwest.

Under those conditions, the ignition energy required to achieve sustained flaming combustion in seasoned cypress framing was less than forty kilojoules per square meter. A single four-pound block of polyol ester gel released twelve thousand kilojoules within sixty seconds of electrical bridge-wire detonation.

The time to total flashover: one hundred and eighty seconds.

Escape time for an occupant asleep in the western bedroom: seventy seconds before carbon monoxide concentration passed twelve hundred parts per million.

She looked across the shimmering plain toward the south.

Two miles away, past the line of dead river oaks, the silver roof of the Sutton homestead glinted like a scrap of tin in the sun.

She opened the secondary database containing the regional weather radar models.

The cold front from the Great Australian Bight was moving eastward at forty knots. The leading edge of the low-pressure trough was forecast to cross the Lower Lachlan basin between ten o'clock Friday evening and two o'clock Saturday morning.

It was the exact meteorological signature of the previous three fires.

Every blaze had been set during the peak barometric drop immediately preceding the wind change, when humidity plummeted below ten percent and wind gusts exceeded thirty-five knots. The violent airflow disguised the sound of ignition and ensured that volunteer brigades could not enter the property before the roof structure collapsed.

She took a photoionization sensor from her equipment case and checked its battery level. Full charge.

She took a gallon plastic jug of distilled water from her emergency kit behind the seat, wrapped a towel around her hand, and cautiously unscrewed the radiator cap. A blast of hot steam vented into the rag. She poured half the jug into the reservoir until the green coolant rose back to the fill mark.

She packed the Toughbook into its padded canvas bag.

She turned the truck key. The starter motor cranked twice, caught, and settled into a steady four-cylinder idle.

Evelyn unfolded the 1928 parish survey map across the hood of the Land Cruiser, holding the corners down with four heavy river pebbles. The linen-backed paper showed the original Crown subdivisions along the Murrumbidgee canal, each block surveyed with precise chain measurements recorded in neat imperial script.

"Look at the junction of Portion Forty-Two and Portion Forty-Three," she said, tracing the blue ink line marking the government easement. "Under the 1912 Irrigation Act, the control weir at Kilometer Eighteen was designated as a common diversion point for four family holdings. But when Murrumbidgee Rural Finance took over the Mercer mortgage, their corporate surveyor filed a boundary amendment with the Registrar General, claiming the entire easement as private access."

Claire Sutton leaned over the fender, her face shaded by the broad brim of her straw hat. "They installed an electronic gate on the canal road three months ago. When I tried to drive my tractor down to check the main water meter, two security guards in a private utility blocked the track and told me I was trespassing on corporate land."

Evelyn checked the legal description against the title registration on her encrypted tablet. "A private mortgage lender has no statutory authority to close a public water easement without ministerial approval. But if the surrounding homesteads burn down and the mortgages are foreclosed, there is nobody left to lodge an objection with the Land and Environment Court. The easement becomes a de facto private canal, funneling twelve thousand megaliters of high-security water directly into Apex's almond plantations."

She packed her brass dividers and folded the survey map along its original linen creases. The wind off the western plains carried the smell of scorching grass and dust, a hot dry weight that pressed against the chest with every breath.

Beside the irrigation ditch, Evelyn inspected the automated flow control gate installed by the regional water authority. The steel sluice gate was operated by an electric motor powered by a twenty-watt solar panel mounted on a steel pole. The telemetry housing was secured with a digital padlock manufactured by an industrial security company in Sydney.

She pulled her camera from her vest and photographed the serial plate on the controller: *Model Tele-Gate 400 / Registered to Murrumbidgee Rural Finance / Account Apex-88*. Under the provisions of the Water Management Act, common diversion points were required to remain under municipal control. By installing private telemetry hardware on the government weir, Apex had effectively privatized the delivery canal.

"When they shut that gate in August," Claire Sutton said, "thirty thousand citrus trees between here and the railway line began to die. We called the Department of Water Resources in Leeton, but the compliance officers said they were understaffed and couldn't inspect private infrastructure disputes without ministerial authorization."

Evelyn traced the hydraulic conduit running from the solar panel to the gate actuator. A bypass valve had been installed on the pressure line to permit manual override from an external hydraulic pump. If someone attached a portable pump to that valve, they could open the gate and divert the entire canal discharge without triggering an electronic alert on the regional water board's central supervisory control computer.

She logged the valve specifications in her notebook, recording the thread diameter and pressure rating. This was the mechanism used to starve the family orchards of water during the crucial flowering period, creating the economic desperation that forced owners to seek private mortgage financing from Apex subsidiaries.

Evelyn walked thirty yards down the embankment to inspect the southern canal siphon. The reinforced concrete pipe, four feet in diameter, passed beneath the government railway easement to supply water to the orchards on Portion Forty-Three. The intake mouth was blocked by an adjustable steel baffle plate, held in position by two heavy threaded turnbuckles.

She pulled her folding brass rule from her vest and measured the gap between the baffle and the concrete sill. Exactly twenty-two millimeters. That restricted the water intake to less than five percent of the normal seasonal volume, cutting off irrigation to the Sutton holdings while maintaining full flow through the corporate bypass flume.

She pulled on her inspection gloves and scraped a sample of red grease from the turnbuckle threads, sealing the grease into an airtight plastic vial. Industrial lithium grease of that grade was used exclusively on corporate pumping equipment, confirming that the diversion baffle had been installed and adjusted by Apex maintenance technicians rather than regional water commission personnel.

She photographed the turnbuckle assembly with her digital camera, capturing the tool marks left on the hex nuts by an adjustable pipe wrench. Every physical manipulation of the water network was recorded in her field notebook with precise dimensional measurements and GPS coordinates.

Evelyn walked along the boundary canal embankment, noting the surveyor pegs driven into the clay soil at fifty-meter intervals. Each peg was painted with fluorescent pink surveyor paint and marked with the initials MRF-ENG.

She checked the coordinates on her handheld GPS. The alignment of the pegs demarcated a planned twenty-meter easement expansion that would encroach directly onto Claire Sutton's orchard title. Murrumbidgee Rural Finance had already commissioned engineering blueprints to widen the canal as soon as the homestead was condemned.

She photographed the surveyor pegs in sequence, logging the bearing and distance between each station. The corporate planning had advanced far beyond paper mortgages; they had laid physical markers across the land in anticipation of the fire.

She checked the cadastral overlay against the aerial photographs of Portion Forty-Two. The electronic boundary coordinates showed that the planned canal diversion would isolate eighty acres of prime river silt, rendering the remaining farm paddocks inaccessible to agricultural machinery. The corporate surveyors had engineered the infrastructure design to make independent farming impossible.

Evelyn copied the boundary coordinates into her field notebook, cross-referencing the survey pins with the aerial photographs taken by the department in 1998. The discrepancy between the historical survey pegs and the new subdivision markers was thirty-four meters to the west.

By shifting the surveyed boundary line thirty-four meters west, the developer had placed the entire concrete pumping flume directly inside the easement claimed by Murrumbidgee Rural Finance. If the water board approved the revised cadastral plan, the Sutton family would lose access to the gravity-fed canal that sustained their southern pastures.

The legal strategy was predatory in its simplicity. Under the provisions of the Crown Lands Consolidation Act, boundary objections required an administrative appeal before the regional land board within twenty-eight days of formal notice. Holst had ensured that the notice was mailed to an obsolete postal address in Wagga Wagga. By the time Claire Sutton learned of the boundary amendment, the statutory appeal window had closed.

She backed out of the roadhouse gravel, swung the wheel south, and turned onto the unsealed track leading toward the Sutton boundary.

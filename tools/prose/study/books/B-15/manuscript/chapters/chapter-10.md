# Chapter 10: The Cadastral Register

The records archive was in the basement, two floors below the water table that no longer existed. The concrete walls wept white efflorescence where lime had leached out of the aggregate over fifty years of river floods. The air down here smelled of damp cardboard, silverfish poison, and the vinegar decay of cellulose acetate microfilm.

Evelyn sat before two flickering green monitors on a metal typing desk. Her field boots were propped on the lower shelf beside a box of discarded fanfold computer paper.

She plugged her encrypted token into the terminal. The Australian Securities and Investments Commission database was slow to respond, its servers churning through query queues from Sydney and Melbourne. She keyed in the mortgage reference numbers from the Merriwa claim file.

The screen refreshed in stuttering horizontal bands.

*Mortgagee: Southern Basin Capital Pty Ltd.*

She clicked the corporate extract. Southern Basin Capital had three registered directors: two corporate services secretaries in Saint Helier, Jersey, and an accounting firm on Collins Street in Melbourne. The share capital was ten thousand dollars.

"Layer one," Evelyn muttered to the empty room.

She pulled out her notebook and uncapped a blue ballpoint pen. She drew a clean box and labeled it *Southern Basin Capital*.

Next, she queried the mortgage on the Oakhaven Winery, the fire from five years back that still lived in the corners of her eyes when she woke in motel rooms. The claim file had been marked closed by Warren Holst within three weeks of the blaze. The owner, an old Italian vigneron named Bartoli, had taken a settlement of eight hundred thousand and died of heart failure six months later in a rental flat in Griffith.

The screen blinked. *Mortgagee: Riverina Water Rights Management Trust.*

She ran the company search on Riverina Water Rights Management Trust. The holding company was *Basin Holdings Alpha Ltd*, registered in the Cayman Islands. But the local nominee director was identical: the same accounting firm on Collins Street.

Evelyn cracked her knuckles. Her joints popped loudly in the concrete cellar.

She opened the third file: Coram Creek, the grazing homestead that had burned in November. The owner had been a widow named Nancy Vance. Vance had held sixty acres of frontage on the Lachlan River. She had refused three cash offers from corporate farm aggregators before her hay shed and weatherboard cottage caught fire at three in the morning while she was visiting her daughter in Wagga Wagga.

*Mortgagee: Murrumbidgee Development Corporation.*

Evelyn tracked the corporate parents up through the ASIC registry. She opened four terminal tabs, splitting the screens. The keyboard was an old mechanical unit with heavy beige keys that clattered like a typewriter.

Four shell companies. Four separate addresses in Sydney, Melbourne, and offshore jurisdictions. But every single one of them resolved to an ultimate beneficial shareholder through a discretionary investment trust: *Apex Agro-Holdings Australia*.

She stared at the green text. The corporate tree hung across the dual monitors like a set of dry branches.

Apex Agro-Holdings was the largest corporate agricultural conglomerate in the Murray-Darling Basin. They controlled forty thousand hectares of almond orchards between Hillston and Narrandera. They owned three cotton gins, five grain handling depots, and a fleet of private water tankers.

"Why burn the houses?" she whispered.

If Apex wanted the land, they had the capital to buy out family farms at twice the market value. Banks were foreclosing across the district anyway; five years of drought had broken the dryland farmers. A corporate buyer with cash reserves did not need matches to drive a grazier off red dust.

She minimized the corporate register and opened the New South Wales Land Titles Portal. She entered the cadastral lot numbers for the three burned properties:

Lot 14, Parish of Merriwa: Frank Miller.
Lot 82, Parish of Oakhaven: Bartoli Estate.
Lot 211, Parish of Coram: Nancy Vance.

She pulled the historical title searches. Each document cost forty-two dollars, billed directly to National Mutual's departmental expense account. Holst's accounts clerk would see the charges on Monday morning, but by Monday the queries would already be logged.

The title sheets scrolled down the monitors.

All three properties had been settled before the First World War. All three had original Crown grants dating back to the late nineteenth century. And attached to each title, recorded in faded copperplate script on scanned microfiche cards, was an un-digitized riparian water right.

Evelyn leaned in until her nose was six inches from the screen.

In New South Wales, the 2004 Water Management Act had severed water rights from land ownership, converting old riparian rights into tradeable water allocations that could be sold on the open exchange like corporate bonds. But certain pre-1914 Crown licenses had never been converted. They were held under perpetual statutory covenants: guaranteed minimum flows, immune from state government curtailment orders, tied directly to the physical land parcel.

As long as the original land deeds existed, the water could not be severed. It could not be traded. It could not be bundled into corporate investment funds.

And the only surviving physical copies of those pre-1914 covenants were stored in the private safes of the homesteads themselves.

Evelyn felt the small hairs on the back of her neck prickle.

In 2018, the state titles office in Sydney had suffered a server migration failure that corrupted fifty thousand scanned historical folios from the western divisions. The registrar had issued a directive: where electronic records were incomplete, ownership disputes would be determined by production of original primary documentation.

If the paper burned, the perpetual water right dissolved into the general pool. And under statutory allocation rules, unclaimed water in the Lachlan and Murrumbidgee catchments was auctioned off to the highest commercial bidder.

Apex Agro-Holdings was the only corporate entity in the district with standing credit lines to absorb unallocated seasonal water at commercial auctions.

She looked at her notebook. The blue ink lines connected the three properties, the four corporate shells, and the holding trust in Melbourne.

The fires were not insurance claims. The insurance payouts were petty cash, an underwriting rounding error. The real prize was the river itself.

Evelyn printed the three title abstracts. The dot-matrix printer in the corner shuddered and began to screech, spitting out tractor-feed paper in crisp white folds. She gathered the sheets, tore off the perforated edges, and folded them into her leather field kit.

She shut down the terminals. The green phosphor faded to black, leaving the basement silent except for the rhythmic drip of condensate from the overhead pipes.

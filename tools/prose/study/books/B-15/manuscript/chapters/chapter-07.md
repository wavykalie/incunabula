# Chapter 7: The 2021 Sign-Off

The desk in room fourteen was lit only by the blue glow of Evelyn's laptop. Outside, the Corowa main street had gone dead, the streetlights flickering yellow through the dust that drifted off the bare paddocks to the north.

She had bypassed the standard claims portal. Using her administrative credentials, she accessed the deep archival vault of National Mutual's legal compliance division.

File: *NM-CLM-2021-0419-OAKHAVEN*.

The file was three hundred and forty megabytes. It contained seventy-two high-resolution photographs, eighteen witness depositions, a four-page report from the New South Wales Rural Fire Service, and the final thirteen-page technical assessment bearing her own name.

Evelyn clicked on the PDF document and scrolled directly to page eleven.

The photograph showed the southeastern corner of the Oakhaven Winery bottling shed. It had been taken at two in the afternoon under a blazing sun that bleached the red brickwork to pale salmon. In the center of the frame, half-buried beneath the collapsed steel purlins, was the concrete floor slab.

She zoomed in three hundred percent.

There, half-obscured by the shadow of a buckled I-beam, was the circular crater of spalled aggregate.

It had been there five years ago. Right in front of her eyes.

Beside the spalling was the rectangular steel plate of an underfloor drainage duct, its cast-iron cover displaced by two inches.

She remembered that afternoon with a clarity that made the back of her throat burn. She had been on her feet for thirty-six hours without sleep. The air in the ruined cellar had been fifty degrees, thick with the stench of spoiled shiraz and charred insulation foam. Her digital camera had overheated twice, forcing her to pack it in a cooler bag between shots.

She remembered Warren Holst standing on the edge of the ruin in his polished RM Williams boots, his silk tie loosened, offering her a bottle of cold mineral water from his air-conditioned sedan.

*You've done an extraordinary job, Evelyn. The local inspector agrees with the transformer surge theory. The whole district is on edge with these bushfires; the last thing the community needs is a protracted corporate claims dispute. Just fill in section four and let the underwriters handle the disbursement.*

She scrolled down to section four: *Cause and Origin Determination*.

The text was written in her own crisp, clinical prose.

*Origin: Identified at the southeastern wall junction adjacent to the primary electrical distribution panel. Heat source: High-resistance thermal fault following grid voltage surge during catastrophic environmental conditions. Fuel: Combustible wall framing and acoustic insulation panels. Classification: Accidental.*

And below that, in the signature block:

*Evelyn Brand, FIEAust, CFI (IAAI)*
*Senior Fire Claims Assessor, Southern Region*
*National Mutual Underwriting Ltd*

She had signed it.

Her signature was a firm, legible script, written with the Montblanc Meisterstück pen her father had given her when she graduated from the engineering faculty at the University of Melbourne thirty years ago.

Her signature had released four million two hundred thousand dollars to Apex Agro-Holdings.

With that four million dollars, Apex had bought up three bankrupt family irrigation farms in the surrounding parish. They had consolidated the titles, canceled the residential water allocations, and transferred twelve hundred megaliters of high-security water to their corporate holding trust in Melbourne.

She scrolled back to the appendix of the Oakhaven file.

Attached to the back of the claim folder was an independent technical risk assessment commissioned by Apex's legal counsel prior to the fire. It was signed by an external engineering consultant: *Raymond Kroll, BEng (Chem), MIEAust, Risk Mitigation Specialist, Melbourne*.

Kroll.

She clicked on Kroll's attached curriculum vitae. Former senior process engineer at a petrochemical refinery in Geelong. Specialist in high-temperature chemical oxidation, industrial fire suppression systems, and thermal hazard modeling.

He had inspected the Oakhaven Winery two weeks before the blaze. He had inspected Frank Miller's farm in Merriwa three weeks before it burned.

The electrical maintenance log attached as Exhibit D recorded weekly transformer inspections carried out by Kroll's firm. Under the comments column, Kroll had written: *High-voltage oil-filled bushings showing dielectric degradation. Potential phase-to-ground flashover risk under elevated ambient temperature conditions.*

He had planted the forensic justification in the maintenance records four days before he ignited the gel.

He had created the paper trail that allowed Warren Holst to look her in the eye and say the transformer had blown three fuses on the main line.

And she had accepted it.

She had taken the maintenance log, verified Kroll's engineering registration number on the Institution of Engineers directory, and cited his report in paragraph twelve of her final determination.

She leaned forward, resting her forehead against the cool edge of the laptop casing. Her eyes burned with the dry grit of the plains.

She opened the technical appendix labeled Annex C: Thermal Calculations. The mathematical models were laid out in rigorous detail, applying finite element heat transfer equations to simulate the dissipation of electrical energy through copper conduit. The author had calculated heat dissipation rates across thirty individual nodes, demonstrating that an electrical arc could ignite adjacent timber frames within eighty seconds.

The calculations were flawless on their face. The mathematical logic was airtight, grounded in standard thermodynamics. But the initial boundary conditions assumed an ambient humidity of zero percent and a fuel moisture content of less than four percent. Kroll had manipulated the input variables to force the model to predict spontaneous ignition.

She leaned back in her desk chair, listening to the monotonous hum of the motel air conditioner. Her own signature at the foot of the document looked brisk, confident, and professional. She had signed it on a Friday afternoon before boarding an evening flight out of Wagga Wagga.

She stares at her own pixelated cursive signature until her eyes water in the dry air.

# Chapter 37: The Packing of the Carton

Evelyn's cubicle on the third floor smelled of cardboard and floor cleaner. The partition walls were gray felt, covered in rectangular unfaded patches where her technical diplomas and state certification certificates had hung for eight years.

A heavy brown cardboard carton sat on the laminate desk.

She packed methodically, wrapping each item in unprinted newsprint before laying it in the carton.

Her reference copy of NFPA 921, the National Fire Protection Association guide for fire and explosion investigations. The book was three inches thick, its red cloth cover split along the spine, its margins filled with twenty years of handwritten notes in blue ballpoint ink.

Her steel depth calipers, calibrated to one-tenth of a millimeter for measuring wood char gradients.

Her diamond-point scribe for testing glass fracture stress lines.

Her father's orchard pruning shears, their carbon steel blades polished bright, their ash handles dark with forty years of sweat and citrus oil.

She laid the shears in the center of the box, cushioned between two wool sweaters.

On the corner of the desk lay her formal letter of resignation, typed that morning on a manual machine at the public library. It was one paragraph, addressed to the provisional liquidator appointed by the Supreme Court to manage the branch's affairs:

*To the Board of National Mutual Underwriting.*
*I hereby tender my resignation as Senior Field Investigator, effective immediately.*
*All active case files and department equipment have been surrendered to the State Crime Command.*
*E. Brand.*

She did not wait for the liquidator's clerk to acknowledge the envelope. She slid it under the office manager's locked door.

She folded the top flaps of the cardboard carton together in an interlocking weave.

She picked up a roll of heavy brown parcel tape. She hooked the end over the lip of the carton and pulled the tape across the seam in a single, steady motion. The tape screeched loudly in the empty cubicle, echoing off the gray felt walls with a sharp, final tear.

She pressed the gummed paper down with her thumb, smoothing out the air bubbles until the box was sealed tight.

She hoisted the carton into her arms. It weighed thirty-five pounds.

The office on the second floor was dead quiet when Evelyn returned to collect her personal effects. The claims department clerks sat at their modular cubicles, their eyes fixed on their monitors, typing with cautious, muted strokes. None of them looked up as she passed.

She placed her Mitutoyo digital calipers, her diamond-point engraving pen, her brass depth gauge, and her pocket loupe into her canvas field bag. Beside them, she packed her three spiral field notebooks, their cardboard covers stained with red dust and motor oil.

On the corner of her desk lay the bronze long-service medallion presented to her on her tenth anniversary with the company. The inscription read: *For Integrity, Diligence, and Technical Excellence in Insurance Assessment*.

She picked up the heavy medallion, weighed it in her palm for a moment, and set it down in the center of the clean green blotter. She did not take it with her. She closed the brass zipper on her canvas bag, pulled the strap over her shoulder, and walked down the carpeted hallway toward the stairwell.

Evelyn walked down the central corridor of the second floor for the final time. The glass doors of the executive suites were locked with electronic security seals, and the fluorescent tubes in the northern wing had been switched off to conserve building power.

She reached her desk in the open-plan assessment division. On the divider screen, she left a yellow adhesive note for the junior assessors who would inherit her territory: *Never sign an origin determination based on corporate telemetry alone. Measure the char depth with your own calipers, verify the draft geometry, and look beneath the foundation slab.*

She carried her canvas bag to the glass elevators, her steps echoing on the gray carpet. When the elevator doors opened, she stepped inside, pressed the button for the basement parking level, and watched the corporate offices rise and vanish behind the steel frame.

She walked out of the cubicle, down the central corridor past the rows of empty desks, and pushed through the heavy fire exit door toward the parking garage.

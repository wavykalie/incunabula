# Chapter 24: The Armed Perimeter

Thursday dawn broke over the Sutton farm without color. The sky was a pale, chalky gray, choked with high-altitude dust blown in from the Simpson Desert five hundred miles to the northwest. The sun rose as a dull copper disc that cast no shadows on the red earth.

Evelyn knelt in the red dust beneath the western veranda of the homestead.

She was sweating through her canvas shirt. The temperature at seven in the morning had already reached thirty-four degrees, and the air was thick with the static crackle of an approaching front.

In her hands was a commercial wireless smoke sensor, modified with a high-gain radio transmitter and a magnetic mounting bracket.

She reached into the dark crawlspace beneath the foundation wall. The space smelled of dry dirt, dead spiders, and fifty years of accumulated cypress resin. She clamped the sensor to the steel reinforcing rod of the third foundation pier, four inches below the western bearer.

She tested the connection on her handheld receiver.

The small green LED on the receiver flashed twice. *Channel 1: Active. Signal: 98%.*

Claire Sutton came around the corner of the veranda carrying a thermos of black coffee and a box of plastic zip ties.

"How many sensors do you have?" Claire asked.

"Six," Evelyn said, wiping the sweat from her eyes with her sleeve. "One on each foundation corner, and two directly beneath the study floor where your deed register is stored. If anyone enters this crawlspace, the infrared tripwire triggers the receiver in the kitchen."

"What about the cameras?"

Evelyn pointed toward the three ancient peppercorn trees that bordered the western paddock.

"Trail cameras in the lower branches," she said. "Camouflaged in bark wrap. High-definition night vision with black-flash infrared emitters. When Kroll approaches the fence line, his vehicle and his license plate will be recorded on solid-state flash drives."

Claire poured black coffee into two plastic mugs. "Will the police come if we call them?"

"I called Detective Senior Sergeant Croft in Sydney this morning," Evelyn said, taking the mug. "He has an unmarked car from the Riverina property crime squad stationed at the crossroads ten miles north. But Croft cannot enter this property until a felony is in progress. If they move too early, Kroll's lawyers will claim he was conducting an authorized environmental survey on behalf of the mortgagee."

"So we have to let him place the fire?" Claire's voice was tight, her knuckles gray as she gripped her mug.

"We have to catch him with the incendiary device in his hands," Evelyn said. "With the chemical block unignited and the electronic timer wired to the charge. That is the only evidence the Director of Public Prosecutions cannot bury."

Claire looked up at the western sky.

The dust cloud was thickening, turning the horizon the color of bruised iron. A line of dark, ragged cumulus clouds was rising behind the dust, their tops illuminated by internal flickers of silent, dry lightning.

The wind hit the house with a sudden, violent shudder.

A sheet of loose corrugated iron on the shearing shed rattled like machine-gun fire. A funnel of red dust spun across the paddock, tore through the vegetable garden, and smashed against the weatherboards, showering the veranda in grit.

Evelyn looked at her handheld receiver.

The six green lights were steady, glowing bright against the gathering gloom of the storm.

"He'll be here before midnight," Evelyn said.

Inside the homestead kitchen, Evelyn checked the two portable fire pumps connected to the concrete rain tank behind the laundry. Both Honda engines had full fuel tanks and clean spark plugs. She unrolled sixty feet of heavy canvas delivery hose along the veranda, connecting a brass fog nozzle adjusted for wide-angle structural protection.

Claire Sutton stood at the kitchen window, checking the action on an old twelve-gauge side-by-side shotgun. Her hands were steady, her movements deliberate and practiced.

"If they come through the canal gate," Claire said, "they won't be expecting anyone to be awake. They think an assessor from National Mutual is sitting in an air-conditioned motel room in Wagga writing up an accidental loss report."

Evelyn pulled her two-way satellite radio from her equipment bag and verified the emergency transmission channel to the state police district command. The signal indicator showed four green bars.

"We don't need a shotgun to stop them, Claire," Evelyn said, looking out through the flywire screen into the moonlit yard. "We have the physical evidence, the telemetry records, and the chemical analysis. All we need tonight is the photograph of Raymond Kroll setting the timer."

Claire Sutton checked the brass primer on the twelve-gauge cartridges, her face illuminated by the amber glow of the kerosene lamp on the kitchen counter.

"We have two hours before the wind reaches its peak," Claire said, looking out through the flyscreen door into the dry yard. "If they come, they will come from the eastern canal gate where the timber bridge crosses the dry ditch."

Evelyn set her digital field recorder on the table and tested the audio microphone. The needle responded with clean, sharp pulses. "We are ready, Claire. Every piece of evidence is documented."

Claire nodded once. She reached behind the kitchen door, picked up her double-barreled shotgun, broke the action to check the two red cartridges in the chambers, and snapped the steel barrels shut with a heavy, metallic clack.

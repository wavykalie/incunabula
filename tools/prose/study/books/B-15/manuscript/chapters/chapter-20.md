# Chapter 20: The Payphone at Redgate

The township of Redgate consisted of a grain silo, a closed railway station, a general store that sold canned beans and diesel fuel, and a single public telephone booth beside the post office.

The phone booth was made of red cast iron, erected in 1952. The glass panels had been replaced with sheet aluminum to prevent vandalism, turning the interior into a metal chimney that trapped the smell of old grease, stale tobacco, and hot copper wiring.

Evelyn picked up the heavy black receiver. She fed four dollar coins into the mechanical slot.

She dialed Warren Holst's direct executive line in Wagga Wagga.

The line clicked twice, the relays clattering through the exchange in Cootamundra. Then the call connected with a burst of static.

"Holst," his voice came through the earpiece, smooth, unhurried, perfectly modulated.

"I found Raymond Kroll's name in your internal audit directive, Warren," Evelyn said. Her voice was flat, echoing against the iron walls of the booth.

A brief pause on the line. The faint rustle of paper being moved on a leather blotter.

"You were instructed to surrender your company property and report to regional headquarters at eight o'clock Monday morning, Evelyn."

"Kroll is an industrial arsonist," she said. "He worked for Apex on the Oakhaven fire in twenty-one. He burned Nancy Vance's homestead in November. He burned Frank Miller's house three weeks ago. And right now he is sitting on the ridge two miles west of Claire Sutton's farm."

Holst sighed, a long, patient exhalation that hissed through the telephone receiver.

"Raymond Kroll is a certified risk engineer with twenty-five years of experience in structural forensics," Holst said. "He is an accredited member of the Institute of Engineers Australia. He has testified as an expert witness in twenty Supreme Court litigations. Your allegations are defamatory, unprofessional, and entirely without evidentiary foundation."

"I have the chemical spectra from the Lidcombe laboratory," Evelyn said. "Marcus Chen identified synthetic polyol ester and aluminum octanoate in the mortar joints at Merriwa. It matches the thermal lance patented by Kroll in nineteen-ninety-nine."

The line fell silent for four seconds. The only sound was the distant hum of the carrier wave between Wagga and Redgate.

"Mr. Chen's preliminary report is an internal working draft," Holst said, his voice dropping half an octave, losing its smooth corporate veneer. "It has not been peer-reviewed. It has not been approved by the department director. And as of two o'clock this afternoon, the Lidcombe laboratory's commercial contract with National Mutual has been referred for procurement review."

"You bought the laboratory director," Evelyn said.

"We manage our external professional relationships with appropriate care," Holst replied. "Now listen to me, Evelyn. Carefully. This is your final professional warning."

"I am listening."

"You are currently operating outside your delegated authority. As of five o'clock this afternoon, your employment with National Mutual has been suspended without pay. Your corporate credit card is canceled. The fleet utility you are driving has been reported to the New South Wales Police as an unauthorized conversion of company property."

Evelyn shifted the receiver to her other ear. Red dust had settled on the rubber cord.

"If you remain on the Sutton property," Holst continued, "you will be arrested for commercial trespass. If you publish any document containing proprietary company information, our legal counsel will file for an immediate emergency injunction in the Federal Court, with personal costs assessed against you. Your superannuation will be frozen pending the outcome of the fraud inquiry."

"And what happens to Claire Sutton?"

"Mrs. Sutton is in default on her commercial mortgage," Holst said coldly. "The lender will exercise its statutory power of sale. That is the operation of the law. You cannot stop it, Evelyn. You have no legal standing, no institutional authority, and no resources."

"I have the 1912 deed," she said.

A sharp intake of breath on the line.

"The deed belongs to the mortgagee under the terms of the collateral schedule," Holst said, his voice tightening. "Surrender it to the branch office on Monday, or you will face criminal charges for theft of financial instruments."

"Come and get it yourself, Warren," she said.

The mechanical coin box clicked. The operator's recorded voice broke in, announcing that her time had expired.

The line went dead with three sharp, rhythmic disconnection tones that buzzed in the metal booth like a hornets' nest.

Evelyn waited while the satellite link routed through the regional exchange in Griffith. Through the scratched perspex window of the telephone booth, she watched three crows pick at a dead rabbit on the verge of the bitumen.

The voice of Donald Vance, her forensic contact at the Australian Securities and Investments Commission, came through the receiver with a hollow electronic delay.

"The ownership chain for Apex Agro-Holdings is layered through five distinct corporate entities," Vance said, his keyboard clattering in the background. "The Australian parent company is capitalized at fifty million dollars, but ninety-nine percent of the voting shares are held by an offshore trust registered in Guernsey. The registered directors in Melbourne are two corporate lawyers from a commercial firm in Collins Street."

"Who has signature authority on the operating bank accounts for Murrumbidgee Rural Finance?" Evelyn asked, shielding her ear from the roar of a passing grain road train.

"One signature only," Vance replied. "Warren Holst, under an executive power of attorney granted in November twenty-twenty-one. The power of attorney explicitly authorizes Holst to execute mortgage foreclosures, property settlements, and water license transfers without board approval."

Evelyn made a precise note in her spiral pad. "And the consulting fees paid to Raymond Kroll?"

"Twenty-eight thousand dollars a month," Vance said. "Invoiced directly to Murrumbidgee Rural Finance under the accounting code *Risk Engineering and Property Stabilization Services*. The payments are approved by Holst within twenty-four hours of submission."

Donald Vance cleared his throat on the line from Sydney. "There is another document here in the corporate registry, Evelyn. An asset pledge agreement signed three weeks ago by Warren Holst on behalf of National Mutual's regional investment arm."

"What did he pledge?" Evelyn asked, holding the receiver tight against her ear.

"The senior mortgage portfolios of twelve regional agricultural holdings, including the Sutton farm," Vance replied. "The pledge agreement was executed in favor of a private debt syndication fund based in Singapore. Under the terms of the agreement, if the underlying mortgage loans are cleared through insurance settlement or property foreclosure before December thirty-first, National Mutual receives an eight percent underwriting bonus."

Evelyn wrote down the syndication details in her notebook. The puzzle pieces locked together with mathematical finality. Holst was not just protecting corporate clients; he was generating private performance bonuses for his own division by accelerating the liquidation of distressed farm properties.

The line crackled with static as the regional telephone exchange routed through Wagga Wagga. Evelyn pressed her thumb against the coin return slot, listening to the automated operator tones.

"We have confirmed the registered office address for Apex Agro-Holdings," Vance added. "A corporate law firm on the forty-second floor of the Rialto Towers in Melbourne. The firm has sixteen partners specializing in corporate debt restructuring and agricultural equity trusts."

Evelyn hung up the heavy receiver. She stood in the hot iron box for thirty seconds, listening to the wind sweep across the empty street outside, rattling the dry galvanized iron on the post office roof.

# Chapter 13: The Scorched Parchment

The sun had dropped behind the western scrub when Evelyn returned to Merriwa. The air had cooled from forty-four degrees to thirty-eight, but the red dirt still radiated heat like an oven floor. A hot northwesterly breeze blew across the bare paddocks, carrying the bitter stench of dead thistles and burnt timber.

The ruins of the homestead looked smaller in the dusk. The corrugated iron roof had collapsed over the kitchen, its twisted sheets resting on charred cypress stumps. A lone brick chimney stood above the debris, blackened on its western face.

Evelyn pulled on her leather work gloves and stepped through the yellow plastic cordon tape that fluttered against the fence posts. Her boots crunched on fragments of vitrified window glass.

She carried an ultraviolet inspection lamp, a dial caliper, and an engineer's steel tape measure.

She walked the perimeter of the foundation, forty paces from the crawlspace hatch. The wind blew from the northwest at fifteen knots. If an incendiary device had been ignited under the floor joists, the drafting effect would have drawn air through the southern foundation vents and expelled smoke and debris toward the southeast boundary fence.

She crossed the bare yard toward the boundary. The soil here was hard clay, split by drought cracks wide enough to swallow a boot heel. A rusted wire mesh fence marked the property line, half-buried under drifting tumbleweeds and dry roly-poly burrs.

She knelt beside the fence. She switched on the ultraviolet lamp.

The 365-nanometer beam turned the dry weeds a pale, ghostly violet. Rabbit droppings glowed amber. Tiny flakes of plastic baling twine shone like neon threads.

She moved along the fence wire by wire, parting the dead thistle stalks with her gloved fingers.

Fifty yards southeast of the chimney, caught in the barb of the second wire, was a scrap of stiff material the size of a playing card.

Evelyn froze. She shielded the lamp with her forearm.

The scrap was not plastic. It was heavy, cream-colored animal vellum, three times thicker than modern paper. Its edges were charred black and curled by intense radiant heat, but the center of the scrap had survived, protected from the flames by the heavy wire twist that held it.

She took a pair of stainless steel forceps from her field pouch. She pried the vellum free from the rusted barb with steady, delicate pressure.

Under the ultraviolet beam, faint lines of handwriting appeared across the surface, written in iron gall ink that had oxidized to deep purple-brown.

*...and the said grantee, his heirs and assigns, shall have perpetual right to divert from the Murrumbidgee River watercourse at the northern boundary of Lot 14, not less than four hundred acre-feet per annum, free of all statutory tolls, levies, and restrictions whatsoever...*

Below the text was the faint indentation of an embossed seal. Under the UV light, the purple ink of the official stamp glowed with a dull, fluorescent ring:

*WATER CONSERVATION AND IRRIGATION COMMISSION, NEW SOUTH WALES, 1912.*

Evelyn let out a long breath. Her pulse hammered against her ribs.

This was the riparian deed. Frank Miller's grandfather had kept it in the tin deed box inside the cedar wardrobe in the master bedroom, directly above the central foundation pier. When the gelled accelerant had detonated beneath the floor, the floorboards had collapsed inward, dumping the wardrobe into the thermal core of the fire.

The upward draft from the eleven-hundred-degree fire storm had caught this single fragment, lifting it into the thermal plume before the flames could consume it, carrying it fifty yards downwind to the boundary fence.

She measured the vellum's thickness with her micrometer: zero-point-six-two millimeters. Animal calfskin vellum prepared with slaked lime. Modern paper would have turned to ash within four seconds at nine hundred degrees, but eighteenth- and early twentieth-century vellum had protein fibers that resisted thermal collapse long enough for the convective plume to loft it clear.

She pulled an archival acid-free evidence envelope from her bag. She slipped the vellum inside using the forceps, folded the flap, and sealed it with red tamper-evident tape.

She knelt beside the fourth foundation stump, unscrewing the protective cap of her dial caliper.

The needle probe pressed into the charred surface of the cypress timber. Cypress pine had a known charring rate under standard ISO 834 fire conditions: zero-point-six-five millimeters per minute. She measured at four points around the perimeter of the stump: twelve millimeters on the western face, fourteen millimeters on the northern face, eighteen millimeters on the central core facing the interior crawlspace.

Eighteen millimeters meant twenty-seven minutes of equivalent standard fire exposure.

Yet the volunteer brigade logs recorded that the structure had collapsed within eleven minutes of the initial triple-zero call from the mail truck driver on the highway.

"Accelerated heat flux," Evelyn murmured to herself.

She recorded the measurements in her field notebook, sketching the isotherm contours around the stump. A fire burning from open residential furnishings could not deliver the radiant flux required to produce eighteen millimeters of char in eleven minutes. The convective draft created by the burning timber had drawn thirty knots of fresh air through the southern foundation vents, feeding an oxygen-rich furnace that peaked above one thousand degrees.

She stood up and walked toward the western corner where the water meter had been mounted. The brass valve had fused to the galvanized pipe, its lead solder melted away in silvery droplets that had pooled in the dust.

Across the road, the lights of a utility vehicle appeared on the crest of the hill.

The vehicle was moving slowly, its high beams sweeping across the dry paddocks. It had no corporate markings, no trade racks on the tray. It reached the boundary of the Miller property, slowed to a crawl, and idled twenty yards from Evelyn's parked truck.

Evelyn stood in the shadows behind the water tank, the ultraviolet lamp switched off. Her heart knocked hard against her sternum.

The vehicle sat in the road for two full minutes, its diesel engine clattering in the hot night wind. The driver's side window was rolled down an inch, the tip of a cigarette glowing red in the dark interior.

Evelyn walked down the central tractor row, her boots sinking two inches into the fine red silt. The orange trees were skeletal, their bark splitting in long vertical fissures that exposed the bleached sapwood beneath. In the third row, she knelt beside an aluminum irrigation riser. The valve was dry, caked with a white crust of alkaline salts.

She pulled her soil probe from her equipment vest and drove the steel shaft eighteen inches into the earth beside the root ball. When she extracted the core, the soil crumbled to powder in her palm. The moisture sensor registered zero point four percent. Under normal cultivation conditions, citrus trees require a minimum soil moisture index of twelve percent to maintain vascular transport.

"My grandfather planted these Valencias in nineteen thirty-two," Claire said, standing at the row head with a pair of rusty pruning shears in her hand. "He lived through the Depression on mutton and river cod, carrying water in galvanized buckets from the canal when the government pumps failed. He never took a shilling of bank credit in his entire life."

Evelyn inspected the Crown survey mark cut into the concrete casing of the irrigation gate. The broad arrow of the New South Wales Department of Lands was still legible beneath the red dust: *Allocation 419, Murrumbidgee Irrigation Area, 1914*. That single brass tablet represented an un-cancelable legal right to divert six hundred megaliters of water annually from the river, immune to modern municipal rationing.

Evelyn touched the dry iron casing of the lateral pump. The metal was burning hot to the skin, heated by forty-two degrees of desert sun. A yellow warning tag was wired to the starter switch, bearing the printed logo of the Murrumbidgee Rural Finance recovery division: *Asset Subject to Mortgage Lien. Unauthorized Removal Prohibited.*

Claire kicked the base of the pump stand with her work boot. "They put that tag on the day after my father went into the hospital. They told us if we turned the diesel engine on, they would report us for theft of mortgaged security."

Then the driver shifted into gear. The tires spit gravel as the truck accelerated away toward the highway, its red taillights disappearing into the dust.

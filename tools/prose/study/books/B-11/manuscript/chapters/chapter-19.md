# Chapter 19: The Night Watch

At two in the morning on Saturday, Sadie lay awake in her narrow bed beneath the sloping ceiling of the attic bedroom.

Outside, the South Yorkshire wind drove waves of sleet against the skylight window, drumming on the glass with an irregular, clattering fury. Through the thin plasterboard partition, she could hear the rhythmic drone of the television in the front room downstairs. Her mother had fallen asleep on the sofa after twelve hours at the poultry packaging line, leaving the shopping channel murmuring to the empty corduroy armchair.

Sadie stared at the ceiling joists.

The cold was in the room.

That chill had nothing to do with the damp drafts of an uninsulated council house. The air carried the absolute, dry, sterile cold of the yesterday school. It rose from the floorboards around her bed, pooling in the wool blankets until her feet felt like lumps of butcher's meat.

She turned onto her side.

In the corner beside the wardrobe, the cast-iron radiator clanked.

*Clink. Clink. Clank.*

It was the cadence of Boiler Three.

She sat up in bed, pulling the duvet around her shoulders.

Her heart beat with a slow, heavy rhythm that felt out of step with her breath. She reached out and touched her bedside clock, an old digital alarm with red LED numbers that glowed in the dark.

02:14.

As she watched the red numerals, the colon between the hours and minutes stopped blinking.

It hung there, frozen in solid red light, refusing to tick over to 02:15.

Sadie held her breath.

From the wall cavity behind the wardrobe, where the old lead water pipe ran down to the kitchen scullery, came a faint sound.

A voice spoke.

The sound came very quiet, muffled by six inches of horsehair plaster and brick dust, but the phonemes were distinct, shaped by stiff, synthetic vocal cords that had not drawn breath in forty years.

*Sa... die...*

Sadie pulled her knees up to her chin, digging her fingernails into the fleece of the duvet.

*Sa... die... Bell...*

The sound died away into the rattle of the sleet against the skylight.

The LED colon on the alarm clock blinked once, and the display shifted to 02:15.

She slid her legs out from under the blankets.

The linoleum floor of her bedroom felt like sheets of river ice against the soles of her bare feet. She walked to the window and looked out through the condensation on the double glazing.

Below her, the terraced street stretched down the hillside toward the canal. The orange sodium street lamps cast long, wet reflections across the asphalt. Every chimney stack was dark, venting thin trails of coal smoke into the freezing rain.

At the foot of the hill, where the railway cutting met the boundary fence of Ashfield Comprehensive, the dark silhouette of the boiler house chimney rose against the bruised clouds.

A single blue light flickered at the base of the stack.

Neither the yellow glare of the security lamps or the red warning beacon on the communications mast. The pale, ghostly blue of the boiler burner shone through thirty feet of solid earth and brickwork.

Sadie pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

The school was not staying in the valley. The frost spread upward along the water mains, following the cast-iron pipes into the foundations of every house in the parish.

Downstairs in the kitchen, the small electric refrigerator hummed with a low, vibrating rattle that vibrated through the floorboards. Sadie walked to the Formica counter and poured half a glass of tap water.

The water was cloudy with air bubbles, tasting faintly of municipal chloramine and old lead pipes. She drank it in three small swallows, watching the red LED indicator light on the microwave oven across the room.

Through the net curtains of the back door, the small garden was dark, the wet grass reflecting the orange glow of the street lamps on the bypass. In the next garden, an empty metal clothesline squeaked in the wind, swinging on its rusted hinge with an irregular, rhythmic pulse.

Sadie sat on the bottom step of the staircase in her dark hallway, listening to the quiet of the council house.

The digital clock on the microwave in the kitchen cast a faint green rectangle of light across the linoleum floor. Through the thin front door, she could hear the occasional hiss of car tires passing on the wet bypass half a mile away, the spray of water on asphalt sounding like waves hitting a shingle beach.

In her coat pocket, her fingers turned the brass counterweight pin she had taken from the boiler hatch.

The metal was cold, smooth as river pebble, holding none of the heat from her palm. She held it up to the green light of the microwave, watching the stamped numbers on the hexagonal collar: *1972-C4*.

She slipped the brass pin back into her pocket, stood up, and climbed the stairs to her bedroom.

Sadie sat on the edge of her mattress in the dark attic bedroom, her hands resting on her knees.

On the bedside table, her old school exercise books from primary school sat stacked in a neat pile. She pulled the top notebook onto her lap, running her fingers over the red sugar-paper cover where she had written her name in rounded infant print: *Sadie Bell — Class 4 — Thorne Junior School*.

Inside, a crayon drawing showed her father standing beside the Thorne Colliery winding gear, his yellow safety helmet tilted back on his head, a red lunch tin in his hand. Below the drawing, her teacher had written in purple ink: *Very neat work, Sadie. A lovely picture of your dad.*

She closed the notebook and set it back on the stack. The house around her was silent, save for the rhythmic clank of the cast-iron radiator pipes in the wall cavity.

Sadie walked to the window of her attic bedroom and pressed her hand against the cold glass.

Outside, the rain had settled into a steady, gray South Yorkshire mist that blurred the street lamps along the canal basin. On the freight line two hundred yards away, an empty coal train rattled past on its way back to Immingham docks, the heavy diesel engine vibrating through the brick foundations of the terraced houses with a low, bone-deep rumble.

She looked at her bedside table where the brass counterweight pin lay beside her digital alarm clock.

The splay was widening. The cold that had started behind Boiler Three was no longer confined to the basement; it was seeping into her house, into her sleep, following the cast-iron pipes into the very bedrock of the town.

She walked back to her bed, pulled the blankets up to her ears, and waited for the morning light.

# Chapter 32: The Mirror of Cruelty

Sadie stood in the center of the foyer, her head bowed.

The smell of coal dust and ozone hung heavy in her throat. Around her, the fifty figures stood with their arms raised, their gray fingers outstretched like the bare branches of winter thorn hedges.

She looked at her right hand.

The skin on her knuckles was dark with boiler soot and grease. For two weeks, she had crept through the pipe basements like a sapper tunneling beneath an enemy trench, nursing her hatred like a glowing coal in her pocket. She had believed she was an avenger, balancing an unfair ledger with secret mechanical precision.

Now she saw what she had built.

The yesterday school had not punished Chloe; it had merely amplified the cruelty, turning Sadie into the same sneering tormentor she had fled from in the dining hall. The malice had fed the fracture in the colliery drift, widening the splay until forty tons of living masonry were ready to collapse into the abyss.

"I'm not doing this anymore," Sadie said.

Her voice was quiet, but in the dead acoustics of the replica corridor, the words were firm.

She opened the fingers of her right hand.

The heavy steel cold chisel fell.

It struck the vinyl floor tiles with a dull, flat thud that didn't echo. It lay between Sadie's sneakers and Chloe's black school loafers, an eight-inch bar of octagonal tool steel, motionless in the purple light.

Chloe's duplicate looked down at the chisel.

A faint shudder passed through the duplicate's neck. The fingers of Chloe's outstretched hands twitched, curling inward toward her palms like drying leaves.

In the second rank, Mr. Henderson's jaw clicked shut.

The blue plastic clipboard in his hands slipped from his stiff fingers, tumbling into the dust with a dry plastic clatter.

Sadie looked up at the ceiling.

Along the central seam of the corridor, where the 1972 concrete slab met the Victorian brick footings of the boiler stack, a thin black fracture had opened. A trickle of fine white plaster dust sifted down through the purple air, dusting Chloe's hair and shoulders with pale powder.

The temporal circuit was unstable.

Without the driving force of Sadie's malice, the splay was losing its coherence. The artificial pressure was seeking an exit, straining against the foundation anchors.

Sadie remembered Garside's words in the workshop: *The valve has to be tripped from inside. If you drop the master bolt while the living school is empty, the temporal circuit collapses on itself.*

She looked down the east corridor toward the central stairwell.

Mounted on the pillar outside the library door was the master fire alarm call point.

The cold chisel lay on the linoleum between them, its octagonal steel body reflecting the purple emergency beacons on the ceiling.

Sadie took three steps backward toward the center of the foyer. The air in the room felt slightly thinner now, losing its oily density as the temporal circuit began to destabilize. Along the north wall, where the display case of athletic cups stood, the glass panels gave a sharp, high-pitched hum that set Sadie's teeth vibrating in her gums.

Above her head, a section of acoustic ceiling tile broke away from its aluminum grid, falling twelve feet to shatter into white fibrous crumbs on the floor.

She looked toward the central pillar where the red fire alarm box was mounted, calculating the distance across the cracked linoleum.

Sadie looked at the walls of the central foyer.

The olive-green gloss paint was flaking away in long, curled ribbons, revealing the coarse cinderblock beneath. Every surface in the duplicate school was coated in that fine, dry dust of crushed coal and pulverized plaster, the residue of decades of accumulated grievance.

She had believed that breaking Chloe's possessions would give her power, that secret vengeance would rebuild the dignity stripped away in the dining hall. Now she saw that the yesterday school was simply an empty stomach, consuming everything that entered its frozen rooms until nothing remained but dead stone and cold air.

She stepped over the fallen chisel, her sneakers crushing a section of broken ceiling tile into white powder as she moved toward the east corridor.

Sadie stepped into the east corridor, leaving the cold chisel behind her on the foyer floor.

The air in the hallway was vibrating with a deep, subsonic drone that rattled the steel radiator brackets along the wall. In the living school, this corridor was always jammed with Year 7 pupils eating salt-and-vinegar crisps between double geography and English. Now, the linoleum was covered in an unbroken blanket of gray plaster dust, undisturbed by a single living footstep.

She looked at the display boards outside Room 12.

The poster of the water cycle had curled into a dry tube, its blue drawing pins rusted into the cork. The rain on the paper didn't fall; the clouds didn't move. Everything in this duplicate building was an unmoving memorial to twenty-four hours that could never be lived again.

She turned toward the central pillar outside the library, her sneakers pressing into the fallen plaster.

Sadie sprinted along the east corridor, her sneakers slapping against the vinyl tiles as plaster dust rained down from the ceiling joints.

Through the glass panel of Room 16, she saw thirty desks shuddering on their metal frames, the wooden tops jumping two inches into the air as the seismic shockwaves traveled up through the foundation piles. On the green chalkboard, the chalk-drawn map of Europe was fracturing into white puzzle pieces that slid down the slate in a dry cascade.

She hit the double doors leading to the central foyer with both shoulders, bursting through into the stroboscopic glare of the emergency beacons.

Sadie stepped sideways, slipping between Chloe's rigid shoulder and the frozen figure of Jessica Mills, and broke into a dead sprint along the linoleum.

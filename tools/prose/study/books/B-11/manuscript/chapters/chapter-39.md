# Chapter 39: The Caretaker's Key

On Monday morning at eight o'clock, the corridors of Ashfield Comprehensive smelled of fresh paint and damp sawdust.

A team of structural engineers from the county council had spent the weekend inspecting the science block. Yellow safety notices were posted outside the west wing doors, declaring the area safe for restricted student transit while permanent steel underpinning was installed beneath the foundation walls.

Sadie walked down the basement stairs, carrying Garside's heavy rubber torch in both hands.

The boiler house door stood open.

Inside, the cellar was bright, illuminated by two new halogen work lamps mounted on tripods. The air was warm and clean, smelling of fresh mortar, machine oil, and sweet tea.

Mr. Garside was kneeling by the recess behind Boiler Three.

He had removed the yellow glass-wool lagging from the foundation wall. In place of the rusted iron hatch, a solid, twelve-inch wall of fresh Staffordshire engineering bricks had been laid into the aperture, bonded with thick ribbons of dark gray waterproof mortar.

On the floor beside Garside lay three heavy steel chains and a massive brass Yale padlock, its hardened shackle greased with vaseline.

Garside wiped his trowel on an oily rag and looked up.

"Morning, lass," Garside said.

"Morning, Mr. Garside," Sadie said. She held out the rubber torch. "I brought your torch back. And your handkerchief. My mum washed it."

She handed him the folded red cotton handkerchief, clean and ironed flat.

Garside took the torch and set it on the workbench. He unfolded the red handkerchief, looked at the clean fabric, and tucked it into the top pocket of his boiler suit.

"Good girl," Garside said.

He picked up the heavy steel chain and wrapped it twice around the cast-iron gas main and the new anchor bracket set into the brickwork, snapping the master padlock shut with a heavy, satisfying click.

"That'll hold it," Garside said, tapping the lock with the wooden handle of his trowel. "Twenty tons of engineering brick and three bags of quick-setting ciment fondu. A tank couldn't get through there now."

He stood up, groaning as his stiff knees straightened, and walked to his workbench.

He poured two mugs of hot tea from his thermos, dropped three lumps of sugar into Sadie's mug, and handed it to her.

"How's the arm?" Garside asked.

"Fine," Sadie said, taking a sip of the sweet, piping-hot tea. "The nurse took the bandage off this morning."

Garside nodded, leaning against the warm steel of Boiler Three.

"Good," Garside said. "Now get yourself upstairs before Henderson catches you out of form room. You've got double science at nine, and Finch doesn't like people arriving late."

Sadie finished her tea, set the empty mug on the bench, and turned toward the stairs.

In the boiler house, the warm air smelled of fresh mortar, pipe oil, and sweet tea from Garside's thermos.

Sadie stood by the workbench, watching the head caretaker wipe the blade of his bricklayer's trowel on an oily rag. The new brick wall behind Boiler Three was solid, twelve inches of dark red Staffordshire engineering bricks bonded with gray ciment fondu, blocking the foundation recess forever.

Above the new brickwork, the heavy iron chain wrapped around the gas main held the brass Yale master padlock securely against the wall.

"My Jenny would have liked you, Sadie," Garside said quietly, setting the trowel on the timber bench. "She was a stubborn lass too. Never knew when she was beaten."

Garside reached into a wooden drawer beneath his workbench, took out a heavy brass washer the size of a saucer, and laid it in Sadie's palm.

The metal was heavy, stamped with the lettering: *ASHFIELD COLLIERY — SHAFT 4 — 1971*.

"Keep that in your pocket, lass," Garside said, his voice gruff but gentle. "Remind yourself that things built by men can always be rebuilt. The coal seam took fifty years to settle, but the school's still standing, isn't it? Concrete and brick. Good honest Yorkshire engineering."

Sadie closed her fingers around the cool brass ring, feeling the heavy metal press against her palm.

She nodded once, slipped the brass token into her blazer pocket, and picked up her canvas satchel from the bench.

Sadie walked to the doorway of the boiler house, the heavy brass colliery token heavy in her blazer pocket.

She turned back to look at Mr. Garside. The head caretaker stood by his workbench, his back to her, carefully cleaning the edge of his bricklayer's trowel with an oily cotton rag. The fresh wall of engineering bricks behind Boiler Three was solid and dark, three heavy steel chains padlocked tight across the mortar joints, sealing the subterranean foundation forever.

The boiler burner flared with a steady, quiet blue flame behind its round mica inspection window, casting long, warm shadows across the dry engineering pavers.

"See you tomorrow, Mr. Garside," Sadie called out.

Garside didn't turn around, but he raised his wrench in a brief, friendly wave. "Mind the steps, Bell. And don't go tracking mud into the library."

Sadie paused on the bottom landing of the basement stairs, looking back through the open doorway into the warm workshop.

Mr. Garside was hanging his heavy pipe wrenches on their steel pegboard, his thick hands moving with slow, practiced precision. Beside the workbench, the kettle on the electric ring gave a quiet, whistling hiss, venting a plume of white steam toward the dark rafters.

On the foundation wall behind Boiler Three, the new engineering bricks gleamed dark red beneath the halogen work lamps, solid and permanent as the bedrock beneath the town.

Sadie turned and climbed the stairs into the noisy corridor above.

Garside watched her climb the concrete steps into the morning light, picked up his trowel, and returned to his mortar board.

# Chapter 14: The Science Lab Spill

On Wednesday morning during second period, Sadie descended through the hatch into the yesterday school.

The silence in the replica building was heavier than before, possessing a subtle, rhythmic pulse that made the small bones behind Sadie's eardrums vibrate. The air smelled of sulfur, damp plaster, and the sharp tang of copper filings.

She carried an eight-inch flathead screwdriver taken from her father's old metal toolbox under the kitchen sink.

She walked up the west service stairs to the first floor.

The door to Science Laboratory 3 stood closed. Sadie turned the brass lever handle and pushed.

The laboratory was frozen in Tuesday morning's double chemistry lesson. Twenty-four wooden benches with black acid-resistant laminate tops were arranged beneath high gas supply pipes. Bunsen burners sat beside iron tripods, their gray rubber hoses coiled across the bench tops like sleeping adders.

At Bench 2, Chloe Rimmer's blue plastic stool sat vacant.

Draped across the wooden backrest of the stool was Chloe's favorite cream-colored wool cardigan, an expensive cable-knit garment from an American boutique in Meadowhall that Chloe wore in defiance of school uniform regulations. Beside the cardigan, resting on the laminate bench, sat Chloe's clear plastic pencil case and an open textbook showing organic hydrocarbon diagrams.

Directly above Bench 2, mounted on the wall between two electrical conduit pipes, was the chemical reagent shelf.

Sadie stood on the wooden crossbar of a spare stool.

On the third shelf, arranged in order of concentration, sat five two-liter glass winchesters of mineral acids. Each bottle was made of dark amber glass, fitted with a heavy ground-glass stopper sealed with yellow paraffin wax.

The second bottle from the left was labeled in faded black print: *HYDROCHLORIC ACID — 2.0 MOLAR — CORROSIVE*.

Sadie reached up. Her hands were cold, but her fingers gripped the neck of the heavy bottle with firm precision.

She did not pull the bottle down. That would leave an empty space that Mr. Finch might notice in the morning. Instead, she took the flat blade of the screwdriver and wedged it beneath the rim of the ground-glass stopper.

She applied steady upward purchase.

The brittle paraffin wax cracked with a faint, papery snap. The ground-glass stopper lifted an eighth of an inch from its tapered seat, resting loose inside the neck.

Next, Sadie slid the base of the bottle forward along the wooden shelf.

She nudged it until the front curve of the heavy glass base overhung the wooden lip by three-quarters of an inch, perfectly balanced on its center of gravity. A vibration through the floorboards—a door slamming, a heavy footstep on the stairs, or the boiler pump kicking in—would be enough to tip the winchester forward over the edge.

It would fall directly onto the stool where Chloe draped her cardigan.

Sadie stepped down from the stool.

She stood in the quiet aisle between Bench 2 and Bench 3, her sneakers planted on the mottled gray linoleum.

Along the east wall of the laboratory, the glass-fronted specimen cupboards reflected the amber strip lights. Inside the cases, jars of sheep hearts and dissected earthworms sat submerged in yellowish formalin, their labeled cards yellowed by forty years of fluorescent exposure. The liquid in the jars was completely clear, showing none of the particulate suspension that drifted through the formalin in the living school.

Sadie looked at the whiteboard by the teacher's desk.

In the living school, the board was always scarred with faint gray ghosts of past equations that the cleaning staff could never quite erase. Here, the white enamel was immaculate, holding Tuesday's marker script with razor-sharp fidelity: *EXOTHERMIC DISSOCIATION OF HYDROGEN HALIDES*.

As her shoes touched the floor tiles, she glanced toward the front of the laboratory.

Mr. Finch stood by the periodic table on the east wall. The chemistry master was frozen mid-lecture, his white laboratory coat stained with brown potassium permanganate spots along the hem. His right arm was raised toward the noble gases column, holding a red dry-wipe marker an inch from the laminated chart.

Sadie looked at his face.

The skin on Mr. Finch's cheeks looked gray and waxy, like the flesh of a chilled chicken carcass. His wire-framed spectacles sat crooked on the bridge of his nose, reflecting twin squares of amber light from the ceiling tubes.

His eyes were wide, unblinking, and fixed on the periodic table.

Sadie turned to walk toward the door.

Behind her, a dry, faint sound cut through the dead air of the room. It was not a footstep or a breath. It sounded like two sheets of dry sandpaper rubbing together inside a sealed box.

Sadie stopped in the center of the aisle.

Sadie walked past the glass display cases of the biology corridor before entering Lab 3. Inside the cases, jars of preserved dogfish and sheep brains sat submerged in pale yellow formalin, their labels yellowed by decades of artificial light. On the lower shelf, a human skeleton made of molded plastic hung from a chromed hook, its hollow eye sockets staring toward the staff room doors.

The air in the science corridor was always three degrees cooler than the rest of the school, chilled by the flat concrete roof and the draft from the high extraction fans. Through the floorboards, the distant thrum of the air handlers sounded like an old diesel locomotive idling on a siding in Doncaster yards.

Sadie walked to the chemical preparation store at the rear of Lab 3.

The door was locked with a brass rim latch, but through the wire-glass inspection square, she could see the rows of brown glass reagent bottles arranged on slatted pine shelves. Above the sink, a printed chart published by the Royal Society of Chemistry in 1968 listed the hazardous properties of volatile organic compounds, its edges yellowed and curled by decades of acid fumes.

On the preparation bench sat a glass desiccator containing blue silica gel granules, frozen in mid-absorption. The granules were as hard and glassy as lapis lazuli beads, reflecting the flat amber tubes on the ceiling with tiny, static points of light.

She stepped away from the door, her trainers whispering across the vinyl floor toward the central aisle.

Sadie walked past the physics darkroom near the fire exit of the science block.

The heavy black curtain across the door was drawn back three inches. Inside, three shallow plastic developing trays sat on the slate workbench, filled with clear stop bath and brown fixer fluid that emitted no chemical odor in the dead air. A pair of red safelights hung from the ceiling, their unlit globes coated in a fine layer of gray dust.

On the drying line above the bench, three photographic prints from a Year 10 optics experiment hung suspended by wooden pegs. The black-and-white prints showed diffraction patterns of laser light passing through double slits, the bright interference fringes sharp and clear on the glossy paper.

She stepped back into the corridor, the silence ringing in her ears like the high whine of an old transformer.

Sadie walked along the row of slate laboratory benches in Lab 3, checking the brass gas taps mounted on the center risers.

Each tap was cast from yellow brass, fitted with an oval control lever that turned with a quarter-turn rotation. She reached out and touched the lever on Bench 4. The metal was rigid, locked in the 'off' position by forty years of hardened grease and cold air.

Above the teacher's demonstration bench, the safety shower pull-chain hung from its galvanized pipe, its triangular brass handle motionless two feet above the linoleum floor. In this duplicate room, no water flowed through the pipes, no gas hissed from the nozzles; the entire apparatus stood frozen in a sterile, permanent stasis.

The hairs along the back of her neck rose. She turned her head slowly, looking back over her left shoulder toward the teacher's podium.

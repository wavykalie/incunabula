# Chapter 11: Locker 142

The lower gym corridor in the yesterday school smelled of decades of dried sweat, cold concrete, and floor sealant that had never quite cured.

Sadie walked with her back pressed against the green steel lockers, her fingers trailing along the row of louvered vents. In this replica hallway, the amber light had a thicker quality, pooling in the corners where the ceiling tiles met the rough breezeblock. Through the high wire-glass windows above the fire exit, the sky showed Monday's flat, zinc-colored clouds, frozen in their drifting crawl across the moors.

She stopped at Locker 142.

A photograph of a white Persian kitten was taped inside the plastic name-card holder, its edges decorated with tiny pink glitter hearts drawn in gel pen. Above the latch, someone had scratched initials into the enameled paint with a compass needle.

Sadie touched the padlock.

It was a heavy brass four-dial combination lock with a hardened steel shackle, bought from the locksmith on Thorne High Street. Chloe always boasted that her older brother had given it to her from his motorcycle kit. On the brass casing, the dials were set to four zeros, locked tight through the steel staple of the locker door.

From her blazer pocket, Sadie produced a yellow plastic cartridge of industrial quick-set epoxy resin.

She had found it twenty minutes earlier in Mr. Garside's maintenance cupboard beneath the west stairs, wedged between a gallon tin of floor stripper and a bundle of wire brushes. The nozzle had been snipped at a sharp angle; the two-part compound inside smelled of sulfur, ammonia, and rotten fish.

The green steel lockers in this wing had been manufactured by an industrial fabrication works in Rotherham in the autumn of 1971. Each locker unit stood six feet high, three bays wide, pressed from fourteen-gauge mild steel sheet and enameled in an olive tone that had darkened over half a century to the shade of stagnant pond water. Along the top and bottom of each door, three rows of six horizontal louvers allowed air to circulate through the interior, carrying the stale reek of forgotten plimsolls, damp towels, and the sweet strawberry mist that the Year 8 girls sprayed into their lockers between double geography and morning break.

Through the upper louvers, Sadie observed the interior of the steel compartment. Taped to the inside of the door was a postcard from the Pleasure Beach in Blackpool showing the roller coaster against a garish sunset, its edges curled by the damp air. On the upper shelf sat Chloe's silver pencil case, a half-empty bottle of coconut hair serum, and three packs of bubblegum. On the lower floor, resting on a sheet of folded newspaper from Doncaster, stood Chloe's white Puma trainers, their leather clean, their pink laces tied in oversized bows.

Beside the trainers lay a small, red velvet jewelry pouch that Chloe always hid beneath her spare socks before PE.

Sadie remembered Chloe bragging in the cloakroom three weeks ago about the gold sovereign ring her uncle had brought her from Spain. "It's real eighteen carat," Chloe had told Jessica Mills, holding her hand under the strip lights so the soft gold flashed. "My uncle says girls in Doncaster only wear brass rubbish from the market. He says you can always tell quality by the hallmark."

Sadie knelt on the cold composite tiles.

Her hands remained steady. The trembling that usually afflicted her fingers in Chloe's presence had vanished, replaced by an icy, methodical focus that slowed her breathing. She uncapped the tube and inserted the pointed plastic tip directly into the keyway and the internal dial tumbler gaps of the brass lock.

She squeezed the plastic tube with both thumbs.

The gray, viscous resin oozed out in a thick ribbon, filling the four dial crevices, sealing the brass wheels against their internal springs, and overflowing around the steel shackle until the entire mechanism was encased in a sticky shell.

Within forty seconds, the resin began to warm, reacting with the air in a faint chemical hiss. The gray paste turned milky white, stiffening into a solid polymer that fused the brass tumblers into a single lump of dead metal.

She wiped the excess paste from the locker face with a bus ticket from her pocket, ensuring the numerals remained visible so nobody would suspect tampering until the dials were touched.

To anyone looking at the lock in the morning, the brass would appear completely normal. Only when Chloe tried to spin the tumblers would she find that the wheels had been cast into solid, immovable stone.

Twenty yards away, standing beside the water fountain, was the frozen figure of Courtney Vance. Courtney was bent over the stainless steel basin, her mouth two inches from the cold chrome spout, a single drop of water hanging suspended in mid-air below the nozzle like a tiny glass bead.

Sadie walked past her without slowing.

The lower corridor stretched eighty yards toward the gymnasium doors. Along the right-hand wall, old framed photographs showed Ashfield Comprehensive's athletic teams from the late nineteen-seventies: rows of grim-faced teenage boys in muddy cotton jerseys, their knees scraped raw from cinder pitches, clutching silver cups with tarnished handles. Above the boys' heads, the brickwork was scarred where a radiator had been ripped out during the winter fuel shortages of 1979.

Sadie reached the double swing doors of the gymnasium.

She peered through the diamond-shaped wired glass panes. Inside the cavernous hall, sixty Year 9 boys were frozen in the middle of a basketball drill. Four brown leather balls hung suspended at various heights beneath the high steel rafters, motionless as museum specimens pinned to velvet boards. Mr. Abbott, the PE master, stood on the center line with a silver pea whistle clamped between his teeth, his clipboard raised in his left fist.

The room smelled of floor polish, canvas gym shoes, and cold sweat.

Sadie stepped back from the doors. The absolute immobility of the sixty boys made her collar itch. She had spent two years wishing the corridors would empty, wishing the shouting crowds would dissolve into thin air so she could cross the school without being shoved into lockers or tripped on the steps. Now that the silence was real, it pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

She checked the wall clock above the trophy cabinet.

11:22 AM.

In the real school, period three was ending. The bell would clang in the corridors above, releasing four hundred hungry pupils into the dining hall gauntlet. If she stayed down here too long, Mrs. Appleby would log her absence on the terminal in Room 14, and Henderson would print another yellow warning slip.

She moved quickly along the row of olive lockers, keeping her elbows tucked close to her ribs.

At the far corner of the lower gym wing, where the building intersected the boiler flue stack, a secondary store room stood behind a sheet-metal door. The stenciled sign read: *APPARATUS & GROUNDSKEEPING — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY*.

The latch on the store room door was unlocked, resting on its brass keeper. Sadie pushed the heavy leaf inward.

Inside, twenty wooden vaulting horses stood stacked against the back wall, their tan suede upholstery scarred by grease spots and decades of damp air. Bundles of cane relay batons lay in a wire basket beside thirty pairs of black rubber plimsolls sorted into fruit crates by shoe size. The room smelled of linseed oil, chalk dust, and the damp horsehair padding of old landing mats.

On a high wooden shelf beside the doorway, Sadie spotted Mr. Garside's secondary oiling kit. A half-empty pint tin of Three-in-One machine lubricant sat next to a roll of heavy cotton rags and a brass-bristled wire brush.

She picked up the cotton rag and wiped the sticky gray residue of the epoxy resin from the tips of her thumbs. The sulfur smell remained ingrained in her cuticles, sharp and sour like rotting cabbage leaves left in a wet ditch.

She dropped the rag back onto the bench beside the wire brush.

Through the narrow high ventilation louvers of the store room, the sound of the living school above was completely absent. In the living building, whenever the Year 9 boys used the gymnasium, the hardwood parquet floor would rumble like thunder, transmitting the vibrations down into the foundations. Here, there was only the dead, sterile hush of the yesterday splay.

Sadie walked back out into the locker corridor.

At the end of the wing, the iron fire door stood four inches ajar, propped open with a wooden wedge. Sadie slid through the gap into the service conduit. The air turned immediately cooler, dropping ten degrees within two strides as the bare earth beneath the foundations absorbed the ambient heat.

Beyond the gymnasium doors, the corridor branched into the boys' changing rooms. Sadie walked past the rows of wooden peg rails where forty blue gym shirts hung suspended in the amber air, their cotton folds stiff as starched linen. The air here was colder than the main hallway, carrying the faint, sharp smell of chlorinated footbaths and carbolic soap that had dried into the tile grouting thirty years ago.

She stopped by the team tactics board on the far wall. A chalk diagram of a corner kick from 1994 remained undisturbed, the white chalk lines crisp and powdery on the black slate. Above the board, a printed county council notice announced: *ALL JEWELLERY MUST BE REMOVED PRIOR TO GYMNASTIC INSTRUCTION — BY ORDER OF THE EDUCATION COMMITTEE*.

Sadie turned back toward Locker 142, her sneakers whispering over the cold floor tiles.

Along the south wall of the gym corridor, the old honors boards recorded thirty years of athletics champions from Ashfield Secondary Modern before the school became a comprehensive in seventy-four. The gold leaf lettering had flaked away from the mahogany veneer, leaving dark gray scars where names of cross-country champions had faded into the grain.

Sadie touched the bottom edge of the board. The wood was bone dry, lacking the faint dampness that seeped into the walls of the living school during wet Octobers. In this hallway, no damp rose from the foundations; no mold formed in the plaster seams. The building existed in a state of suspended preservation, trapped in the twenty-four-hour lag like an insect under a glass paperweight.

She walked past the drinking fountain where Courtney Vance stood frozen, her shoes making no sound on the green vinyl tiles.

Sadie explored the narrow space behind the gymnasium apparatus store in the yesterday school.

Stacked against the breezeblock wall were thirty wooden agility benches from the nineteen-fifties, their varnish scarred by decades of rubber gym shoes, their iron corner brackets painted with chipped black enamel. In the corner stood an old medicine ball made of heavy brown cowhide, its thick seams split along the bottom, spilling dried horsehair padding onto the concrete floor.

She ran her hand along the smooth leather of the ball. The hide was cold, stiff as ungreased harness leather, holding the faint, sour odor of fifty years of teenage sweat and floor sweeping wax.

Above her head, high ventilation louvers opened onto the service shaft beneath the west stairs.

Through the narrow iron slats, she could hear the absolute, dead hush of the duplicate building. In the living school, whenever the gym classes ran shuttle relays, the hardwood floor would thunder like an iron bridge under a freight train. Here, there was only the forty-five-hertz electrical drone humming through the concrete slab.

She walked back to Locker 142, her hands steady, her fingers ready to apply the final coat of epoxy resin to the brass combination dials.

Sadie walked past the equipment cage in the lower gymnasium, her sneakers whispering over the cold floor tiles.

Inside the wire enclosure, thirty leather medicine balls sat stacked in wooden racks, their brown cowhide covers scuffed by decades of use. Beside them, two dozen wooden vaulting boxes were piled four high, their suede tops stained with white magnesium carbonate dust from gymnastic chalk.

She paused by the door of the physical education office.

Through the wired glass pane, Mrs. Finch's desk sat frozen under the amber strip lights. A stack of orange cross-country bibs was piled beside an open register, a silver whistle resting on a green lanyard across the desk calendar. On the wall above the desk, an aerial photograph of Ashfield Comprehensive taken in 1976 showed the brand-new science wing standing raw and bright against the dark spoil heaps of the colliery.

Sadie walked to the end of the lower gym corridor where the emergency fire exit opened onto the gravel service yard.

Through the wire-glass inspection pane, the sky over the bicycle sheds was frozen in Monday morning's flat zinc light. A plastic supermarket carrier bag was suspended four feet above the chain-link fence, caught in mid-flight by a gust of wind that had ceased blowing twenty-four hours ago, its red lettering reading *THORNE CO-OP* against the gray clouds.

She touched the horizontal panic bar on the door. The painted steel was cold as ice, locked solid into its latch keepers by the temporal stasis of the replica wing.

Sadie looked at the plastic name card holder on Locker 142.

Behind the photograph of the white kitten, Chloe had slipped a cut-out horoscope from a teenage magazine: *Scorpio — Someone is watching your every move this week. Don't let jealousy ruin your weekend plans.* Sadie ran her fingernail along the plastic edge of the holder. The plastic was brittle from cold, smelling of cheap perfume and stale bubblegum that had dried into the steel crevices.

Sadie examined the latch of Locker 142 once more before stepping away into the corridor.

The olive-green enamel around the handle was worn smooth where Chloe's knuckles had brushed against the metal twice a day for two terms. Above the latch, someone had scratched a tiny cartoon heart into the paint with a compass needle, enclosing the letters *C.R. + R.F.* in crooked strokes.

Sadie touched the cured epoxy on the brass dials. The polymer was cold and hard as granite.

She climbed the iron rungs set into the concrete wall of the boiler shaft, gripped the hatch counterweight, and pulled herself through into the dark warmth behind Boiler Three.

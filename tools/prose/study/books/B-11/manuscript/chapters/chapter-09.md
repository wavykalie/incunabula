# Chapter 9: The Realization of Power

During the mid-morning break, Sadie avoided the covered walkway where the Year 8 girls clustered in their tight, whispering circles.

The metal fire escape at the rear of the technology block offered a quiet refuge. The steps were made of galvanized steel grating that let the cold October wind blow straight up through her tights, but nobody ever came up here because the fire exit at the top was alarmed and padlocked from the inside.

She leaned against the wet iron handrail, looking down at the school.

From the third-floor landing, Ashfield Comprehensive looked small, squalid, and gray. The three flat-roofed blocks had been built in the early seventies from pre-cast concrete panels that had weathered to the color of wet newsprint. Below her, the central quad was a sea of black umbrellas and steaming green blazers.

Near the bike sheds, Chloe Rimmer stood surrounded by her court.

Chloe was holding court, waving her arms dramatically as she re-enacted the destruction of her pencil to an audience of five Year 9 girls. Even from forty feet up, Sadie could hear the shrill cadence of Chloe's complaints, punctuated by the high, barking laughter of Jessica Mills.

Sadie opened her right fist.

In her palm lay the flattened steel spring from the pencil. The thin wire was bent into a misshapen zigzag, its silver coating scratched by the gravel on her shoe sole.

She turned the wire over with her index finger.

The causal link was absolute.

Altering something in the yesterday school did not shift the past immediately; yesterday’s Sadie had still sat at that desk, had still chewed her pen, had still carried her milk-stained rucksack home in the rain.

The change did not move backward. It waited.

It waited twenty-four hours, coiled inside the fabric of the building like a spring under tension, and then released itself into today’s reality at the exact minute she had enacted it.

She had snapped the pencil at ten-fifteen on Monday in the duplicate room.

The pencil had snapped at ten-fifteen on Tuesday in Chloe's hand.

Sadie leaned over the handrail, her pulse thumping in her ears with a slow, heavy rhythm.

Months had passed since her father packed his three nylon sports bags into the back of his brother’s Ford Cortina and driven south to Northampton. Since that morning, she had been a ghost in this school. She had learned to walk without looking at faces; she had learned to eat her lunch in cold corners where the CCTV cameras had blind spots; she had learned to absorb insults, shoves, stolen homework, and kicked shins with her teeth clamped shut, waiting for the three-thirty bell to release her to an empty terraced house where her mother worked eleven-hour shifts.

Remaining invisible had seemed like her only armor.

That belief had proved hollow. Invisibility did not stop them; it only made them bolder, like stray dogs circling a sheep that had fallen into a ditch.

Down in the quad, Chloe took out a packet of strawberry chewing gum, peeling the silver wrapper with a flick of her manicured thumb. She gave a stick to Jessica, gave one to Courtney, and shoved the remaining three into the pocket of her cream cardigan with an arrogant toss of her blonde hair.

Sadie felt the cold iron of the handrail biting through her woolen gloves.

An iron hatch sat behind Boiler Three that nobody knew existed.

An entire school—an exact, silent, empty copy of yesterday’s Ashfield—lay thirty feet below the ground, waiting for her every afternoon at lunch.

Whatever she touched in that school, whatever she opened, loosened, broke, or destroyed, would strike this school tomorrow like a delayed lightning bolt.

She closed her hand around the crushed spring, feeling the sharp wire ends prick the meat of her thumb.

Direct conflict in the dining hall was unnecessary. Weeping in Mr. Henderson’s office while he tapped his red dry-erase marker and defended his school’s behavioral statistics was pointless.

A hidden mechanism was in her hands.

She pocketed the spring, walked down the metal fire escape, and headed toward the west basement door.

The wind on the metal fire escape was rising from the east, carrying the smell of wet coal smoke from the valley allotments.

Sadie stood on the top grating, looking down through the steel mesh at the quad below. The students looked like small green beetles scuttling between the concrete blocks, their voices muffled by the steady drumming of the rain on the flat roofs.

She took the flattened spring from her pocket and rolled it between her thumb and forefinger.

The rules were establishing themselves in her mind, clear and immutable as the laws of chemistry:

First: the duplicate school was not a parallel universe; it was yesterday's Ashfield, operating exactly twenty-four hours in the past.

Second: whatever physical damage was inflicted inside the duplicate school did not alter the past; yesterday remained as it had been lived.

Third: the physical consequence waited for twenty-four hours to elapse, manifesting in today's real world at the exact minute and second of the original act.

She looked down at the bike sheds where Chloe Rimmer was still pacing back and forth, waving her arms, complaining to anyone who would listen.

Sadie's lips parted in a cold, silent breath.

She had endured six months of being hunted through corridors, of having her bags emptied into recycling bins, of being tripped on concrete stairs while other girls laughed.

Now she had the key to the foundation.

Sadie walked past the railway coal siding on her way home, her hands tucked deep inside her blazer pockets.

The rusty iron hopper wagons stood on the overgrown sidings, their black steel sides stenciled with faded white lettering: *NCB SOUTH YORKSHIRE AREA*. Between the rotting wooden sleepers, wild rosebay willowherb had seeded in thick, brown thickets, rattling in the cold autumn breeze.

She looked at the high brick chimney of Ashfield Comprehensive rising above the slate roofs of the terrace houses.

The causal link between yesterday and today was an invisible lever. A child with a spanner or a pair of pliers in the replica corridors could bring down the whole school, brick by brick, without anyone ever seeing her touch a single bolt in the living world.

She turned from the railing, walked down the galvanized steel fire stairs, and headed for the west entrance.

# Chapter 24: The Threat of Thursday

Sadie woke at 05:30 on Friday morning drenched in cold sweat, her cotton nightshirt plastered to her ribs.

The realization struck her with the physical violence of a blow to the solar plexus.

The fire extinguisher had fallen in Wednesday's yesterday school.

That meant the causal wave was traveling forward through the twenty-four-hour temporal conduit. At 08:45 AM on Friday morning—in exactly three hours and fifteen minutes—the rusted bracket on the top landing of the west stairs would shear in the real, living school.

At 08:45 AM, the west stairs were packed with six hundred pupils rushing from registration to period one.

Year 7 children, carrying heavy bags of books; Year 8 girls clustered around the mirrors; teachers carrying stacks of marking.

If twenty-five kilograms of steel fell from the top landing into that packed, surging crowd, someone would be killed. The cylinder would crush a skull or shatter a spine on the concrete steps.

Sadie threw off the duvet, her hands shaking so hard she could barely pull on her bottle-green skirt.

She didn't wait for her mother to wake. She didn't eat breakfast. She grabbed her rucksack, shoved her feet into her school shoes without tying the laces, and burst out into the dark, rain-swept streets of Ashfield.

The autumn gale screamed down the valley from the moors, tearing at her blazer lapels and driving freezing rain directly into her face.

She ran down the hill past the railway crossing.

The town was still asleep. A single milk float rattled along Thorne Road, its yellow headlights cutting through the gray mist. The street lamps buzzed with cold humidity.

She reached the school gates at 07:45 AM.

The tall wrought-iron gates were padlocked shut with an iron chain. Mr. Garside did not unlock the perimeter until eight o'clock.

Sadie ran along the chain-link fence behind the playing fields until she reached the gap near the groundskeeper's shed where the wire had been peeled back by truant boys. She scrambled through the wet mud, tearing the hem of her green skirt on a rusted barb, and dropped onto the soggy grass of the football pitch.

The school stood dark and silent against the bruised dawn clouds.

Only the sodium security lights burned above the kitchen entrance, casting long, orange bars across the wet asphalt of the playground.

Sadie ran down Thorne Road in the gray morning drizzle, her rucksack banging against her shoulder blades with every stride.

The terraces of Ashfield were dark, their chimney pots venting thin trails of wood smoke into the damp mist. A diesel bus passed her at the crossroads, its interior lit with pale yellow strip tubes, carrying shift workers in fluorescent jackets toward the distribution warehouses on the motorway junction.

She didn't stop for breath at the railway bridge. She scrambled up the cinder embankment, jumped the low concrete retaining wall, and sprinted across the school playing fields toward the west wing.

The clock on the municipal church tower struck eight o'clock as her shoes hit the wet asphalt of the playground.

Sadie sat at her small kitchen table by the window, a ruled exercise book open beneath the bare light bulb.

She drew a timeline in blue biro across the page: *08:30 — Gates open. 08:40 — Registration bell. 08:45 — Period One transition.*

At 08:45, the west stairwell carried more traffic than any other junction in the school. Over two hundred students moved down from the second-floor humanities block while another hundred climbed from the lower dining hall.

She underlined the time three times in red ink. If she didn't reach the landing before the 08:45 bell, the falling steel cylinder would hit the crowd like a bomb.

Sadie stood by the canal lock gates in the predawn mist, watching the dark water swirl around the timber sluices.

Across the water, the street lamps of Thorne Road were reflecting off the wet asphalt like yellow bars. She checked the face of her digital wristwatch: 06:15 AM. In two hours and thirty minutes, the morning registration bell would sound, sending six hundred students into the west wing corridors.

She knew the exact pace of the morning crowd: the Year 7s hurrying to avoid late marks, the Year 8 girls clustering around the mirrors in the lower cloakroom, the teachers carrying stacks of marked homework toward the staff room.

If the extinguisher tore free from the cinderblock while the flight was packed, the falling steel would crush whoever was standing on the third step.

She tightened the straps of her rucksack and began to run toward the railway bridge.

In the small kitchen of her council house, Sadie packed her canvas satchel under the yellow light of the bare bulb.

She tucked a small rubber torch into the bottom corner, followed by a spare pair of thick wool socks and her blue exercise book. On the kitchen counter, the small electric radio was murmuring the early morning shipping forecast for Tyne, Dogger, and Humber: *Wind southwest, force six to gale eight, rain or sleet, visibility moderate to poor.*

She zipped the bag shut, pulled on her bottle-green blazer, and slipped quietly through the front door into the dark, rain-swept street.

Sadie sprinted across the tarmac toward the west wing fire doors, her wet shoes squeaking with every stride.

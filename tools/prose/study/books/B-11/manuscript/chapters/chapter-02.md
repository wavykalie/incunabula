# Chapter 2: The Dining Hall Gauntlet

The smell of the dining hall on a wet Tuesday hit the throat like hot bleach and old cabbage.

Sadie stood in the doorway by the fire exit, her canvas rucksack held tight against her stomach with both arms. Two hundred students were wedged along the gray folding tables, their knees bumping under the plastic laminates, their wet blazers steaming in the humid roar of the room. The queue for the hot meal counter stretched all the way back to the double doors: three dozen Year 7 boys jostling for rectangular slabs of cheese pizza while Mrs. Clough, behind the aluminum sneeze guard, slapped scoops of yellow instant mash onto divided plastic trays with a wet thwack.

Mr. Henderson stood upon the stage steps, his voice barking across the din. He held a blue plastic clipboard under his elbow and a bamboo travel mug in his right hand. The silicone seal around the lid had split, leaving a brown smear of milky coffee down the side of his thumb.

"Keep moving, Year Eight," Henderson called out, waving his clipboard toward the tray return racks. "Trays to the washer window before the ten-past bell, or whole tables stay behind for detention. That means you, Ryan."

Sadie had four minutes before the bell to reach the west stairs without crossing the central aisle, but the narrow lane between the bench ends was jammed with wet parkas and sports bags.

She took three steps forward, keeping her head down, her eyes fixed on the scuffed gray floor tiles.

"Well look who crawled out of the drain," a voice said.

Chloe Rimmer sat on the corner of the end table, her gray uniform skirt rolled three times at the waistband to bring the hem four inches above her knees. Her blazer was draped over the back of her plastic chair, revealing a tight white cotton shirt with the collar turned up. Between her manicured fingers she twirled a bright pink mechanical pencil, tapping the eraser against the plastic edge of a half-empty tray.

Jessica Mills sat beside her, chewing on a strip of strawberry bubblegum, while Courtney Vance guarded the gap between their bench and the tray return stack.

"Where is your lunch, Bell?" Chloe asked. She spoke without shouting, yet her voice had that carrying, flat northern sharpness that cut straight through the clatter of cutlery and plastic beakers. "Did your mum forget to buy bread again, or did you eat it out of the bin behind the kitchen?"

"Leave it, Chloe," Sadie muttered. Her voice emerged too quiet, muffled against the canvas of her bag.

"What did you say?" Chloe stood up. She was four inches taller than Sadie, her shoulders squared under the white shirt. She smelled of artificial strawberry lip-gloss and the sweet coconut mist she sprayed in the girls' toilets after second period. "Look at me when I speak to you. You got no manners?"

"I am going to the library," Sadie said, trying to squeeze through the eighteen-inch space between Courtney's chair and the waste bin.

Courtney shifted her hips, blocking the passage with her knee. "Library is shut, Bell. Year Eleven exams."

Sadie tried to turn back toward the fire exit, but two Year 9 boys carrying full trays of curry sauce pushed past her, shoving her sideways against the edge of Chloe's table. Her canvas rucksack slipped down her forearm, its brass zip gaping open where the pull-tab had broken off three months ago.

Chloe looked down into the open bag. Her eyebrows twitched with sudden delight.

"Oh, look at this," Chloe laughed, turning to Jessica. "She has got her geography book in here. Look at the corners. All chewed up."

"Give it back," Sadie said, reaching for the bag.

Chloe moved faster. Her fingers hooked into the strap of the rucksack, dragging it onto the table surface. With her other hand, she picked up a small, open carton of Cravendale chocolate milk from Jessica’s tray. It was three-quarters full, lukewarm and skinning over at the spout.

"You look thirsty, Sadie," Chloe said, her smile wide and cold. "You look like you need some proper calcium. That is what Henderson says in assembly, isn't it? Healthy bones."

She tipped the carton upside down directly over the open mouth of the rucksack.

The brown, syrupy liquid poured in a thick cord over Sadie's lined exercise books, soaking through the blue cardboard cover of her atlas, flooding the fabric pencil case with its chewed biros and broken eraser. It pooled at the bottom of the canvas, dripping through the seam onto Sadie's school shoes with a soft, heavy patter.

Jessica shrieked with laughter, clapping both hands over her mouth. At the next table, four boys turned round, banging their plastic spoons against their trays in rhythm.

A chorus of jeers rose from the benches.

Sadie stood frozen. A drop of chocolate milk had splashed onto the white collar of her shirt, soaking into the cotton fibers like a brown tear. The smell was suffocating—sweet, rancid dairy and cocoa powder.

From the far end of the hall, Mr. Henderson blew his plastic whistle. The sharp, piercing trill silenced the immediate circle of tables for three seconds.

"Right, that is enough noise on Table Four," Henderson called out, not looking up from his clipboard. "Clear your rubbish. The bell is going in two minutes."

Chloe dropped the empty milk carton onto the tray with a soft plop. She wiped her thumb against her paper napkin, looked Sadie straight in the eyes, and smiled.

"Clumsy," Chloe whispered. "You really ought to zip your bag, Bell."

Sadie grabbed the wet canvas straps, hauled the dripping rucksack to her chest, and ran for the fire door.

The disabled toilet beside the music block was the only room in the west wing without a frosted glass panel in the door.

Sadie slipped inside, turning the heavy brass thumb-turn until the deadbolt clicked into the strike plate. She leaned against the cold porcelain basin, her chest heaving, the stench of sour chocolate milk rising from her blazer lapels in humid waves.

Her hands were shaking so hard she could barely turn the chrome cross-head tap.

The water spluttered from the nozzle in a freezing, chlorinated jet that splashed over her wrists. She took a wad of coarse brown paper towels from the plastic dispenser and soaked them under the flow. The paper turned to wet brown sludge in her fingers, tearing into soggy strips as she scrubbed at the dark stains on her green wool skirt.

The milk had penetrated the fabric completely. Where the dairy fat had dried into the weave, the wool had stiffened into a brittle, crusty board that smelled like vomit.

She looked up into the mirror above the basin.

The glass was scratched with compass points carved into the silver backing. In the cracked reflection, Sadie saw her own face staring back: pale skin, red eyes, a smudge of gray soot across her right cheekbone, and her brown hair escaping from its scrunchie in wild, wet wisps.

From the corridor outside, the heavy thud of soft-soled shoes approached the door. A hand rattled the handle twice with an impatient, aggressive jerk.

"Someone in here?" Courtney Vance's voice called out through the timber. "Jessica, the disabled door is locked. Bell is probably in there crying her eyes out."

A chorus of shrill laughter echoed down the corridor, followed by the clatter of lockers being slammed shut.

Sadie held her breath, her back pressed against the tiled wall, until the footsteps faded toward the lower gym. She crumpled the wet paper towels into the metal bin, pulled her damp rucksack onto her shoulders, and slipped out into the empty hallway.

The west corridor outside the dining hall was jammed with Year 7 and Year 8 students trying to escape the rain.

The air smelled of wet duffle coats, damp trainers, and the vinegar that seeped from the cafeteria chip counter. Along the wall, the lockers were painted a chipped institutional teal that showed scratches of gray undercoat beneath the enamel. Every locker door was dented where boys had kicked them shut with steel-toed boots.

Sadie squeezed past the queue by the library entrance, her head down, her rucksack clutched to her stomach.

Near the trophy cabinet, Courtney Vance and Jessica Mills were leaning against the glass case, watching the corridor crowd with sharp, predatory eyes. The trophy cabinet held thirty years of faded silver-plated cups won by Ashfield teams in the 1980s. The velvet lining behind the glass had faded to the color of wet sand, covered in a fine layer of gray dust that had seeped through the sliding wooden doors.

"There she goes," Courtney said, her voice cutting through the noise of the hallway. "Look at the back of her skirt. She has got chocolate milk right down to her socks."

Jessica snorted, blowing a pink bubblegum sphere that popped with a wet snap against her lips. "Smells like a dairy farm. I bet her mother does not even own a washing machine."

Sadie kept walking. She did not turn her head.

She had learned in infant school that answering back only prolonged the game. If you shouted, they laughed; if you cried, they mocked your tears; if you ran, they chased you down the stairs like terriers after a rabbit in a drain. The only defense was absolute, wooden silence—to turn your face into blank plaster and let the insults slide off your skin like water off greasy linoleum.

Inside the west stairwell, two older girls from Year 11 were smoking menthol cigarettes behind the fire-hose reel, their knees propped against the cast-iron risers. The smoke hung in pale blue layers beneath the ceiling, smelling of mint, burning paper, and damp nylon coats. They didn't look at Sadie as she squeezed past, their conversation continuing in low, urgent murmurs about a boy who worked the weekend shift at the petrol station on Thorne bypass.

Sadie held her breath, pressing her shoulder against the damp brickwork to avoid brushing their elbows. The stairwell smelled of fifty years of institution: floor sweeping compound, copper pipe water, and the sour vinegar fumes that drifted up from the kitchen larders below.

In the crowded corridor outside the dining hall, the prefects in their maroon blazers were trying to direct the queue of Year 7 pupils toward the water station.

"Single file against the lockers!" a tall boy shouted, his voice cracking with adolescent strain. "Keep moving! Anyone caught pushing will lose their library privilege for a fortnight!"

Sadie squeezed past the queue, her wet canvas rucksack held against her stomach.

The smell of the dining hall had followed her into the corridor: hot frying fat, vinegar, and the damp wool of two hundred rain-soaked parkas drying on the cloakroom hooks. On the wall beside the trophy cabinet, someone had taped a handwritten poster for the school production of *The Crucible*, its edges torn where boys had picked at the Sellotape during break.

Near the library entrance, Courtney Vance and Jessica Mills were leaning against the radiator, watching the hallway crowd.

"Look at her hair," Courtney whispered, pointing a manicured finger toward a Year 7 girl with crooked plaits. "She looks like she fell out of a hedge backwards. I bet her mum buys her uniform off the market stall."

Jessica giggled, blowing a pink bubblegum sphere that popped against her teeth. "Did you see Chloe dump the milk on Sadie Bell? Her face went completely red like a beetroot. She looked like she was going to wet herself."

Sadie walked past them with her chin down, her sneakers squeaking over the wet vinyl tiles.

In the girls' cloakroom beside the disabled toilet, two Year 8 girls were re-applying strawberry lip gloss in front of the chipped mirror.

"Chloe told Courtney that Sadie Bell doesn't even have a shower in her house," one girl whispered, blotting her lips on a paper towel. "Said they have to boil the kettle on the gas stove just to wash their hair. That's why she always smells like fried bread."

The other girl laughed, capping her gloss tube. "Her mum works nights at the Thorne turkey plant. My brother says the whole street down by the canal smells like chicken feathers."

Sadie stood behind the partition door, her fingernails pressing into the fabric of her damp satchel until her knuckles ached.

She waited until the swing doors clicked shut and the sound of their rubber trainers faded down the corridor before stepping out into the empty room. She looked at her reflection in the cracked mirror: a pale, narrow face framed by damp dark hair, two dark circles beneath her eyes, and a smear of yellow pipe dust along her jaw.

In the west stairwell, Sadie stood behind the fire doors for two minutes, listening to the roar of the school above.

Through the wired glass panel, she could see the entrance to the lower library. Mr. Vance, the headteacher, was walking down the corridor in his dark charcoal suit, accompanied by two school governors carrying leather briefcases. They walked with that slow, measured authority of adults who owned the bricks and mortar, their voices discussing heating oil allocations and roof asphalt grants.

Neither of them looked toward the basement stairs. To the authorities who ran Ashfield Comprehensive, the space below the concrete floor was merely an engineering conduit, a dark cellar of pipes and pumps that kept the radiators warm during the November frosts.

Sadie walked past the English department noticeboards where Mrs. Appleby had pinned the autumn reading list.

The typed sheets of paper were curling at the corners, the names of novels printed in purple stencil ink: *A Kestrel for a Knave*, *The Machine Gunners*, *Lord of the Flies*. Next to the reading list, someone had pinned a newspaper clipping about the closure of the railway locomotive works in Doncaster, its photograph showing three middle-aged men in cloth caps standing outside the locked depot gates.

In the queue for the hot meal counter, two dinner ladies were wiping down the stainless steel food warmer with yellow dishcloths.

"Look at that steam off the cabbage," Mrs. Clough muttered, adjusting her white hairnet with the back of her wrist. "The thermostat on the serving well has been sticking since Monday. If the council doesn't send an engineer before Friday, we'll be serving cold beans to Year Nine."

Sadie moved past them, keeping her head low, her canvas rucksack held against her ribs.

She pushed through the fire doors onto the west basement stairs, the heavy rubber draft excluder scraping the concrete with a long, dull sigh.

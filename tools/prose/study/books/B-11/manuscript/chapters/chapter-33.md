# Chapter 33: The Fire Alarm Lever

The air in the east corridor fought against Sadie's chest like wading through cold engine oil.

Her legs burned as she pumped her thighs, her school shoes slapping against the vinyl tiles. Behind her, the fifty yesterday figures turned in a single, grinding movement, their shoes dragging across the floor as they surged into the hallway in pursuit.

*Scritch. Thud. Scritch. Thud.*

The noise of their movement was like an avalanche of gravel shifting down a spoil heap.

Sadie reached the central pillar outside the library.

The master fire alarm call point was a heavy cast-iron box, painted bright red with an enamel front plate manufactured by the Gent Company of Leicester in 1968. Inside the circular brass bezel, a thick disc of wired plate glass protected a vertical brass trip lever.

Above the bezel, the stenciled lettering read: *IN CASE OF FIRE — BREAK GLASS — PULL LEVER HARD*.

Sadie did not have the chisel.

She turned her back to the pillar, planted her left hand against the brickwork for balance, and brought the hard rubber heel of her right shoe up in a violent, mule-kick strike against the circular glass bezel.

*SMASH.*

The heavy wired glass fractured into a spiderweb of white cracks.

She kicked it a second time.

The glass gave way, collapsing inward in a shower of sharp, jagged diamonds that fell into the bottom of the iron housing.

Sadie reached her hand into the aperture.

A sharp shard of broken glass sliced through the fleshy meat of her right thumb, but she didn't feel the cut. Her fingers gripped the cold brass handle of the internal trip lever.

The brass was stiff, coated with thirty years of grease and coal dust.

Sadie planted both trainers against the floor tiles, gripped the lever with both hands, and threw her entire body weight downward.

For a second, the mechanism resisted.

Then, deep within the cast-iron casing, a heavy clockwork spring snapped free with a sharp, mechanical chime.

The brass trip lever plummeted down through its arc, hitting the lower stop with a deafening *CLACK*.

Inside the walls of the duplicate school, thirty electrical relays closed simultaneously.

A high-voltage pulse of twenty-four volts direct current surged through the copper conduits, through the floorboards, and into the master bells mounted at twenty-yard intervals along every corridor.

Sadie gripped the brass trip lever inside the shattered glass call point, her fingers slick with blood from her cut thumb.

The clockwork mechanism inside the iron box was whirring with frantic haste, its brass gears spinning against an internal governor. Across the corridor, the red strobe beacons began to flash with a blinding, mechanical rhythm, bathing the green cinderblock walls in pulses of crimson light.

Behind her, the fifty yesterday figures were surging forward in a solid, unyielding mass.

Their shoes struck the floor with a terrifying, rhythmic roar like the rumble of coal wagons descending an incline drift. The purple light in the corridor flickered violently, dying away into the crimson glare of the emergency beacons.

The master fire alarm bells continued to scream along the upper corridors, an unbroken sonic assault that rattled the iron reinforcement rods inside the concrete ceiling.

Through the wired glass panel of the library doors, Sadie saw books tumbling from their shelves, fifty years of geography textbooks and historical atlases hitting the carpet in fluttering clouds of dry paper. The blue fluorescent tubes overhead flickered in rapid, erratic pulses, casting harsh, stroboscopic shadows across the cracking linoleum.

She pulled her bleeding thumb from the call point, wiped the blood on the hem of her blazer, and sprinted toward the west service exit.

The iron Gent & Co call point was vibrating violently against the brick pillar, its red enamel housing hot to the touch.

Inside the shattered bezel, the brass trip lever remained locked in the down position, its heavy internal clockwork spring whirring like an angry hornet. Blue electrical arcs jumped between the conduit junction boxes along the ceiling, spitting bright sparks that smelled of ozone and fried plastic.

Behind Sadie, the fifty yesterday figures were moving through the falling dust in a single, silent wedge.

Their faces were blank, their unblinking eyes reflecting the crimson flash of the fire alarm beacons with an unnatural, glassy glare. They reached out with fifty pairs of gray hands, their stiff fingers clawing at the empty air as they surged down the corridor toward her.

Sadie didn't hesitate. She threw her weight against the library fire doors and sprinted down the west stairs.

Inside the iron call point, the high-voltage electrical coils were glowing with a pale, ethereal blue light that cast eerie shadows across Sadie's bleeding knuckles.

The master alarm bells along the humanities corridor were vibrating so violently that three of the heavy cast-bronze gongs cracked down their centers, their metallic clatter shifting into a screaming, dissonant shriek that shattered the glass in every trophy cabinet along the hall.

Sadie pulled her arm back from the shattered bezel, tucked her head into her chest, and sprinted down the west corridor toward the service doors.

Then the sirens began to scream.

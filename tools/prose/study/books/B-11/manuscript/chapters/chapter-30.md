# Chapter 30: The Circle in the Hall

They stepped out from every classroom door along the eighty-foot corridor.

Fifty figures emerged into the purple gloom: students in green blazers, teachers in starched shirts, canteen workers in white cotton aprons stained with grease.

They moved without a sound.

Their rubber soles made no impact on the vinyl tiles, gliding across the floor like chess pieces moved by an invisible hand.

They formed a tight, unbroken circle around Sadie, thirty feet in diameter, blocking every exit.

In the front rank stood Chloe Rimmer.

Yesterday's Chloe was wearing her bottle-green blazer, her white blouse unbuttoned at the neck, the scrape on her cheekbone dark and encrusted with black dried blood. Her hands hung at her sides, her fingers curved into rigid, claw-like postures.

Beside Chloe stood Jessica Mills, Courtney Vance, and Mr. Henderson.

Henderson's spectacles were missing; his eyes were two hollow, dark sockets that seemed to drink the purple light. His jaw was slack, his gray tongue protruding between his yellow teeth.

Sadie gripped the cold chisel in her right hand, holding the steel point toward Chloe's chest.

"Stay back," Sadie whispered.

Her voice had no volume. The words dropped into the dead air and died on her lips.

The circle took one synchronized step inward.

The floor tiles groaned beneath fifty pairs of feet. The purple light from the ceiling tubes dimmed to a dull, violet ember, the transformers buzzing in a dissonant, screaming chord that made Sadie's vision blur.

Chloe lifted her head.

Her blue eyes—the eyes that had mocked Sadie across the dining hall for eighteen months—were glassy, dead, and empty as dry river stones.

Chloe's jaw opened with a dry, papery tear.

"Sadie," Chloe's voice whispered.

It was not a human voice. It was the voice of the building itself: the sound of steam pipes hissing in winter, of wet raincoats steaming in crowded cloakrooms, of chalk grinding into slate, of a hundred cruelties spoken behind locker doors.

"You wanted us to stop," the voice murmured. "Now we've stopped. Forever."

The fifty figures raised their arms in unison.

A forest of stiff, gray hands reached out through the purple gloom, their fingers stretching toward Sadie's face.

The fifty yesterday figures in the purple corridor stood three feet from Sadie, their outstretched hands trembling with unnatural tension.

In the front row, yesterday's Chloe tilted her head, her dead blue eyes reflecting the violet glow of the transformer tubes. A faint, sickening smell of old coal dust and scorched wool rose from her green blazer.

Sadie raised the cold chisel in both hands, her back pressed against the frozen iron hatch, her teeth clamped shut as the circle took another synchronized step forward.

The fifty figures in the purple corridor stood motionless, their outstretched fingers trembling in the dead air.

From their closed lips came that collective, sibilant whisper, vibrating through the linoleum tiles like the hum of an underground power cable.

*Sadie Bell... you broke the pencil in the front row... you locked the locker with the resin... you poured the acid on the cardigan... now you stay with yesterday...*

Sadie tightened her grip on the cold chisel, her knuckles burning with cold, her shoulder blades pressed flat against the freezing iron plate of the hatch.

The circle of fifty yesterday figures in the central foyer began to sway.

The movement was slow, rhythmic, and perfectly synchronized, thirty pairs of shoulders tilting to the left, then to the right, like reeds swaying in an underground current. The purple light tubes overhead buzzed with an erratic, high-frequency rattle that set the glass panes in the trophy cabinet vibrating.

In the front row, yesterday's Mr. Henderson raised his right hand, his gray fingers pointing directly at Sadie's throat.

"You brought the cold down the stairs, Sadie Bell," the collective whisper murmured, the sound vibrating in her dental fillings. "Now the bell is going to ring for you."

Sadie backed her shoulders against the freezing iron plate of the hatch, raising the cold chisel with both hands.

The fifty yesterday figures in the purple corridor stood three feet from Sadie, their bodies casting long, overlapping shadows across the linoleum.

From their stiff, unmoving throats came the steady murmur of school memories: missed homework marks, detentions logged on blue cards, sports days run in the rain, the dull ache of sitting in the back row hoping nobody would call your name. The collective weight of thirty years of teenage anxiety was pressing into the corridor like high-pressure steam behind an iron valve.

Sadie held the cold chisel steady in both hands, her breath misting white in the freezing air, her spine pressed against the steel plate of the hatch.

Sadie backed her shoulders against the iron hatch, feeling the freezing steel bite through her blazer, and raised the cold chisel.

# Chapter 15: The First Turned Head

Mr. Finch had not moved his arm.

His right hand remained extended toward the periodic table, the red marker still suspended an inch from the square for Argon. His white coat hung in rigid, starched folds; his brown leather shoes remained planted firmly on the gray linoleum.

But his eyes were different.

Two minutes ago, when Sadie stood by the reagent shelf, Mr. Finch's dark brown irises were locked on the upper corner of the chart, staring at atomic weights.

Now, both eyeballs had rotated fully in their sockets.

The pupils were shifted hard to the left, peering directly down the aisle toward the space between Bench 2 and Bench 4.

They were looking at Sadie.

The whites of his eyes were mapped with tiny, thread-like red capillaries that seemed unusually dark, almost black in the amber light. There was no eyelid flutter, no moisture film, no subtle micro-movement of the surrounding facial muscles. The eyes sat motionless in their corners, staring at her with a flat, predatory focus that felt heavier than an iron bar laid across her shoulders.

Sadie's breath froze in her throat.

"Mr. Finch?" she whispered.

Her voice had no resonance in the room. The syllables dropped into the dead air and vanished instantly, leaving no echo among the glass beakers and iron retort stands.

The teacher did not blink.

From somewhere deep within his throat, behind the closed teeth and the rigid jaw, came a low, liquid click. It sounded like an air bubble rising through heavy motor oil, bursting softly against the back of his palate.

Sadie took half a step backward.

Her left heel came down on the floor tiles. The flat pop of the impact seemed to trigger something in the room's geometry. The amber light from the fluorescent tubes dimmed by a fraction of a lumen, shifting toward a bruised, purplish tint that clung to the shadows beneath the workbenches.

In the second row, the frozen figure of Jessica Mills sat with her pen raised.

Sadie looked at Jessica.

Jessica's head was tilted at the same angle as before, her mouth half-open in a laugh. But as Sadie watched, the tendon running along the side of Jessica's neck tightened. A single muscle in Jessica's right cheek twitched once, a tiny, spastic shudder that pulled the corner of her lip a sixteenth of an inch higher.

The replica people were not statues.

They were clocks that had been wound too tight, their gears straining against an escapement that was beginning to slip.

A cold draft washed across the floor, smelling of stagnant coal-seam water and old brick dust. It pooled around Sadie's ankles, so cold that her shins ached through her school tights.

Sadie stared at Jessica's raised hand.

The gel pen held between Jessica's thumb and forefinger was a glitter pen filled with purple ink. In the living school, the tiny metallic flakes inside the ink suspension settled to the bottom of the barrel after an hour. Here, the silver flakes were frozen in mid-drift, but as Sadie watched, a single glitter grain shifted a fraction of a millimeter, catching the dim purple light with an unnatural, diamond-bright glint.

Beside Jessica, Courtney Vance sat with her head turned toward the window.

Courtney's jaw line remained rigid, but the collar of her blouse gave a minute, paper-thin rustle. A single black curl of her perm drifted a fraction of an inch to the side, settling against the green wool of her blazer lapel.

The room was waking up.

Not into life, but into an unnatural, synthetic mimicry of movement that possessed neither warmth nor pulse.

Sadie felt the blood drain from her face. Her fingers curled around the metal shaft of the flathead screwdriver in her pocket, the cold steel biting into her knuckles.

From the front podium, Mr. Finch's jaw opened a quarter of an inch.

The motion was accompanied by the sickening sound of dry cartilage tearing. A faint puff of gray dust—chalk dust mixed with something that smelled of dry bone—drifted out from between his discolored incisors.

Sadie did not wait for the teacher to speak.

Sadie stood in the center aisle of Lab 3, watching the frozen chemistry class. On Bench 4, two Year 9 boys were frozen in the middle of lighting a splint, the match head caught in a tiny orange flare of sulfur that cast no heat and emitted no smoke. Between the two boys, an open chemistry textbook was propped against an iron stand, its glossy page displaying a diagram of electron shells.

She took a slow, deliberate breath. The air smelled of cold zinc and sulfuric acid, but beneath the chemical tang was that persistent, dead odor of ancient coal dust that drifted from the boiler basements.

Sadie stepped three paces to her right, moving behind the wooden pedestal of Bench 3 to break Mr. Finch's line of sight.

Through the narrow space between two iron retort stands, she watched the teacher's motionless profile. Mr. Finch's spectacles sat crooked on the bridge of his nose, the left wire temple piece mended with a tiny wrap of blue electrical tape. Beneath his starched white collar, the tendon on the left side of his throat was rigid, prominent as a cord of braided hemp pulled taut across an edge.

In the second row, Courtney Vance's hand remained suspended six inches above her open ring binder.

Sadie watched Courtney's manicured fingers. On the thumbnail of Courtney's right hand, a tiny decal of a black butterfly was stuck to the pink varnish. As Sadie stared, the edge of the decal lifted a fraction of a millimeter, peeling back with a dry, papery sound that was lost in the dead air.

Sadie looked at the second row of benches in Lab 3 where the other students sat frozen.

On the far right, Jessica Mills's hand remained elevated, her ballpoint pen poised above an open exercise book. The silver charms on Jessica's charm bracelet—a tiny silver boot, a horseshoe, and a miniature Eiffel Tower—hung motionless against her wrist, their links reflecting the flat amber tubes without a flicker.

Beneath the bench, a forgotten tennis ball lay wedged between the cast-iron stool leg and the radiator pipe. The yellow felt was worn smooth along the seams, coated with gray lint from forty years of sweeping compound.

Sadie touched the edge of the laminate bench. The surface felt dense and cold, completely absorbing the warmth of her thumb within two seconds.

Sadie stepped closer to the teacher's podium, her eyes fixed on Mr. Finch's profile.

On the lapel of the chemistry teacher's white coat, a small enamel badge bore the emblem of the Royal Institute of Chemistry. The yellow enamel was chipped near the crown, revealing a tiny fleck of oxidized brass beneath the glaze.

As Sadie watched, a single hair from Mr. Finch's left eyebrow broke away, falling three inches through the purple air before coming to an abrupt, frozen halt two inches above the whiteboard marker tray.

The room was slipping. The temporal anchor that kept yesterday pinned in place was fraying like an old rope under too much strain.

She spun on the balls of her feet, caught the door handle with both hands, and threw her weight against the wood.

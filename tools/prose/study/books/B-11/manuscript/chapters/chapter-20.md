# Chapter 20: The Unblinking Crowd

On Monday lunchtime, Sadie returned to the duplicate building.

She knew she shouldn't go. Every instinct in her body, every ache in her chilled bones warned her to leave the iron hatch bolted and never look behind Boiler Three again.

But the need was stronger than fear. She had to know if the yesterday world had reset. She had to know if the frozen figures had forgotten what she did.

She crawled through the lagging, lifted the counterweight latch, and stepped through the hatch into the lower corridor.

The air in the hallway was freezing, thick with a smell like burning electrical insulation.

Sadie walked toward the central foyer where the main corridor intersected the humanities wing.

Along the green cinderblock walls, the display cases containing athletic trophies were completely dark. The fluorescent tubes on the ceiling hummed with a low, droning frequency that made Sadie's teeth vibrate in their sockets.

She turned the corner into the main entrance hall.

She stopped dead.

Standing in the center of the eighty-foot hallway was a crowd of thirty people.

They were all pupils and staff from Friday's school.

In the front rank stood Chloe Rimmer, Jessica Mills, and Courtney Vance. Behind them stood Mr. Henderson, Mr. Finch, Mrs. Appleby, and twenty other students from Year 8 and Year 9.

They were not in classroom rows. They were not walking toward lockers or queuing for lunch.

They were standing shoulder to shoulder in a wide, shallow crescent that completely blocked the corridor from wall to wall.

None of them were moving.

Their bodies were rigid, locked in the flat, amber light of the ceiling tubes. Mr. Henderson's hands were at his sides, his fingers curled into stiff hooks. Chloe stood in her green blazer, her face pale, her lips parted in an expression of frozen inquiry.

Sadie took a step forward.

As her shoe touched the linoleum, thirty heads turned in unison.

The movement was instantaneous, a sharp, mechanical click like thirty camera shutters firing at the exact same microsecond.

Every pair of eyes in the corridor locked onto Sadie's face.

Sixty unblinking irises—blue, brown, hazel, green—stared at her with an absolute, terrifying intensity. There was no hatred in their faces, no anger, no human malice. There was only a cold, mechanical hunger, the look of an empty room waiting for someone to step inside and turn on the light.

Chloe's jaw dropped open half an inch.

From her throat came the dry, sandpaper rattle Sadie had heard in the chemistry lab, followed by a faint, whistling intake of air.

Sadie backed away toward the boiler hatch.

As she retreated, the entire crowd took one synchronized step forward.

Their shoes hit the vinyl tiles with a single, flat, dead impact that made Sadie's teeth click together in her gums.

She looked at Mr. Henderson in the second row.

The Head of Year 8 was still holding his plastic clipboard, but his fingers had crushed the blue plastic frame until the clipboard buckled in the middle. His mouth was open, revealing teeth covered in a gray, fibrous coating like boiler lagging.

"Sadie," a voice rasped from the center of the crowd.

It was not Chloe's voice. It was a composite sound, twenty voices speaking through a single crushed throat, flat and devoid of pitch.

"You left your books in the water."

Sadie backed away another three yards.

The crowd took another synchronized step. The vinyl tiles groaned beneath thirty pairs of hard rubber soles.

"You broke the lead," the chorus whispered, the words drifting across the cold floor like dry leaves. "You poured the acid on the wool. Now you have to stay until the bell."

Chloe's arms began to rise.

Her manicured fingers, tipped with clear nail polish, reached out toward Sadie's face. The joints of her knuckles cracked with loud, dry snaps as the stiff tendons stretched.

Sadie did not wait to see them take a third step.

She spun on her heels, broke into a dead sprint down the service corridor, and hurled herself through the open iron hatch into the roar and heat of the living boiler room.

She grabbed the counterweight bar with both hands, slammed the iron plate shut, and dropped the heavy latch into its keeper.

Along the walls of the main entrance hall, the framed photographs of former headteachers stared down from the oak paneling. Their black-and-white faces looked stern and distant, their clerical collars stiff beneath their academic gowns.

Below the portraits, the thirty frozen figures of the yesterday crowd stood in their silent crescent, the amber light glinting off thirty pairs of unblinking eyes.

Sadie felt the cold from the linoleum seeping through the rubber soles of her shoes, chilling the small bones of her feet until they ached with a dull, leaden weight.

In the center of the crescent of frozen students, yesterday's Chloe Rimmer stood with her arms hanging at her sides.

Sadie looked at Chloe's school shoes. The black leather loafers were polished to a high shine, decorated with small gold snaffle bits across the instep. In the living school, Chloe took immense pride in those shoes, cleaning them every morning with a tin of cherry blossom polish she kept in her locker. Here, the leather was dull, coated in a fine layer of gray plaster dust that had drifted down from the ceiling joints.

Beside Chloe, Jessica Mills was frozen in mid-gesture, her right hand raised toward her earlobe as if checking a silver earring. Her braces caught the amber light with a dead, metallic sheen.

None of them moved their limbs, but thirty pairs of eyes continued to follow Sadie's every step with absolute, mechanical coordination.

Sadie examined the faces of the thirty figures blocking the central foyer.

In the second rank, behind Chloe Rimmer, stood Ryan Fletcher, the Year 9 rugby captain. Fletcher was wearing his muddy cotton sports jersey, his thick forearms crossed over his chest, his jaw set in a rigid scowl. His hair was damp with sweat that had frozen into stiff, crystalline spikes across his forehead.

Beside Fletcher stood Mrs. Clough, the canteen supervisor, holding an aluminum soup ladle in her right fist. Her white cotton overall was stained with gravy spots that had darkened to the color of dried liver.

Every face was turned toward Sadie, thirty pairs of unblinking eyes tracking her movements with flawless, mechanical synchronization.

Sadie stood in the center of the foyer, looking at the thirty frozen figures assembled before her.

In the third rank, Mr. Henderson's gray hands were locked in their claw-like grip around the buckled plastic clipboard. The papers inside the clip—the daily attendance printouts for Form 8H—were yellowed and curled at the corners, the printed dot-matrix names legible beneath the purple light.

Beside Henderson, Mrs. Appleby stood with her correction pen raised, her red ink cartridge frozen half-empty inside the clear barrel.

None of them breathed. None of them blinked. But thirty pairs of glassy pupils remained locked on Sadie's face, their gaze as heavy as thirty iron weights laid across her ribs.

Sadie stood in the center of the eighty-foot entrance foyer, her back to the locked service hatch.

The amber light from the ceiling tubes had taken on an oily, violet sheen that clung to the green painted walls like smoke. Along the perimeter of the hall, the display cabinets containing thirty years of cross-country trophies were completely dark, their glass panes reflecting the thirty unblinking figures of the yesterday crowd.

In the front row, Chloe Rimmer stood with her hands hanging at her sides, her green blazer loose on her narrow frame.

Sadie looked at Chloe's face.

The skin on Chloe's cheeks had lost all its natural color, turned the dull, waxy gray of lard left in a cold larder. The scrape on Chloe's cheekbone was dark, encrusted with a black crust of clotted blood that didn't bleed.

Beside Chloe, Mr. Henderson's gray hands were still gripping his shattered clipboard, the blue plastic frame buckled in the middle, the attendance papers curled like dead birch leaves.

"You took our time, Sadie," the collective whisper murmured, forty voices speaking through a single crushed throat. "You took our Monday. You took our Tuesday. Now you have to give them back."

The crowd took another synchronized step forward, their shoes hitting the vinyl tiles with a single, flat, dead impact that made Sadie's teeth vibrate in her gums.

In the center of the crescent of thirty figures, yesterday's Mr. Henderson stood with his arms rigid at his sides.

Sadie looked at the pastoral incident forms clamped inside Henderson's buckled blue clipboard. The top sheet bore her own name, printed in block capital letters: *BELL, SADIE — FORM 8H — UNIFORM INFRACTION / CORRIDOR WANDERING*. Beside the date, Henderson had written in red biro: *Subject exhibits persistent evasive behavior during lunch intervals. Avoids peer contact in high-density zones.*

She looked up at Henderson's face.

The skin beneath his gray stubble was as hard and unyielding as plaster of Paris. His eyes were wide and dry, the pupils fixed upon her forehead with an empty, mechanical focus that felt colder than the air off the canal.

In the center of the eighty-foot hallway, Sadie looked at the thirty frozen students and teachers.

On the left of the crescent, Ryan Fletcher stood in his muddy rugby kit, his hands clenched into fists, his fingernails showing tiny crescents of dried turf from the school pitch. In the living school, Fletcher had thrown Sadie's lunchbox into the perimeter nettles during her first week in Year 7, laughing with his friends while she crawled through the ditch to retrieve it.

Here, Fletcher's mouth was locked in that same ugly sneer, but the sneer had set like plaster, empty and dead in the violet glare of the ceiling tubes.

Sadie looked at the thirty frozen figures in the entrance hall one last time before stepping backward toward the service doors.

The yellow ceiling tubes hummed with that low forty-five-hertz electrical drone that seemed to vibrate directly through the soles of her shoes into her shin bones. In the front row, Chloe Rimmer's fingers remained rigid at her sides, her unblinking blue eyes locked on Sadie's face with cold, mechanical patience.

Sadie backed through the door, pulled the iron leaf shut, and turned the heavy brass latch.

On the other side of the quarter-inch steel plate, three heavy thuds sounded against the iron, rattling the red oxide primer in a shower of dry flakes onto Sadie's hair.

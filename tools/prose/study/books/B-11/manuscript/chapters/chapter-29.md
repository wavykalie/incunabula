# Chapter 29: The Trapped Lunch Hour

On Monday at 12:15 PM, the living school was in the grip of the lunch interval.

With the west wing sealed behind plywood barricades and warning tape, the remaining corridors were crammed with restless, noisy children. Rain lashed against the high dining hall windows in relentless gray waves, trapping six hundred students indoors.

Sadie slipped past the yellow warning barrier outside the boiler house door.

She carried Garside's brass master key in her pocket and a heavy cold chisel in her fist.

She crawled through the yellow lagging behind Boiler Three. The heat in the recess was suffocating, baking the dust on the cast-iron pipes into a stinging vapor that burned her nostrils.

The iron hatch stood closed.

Sadie took a deep breath, gripped the counterweight bar, and heaved.

The iron plate swung inward with a dry screech.

She scrambled through the aperture on her knees, dropping onto the vinyl linoleum of the duplicate corridor.

The cold hit her like a bucket of freezing river water.

The air in the hallway was dead, sterile, and smelled of burning electrical wiring mixed with ancient pulverized coal dust. The purple tint in the fluorescent light had deepened, casting long, bruised shadows along the cinderblock walls.

Sadie turned back toward the hatch to set the wooden wedge beneath the door.

Before her fingers could touch the timber, the heavy boiler-plate door slammed shut with the force of a hydraulic press.

*BANG.*

The sound boomed down the service corridor like a cannon shot.

The forged iron counterweight bar on the exterior dropped with a heavy, metallic clank. The primary drop-latch fell into the cast keeper, and through the seam of the plate, Sadie heard the sickening sound of an iron deadbolt sliding home into its socket.

She lunged forward, grabbing the interior handle with both hands.

"No!" she grunted, throwing her full weight backward.

The handle did not move. It was locked solid, frozen into the frame by forty tons of subterranean pressure.

From the other side of the quarter-inch boiler plate, there was no sound of Boiler Three, no roar of the gas burner, no hum of the circulating pump.

There was only the absolute, suffocating silence of the duplicate school.

She was locked in.

Sadie turned around slowly, her back pressed against the freezing iron plate.

The corridor stretched away into the dim purple gloom.

At the far end of the hall, where the service conduit met the humanities wing, a dark shape detached itself from the wall and stepped into the center of the linoleum.

Then another.

Sadie pressed her ear against the freezing iron plate of the locked hatch.

From the other side of the quarter-inch boiler plate, there was no sound of living human activity. The living boiler house, with its roaring burners and clanking steam pipes, was completely cut off, separated from her by thirty tons of dead temporal mass.

She struck the iron plate with the cold chisel.

The impact produced a dull, leaden thud that died instantly against the steel, leaving no echo in the freezing corridor.

She was trapped.

Sadie moved along the foundation wall of the duplicate boiler room, checking every mortar joint with the tip of her cold chisel.

Ten feet from the locked hatch, behind a bundle of rusted electrical conduits, she found the old coal delivery chute. The iron intake flap had been removed in the nineteen-seventies, replaced by three courses of dark red Staffordshire engineering bricks bonded with black mortar.

She struck the brickwork with the chisel.

The steel bit made no impression on the mortar, producing only a dull, muffled click that died against the masonry. There was no way out through the foundation walls; the splay was sealed tight around her.

Sadie walked the length of the duplicate boiler room, her trainers kicking through drifts of old lagging dust that coated the pavers like yellow snow.

At the far corner of the pit, where the steam condensate pipe entered the floor, she found a narrow drainage gully covered by an iron grating. She knelt down, gripped the rusted bars with both hands, and heaved.

The grating was cemented into the concrete frame, sealed by forty years of hardened boiler slag and mineral scale.

Through the narrow iron slots, she could see only black, stagnant mine water sitting six inches below the floor, completely still, reflecting the amber light of the corridor like a sheet of polished obsidian.

There was no way out through the drainage lines. She was sealed inside the splay.

Sadie stood in the center of the duplicate boiler room, looking at the heavy cast-iron boilers that mirrored the living cellar above.

The mica inspection windows on Boilers One, Two, and Three were dark, cold as pond ice. She pressed her hand against the sheet-metal casing of Boiler Two. In the living school, the casing was always warm enough to dry damp gym towels in ten minutes; here, the metal was chilled to forty degrees, smelling of cold soot and engine grease that had never been heated.

Above her head, the four-inch gas supply line hung silent in its pipe hangers, completely devoid of the vibration that usually made the basement rafters hum.

And another.

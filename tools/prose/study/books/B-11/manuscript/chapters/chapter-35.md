# Chapter 35: The Caretaker's Crowbar

On the living side of the foundation wall, Mr. Garside stood in the boiler house pit, drenched in sweat.

The basement air was screaming. High-pressure steam hissed from the expansion valves of Boiler Three, filling the dark cellar with blinding white vapor. The concrete floor beneath his heavy work boots was vibrating with a terrifying, five-cycle-per-second shudder that rattled the pipe wrenches on his tool racks.

In his calloused hands, Garside held a five-foot hexagonal steel crowbar, drop-forged from Sheffield high-tensile alloy.

He jammed the beveled chisel tip of the bar into the seam between the iron hatch and its cast frame, directly beneath the frozen drop-latch.

"Come on, you bastard," Garside growled through his teeth.

He threw his entire fourteen-stone weight onto the end of the long steel bar.

The forged crowbar bent into a shallow arc, the steel groaning under forty tons of subterranean torque.

Inside the seam, the iron drop-latch shivered. The brass pivot pin that had held the counterweight for half a century began to shear, spitting tiny flakes of bright yellow metal into the lagging dust.

Garside heaved again, his boots skidding across the greasy pavers.

*SNAP.*

The brass pin severed cleanly.

The heavy iron counterweight bar flew off the spindle, crashing into the brick foundation with a dull clang.

Garside kicked the iron door with the steel toe of his boot.

The hatch flew inward into the dark void.

A blast of freezing gray dust poured through the opening, smelling of pulverized coal and ozone.

Through the cloud of swirling debris, a small hand appeared, its fingers clawing at the rough engineering bricks of the threshold.

Garside dropped the crowbar.

He reached through the rectangular opening with both arms, grabbed Sadie by the shoulders of her green wool blazer, and hauled with all the strength in his thick back.

Sadie's body flew through the hatch, tumbling across the greasy pavers into the hot, sulfurous air of the boiler pit.

She hit the floor on her hands and knees, coughing violently, her face coated in a thick mask of white plaster dust and black soot.

Garside did not pause.

He grabbed the five-foot crowbar, shoved Sadie behind the bulk of Boiler Three with his forearm, and lunged toward the foundation wall.

Behind the open hatch, the duplicate corridor was gone.

There was only a roaring vortex of black shale and churning dust as the 1912 drift seam collapsed into itself.

With a final, thunderous roar, thirty tons of wet sandstone and shale surged forward, filling the rectangular hatch opening with a solid wall of packed earth, crushed brick, and coal slack.

The iron frame of the hatch buckled inward by six inches, crushed flat against the Victorian masonry like a tin can under a steamroller.

The roar died away into a dull, subterranean rumble that faded into the deep bedrock.

Silence fell over the boiler house, broken only by the steady, rhythmic hiss of steam from Boiler Three's burner valve.

Garside leaned his back against the warm steel jacket of the boiler, his chest heaving, his face gray with dust. He looked down at Sadie, who sat on the floor with her knees pulled tight against her ribs, her breath coming in shallow, shuddering gasps.

Garside reached into his boiler suit pocket, brought out a clean red cotton handkerchief, and dropped it into her lap.

In the living boiler room, the air was white with scalding steam from Boiler Three's relief valve.

Mr. Garside stood over Sadie, his thick hands planted on his knees as he fought for breath. His blue boiler suit was soaked with sweat and black coal slurry, his flat cap knocked askew over his left ear. On the floor beside his work boots, the five-foot hexagonal crowbar lay smoking against the wet pavers, its chisel tip bent into a permanent hook by forty tons of subterranean pressure.

He looked down at the rectangular opening behind Boiler Three.

The fresh wall of wet sandstone and colliery shale was solid, completely sealing the aperture from floor to ceiling. A single trickle of black mine water seeped through a mortar joint, trickling into the sump drain with a rhythmic, quiet drip.

Mr. Garside leaned against the warm casing of Boiler Three, wiping his forehead with the greasy sleeve of his boiler suit.

On the floor between his boots, the heavy Sheffield crowbar lay coated with gray dust, its forged chisel tip scarred by its encounter with the iron plate. He took a battered silver tin from his pocket, rolled a thin cigarette with shaking fingers, and struck a brass match on the boiler frame.

"Twenty-eight years since Jenny came out of there," Garside said, blowing a thin stream of gray smoke into the steam-filled air. "I spent twenty-eight years waiting for someone else to find that latch. I should have bricked it up in ninety-six, but the council surveyors told me the ventilation drift had to stay vented for air balance. Fools. Academic fools who never worked a coal seam in their lives."

He looked at Sadie, his eyes softening behind his thick gray brows.

Mr. Garside picked up his red cotton handkerchief from the floor of the boiler pit and wiped a streak of black coal grease from his jaw.

"The drift seam has collapsed all the way to the railway embankment," Garside said, pointing with his wrench toward the sealed brickwork behind Boiler Three. "Forty tons of shale and wet sandstone. It's solid now, Sadie. The earth has taken its own back."

He walked to the primary gas valve on the main supply line and checked the brass pressure gauge with his thumb.

The needle stood steady at two point four bar, vibrating gently with the steady pulse of the burner. The high-pitched scream in the pipes had vanished, replaced by the normal, comforting thrum of the hot water circulating pumps pushing warmth up into the living classrooms above.

Sadie sat on the wooden crate, sipping the hot, sugary tea from Garside's enamel mug, feeling the warmth spread slowly down into her frozen toes.

Mr. Garside knelt beside Sadie on the oil-stained pavers of the living boiler room, holding a damp cotton rag against the cut on her thumb.

"Easy, lass," Garside muttered, his voice rough and steady in the humid basement air. "You're back on the living side now. Breathe the steam in. Clears the coal dust out of your throat."

Sadie leaned back against the warm brick chimney of Boiler Three, drawing the thick, humid boiler house air deep into her lungs. The heat from the return pipes washed over her shivering shoulders like a heavy woolen blanket, driving the freezing numbness from her bones.

In the living boiler room, the steam was clearing slowly, venting through the high louvers into the wet autumn air outside.

Mr. Garside dropped the heavy hexagonal crowbar onto the pavers with a dull clang that echoed off the cast-iron boilers. His chest heaved beneath his blue boiler suit, his forehead dripping with sweat, his face gray with pulverized lagging dust.

He looked down at the brick foundation wall behind Boiler Three.

The rectangular opening was gone, filled with a solid mass of crushed sandstone, shale, and black mine water that had set like stone under forty tons of subterranean pressure. The iron frame of the hatch was buckled inward, crushed flat against the Victorian masonry.

"It's over, Sadie," Garside said, his voice hoarse with exhaustion. "The seam has closed. The drift is sealed forever."

He reached out a heavy, calloused hand and helped Sadie up from the greasy floor.

Sadie stood on her feet, leaning against the warm casing of Boiler Three, feeling the steady, rhythmic pulse of the living burner vibrating through the steel into her palms.

In the living boiler room, the roar of collapsing shale on the other side of the foundation wall died away into a faint, rhythmic settling sound.

Mr. Garside took a heavy steel trowel from his toolbox, scooped up a dollop of gray waterproof mortar from a bucket, and smoothed it across the fractured seam where the iron hatch had been seated.

"That'll hold until the council contractors arrive with the concrete pumps on Monday," Garside said, wiping his brow with the back of his hand. "We'll pump ten tons of grout into the return heading. Fill the whole void solid right up to the sandstone cap."

Sadie stood beside Boiler Three, her fingers resting on the warm metal jacket, listening to the hot water rushing through the four-inch heating mains toward the living classrooms above.

In the living boiler room, Mr. Garside stood with his back to Boiler Three, watching the steam drift slowly out through the high ventilation grating.

He looked at the fresh mortar on the foundation wall, then down at Sadie, who sat on the wooden crate with her hands wrapped around the warm enamel mug of tea.

"You've got a bit of your dad in you, Sadie," Garside said, a faint smile touching his leathery lips. "George Bell was a stubborn fitter. Never let a broken pipe beat him. He would have been proud of the way you held that latch."

Mr. Garside picked up his heavy brass master key ring from the workbench and clipped it to his leather belt with the iron carabiner.

"We'll have the district surveyors here at first light on Monday," Garside said, pouring the last drops of hot tea from his thermos into his mug. "They'll drill three test cores through the boiler raft and pump forty tons of grout into the old return airway. When they're done, this basement will be as solid as the rock under Doncaster castle."

"Wipe your face, lass," Garside said under his breath. "The bell's going in five minutes."

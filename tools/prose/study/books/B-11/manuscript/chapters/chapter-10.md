# Chapter 10: The Caretaker's Warning

The corridor leading down to the boiler room was lit by a single bare sixty-watt bulb encased in a heavy wire cage.

Sadie took the concrete steps one by one, keeping her weight on the outside edges of her rubber soles to prevent the stairs from echoing. The smell of the basement was always the same: sulfur, damp lagging, and the oily sawdust compound that the cleaning staff used to sweep the corridors after four o'clock.

She reached the bottom landing, turned the corner past the gas meter manifold, and stopped dead.

A yellow beam of light slashed across the pipe passage, cutting through the steam gloom like a blade.

Mr. Garside stood by the threshold of Boiler Three.

He was a thickset man of sixty-one with heavy, rounded shoulders, a limp in his left leg that dragged his work boot along the floor with a rhythmic scuff, and skin the color of old sailcloth. He wore blue cotton bib-overalls over a gray flannel work shirt, his sleeves rolled to his forearms to reveal thick, corded tendons and a faded blue tattoo of an anchor on his right wrist.

In his left hand he held an industrial aluminum torch; in his right hand, a two-foot steel Stillson wrench rested across his thigh like a club.

From the heavy leather tool belt around his waist hung a brass ring holding sixty master keys, which clinked with every shift of his boots.

He did not shine the torch into Sadie’s eyes. He dropped the beam to her knees, illuminating the bottle-green hem of her skirt and her synthetic school shoes.

"You're Bell's girl," Garside said. His voice was gravelly and low, scraped raw by forty years of boiler soot and cheap rolling tobacco.

Sadie stood frozen against the cinder-block wall, her hands tucked deep into her blazer pockets.

"Yes, Mr. Garside."

"What are you doing down here again, lass? Henderson caught you two weeks back. He told you the basement is out of bounds."

"I was looking for my PE bag," Sadie lied. The excuse was flimsy, thin as paper, but her voice stayed level. "I thought I left it by the gym stairs."

Garside snorted. It was a wet, dismissive sound that rattled in his chest. He turned his heavy body slowly, his wrench clinking against the gas pipe flange as he leaned against the boiler casing.

"Gym stairs are forty yards behind you, through two fire doors," he said. He shone the torch beam into the narrow gap behind Boiler Three, sweeping the yellow circle across the yellow fiberglass lagging, the cold water return, and the soot-stained brick foundation. "You weren't looking for no PE bag. You've been squeezing behind the risers."

Sadie's breath caught in her throat. She looked at the iron hatch behind the header tank.

In the yellow beam of the caretaker’s torch, the hatch looked ordinary—just a square plate of dark, pitted iron set into the Victorian masonry, its drop-latch resting in the groove.

Garside stepped closer, his left boot dragging on the concrete. The smell of him filled the narrow corridor: WD-40, coal dust, wet wool, and the bitter tang of peppermint humbugs.

He stopped three feet from her. His pale, rheumy blue eyes peered down at her from under bushy gray eyebrows.

"Listen to me, girl," Garside said, his voice dropping an octave into a low, rumbling warning that vibrated through the iron pipes behind his head. "You think you're clever, hiding down here where the monitors cannot find you. You think this school is just classrooms and corridors and bad rules from Henderson."

Sadie didn't answer. She kept her chin down.

"This building was put up in 1972," Garside continued, his thumb tracing the worn steel jaw of his wrench. "I was twenty-two when they poured the footings. They built the whole humanities block right over the top of the Old Victoria pithead. They said the shaft was capped with four yards of mass concrete, but the ground under this valley has never settled. It shifts when the water rises in the winter. It breathes."

He raised the torch, pointing the beam directly at the iron hatch behind the pipes.

"There are spaces under this floor that do not belong to the local education authority, lass. Things that were built into the foundation to let the pressure off when the coal gas backed up. Some of them doors do not lead where you think they lead."

He lowered the torch and looked straight into her face.

"My daughter Jenny went down into the crawlways back in ninety-six," Garside said, his voice tight and quiet. "She was Year Nine. Thought she was hiding from some girls who were giving her grief over her teeth. She came out three days later through the boiler sump, shivering with a fever that took two months to break. She never went back to school. She could not look at a clock without screaming."

He reached out with his heavy, calloused hand and gripped the shoulder of Sadie's blazer. His fingers were hard as iron pipe clamps.

"You stay out of the pipe runs, Bell," he said, turning her toward the concrete stairs. "If I catch you behind Boiler Three again, I will not send you to Henderson. I will put a three-inch Yale padlock through that hatch and weld the hasp to the frame. Now get up them stairs before the third bell."

He released her shoulder.

Sadie didn't run. She walked up the concrete steps with steady, measured strides, her hand on the cold iron rail, listening to the heavy clinking of Garside's brass master keys in the cellar below.

The boiler room seemed to expand in the gloom behind Garside's heavy shoulders.

Beyond Boiler Three, where the brick foundations met the bedrock of the valley, a low arched passage led into the old coal cellars. The county council had abandoned the coal bunkers in 1984, leaving forty tons of slack and shale buried beneath thirty years of maintenance debris: broken cast-iron radiator sections, rolls of rusted chain-link fencing, and stacks of wooden school desks with cast-iron legs.

Garside pointed his heavy torch beam into the mouth of the coal passage.

"They talk about health and safety on their training courses," Garside said, spitting a grain of tobacco onto the concrete. "Henderson has got thirty folders of risk assessments in his office. He thinks a clipboard stops a building from falling down."

He laughed—a low, dry wheeze that shook his bib-overalls.

"This valley has six coal seams running through the gritstone, lass. Victoria, Silkstone, Barnsley Bed. Men worked four hundred yards beneath these floor slabs with tallow candles and iron picks when Queen Victoria was a girl. When the pit flooded in twenty-four, the water rose through the old workings, pushing the mine gas up into the gravel beds."

He stepped closer to Sadie, the master keys clinking on his belt.

"The engineers who designed this school knew the ground was unstable," Garside continued, his voice dropping into a harsh whisper. "They built the foundation with pressure vents every sixty feet. But that hatch behind Boiler Three isn't a gas vent. It's an opening into the fold."

Sadie looked at the dark seam behind the pipes.

"The fold?" she asked.

"The place where the days get stuck," Garside said. "You think yesterday disappears when the midnight bell rings, lass? It doesn't go anywhere. It gets squeezed down into the stone, like water pressed out of a wet sponge. That hatch opens right into yesterday's squeeze."

He pointed the Stillson wrench toward the basement stairs.

Garside turned his torch toward the east foundation footing where the 1972 concrete joined the Victorian brickwork. A two-inch vertical crack ran down the wall, packed with dry oakum and black bituminous mastic.

"Look at that expansion seam," Garside said, shining the beam into the crevice. "The Coal Board surveyor told the governors back in seventy-one that the No. 4 return drift was dry. Liars, the lot of them. That drift was driven through forty feet of sandstone over the Barnsley bed. When they flooded the lower workings in eighty-five, the water had nowhere to go. It sits under our feet right now, sixty thousand gallons of black mine slurry, waiting for an air pocket."

Sadie looked at the crack. Deep inside the gap, where the beam of the rubber torch failed to penetrate, something clicked. It was a faint, metallic tick, like the sound of an iron needle dropping onto an anvil.

"That's the seam shifting," Garside muttered, tapping the masonry with his wrench. "Every time the temperature drops five degrees on the moors, the brickwork breathes half an inch."

Mr. Garside shone the beam of his heavy rubber torch into the dark recess behind the hot water calorifiers.

"Look at those pipe supports," Garside said, pointing with his twelve-inch wrench. "Six-inch cast iron, bolted into the foundation slab in seventy-two. Every spring when the thaw comes off the moors, the bolts groan like anchor chains. The Coal Board surveyors knew the ground was unstable, but the county council was in a rush to build. They threw this wing up in eighteen months to hit an election deadline."

Sadie looked at the massive iron brackets. The concrete around the anchor studs was chipped and cracked, fine white dust sifting down onto the basement floor.

"My Jenny found that door when they were pouring the footings for the sports hall," Garside continued, his voice dropping into a rasping rumble. "She was twelve years old. Quiet lass, just like you. She'd sit by the boiler inspection glass eating her dinner because the girls in her form were giving her grief about her second-hand blazers."

He looked at Sadie, his weathered face tight with an old, unhealed grief.

"She thought that door was a refuge, Sadie. But the past isn't a shelter. It's a sponge that absorbs everything you bleed into it, and when it's full, it squeezes it right back out into the living world."

Mr. Garside picked up a heavy cast-iron flange from the workbench, weighed it in his grease-stained palm, and set it down with a sharp metallic clink.

"The Coal Board engineers had an acoustic listening station in the pit bottom back in seventy-four," Garside said, his voice dropping into a low rasp. "They were monitoring seismic drift from the Thorne workings. Every Wednesday at midnight, the seismograph in the engine shed would trace three identical spikes on the paper drum. Exactly twenty-four hours apart, to the second."

He pointed the jaws of his pipe wrench toward the brick chimney of Boiler Three.

"They thought it was water hammer in the sump lines. But water hammer doesn't keep time like an electric clock, Sadie. The earth down here is trapped in an echo. When you walk through that hatch, you're stepping right into the middle of the groove."

Mr. Garside took a roll of blue insulating tape from his pocket, tore off a three-inch strip with his teeth, and wrapped it around the handle of his pipe wrench.

"When they flooded the Thorne shaft in eighty-nine, the water rose forty feet in twenty-four hours," Garside said, smoothing the tape with his thumb. "The old pitmen said you could hear the timber props snapping five miles away across the moors, like dry twigs breaking under a boot. The ground doesn't forget being hollowed out, Sadie. It's always looking for a way to settle back down into the coal."

He tapped the steel toe of his work boot against the cellar floor, listening to the dull, leaden thud of the concrete slab.

Garside turned his flashlight beam toward the corner of the boiler house where thirty bags of dry rock salt were stacked on wooden pallets.

"The council brings the salt in October for the school paths," Garside said, kicking the lowest sack with his heavy boot. "Twenty-five kilo bags from the salt mines in Cheshire. If we get a hard frost before November, I'll be spreading this across the bus turnaround at six in the morning."

Sadie nodded, watching the yellow dust drift through the flashlight beam.

"Now get out of my cellar, Bell. Before I lock you down here with the coal."

# Chapter 4: The Silent Hallway

The iron hatch did not click shut behind her, but the moment Sadie’s boots cleared the threshold, the temperature dropped five degrees.

She stood up slowly, ducking her head until she was certain the ceiling cleared her scalp. The corridor was seven feet high, lined with standard acoustic ceiling tiles held in a white aluminum grid. Three of the tiles near the center were water-stained, marked with yellow, kidney-shaped rings where an upstairs radiator had leaked two winters ago.

She turned in a full circle.

Behind her, the opening in the wall was not an iron door. It was an ordinary fire exit door framed in painted pine, propped open three inches by a red rubber wedge. Through the gap, she could see the glowing blue eye of Boiler Three's pilot flame, flickering like a small gas jet in the dark cellar thirty feet away.

She turned back to the corridor. The light had no source she could identify. The fluorescent strip lights overhead were unlit, their glass tubes gray and dead inside their metal housings, yet the entire hallway was filled with that pale, sourceless amber gloom. It cast no sharp shadows; when she raised her hand, her fingers left only a soft, blurred gray smudge against the teal paint of the dado rail.

She took four steps forward.

Her school shoes were leather-look synthetic lace-ups with thick rubber soles. In the real school, they squeaked loudly on the polished linoleum, announcing her arrival at every corner.

Here, they made no sound at all.

She lifted her foot and brought the heel down hard against the tile. Nothing. No thud, no click, no vibration through the floorboards. The impact was deadened instantly, swallowed by the cold air like a stone dropped into a barrel of flour.

She stopped outside Room 12.

The door was pine with a square panel of wire-reinforced Georgian glass. Through the diamond mesh, she could see the history classroom: five rows of two-person desks with blue plastic chairs, the pull-down map of Europe during the Napoleonic Wars hanging slightly crooked from its brass hook, and Mr. Finch's battered oak desk by the radiator.

On the front desk, an open ring binder lay beside an empty plastic pencil case.

Sadie moved past Room 12 toward the central foyer where the corridor joined the main stairwell. The noticeboards along the right wall were covered in green felt, pinned with blue and yellow notices. She leaned close to read them.

*YEAR 8 NETBALL TRIALS — THURSDAY 17 OCTOBER.*
*ALL PUPILS MUST WEAR CLEAN WHITE TRAINERS.*

She looked at the corner of the sheet. The drawing pin had a cracked yellow plastic head; Sadie remembered seeing it on Monday morning when she had stood by the board trying to hide from Courtney Vance.

She walked out into the foyer.

Above the library doors, the electric school clock hung inside its square steel cage. The black minute hand pointed straight at the nine; the hour hand sat midway between the twelve and the one.

Twelve-forty-five.

That was lunch hour. The middle of lunch hour.

She walked up to the double doors of Room 14, the English room where Miss Finch taught Year 8. The door was propped open with a wooden block. Sadie stood on the threshold, her fingers resting lightly against the pine doorframe.

Inside, the classroom was not empty.

Sitting in the third row, in the seat next to the radiator where the pipe always leaked, was a girl wearing a bottle-green uniform jumper and a white collared shirt. Her brown hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail with a fraying black scrunchie. Her shoulders were hunched forward over a lined exercise book; her left hand was tucked under her thigh while her right hand held a blue Bic biro between two ink-stained fingers.

The girl was not moving. Her chest did not rise or fall; her hair did not stir.

Sadie walked three paces down the aisle, her throat tightening until she could barely draw breath through her nose.

She looked down at the girl’s face.

The girl was twelve years old, with pale skin, a small scar through her left eyebrow from a fall off a tricycle in infant school, and a dark, mottled bruise on her left kneecap visible through her wool tights.

Sadie was looking at herself.

The classroom door of Room 13, where Mr. Finch taught history, stood slightly ajar.

Sadie pushed the wood with one finger. The hinges moved with absolute silence, turning on greased steel pins without the faintest squeak. She stepped inside, her rubber soles soundless on the green linoleum.

Thirty-two desks were arranged in neat rows facing the blackboard.

At the front of the room, on the third desk in the center row, lay an open exercise book. The handwriting was neat, written in black fountain pen: *The Causes of the Industrial Revolution in the West Riding of Yorkshire.* At the bottom of the page, a drop of dried blue ink formed a raised bump on the paper.

Sadie touched the ink drop. It was completely hard, dry and cold as glass.

She walked to the window.

The glass in the steel-framed casement was cold against her palms. Outside, the view of the school playing fields was sharp and clear, bathed in the desaturated gray light of Monday midday. Two hundred yards away, by the perimeter fence where the birch trees bordered the railway line, a magpie was caught in mid-flight between two branches.

Its black-and-white wings were spread wide, tilted at an angle of twenty degrees against the breeze.

The bird was not falling. It hung suspended in the gray air like an insect trapped in amber, five feet above the chain-link fence, frozen between one wing-beat and the next.

Sadie watched it for sixty seconds.

The feathers did not ruffle in the wind. The leaves on the birch branch below remained rigid, their pointed tips angled toward the canal bridge.

Sadie stepped out of Room 11 and walked ten yards down the silent hallway to the biology store room.

The frosted glass door was half unlatched. Inside, four wooden cabinets stood against the rear brickwork, their shelves packed with glass jars containing sheep lungs, pickled dogfish, and wax models of human cell structures. Everything was frozen in perfect, amber clarity. On the teacher's prep bench, a half-empty mug of instant coffee sat beside an open register, a thin white skin across the brown liquid, completely undisturbed by mold or evaporation. Beside the mug, an electric kettle was plugged into the wall, its rocker switch depressed in the 'on' position, yet the red indicator lamp did not glow.

She touched the plastic casing of the kettle. It was cold as marble. In this duplicate wing, energy didn't flow through wires; things simply occupied space without combustion, without current, without the friction that wore out living gears.

Sadie walked past the geography department book store on the ground floor of the duplicate school.

Through the glass panel, fifty copies of the *Modern School Atlas* sat stacked on a wooden shelf, their blue buckram spines identical to the one ruined in her satchel. She reached through the door, lifted the top volume from the pile, and weighed it in her palm. The paper inside was dense and cold, smelling of fresh lithographic ink from thirty years ago.

When she flipped the pages, the colored relief map of South Yorkshire showed the railway lines and collieries of 1975: Thorne, Hatfield, Brodsworth, and Ashfield Main, their pithead icons printed in sharp black squares across the green contours of the Don valley.

She turned away from the window, a cold shiver running across her ribs, and walked toward Room 14.

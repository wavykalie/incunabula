# Chapter 38: The Shared Umbrella

The roar of the rain on the black nylon sounded like dried peas rattling in a tin basin.

Underneath the umbrella, the air was suddenly still.

Sadie looked at Chloe.

Chloe's face was pale, her lips tinged with faint blue from the cold. The mascara around her eyes was completely washed away, leaving her lashes pale and spidery. Without the sharp, painted armor she wore in the corridors, Chloe looked twelve years old, fragile and exhausted.

"Your thumb's bleeding," Chloe said.

Her voice was thin, rough from cold, but it was entirely human. There was no mechanical echo, no synthetic hiss, no sandpaper scrape from behind her teeth.

"It's just a scratch," Sadie said.

Chloe shifted the umbrella handle to her left hand. She reached into the pocket of her wet jumper, brought out a small, foil-wrapped chocolate bar, and snapped it in half with her thumbs.

She held out the larger half toward Sadie.

"My mum packs two every Friday," Chloe said, looking down at the chocolate. "In case I get hungry on the bus."

Sadie looked at the offered piece of chocolate. The milk chocolate was pale at the edges, slightly squashed from Chloe's pocket.

Sadie took it with her uninjured left hand.

"Thanks," Sadie murmured.

She put the chocolate into her mouth. It tasted sweet, rich, and real, melting against her cold tongue with a warmth that spread down into her chest.

They stood side by side under the black nylon canopy for three minutes without speaking.

Across the field, Mr. Henderson was calling names from Form 8H: "Barker? Present. Bell?"

"Present!" Sadie called out.

Henderson looked up from his clipboard, pushed his wet spectacles up his nose, and ticked the box with a red biro. He glanced at Chloe standing beside Sadie under the umbrella, paused for half a beat, and continued down his list: "Bennett? Present."

Chloe looked over at the crowd of Year 8 girls near the pavilion.

Jessica Mills was laughing with two older boys, her pink umbrella tilted back to show her face.

"They're not my friends," Chloe said, her eyes fixed on the grass. "They only hung around because my brother had a car."

Sadie looked at her. "I know."

"I'm sorry about your bag," Chloe whispered. "And the milk."

The words were spoken without tears or drama. They were flat, simple, and heavy, like small river stones dropped into deep water.

Sadie felt a tight knot in her throat loosen, melting away like sugar in hot tea.

"It washed out," Sadie said.

Chloe nodded once, keeping her eyes on the rain falling beyond the edge of the umbrella.

Under the nylon canopy of the umbrella, Sadie chewed the piece of chocolate, feeling the warmth spread through her stiff jaw muscles.

Chloe glanced down at Sadie's right hand, where Garside's red handkerchief was wrapped tightly around the cut thumb. "You should get Mrs. Briggs to put some antiseptic on that," Chloe murmured, her voice barely louder than the patter of rain on the fabric above. "The nurse has got cream that stops it throbbing."

"I'll get some at break," Sadie said.

They stood together in silence, the rain drumming against the black nylon, watching the teachers pace up and down the muddy lines of children.

Under the shelter of the umbrella, Sadie pulled her sleeves over her cold knuckles, watching the steam rise from Chloe's wet jumper.

"My dad lives in Northampton now," Sadie said, the words emerging almost without her intending them. "He works in a distribution depot near the M1. He sends a twenty-pound postal order every month in a plain brown envelope with no letter inside. My mum puts it in a tin on top of the fridge for the electricity meter."

Chloe looked down at the mud between their shoes.

"My dad's in Leeds," Chloe murmured. "He went with a woman who manages a tanning salon on the ring road. He took his toolkit and the good television. He left five pounds on the bread bin."

They stood side by side in the cold, the rain beating on the nylon canopy above them, sharing the quiet weight of things that couldn't be broken or undone.

Under the black golf umbrella, Sadie broke the remaining piece of chocolate in two and handed the smaller half back to Chloe.

Chloe took it with a faint nod of thanks, slipping the sweet chocolate between her lips. The rain drummed against the black canopy above them with a rhythmic, steady patter that seemed to wash away the noise of the crowded field.

Ten yards away, Jessica Mills and Courtney Vance were huddled together under Courtney's pink umbrella, shivering in the damp wind, glancing over at Chloe with uncertain, uneasy expressions.

"They won't speak to me on Monday," Chloe murmured, chewing the chocolate slowly.

"They won't speak to me either," Sadie said.

Chloe looked at Sadie, and for the first time in two years, a small, genuine smile touched the corners of her eyes. "Good," Chloe said. "We don't need them."

Under the canopy of the black umbrella, the silence between the two girls felt clean and still, like the quiet of the library before the morning bell.

Across the field, Mr. Henderson blew his plastic whistle to signal the end of the roll call. The six hundred children began to move in a disorganized, chattering herd toward the asphalt path, their laughter and complaints echoing off the brick walls in the damp autumn air.

Chloe looked at Sadie, her hand resting on the wooden umbrella handle. "Do you want to walk down the path together?"

Sadie adjusted the strap of her canvas satchel on her shoulder. "Yeah," Sadie said. "Let's walk down."

Neither of them spoke again until the all-clear whistle blew from the pavilion.

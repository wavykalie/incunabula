# Chapter 16: The Panic in the Tunnel

The wooden door of Science Lab 3 resisted like lead.

Sadie threw her shoulder against the panel. The brass latch screeched inside its strike plate with the sound of grinding stones, then gave way. She burst into the first-floor corridor, stumbling across the threshold onto the yellow linoleum.

She did not look back into the laboratory.

She ran.

Running in the yesterday school was like running through a dream underwater. Her trainers struck the floor with furious haste, her thighs burning as she sprinted along the green walls, but the dead acoustics swallowed every sound. There was no thunder of running feet, no rustle of her blazer against her ribs, only the sound of her own ragged breath tearing through her trachea.

Along the corridor, forty classroom doors stood shut behind wired glass.

Through the glass panes, Sadie caught blurred glimpses of frozen rooms: Year 7 geography students with colored pencils poised over rainfall maps; Mrs. Appleby holding a red correction pen above an open exercise book; Mr. Henderson standing by his office filing cabinet with an attendance printout in his hand.

At the top of the west stairwell, she reached the fire doors.

She hit the horizontal crash bar with both forearms. The bar clanged down, releasing the latch with a dull rattle that vibrated through the metal frame.

She plunged down the concrete stairs three steps at a time.

Her hand skimmed along the square steel handrail. The enamel paint was peeling in jagged flakes, cold as frostbite against her palm. At the half-landing, where the tall wire-glass window overlooked the bicycle sheds, she glanced outside.

The playground below was still locked in Tuesday's gray gloom.

Two Year 9 boys stood frozen in mid-kick behind the sheds, a football suspended eighteen inches above the wet asphalt like a black-and-white stone suspended on an invisible wire.

Sadie reached the ground floor and threw herself through the lower fire doors into the service corridor.

The air here was freezing. A pale rime of frost had formed along the cast-iron return main running beneath the ceiling, crystallizing the pipe dust into tiny white needles that caught the amber light.

She reached the iron service hatch behind Boiler Three.

The quarter-inch boiler plate door was closed.

Sadie grabbed the forged iron counterweight bar with both hands.

"Move," she grunted, planting her trainers against the brickwork.

The metal burned her palms with intense, dry cold. The iron bar refused to budge. The drop-latch was wedged deep into its cast keeper, glued in place by a thin crust of white frost that had crept along the brass spindle.

Through the cinderblock wall behind her, she heard footsteps.

They were not human footsteps. They were slow, heavy, and irregular, dragging across the concrete tiles of the upper corridor with a wet, scraping sound like a sack of wet salt being pulled across flagstones.

*Scritch. Thud. Scritch. Thud.*

Sadie whimpered. She drew her right foot back and kicked the counterweight bar with the steel eyelets of her trainer.

The impact sent a shock of pain through her ankle, but the white frost on the spindle fractured with a sharp *tink*.

She grabbed the bar again, leaned back with all forty kilograms of her weight, and heaved.

The iron latch cleared the keeper.

The hatch swung inward with a groan of iron hinges. A blast of superheated air, smelling of coal dust, burner gas, and engine oil, rushed over Sadie's face from the living boiler room.

She dove through the rectangular opening on her stomach.

Her knees scraped across the rough engineering pavers of the cellar floor. She spun around on her hips, caught the cold iron rim of the hatch, and slammed the door shut into the masonry frame.

The drop-latch fell into the keeper with a deafening *CLANG* that rattled the sheet-metal ductwork of Boiler Three.

Sadie collapsed against the warm brick chimney, her forehead pressed against the soot-dusted mortar, sobbing in heavy, shuddering gasps while the blue burner flame flickered steadily behind its mica window.

Above her head, the concrete ceiling shuddered as five hundred living students poured out of period two into morning break.

The normal, messy, imperfect roar of school life washed down through the ventilation louvers: shouting voices, slammed locker doors, the blare of a radio in the kitchen block, the scrape of chairs across floorboards. It was loud, ugly, and abrasive, but to Sadie's ears, it was the sound of oxygen returning to a suffocating lung.

At the half-landing of the west stairs, Sadie stopped to catch her breath, her hand clutching the square steel handrail.

Through the wire-glass window, the playing field below was locked in Tuesday's gray drizzle. The two Year 9 boys frozen behind the bike sheds had not moved an inch. Their black-and-white football hung suspended eighteen inches above the asphalt, its synthetic seams clean and motionless against the gray sky.

Across the valley, the high cooling towers of the defunct power station rose like gray tree stumps against the clouds, venting plumes of frozen steam that stood rigid as columns of carved soap.

At the foot of the west service stairs, Sadie paused by the emergency fire-hose reel mounted inside its circular recessed cupboard.

The heavy red rubber hose was coiled around its central iron drum, its brass nozzle resting in a spring-loaded clip. Across the glass door of the cabinet, someone had scrawled a crude caricature in red wax crayon during the previous term, half-rubbed out by cleaning rags but still legible beneath the amber light.

Behind the concrete wall of the stairwell, the sound of the footsteps grew louder.

It was a slow, heavy drag, the sound of hard-soled leather shoes scraping across concrete tiles with mechanical persistence. With every third stride came a dull, metallic thud, like an iron bar being dropped onto flagstones from a height of two inches.

Sadie turned and sprinted toward the boiler room hatch, her canvas rucksack bouncing against her spine.

Sadie fled past the language laboratory at the end of the east corridor.

Through the double glass doors, thirty wooden listening carrels stood in neat rows, each equipped with a reel-to-reel tape deck and a pair of heavy black bakelite headphones. On the front teacher's console, thirty small red indicator lamps were frozen mid-glow, their filament light casting pale crimson circles across the varnished mahogany.

The air in the stairwell turned sharper, colder, smelling of damp limestone and wet soot.

Every third stride Sadie took, her school satchel banged against her hip with a dull thud that died instantly against the concrete walls. Behind her, the dragging sound in the hallway was accelerating, the scrape of hard leather heels rhythmically closing the distance.

Sadie burst through the fire doors onto the lower boiler room landing, her school shoes skidding across the grit on the concrete.

The air in the basement corridor smelled of damp coal slack, machine oil, and the hot sulfur fumes venting from Boiler Three's flue duct. Through the open iron door of the workshop, she caught a glimpse of Garside's workbench, the yellow bulb hanging from its braided cord casting long, warm shadows across the rows of pipe wrenches and oil tins.

Behind her, on the concrete steps of the west stairs, the dragging sound came to an abrupt halt.

The silence that settled over the basement was heavy and absolute, broken only by the steady, rhythmic clank of the condensate pump pushing water into the heating risers.

Sadie ran down the first-floor corridor of the duplicate school, her school shoes slapping against the yellow linoleum.

Through the glass inspection panes of forty classroom doors, she saw the frozen rooms of Monday morning. In Room 18, Mrs. Appleby stood with her chalk poised above a list of French adverbs. In Room 20, Mr. Henderson was opening a filing cabinet, an attendance sheet frozen in his fingers.

As Sadie passed each door, a minute shudder passed through the glass.

The frozen teachers and students were not moving their limbs, but their heads were subtly, almost imperceptibly tilting toward the corridor. In Room 19, an entire row of Year 7 pupils had rotated their necks by two degrees, their glassy pupils tracking her sprint through the wired glass.

She hit the fire doors at the top of the west stairwell with both forearms, bursting through into the cold air of the concrete shaft.

Behind her, on the first-floor linoleum, the dragging footsteps were accelerating, the sound of hard leather soles scraping across the floor with terrifying, mechanical persistence.

*Scritch. Thud. Scritch. Thud.*

She plunged down the stairs three steps at a time, her fingers skimming along the freezing steel handrail.

Sadie plunged down the final flight of the concrete stairs, her canvas rucksack held tight against her ribs.

At the half-landing, she glanced back through the square steel railings.

At the top of the stairwell, a dark silhouette stood against the wired glass doors of the humanities block. It did not move with human haste; it stood motionless in the amber light, its head tilted downward toward the well, its glassy eyes reflecting the fluorescent tubes with a dull, violet glow.

The sound of the dragging footsteps had ceased.

The silence that settled over the concrete shaft was terrifying, heavy as wet sand pouring down a chute. Sadie threw her weight against the ground-floor fire doors and burst through into the service corridor.

Sadie threw her shoulder against the fire doors at the foot of the west stairs, stumbling out onto the concrete landing of the service corridor.

The air in the basement was freezing, smelling of cold sulfur, damp coal shale, and the bitter rime that had formed along the cast-iron steam mains. Above her head, the emergency lighting boxes were dark, their small red pilot lamps unlit in the duplicate silence.

She looked back up the concrete flight.

Through the wire-glass panels of the upper fire doors, the amber light of the first-floor corridor was flickering with that strange, purplish oscillation, casting long, barred shadows down the steps like iron railings.

Sadie paused on the concrete landing of the west stairs, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

The air in the stairwell was utterly dead, holding the faint, dry scent of forty years of concrete dust and peeling lead primer. High above her head, the square wire-glass skylight was gray with Monday morning's frozen rain, shedding a dim, amber gloom over the concrete steps.

She took a slow, deep breath, tightened her hands around her rucksack straps, and hurried down into the service conduit.

She pulled her knees to her chest and sat in the hot dark behind the return pipes until the trembling stopped.

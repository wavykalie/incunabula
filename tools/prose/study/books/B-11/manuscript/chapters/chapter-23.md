# Chapter 23: The Accidental Push

The spanner slipped from Sadie's fingers.

It hit the concrete floor of the landing with a sharp, ringing clang that shattered the dead silence of the stairwell like a dropped mirror.

Sadie gasped, lunging forward to grab the metal tool before it rolled over the edge.

Her right shoulder collided heavily with the red cylinder mounted on the wall bracket behind the fire doors.

It was an old ten-liter water-gas fire extinguisher, installed during the 1972 construction of the science wing. The cylindrical steel vessel weighed twenty-five kilograms, filled with water and a pressurized carbon dioxide cartridge. Forty years of damp air in the unheated stairwell had rusted the wall mount into a brittle crust of brown iron oxide.

When Sadie's weight slammed into the cylinder, the upper retaining hook sheared cleanly through its rusted rivet.

The heavy steel extinguisher tore away from the cinderblock wall.

"No!" Sadie lunged, her hands clawing for the rubber hose.

The wet rubber slipped through her palms.

The twenty-five-kilogram extinguisher plunged down the first flight of concrete steps.

The impact was deafening. The steel base of the cylinder struck the third tread with an explosive crash that gouged a two-inch chunk out of the concrete nose. It bounced, cartwheeled through the air, and slammed into the brick wall of the half-landing with the force of an artillery shell, pulverizing the plaster into a white cloud of dust.

On the bottom tread, Chloe's frozen figure did not move.

The heavy cylinder missed Chloe's left shoulder by four inches, tumbling down the second flight of stairs with a succession of violent, bone-rattling impacts: *BANG. CRASH. BOOM.*

At the foot of the west stairs, the extinguisher struck the steel fire doors, denting the lower panel inward by three inches before coming to rest in the corner beside the radiator.

The brass valve head had cracked on impact.

A violent, high-pressure jet of stagnant water and carbon dioxide hissed from the broken nozzle, filling the ground-floor corridor with a foul, rotting mist that smelled of fifty years of stagnant pipe scale and iron rust.

Sadie stood on the top landing, her hands clamped over her mouth, her eyes wide with terror.

The noise of the collapse had echoed through every pipe, rafter, and ventilation duct in the duplicate building.

Down on the half-landing, Chloe remained frozen in her seated posture, her face pressed against her knees. But around her, the air was shimmering with intense, purple distortion waves.

From the first-floor corridor behind Sadie, the heavy double doors of the humanities block rattled violently in their frames.

Footsteps began to run.

Not dragging steps now. Fast, heavy, pounding footsteps, twenty pairs of hard soles sprinting along the yellow linoleum toward the stairwell with frantic, mechanical urgency.

The iron extinguisher rolled against the base of the radiator on the lower landing, its red paint scarred with long, silver gouges from the concrete steps.

A cloud of white powder and pulverized plaster hung in the stairwell air, drifting down through the wire-glass windows like frozen snow. On the wall above the third tread, the fractured mounting bracket projected from the cinderblock like a broken tooth, its jagged iron edges bright silver in the amber gloom.

Sadie looked down at her hands. Her palms were smeared with orange rust and black grease from the bracket collar.

From the first-floor landing, the sound of running footsteps grew louder, pounding along the corridor toward the west doors with terrifying speed.

The ten-liter fire extinguisher lay motionless on the basement landing, half-submerged in a drift of white sodium powder and iron flakes.

The copper discharge pipe on the valve head had sheared cleanly through its soldered joint, venting a thin, white curl of carbon dioxide that froze onto the concrete tiles like hoarfrost. On the cinderblock wall above, the two lead expansion sleeves that had held the mounting bracket were pulled three inches out of their drill holes, their metal deformed by sudden, violent torque.

Sadie took a ragged breath, wiped the cold sweat from her forehead with her sleeve, and retreated toward the boiler room hatch.

The ten-liter extinguisher bounced off the fifth concrete step with a sickening impact that gouged a white scar across the masonry.

A cloud of fine calcium carbonate and iron oxide erupted into the air, filling the stairwell with a choking fog that tasted of dry lime. The brass discharge horn snapped off against the steel baluster, cartwheeling into the lightwell before striking the floor tiles fifteen feet below with a sharp ringing crack.

On the cinderblock wall of the upper landing, the two lead wall sleeves remained embedded in the stone, their internal threads stripped bare by the sudden dynamic failure.

Sadie stood with her back pressed against the fire doors, her hands trembling so violently she could barely grip her canvas satchel, listening to the high-pressure hiss of escaping gas from the fractured cylinder.

Sadie knelt on the landing and examined the sheared collar of the wall bracket.

The gray cast iron had fractured across the central mounting lug, displaying a crystalline, sugary grain that looked like broken cast aluminum. There were no traces of gradual fatigue or rust inside the fracture plane; the metal had sheared under an instantaneous, catastrophic dynamic shock that had exceeded the tensile capacity of the iron.

She picked up a small flake of red paint from the concrete. The paint was dry, brittle as an eggshell, smelling faintly of sulfur and burned coal dust.

She dropped the flake back into the dust and hurried toward the service stairs.

Sadie turned and fled down the opposite stairs toward the boiler room.

# Chapter 17: The Acid Burn Tomorrow

On Thursday afternoon at 14:10 PM, Science Laboratory 3 in the living school was suffocatingly warm.

The afternoon sun had broken through the autumn clouds, baking the flat asphalt roof of the science block until the tar smell seeped down through the ceiling vents. Twenty-four students in Year 8 sat along the laboratory benches, listening to Mr. Finch explain the reaction between magnesium ribbon and dilute hydrochloric acid.

Chloe Rimmer sat at Bench 2, chewing a piece of spearmint gum.

She wore her school blouse unbuttoned two inches lower than regulations allowed, her bottle-green blazer hung neatly over the back of her stool. Over the back of the neighboring stool, draped within easy reach of her hand, was her cream-colored cable-knit wool cardigan.

Sadie sat at the rear bench beside the chemical fume cupboard, her hands flat on the black laminate.

Her fingers were still stiff from the cold of the yesterday tunnel. A small scab had formed on her left palm where the frozen iron hatch had pinched the skin.

She watched the high reagent shelf above Bench 2.

The two-liter amber glass winchester sat on the third shelf, its base overhanging the wooden edge by three-quarters of an inch. Beneath the strip lights, the ground-glass stopper sat loose in the neck, tilted slightly to the right.

14:14 PM.

"Now," Mr. Finch said, walking down the center aisle with a box of matches. "When we introduce the magnesium into the test tube, we expect an immediate effervescence. What gas is being evolved, class?"

"Hydrogen," Courtney Vance said, twirling a strand of hair.

"Correct. And how do we test for hydrogen?"

Before Courtney could answer, a loud, violent shudder ran through the laboratory floorboards.

Down in the basement thirty feet below, the secondary circulating pump for the heating main kicked on with an industrial hydraulic hammer, sending a shockwave vibrating up through the four-inch steel water risers.

The vibration traveled into the cinderblock wall, through the wooden mounting brackets, and onto the chemical reagent shelf.

The two-liter amber bottle rocked once.

The loose ground-glass stopper popped out of the neck, tumbling over the wooden lip and shattering into three pieces on the bench surface.

A split second later, the heavy winchester tipped forward.

"Look out!" someone screamed from Bench 4.

The bottle fell through five feet of air, striking the metal crossbar of the stool before hitting the floor tiles with a hollow crash. Two liters of two-molar hydrochloric acid exploded outward in a foaming, hissing wave.

Chloe shrieked, leaping backward off her stool.

She was fast enough to avoid the spray on her legs, but the foaming wave caught the wooden backrest of the spare stool full force. The acid soaked instantly into the cream-colored wool cardigan.

Within three seconds, a thick, white chemical smoke rose from the fabric.

The delicate wool fibers hissed, bubbling into a gray, gelatinous slime as the acid chewed through the cable knitting. A sickening reek of burned hair, scorched sulfur, and boiled plastic filled the laboratory air.

"My cardigan!" Chloe wailed, her hands clutching her cheeks. "My cardigan! It's ruined!"

She lunged forward to grab it, but Mr. Finch caught her by the shoulders and shoved her back.

"Don't touch it, Chloe! It's concentrated acid! Stand back, everyone! Get by the door!"

Mr. Finch rushed to the emergency sand bucket by the whiteboard, scooped up two handfuls of yellow sand and sodium bicarbonate, and dumped them onto the smoking mass of wool on the floor. The mixture hissed violently, vomiting a cloud of acrid steam that set half the class coughing into their sleeves.

Chloe dropped onto her knees by the sinks, burying her face in her hands.

She was crying openly, heavy, shuddering sobs that shook her shoulders and smeared her mascara down her cheeks in dark, greasy tracks. Her clique—Jessica, Courtney, the boys from Bench 1—stood five feet back, staring at her with wide, uncomfortable eyes, nobody stepping forward to touch her.

From the back row, Sadie watched Chloe cry.

The victory was complete. Chloe was stripped of her poise, weeping on the dirty linoleum like a five-year-old who had dropped her ice cream in the gutter.

Yet as Sadie watched, a strange, sour taste rose in her throat.

She remembered the sight of Mr. Finch's frozen eyes turning in their sockets. She remembered the sandpaper sound in his throat.

In the medical room after the incident, the smell of scorched wool and vinegar still clung to the air. Mrs. Briggs had spent twenty minutes scrubbing the linoleum with soda crystals, grumbling under her breath about careless teachers and council budgets.

Chloe's mother had arrived at the school gates in a blue Vauxhall Astra, her face pale with fury, her high heels clicking aggressively along the corridor tiles. Henderson had met her outside the Year Office, his travel mug trembling in his hand as he attempted to explain the mysterious failure of the chemical reagent shelf.

Sadie had watched from the bench outside the library, her hands folded neatly in her lap, completely invisible beneath the shadow of the noticeboards.

In the living classroom, after the fire bucket had been dumped and the acid neutralized, the stench of burned wool and sulfur lingered like a physical presence.

Mr. Finch stood by his demonstration bench with a clipboard, his hands shaking as he took the names of students who had been seated within six feet of the spill. His white coat was smeared with brown potassium stains, and his tie had been pushed crooked during the scramble for the sand bucket.

"Nobody leaves this room until the nurse arrives," Finch barked, though his voice lacked its usual commanding authority. "Keep your hands away from your clothes. If anyone feels an itching sensation on their skin, speak up immediately."

In the corner by the sinks, Chloe was still weeping, her head down against the porcelain basin, surrounded by soaked paper towels and white bicarbonate powder.

In the humanities corridor outside Lab 3, Mrs. Briggs was spreading damp sawdust across the vinyl tiles with a wide yard broom. The red sweeping compound smelled of pine oil, paraffin, and wet dirt, masking the acrid stench of the burned wool cardigan.

A small crowd of Year 7 pupils lingered near the display cabinets, staring through the wired glass at the charred brown stain on the laboratory floor.

"Mr. Finch had to use three bags of sand," one boy whispered, his eyes wide behind thick spectacles. "My sister says the bottle blew up like a grenade because someone put a battery in the acid."

Sadie walked past them with her eyes down, her canvas satchel held tight against her ribs, blending into the lunchtime crowd.

In the living science lab, Mrs. Briggs was packing the broken fragments of the amber glass winchester into a cardboard box lined with heavy plastic.

"Two liters of concentrated acid," the cleaner muttered, her rubber gloves squeaking against the glass. "If that girl had been sitting on that stool when the bottle went over, she'd be in Doncaster Royal right now with third-degree burns across her legs."

Mr. Finch stood by the whiteboard, writing an emergency incident report in his hardback laboratory log. His hands were still trembling slightly, the black ink of his fountain pen smudging across the ruled lines as he recorded the time and volume of the chemical spill.

The damage was done, but the cost had begun to tick in the dark.

# Chapter 36: The Real Friday Morning

Above the basement ceiling, the master fire alarm bells of Ashfield Comprehensive erupted into life.

The shrill, electric clatter echoed through every corridor, classroom, and gymnasium in the living school, vibrating through the double-glazed windows and cutting through the lunch hour chatter like a siren.

Six hundred pupils poured out of the dining hall in a chaotic, shouting wave.

Teachers strode down the corridors in their dark coats, blowing whistles and herding children toward the fire exits.

"Single file, Year Eight!" Mr. Henderson's voice barked from the main foyer. "Keep moving! Leave your bags on the tables! Move straight to your designated assembly points on the bottom field!"

Sadie climbed the west service stairs in the crowd of retreating students.

Her green blazer was covered in yellow pipe lagging and white plaster dust; a smear of soot ran across her forehead, and her right thumb was wrapped in Garside's red handkerchief. Nobody looked at her. In the confusion of the evacuation, every child was running, shouting, and jostling for the doors.

She pushed through the external fire exit and stepped out into the cold South Yorkshire drizzle.

The damp air washed over her burning cheeks like ice water. She took a deep, shuddering breath, filling her lungs with the sweet, wet smell of autumn mud, rotting leaves, and rain-swept grass.

Around her, six hundred children were streaming down the asphalt path toward the football pitches.

At the edge of the playground, Sadie turned and looked back at the school buildings.

The 1972 science extension stood dark beneath the bruised clouds.

As she watched, a low, muffled tremor shook the ground beneath her school shoes.

Near the base of the boiler house chimney, where the concrete foundation slab met the gravel perimeter path, the asphalt gave a sudden, sickening sag. A three-foot depression opened in the turf, the grass peeling away like sod from a fresh grave as forty tons of subterranean earth settled into the collapsed colliery drift.

A cloud of brown dust puffed up through the drainage grating beside the boiler doors, then vanished in the driving rain.

The building settled half an inch on its footings, its brick expansion joints creaking once before locking solid into the bedrock.

Sadie stood on the wet path, the rain soaking into her woolen shoulders, watching the empty chimney stack.

The blue pilot light was gone.

On the wet tarmac of the bus turnaround, three fire appliances from Doncaster Central stood with their blue beacons flashing, their diesel engines idling in the rain.

Firefighters in yellow protective suits and black rubber boots were unrolling heavy canvas hoses across the grass, while the station officer conferred with Mr. Vance beneath a large black umbrella. The smell of diesel exhaust, wet asphalt, and rainwater filled the air, sweeping across the crowded playing fields in cold gusts.

Sadie stood in the Form 8H line, watching the yellow beacons of the fire trucks reflect off the wet brickwork of the science extension.

The building stood dark, quiet, and motionless, its foundations firmly settled into the earth.

Across the bus turnaround, the flashing blue beacons of the fire engines swept over the crowded lines of children assembled on the playing field.

The station officer stood by the front bumper of the lead appliance, writing notes on a yellow clipboard while speaking to Mr. Vance. "We've got structural movement in the lower basement raft," the firefighter said, his voice carrying above the hum of the diesel engines. "Looks like secondary subsidence into an unmapped colliery heading. The building is stable for now, but nobody goes into that west wing until the Coal Authority completes a core survey."

Sadie stood in the Form 8H line, feeling the cold rain run down her neck, watching the dark silhouette of the boiler house chimney stand silent against the clouds.

The rain had eased to a fine South Yorkshire drizzle that hung in the air like damp wool.

Across the playing fields, the six hundred pupils of Ashfield Comprehensive were beginning to reassemble into their form groups on the tarmac path. Teachers with clipboards were shouting instructions, their voices muffled by the low clouds that hung over the valley.

Mr. Henderson walked along the Form 8H line, his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, his green tie spotted with raindrops. He stopped beside Sadie, looking down at her torn blazer hem and the clean handkerchief wrapped around her thumb.

"Are you all right, Sadie?" Henderson asked, his voice softer than she had ever heard it.

"Yes, sir," Sadie said, looking him in the eye. "I'm fine."

Henderson nodded once, made a small pencil mark on his register, and moved down the line to check Ryan Fletcher.

On the wet playing field, the six hundred students of Ashfield Comprehensive were watching the yellow fire appliances idle on the bus turnaround.

A team of three firefighters in heavy black waterproofs walked along the perimeter gravel path, carrying a portable gas detector and a telescopic measuring rod. They stopped by the boiler house ventilation grating, lowered the sensor into the void, and noted the readings on a clipboard before conferring with the station officer.

Sadie stood in the Form 8H line, her canvas satchel clutched tight to her chest, watching the white steam rise from the rain-soaked roofs of the school buildings.

Behind the dark basement windows, there were only silent shadows, sleeping pipes, and twenty thousand tons of solid earth.

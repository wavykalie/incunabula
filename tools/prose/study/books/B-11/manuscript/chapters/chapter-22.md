# Chapter 22: The Broken Handrail

The plan for Thursday had been simple and mechanical.

Sadie had spent Wednesday evening sitting on the edge of her bed with a ruled notepad, calculating the transit times between the second-floor humanities classrooms and the lower dining hall. During the ten-minute break between period two and period three, two hundred students rushed down the west stairwell in a single, packed herd.

If the top bracket of the steel handrail gave way, anyone leaning their weight against the rail would lose their footing on the second flight.

Chloe Rimmer always ran down that stairwell on Thursdays, hurrying to get to the mirror in the girls' cloakroom before the bell.

Sadie entered the yesterday school at 08:15 AM, carrying an eight-inch steel spanner wrapped in a cotton rag.

She climbed to the top landing of the west stairs.

The stairwell was quiet, lit by the gray morning light coming through the high wire-glass windows. Outside, the rain was frozen on the glass panes in long, stationary tracks like lines of clear silicone sealant.

She knelt by the top mounting bracket of the handrail.

The bracket was made of cast iron, secured to the cinderblock wall by two three-quarter-inch hex bolts driven into lead expansion sleeves. Sadie fitted the jaws of the spanner around the upper bolt head.

The metal was freezing. Frost coated the hexagonal corners of the bolt.

She leaned her shoulder against the spanner, preparing to heave with all her weight to crack the thread.

From the half-landing fifteen feet below, a sound reached her ears.

It was not the dragging scraping sound of the yesterday crowd. It was a human sound: a thin, ragged sniffle followed by a hitched intake of breath.

Sadie froze, her fingers locked around the shaft of the spanner.

She lowered herself onto her elbows, crawled to the edge of the concrete landing, and peered down through the gap between the steel balusters.

Sitting on the bottom tread of the half-landing was yesterday's Chloe Rimmer.

Chloe was alone.

Her green blazer was dumped in a heap on the dirty concrete beside her. She had pulled her knees up beneath her gray school skirt, her forehead pressed against her kneecaps, her arms wrapped tight around her shins. Her shoulders were shaking with violent, silent sobs.

Sadie stared down through the railings.

In the living school, Sadie had never seen Chloe by herself. Chloe was always the center of an entourage, shielded by Jessica's sycophancy and Courtney's spite, projecting an image of effortless, untouchable cruelty.

Down on the cold concrete tread, Chloe looked tiny.

Her blonde hair was unbrushed at the back, tangled around her green scrunchie in messy knots. Her white school shirt had a tear along the left armpit seam, mended clumsily with black thread that didn't match the cotton. On the back of Chloe's bare calf, two inches above her white ankle sock, was a dark, yellowing bruise the shape of a man's thumb.

Chloe lifted her head.

Her eyes were swollen and bloodshot, her face stained with wet mascara that had run down into the corners of her mouth. She reached into her blazer pocket, pulled out a crumpled photograph, and pressed it against her lips.

Through the gap in the railings, Sadie could see the image: a man in an oil-stained shell suit standing in front of a blue Ford Cortina, his arm around a small girl in infant school uniform. Across the bottom of the photograph, someone had written in biro: *Don't ring the house again.*

Chloe buried her face in her knees again, her thin frame trembling with muffled sobs.

Sadie knelt on the top landing, the cold steel spanner heavy in her lap.

For eight months, Sadie had hated Chloe with an intensity that burned like swallowed acid. She had dreamed of seeing Chloe broken, humiliated, and stripped of her sneering power.

Now, looking down at the crying girl on the stairs, Sadie felt no surge of triumph.

She saw only the bruise on Chloe's leg. She saw the mismatched thread on Chloe's torn shirt. She saw the exact same loneliness that filled her own empty house on Tuesday evenings when the microwave dinged and her mother sat staring at the muted television screen.

Sadie looked at the cast-iron bracket in front of her.

The spanner remained seated on the hex bolt. One heave would loosen the sleeve, setting the trap that would break Chloe's collarbone tomorrow morning.

Sadie's fingers uncurled from the spanner handle.

Sadie watched yesterday's Chloe from the top of the west stairs.

Through the railings, she could see the crumpled photograph resting on Chloe's lap. The man in the shell suit had his arm around Chloe's neck, his face smiling into the camera with that easy, careless confidence that Sadie had seen in older boys outside the chip shop on Friday nights. Across the white border of the print, the ink was smudged where Chloe's tears had soaked into the glossy paper.

Chloe reached up with a trembling hand, wiped her nose on the sleeve of her green blazer, and pulled her knees tighter against her chest.

Sadie stood motionless on the landing above. The cold steel spanner in her hand felt impossibly heavy, like a chunk of ballast dragged from the bottom of the canal.

Sadie remembered a Tuesday afternoon in November of the previous term.

She had been sitting on the radiator bench outside the girls' changing room, waiting for her bus pass replacement, when Chloe and Courtney had walked past with their sports bags. Chloe had deliberately kicked Sadie's canvas rucksack down the concrete stairs, watching it tumble into the basement landing while laughing loudly about girls who wore secondhand skirts from the church jumble sale.

Sadie had spent twenty minutes picking up her scattered exercise books from the damp concrete while the rain poured down the lightwell.

Now, looking down through the stair railings at Chloe weeping into her sleeves, the memory felt cold and distant, stripped of its bitter sting.

Sadie looked at the square steel handrail running down the west stairs.

The iron brackets had been forged at an engineering works in Rotherham in the autumn of 1971, their gray primer chipped around the counter-sunk screw heads. Garside had told her once that a boy in Year 10 had tumbled over this same handrail during the snowstorms of 1981, breaking his arm against the radiator casing below.

If the upper bracket gave way while Chloe was descending, there would be no warning. The steel rail would pivot outward like a loose gate, throwing Chloe sideways into thirty feet of empty air above the basement flight.

Sadie looked down through the balusters at Chloe's tear-stained face.

The anger that had sustained her for two weeks felt suddenly hollow, a bitter ash that left her throat dry. She set the steel spanner down on the cold concrete landing, pulling her knuckles back into the wool cuffs of her blazer.

Sadie looked down through the steel balusters at the tear in Chloe's school shirt.

The torn fabric revealed a pale shoulder blade marked with a faint, yellowing bruise where Chloe had bumped against a doorframe at home. For eighteen months, Chloe had projected an image of effortless invulnerability, supported by her designer bags, her manicured nails, and the cruel laughter of her court.

Now, stripped of her audience, Chloe looked as fragile and discarded as an empty crisp packet blowing across the wet football pitch.

Sadie set the spanner down on the cold landing, her fingers drawing back into the safety of her cuffs.

Sadie watched yesterday's Chloe from the top of the west stairs, her fingers gripping the cold steel spanner.

Through the balusters, she could see Chloe's shoulders shaking with muffled sobs. Chloe was alone, huddled on the bottom tread of the half-landing with her knees pulled up to her chest, her green blazer dumped on the concrete beside her like an old rag.

On the floor beside Chloe's shoe lay the crumpled photograph of her father standing by the blue Cortina.

Sadie remembered her own mother sitting on the edge of the kitchen stool on Saturday mornings, counting the copper coins from her purse to see if there was enough for gas and a loaf of bread. She remembered the feeling of wearing shoes with worn-down heels, walking with her toes curled tight to keep the sole from flapping against the pavement.

She had thought Chloe was different. She had thought Chloe lived in a warm, bright house where nobody argued about heating bills, where mothers didn't return from poultry shifts with swollen, red fingers.

Now she saw that Chloe was drowning in the exact same cold water, fighting to keep her head up by pushing everyone else down.

Sadie looked at the cast-iron bracket in front of her, then down at the steel spanner in her hand.

The vengeful impulse died in her chest, replaced by a dull, aching weight that felt heavier than the iron tool.

Sadie stood on the top landing of the west stairs, looking down at Chloe's seated figure through the balusters.

The stairwell smelled of wet concrete, peeling lead paint, and the faint, sweet trace of the coconut hair mist Chloe always sprayed on her wrists before break. Beside Chloe's foot, a torn chocolate bar wrapper was caught in the floor drain, its red foil glinting in the gray light from the high wire-glass window.

Chloe lifted the crumpled photograph to her lips again, pressing the faded paper against her teeth as her shoulders shook with muffled sobs.

Sadie felt the spanner in her hand grow cold. The bitter impulse that had brought her to the top of the flight had dissolved completely, leaving only a dull, hollow ache in the center of her ribs.

Sadie looked at the square steel handrail running down the west stairs, tracing the chipped enamel paint with her forefinger.

The stairwell had been constructed from pre-cast concrete sections manufactured by a building supplies works in Mexborough in 1971. Along the underside of the third tread, a raised foundry mark remained legible: *MEX-CON — 1971 — HEAVY DUTY*.

She looked down at Chloe, who sat weeping on the half-landing with her face buried in her knees.

The physical machinery of retribution lay within reach of Sadie's hand, but the desire to use it was gone, burned away by the sight of Chloe's fragile, shaking shoulders.

Sadie looked at the high wire-glass window of the west stairwell where the autumn rain was running down the panes in heavy, gray cords.

Across the valley, the cooling towers of Thorpe Marsh stood against the darkening moors, venting thin, pale plumes of steam into the low clouds. On the railway embankment below the school fence, a single yellow maintenance railcar was idling on the siding, its warning beacon casting a rhythmic amber pulse across the wet ballast.

Sadie stood on the top landing of the west stairs, listening to the rain drumming against the exterior windows.

The sound of the storm was steady, a heavy, gray deluge sweeping across the football pitch from the moors. On the landing below, Chloe had stopped sobbing, sitting motionless on the concrete tread with her forehead pressed against her knees, her white socks soaked with moisture from the lightwell.

Sadie slipped the steel spanner into the deep pocket of her blazer and turned toward the humanities wing.

She pulled the tool away from the bolt and set it down on the concrete landing beside her knees.

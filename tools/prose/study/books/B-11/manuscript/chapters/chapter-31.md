# Chapter 31: The Confrontation with Yesterday

The circle of fifty yesterday figures stood motionless in the purple gloom of the central foyer.

Sadie stood in the center of the ring, her spine four feet from the locked boiler room hatch. Her knuckles were white around the cold chisel, the steel shaft slick with palm sweat. The air smelled of ozone, stagnant coal dust, and the sour chemical stink of floor polish that had cured forty years too long.

From the front rank of the circle, yesterday's duplicate of Chloe Rimmer took two slow strides forward.

Chloe's shoes made no sound on the vinyl floor.

Her skin looked translucent in the dim violet light, the pale blue veins in her temples showing through the waxy flesh like ink lines on tracing paper. The dark scrape on her left cheekbone was dry, encrusted with a black rind of clotted blood. Her green school blazer hung loose on her narrow shoulders, the brass buttons tarnished to the shade of wet pennies.

She stopped four feet from the point of Sadie's chisel.

"Put that down, Sadie," Chloe's duplicate whispered.

The voice was not loud. It was a dry, papery murmur that came from somewhere behind Chloe's throat, vibrating through the teeth without the breath of living lungs.

Sadie kept the steel point leveled at the space between Chloe's collarbones. "Get away from me."

"Why do you hate me?" Chloe asked.

Her unblinking blue eyes were fixed on Sadie's pupils. There was no mockery in the gaze, no cruel smirk curling the corners of her thin lips. Her expression was completely flat, empty as an unpainted wall.

"You poured milk in my bag," Sadie said, her voice shaking with rage. "You tripped me by the fountain. You made everyone in the dining hall laugh at me."

"I was frightened," Chloe whispered.

The duplicate's jaw moved with a stiff, mechanical rhythm.

"My dad packed his clothes in three bin bags on Sunday night," Chloe said. "He threw his house keys on the kitchen table and told my mother he was never coming back to South Yorkshire. He called her a parasite. He called me a little parasite."

Sadie stared at the duplicate.

"I came to school on Monday morning with five pounds my gran gave me," Chloe continued, her voice level and devoid of inflection. "My shoes were pinching my toes. I saw you sitting in the corner by the water fountain with your lunch in your lap, looking at your atlas. You looked like you didn't care about anything. You looked like nobody could touch you."

"You broke my knee against the tiles," Sadie said, her throat tightening.

"I wanted to see you hurt," Chloe whispered. "I wanted someone else in this building to feel like they were drowning in a bucket of cold grease."

She took half a step closer.

The point of Sadie's cold chisel touched the green wool of Chloe's blazer, resting directly over the embroidered school crest.

The wool yielded beneath the steel point. Beneath the fabric, the chest felt as hard and unyielding as a slab of cured concrete. There was no heartbeat beneath the ribs, no expansion of lungs, no warmth radiating from the flesh.

"Did it fix your bag, Sadie?" Chloe asked.

Her hand rose, slow and stiff, pointing toward the ceiling where the purple transformers hummed in the shadows.

"You broke my pencil. You broke my lock. You poured the acid on my cardigan. Did any of it make you warm at night? Did any of it make your dad come back from Northampton?"

Sadie's chest heaved.

The words struck her like blows from an iron rod. She looked past Chloe at the ring of fifty figures: Mr. Henderson holding his crushed clipboard, Jessica Mills with her open mouth, Courtney Vance with her rigid neck, forty other children standing in the cold purple light.

They were not demons. They were not monsters from a horror film.

They were the preserved residue of every cruel word, every shoved shoulder, every whispered insult that had ever accumulated in the corridors of Ashfield Comprehensive, trapped twenty-four hours behind the living world and waiting for someone to give them a reason to move.

Sadie's arm began to tremble.

The cold chisel wavered in her grip. The steel point slipped from the wool of Chloe's blazer and dropped an inch.

"You're not Chloe," Sadie whispered.

Around the perimeter of the circle, the other yesterday students began to whisper.

It was not loud, but a collective sibilant murmur that traveled through the floor tiles into Sadie's shoes. The voices were dry and papery, speaking names of forgotten things: old exercise books, spilled inkwells, lost bus passes, lunch money stolen in the cloakrooms. The grievances of thirty years were speaking through the stiff jaws of the children.

Sadie looked at yesterday's Jessica Mills. A tear of white plaster dust had formed at the corner of Jessica's eye, running down her waxy cheek like a trail of powdered lime. Beside Jessica, Mr. Henderson's gray hands were still twitching, his fingers seeking his missing pen.

"You think you're different from me," Chloe's duplicate whispered, her dead eyes locked on Sadie's face. "You think because you hide in the pipework, you don't carry the coal dust inside you."

Sadie looked into the duplicate's eyes.

There was no warmth there, but neither was there the haughty cruelty that had tormented her in the living corridors. She saw only the mirror of her own isolation: two girls growing up on opposite sides of the railway cut in damp brick terraces, eating microwave meals on plastic trays while their mothers worked twelve-hour shifts at the distribution warehouses.

"My mum doesn't come home until half six," Sadie said, her voice dropping into a quiet, conversational cadence that felt strange in the dead air. "She sits in the armchair with her boots unzipped, staring at the lottery numbers. She's too tired to ask about my day. She's too tired to ask if anyone pushed me."

Chloe's duplicate stood motionless, her pale fingers twitching once against her blazer hem.

"My mum cries in the pantry," the duplicate whispered. "She keeps the door shut and turns the extractor fan on so nobody hears. She thinks if she stops crying, the bailiffs will take the television."

Sadie looked at the pale face of yesterday's Chloe Rimmer.

In the living school, Chloe's hair was always styled with strawberry-scented mousse, her nails painted with clear lacquer that glinted in the classroom strip lights. Here, in the dim violet light of the duplicate foyer, the facade had peeled away completely. The blonde hair was tangled and damp, the fingernails bitten down to the pink quick where Chloe had chewed them during silent reading tests.

"You thought you were breaking things because I was cruel," the duplicate whispered, her voice dry as dry leaves in a gutter. "I was cruel because if you don't make people look away, they see your shoes have holes in the soles. They see your mum's coat is ten years old."

Sadie lowered the cold chisel until the steel point rested on the linoleum between her shoes.

"I know," Sadie said, her throat tight with a pain that had nothing to do with bruises. "I know about the shoes."

Sadie looked into the duplicate's eyes, seeing the faint reflection of the purple emergency beacons pulsing in the glassy irises.

"You don't have to stay here," Sadie said quietly, her voice steady in the dead air. "You don't have to spend forty years standing by the lockers waiting for someone to drop their books."

Chloe's duplicate stood motionless for five seconds, the wind from the collapsing ceiling ruffling the torn collar of her white blouse. A single, faint tear of white plaster dust ran down her cheek, leaving a pale track across the gray skin.

"The bell's going to ring, Sadie," the duplicate whispered, her voice fading into the rising sonic roar of the alarm. "Get yourself out before the roof comes down."

In the purple gloom of the central foyer, Sadie stood four feet from Chloe's duplicate.

The fifty yesterday figures stood in their wide crescent, their faces illuminated by the frantic red strobe of the fire alarm beacons. Above their heads, the acoustic ceiling tiles were buckling, showers of white fibrous dust sifting down through the air like snow.

"You wanted to see me broken," the duplicate whispered, her dead eyes locked on Sadie's face. "You wanted everyone to see that Chloe Rimmer was small and ugly and frightened."

Sadie shook her head slowly.

"I did," Sadie said, her voice quiet in the roaring air. "I wanted you to feel like I felt in the dining hall. I wanted you to drown in the milk."

The duplicate tilted her head, her waxy cheek catching the crimson strobe.

"Did it help, Sadie?"

Sadie looked down at her hands, smeared with boiler soot and dried blood.

"No," Sadie whispered. "It just made the cellar colder. It just brought the coal dust into my bed."

She took a step back from the duplicate, turning her face toward the library doors.

In the purple gloom of the central foyer, Sadie looked at the ring of fifty yesterday people standing shoulder to shoulder.

She saw Mrs. Appleby, her French grammar book clutched to her starched blouse; she saw Ryan Fletcher in his muddy rugby jersey; she saw thirty children from Form 8A and Form 8H, all frozen in the posture of students waiting for a bell that would never ring.

"You're not real," Sadie said, her voice echoing softly against the cinderblock walls. "You're just the grease left on the tiles. You're just the milk that dried in the carpet."

Chloe's duplicate stood motionless, her unblinking blue eyes reflecting the crimson flash of the fire alarm beacons.

"We're yesterday, Sadie," the duplicate whispered. "And yesterday never goes home."

In the purple gloom of the duplicate foyer, Sadie looked at the circle of fifty yesterday figures standing around her.

She saw the familiar faces of her classmates: Jessica Mills, Courtney Vance, Ryan Fletcher, forty other children who had spent eighteen months laughing at her second-hand blazers and her quiet lunches behind the bicycle sheds.

None of them were moving. None of them were laughing now.

They were trapped in the amber stasis of their own past malice, unmoving monuments to a school day that would never finish.

In the purple gloom of the central foyer, Sadie looked at the fifty yesterday figures standing in their silent circle.

She noticed that the dust on their shoulders was beginning to swirl, caught in a minute, spiraling vortex that rose toward the ceiling vents. The temporal stasis was breaking down; the twenty-four-hour lag that had kept them pinned in place was dissolving into the sonic vibrations of the master alarm.

"I'm yesterday," the duplicate said. "I'm the day you can't get back."

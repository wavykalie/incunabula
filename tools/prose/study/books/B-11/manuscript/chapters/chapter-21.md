# Chapter 21: The Acoustic Drift

The air in the humanities wing of the yesterday school had developed an audible grain.

Sadie stood outside Room 16, her shoulder blades pressed against the painted cinderblock. When she held her breath, the silence was no longer an empty void; it possessed a low, continuous electrical hum, like the sound of an ancient cathode-ray television set left on a dead channel in an empty house. The pitch hovered at forty-five hertz, vibrating through the linoleum tiles directly into the soles of her shoes.

She brought out an old plastic dictaphone she had taken from her father's desk drawer.

The black plastic casing of the recorder was scratched, its red record button faded to the color of tinned salmon. She pressed the mechanical button down with her thumb.

The twin spools inside the clear cassette door turned with a dry rattle.

Sadie held the small condenser microphone two inches from the timber door of Room 16.

Inside the classroom, thirty Year 7 pupils were frozen in Monday afternoon's French lesson. Mrs. Appleby stood by the overhead projector, her hand pointing toward an acetate sheet displaying French verb conjugations. On the acetate, the black ink of the word *vouloir* was sharp and clear, but the projected square of light on the whitewashed wall was shimmering with a faint, lavender oscillation.

Sadie adjusted the earphone in her left ear.

Through the cheap plastic earpiece, the room's acoustic profile hissed with magnetic tape hiss, but beneath the tape noise was another layer.

It sounded like voices speaking through a pipe submerged in thick grease.

*...attend... Sadie...*

Sadie pulled the earphone from her ear, her pulse knocking hard against her collarbone.

She pressed her bare ear directly against the timber of the door.

The wood felt colder than the surrounding cinderblock, chilled to the point where moisture from her skin condensed into a tiny wet circle on the varnish. The sound came through the grain of the door frame with distinct phonetic clarity. It was not French verbs. It was the dry, synthetic whisper of thirty children murmuring together in a tight, uncadenced unison.

*Sadie Bell... the door is unlatched... Sadie Bell...*

She stepped back from the door.

Down the long corridor, the yellow ceiling tubes flickered once. The light dropped to forty percent brightness, shifting toward that bruised, purplish tint before recovering with an audible hum from the transformer ballasts.

At the far end of the wing, the double doors leading to the science block swung inward by three inches.

There was no draft. The air pressure in the hallway remained completely flat, sealed behind forty tons of brick and foundation concrete. Yet the heavy oak doors, fitted with hydraulic door-closers manufactured in Birmingham, moved against their internal oil dampers with a slow, creeping deliberation.

Through the three-inch gap, Sadie saw the floor of the science corridor.

A trail of dark gray dust stretched along the center of the linoleum tiles. It looked like crushed coal shale mixed with yellow boiler lagging, scattered in small, rhythmic patches eighteen inches apart, leading from the foot of the west stairs toward the central foyer.

She walked toward the gap, her trainers making no sound on the floor.

As she drew level with the fire alarm call point on the central pillar, she stopped.

The small red metal box had changed.

In the living school, the red paint was chipped around the keyhole, and the clear glass element bore the manufacturer's white lettering: *BREAK GLASS — PRESS HERE*.

Here, the red paint was peeling away in dry curls, revealing dull gray lead underneath. The white lettering on the glass had shifted, the printed characters rearranging themselves into a single, jagged word: *STAY*.

Sadie did not touch the glass.

Sadie walked past the geography department noticeboards on the first floor.

A large, colored map of the British Isles from 1982 was pinned to the cork backing, its blue plastic pushpins oxidized to a dull chalky white. Above the map, someone had pinned a newspaper clipping from the *Sheffield Star* announcing the closure of the Thorne Colliery shaft: *END OF AN ERA FOR SOUTH YORKSHIRE COAL*.

She held the dictaphone microphone toward the keyhole of Room 18.

Inside the empty classroom, thirty desks sat facing the blackboard. On the tape, beneath the constant magnetic hiss, the whispering sound continued, rising and falling in rhythmic cadences like steam escaping from a fractured radiator pipe.

*Sadie Bell... the door is unlatched... the coal is rising...*

Sadie clicked the stop button on the dictaphone, tucked the plastic machine into her pocket, and walked down the east stairs.

Sadie pushed open the swing doors of the music department at the end of the lower corridor.

Inside the rehearsal room, an old upright Chappell piano stood against the acoustic-tiled wall, its dark mahogany casing scarred by decades of student cigarette burns and carved initials. Sadie reached out and pressed middle C with the tip of her forefinger.

The ivory key depressed with a stiff, wooden resistance.

Inside the cabinet, the felt-covered hammer struck the steel string with a flat, muffled *thump*. The sound produced no musical tone, only a dry, leaden vibration that traveled through the iron frame into the floorboards, humming at forty-five hertz before dying away into the dead air.

Sadie walked to the art department storage room at the end of the second-floor corridor.

The room smelled of dried clay, linseed oil, and powdered tempera paints. On the central trestle table, twenty ceramic palettes sat in rows, each holding dried dollops of ultramarine, burnt sienna, and cadmium yellow that had solidified into hard, glassy buttons forty years ago. Above the sink, a rack of hog-bristle brushes hung upside down, their wooden handles bleached by decades of tap water.

She held the microphone of the dictaphone close to the dark cavity beneath the kiln table.

In the recording, beneath the background tape hiss, the whisper in the walls was rising in pitch, shifting from forty-five hertz to a shrill, singing tone like wind whistling through telegraph wires.

*Sadie Bell... Form 8H... your desk is in the corner... your seat is waiting...*

Sadie clicked the cassette recorder off. The silence that rushed back into the room was heavy, pressing against her eardrums like cold lead.

Sadie walked to the biology laboratory at the end of the science corridor.

On the demonstration bench, four brass monocular microscopes sat beneath glass bell jars. Sadie lifted the jar from the first microscope and peered through the brass eyepiece. The specimen slide was an onion skin stained with iodine, the hexagonal cells and dark nuclei frozen in razor-sharp focus beneath the forty-power objective lens.

Inside the barrel of the microscope, the reflection of the ceiling tubes was steady and cold, devoid of the minute vibrations that always made living microscope stages tremble under high magnification.

She lowered the bell jar back over the brass instrument, the glass rim seating into the wooden base with a soft, airtight click.

She backed away toward the west stairwell, keeping her eyes fixed on the three-inch opening of the science doors until she reached the iron rungs of the boiler shaft.

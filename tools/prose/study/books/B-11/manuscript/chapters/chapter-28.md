# Chapter 28: The Shifting Blueprint

Mr. Garside stood up from the stool, walked to the tall steel plan chest against the back wall, and pulled open the bottom drawer with a screech of ungreased slides.

He brought out a roll of heavy, blue-tinted architectural linen, tied with a length of hairy jute twine.

He cleared the jars of screws from the center of the workbench with a sweep of his forearm, untied the twine, and unrolled the sheet across the timber. He held the corners down with four heavy cast-iron sash weights.

The title block in the bottom right corner was printed in neat draughtsman's lettering:

**WEST RIDING COUNTY COUNCIL — ARCHITECT'S DEPARTMENT**
*Project: Ashfield Comprehensive Secondary School (Phase II Extension)*
*Drawing No: WR-71-4409-S*
*Date: 14 September 1971*
*Approved: H. M. Senior Surveyor (Coal Board Liaison)*

Sadie leaned over the bench.

The blueprint showed the subterranean foundations of the science wing, drawn in crisp white lines against the Prussian blue linen. Deep concrete pile caps were indicated by cross-hatching, driven fifty feet into the underlying sandstone.

Between Boiler Three and the ventilation chimney, where the brick wall met the foundation footing, a rectangular hatch was drawn in bold white ink.

Above the hatch, a hand-lettered note had been added in red drafting pencil, stamped with an official colliery seal:

*CAUTION: Sub-surface geological anomaly logged at Grid Ref 44/891. Colliery seam failure 1912. Foundation slab designed with continuous temporal pressure relief aperture C-4. DO NOT WALL IN. DO NOT APPLY PERMANENT MORTAR. Relief aperture must remain unsealed to allow atmospheric equalization between surface strata and mine workings.*

Garside pointed a thick, grease-stained thumb at the red note.

"They knew," Garside said. "The Coal Board surveyors knew what was down here before they poured the first yard of concrete. They left that hatch as a blow-off valve so the pressure wouldn't crack the main foundation beams."

Sadie traced the white lines of the blueprint with her index finger.

As she touched the linen, her breath caught.

The blue paper was warm beneath her fingertip.

The white lines representing the basement corridor—lines that had been printed with ferrocyanide emulsion in 1971—were shifting.

Very slowly, with the creeping imperceptibility of an hour hand on a tower clock, the lines of the lower corridor were widening. The narrow service tunnel behind Boiler Three was bowing outward, its walls curving into the foundation footings of the main school.

"Look," Sadie whispered, pulling her hand back.

Garside peered down through his wire-rimmed reading glasses. His weathered face tightened into an expression of grim, exhausted comprehension.

"The splay is expanding," Garside muttered. "It's eating into the structural footings. If the replica corridor widens another three feet, the basement floor under Boiler Three will drop right into the old coal workings."

"How do we stop it?" Sadie asked, looking up at him.

Garside straightened his back, his hands resting on his hips. He looked at the boiler house wall, through thirty feet of solid earth toward the dark recess behind the pipes.

"The valve has to be tripped from inside," Garside said. "The primary counterweight bar on the replica side has an emergency trip shear. If you drop the master bolt from the inside while the living school is empty, the temporal circuit collapses on itself. The splay snaps shut like an empty purse."

Sadie looked at the blueprint. "What happens to the person inside?"

Garside did not answer for five seconds. He reached into his pocket, took out a heavy brass master key on an iron ring, and laid it gently on the blue paper.

Sadie traced the red drafting pencil lines on the 1971 blueprint.

In the margin beside the foundation footing, a handwritten surveyor's note in faded purple ink caught her eye: *Vibration frequencies recorded during drift excavation: 45.2 Hz continuous. Source unknown. Unrelated to colliery machinery.*

"That's the frequency," Sadie whispered, looking up at Garside. "The hum in the walls."

Garside nodded grimly, tapping the blueprint with his calloused finger. "Forty-five hertz. That's the frequency of the coal seam. When the pressure builds up, the whole building resonates like an iron bell."

Sadie looked at the cross-section drawing in the margin of the 1971 blueprint.

The geological strata were indicated by precise draughtsman's hatching: six feet of topsoil and boulder clay, thirty feet of Middle Coal Measures sandstone, followed by the dark, fractured band of the Barnsley coal seam. A thick red dotted line marked the fault plane where the strata had sheared during the 1912 explosion, slanting downward directly beneath Boiler Three.

"The whole school is balanced on that fracture," Garside said, pointing with his pencil. "It's like a seesaw made of concrete and sandstone. You put too much weight on the past, the present goes tipping into the dark."

Sadie examined the foundation elevation on the 1971 blueprint.

Beneath Boiler Room Three, the draughtsman had indicated the subterranean void with cross-hatched red lines labeled: *TEMPORAL SPLAY RELIEF CHAMBER C-4*. A small handwritten note in the margin was signed by the Chief Inspector of Mines: *Chamber must remain unblocked. Relieves barometric displacement between abandoned 1912 workings and surface atmosphere.*

"The splay isn't a room," Sadie said, looking up at Garside through the dim workshop light. "It's a gap between Monday and Tuesday."

Garside nodded grimly, blowing a stream of blue cigarette smoke toward the ceiling. "A gap that's getting wider every time you drop an iron bar down those stairs, lass."

Sadie looked at the official stamp in the bottom left corner of the 1971 architectural drawing:

**APPROVED FOR CONSTRUCTION UNDER THE MINES AND QUARRIES ACT 1954**
*Colliery Liaison Officer: J. R. Braithwaite, C.Eng., M.I.Min.E.*
*National Coal Board — Doncaster Area Headquarters*

The ink of the stamp was faded purple, the rubber imprint slightly smudged along the upper border. Beside the stamp, a handwritten inspector's note in red biro read: *Subterranean acoustic resonance monitored during piling operations. Frequency: 45.2 Hz. Piling accepted subject to annual foundation leveling survey.*

"They accepted it," Sadie murmured, her fingers tracing the purple ink. "They knew the ground was humming, and they poured the concrete anyway."

"You have five minutes before the seal sets into solid mortar," Garside said quietly. "If you're not back through the hatch before the fifth minute strikes, you'll be one day behind for the rest of your life."

# Chapter 27: Garside's Confession

At four o'clock on Friday afternoon, the school was deserted.

The closure of the west wing had prompted the Headteacher to dismiss all pupils at lunchtime. The endless corridors stood dark, the heating turned down to frost-protection levels, the air smelling of damp plaster and copper water pipes.

Sadie sat on an upturned wooden crate inside Mr. Garside's workshop in the basement.

The workshop was a narrow brick vault beneath the kitchen block, crammed with workbenches, vices, rolls of lead flashing, and jars of assorted iron screws sorted by length. An old electric kettle whistled gently on an asbestos tile beside an enamel teapot.

Mr. Garside poured two mugs of dark, steaming tea, dropped three heaped spoons of granulated sugar into Sadie's mug, and pushed it across the scarred wooden bench.

"Drink it," Garside said gruffly. "You're shaking like a wet terrier."

Sadie wrapped both hands around the hot ceramic mug. The warmth seeped into her stiff fingers, easing the dull throbbing in her bruised elbow.

Garside sat down on a heavy oak stool, lit a roll-up cigarette with a brass petrol lighter, and blew a thick plume of blue smoke toward the soot-stained rafters.

"You pushed her," Garside said.

Sadie kept her eyes fixed on the dark tea. "The bracket broke."

"I know the bracket broke," Garside rasped. "I picked the pieces up off the stairs with a dustpan. The iron sheared clean through. No rust in the middle. Just pure, cold shock."

He tapped the ash from his cigarette into an empty tobacco tin.

"You were through that hatch again on Wednesday."

Sadie said nothing.

"Don't lie to me, lass," Garside said, his voice dropping into a low, gravelly hum. "I saw the soot marks on your cuffs. I smelled the lag dust on your skirt when Henderson was fussing over you in the medical room."

He leaned forward, placing his heavy, grease-calloused forearms on the bench between them.

"My Jenny found that door in ninety-six," Garside said in a low voice. "She was having a rough time of it with the girls in Form 9. Same sort of nonsense—whispers, stolen books, nasty notes pushed through the locker louvers. She thought that door was a hiding place. A quiet room where nobody could lay a finger on her."

"What did she do?" Sadie whispered.

"She stayed in there too long," Garside said. "She thought she could use it to fix things. She took an exercise book that belonged to one of the girls who was tormenting her and threw it in the incinerator chute in the duplicate cellar."

Garside took a slow drag on his cigarette, the tip glowing dull orange in the basement gloom.

"The next day, that girl's house in Mexborough had a chimney fire. Burnt the kitchen right out. The girl wasn't hurt, but Jenny knew. She knew what she'd done."

"The yesterday school isn't just an echo," Sadie said.

"No," Garside said, shaking his head. "It's a pressure valve. In seventy-two, when they built this wing, the county architects hit the old drift shafts of the Ashfield Main seam. The coal seams were full of firedamp and water. But there was something else down there—a temporal fracture where the strata had slipped thirty feet during the colliery explosion of 1912."

He picked up a heavy iron bolt from the bench and set it down with a click.

"The school above bleeds into that fracture. All the malice, all the anger, all the spite of two thousand kids crammed into concrete boxes—it drains down into that cellar like dirty wash water. It builds a replica. A cold, hungry duplicate that feeds on every grievance you bring through that hatch."

Garside looked at her with his hard, gray eyes.

"The more you break down there, Sadie, the wider the splay opens. You broke the pencil. You broke the lock. You tipped the acid. And on Wednesday, you knocked that extinguisher down the stairs."

Sadie's mug trembled in her grip, tea spilling over the rim onto the wooden bench. "I didn't mean to. It was an accident. I tried to catch it."

"Doesn't matter to the cellar whether it was an accident or not," Garside said. "The causal wave doesn't care about your motives. It just delivers the blow on time. And now the splay is wide enough to let things through."

He leaned closer, his breath smelling of dark tobacco and peppermint.

Garside poked the glowing embers of his roll-up cigarette against the rim of his tobacco tin, the blue smoke swirling into the damp air of the workshop.

"My Jenny was never the same after she came out of that cellar," Garside said, his voice dropping into a rasping murmur. "She'd sit by the kitchen window on Sunday afternoons, staring at the canal basin for four hours without blinking. When I asked her what she was looking at, she told me she was watching the coal barges from nineteen-fifty. Said she could hear the pit ponies whinnying under the bridge."

He looked at Sadie, his gray eyes hard and steady in the basement gloom.

"The past doesn't forgive, lass. It just waits for you to slip through the seams."

Garside reached into a metal tobacco tin on his workbench, pulled out an old brass disc the size of a two-shilling coin, and laid it on the timber between them.

The brass was stamped with deep, industrial numerals: *THORNE COLLIERY — 412*.

"That was my father's pit tally," Garside said, his thumb rubbing the worn brass rim. "He worked the Barnsley bed for forty-two years. When the shift went down, you hung your token on the board by the shaft. When you came up, you took it off. If a token stayed on the board after the cages stopped, they knew someone was left in the workings."

He looked at Sadie with his hard, gray eyes.

"That door behind Boiler Three has got a tally of its own, lass. You make sure you take yours off the board before the cage goes down."

Mr. Garside took a heavy iron key from his leather belt, walked to the corner of his workshop, and unlocked a green steel cabinet marked *COLLIERY RECORDS 1971–1985*.

He pulled out a hardback surveyor's daybook bound in black oilcloth, its edges worn down to the gray millboard. He flipped through the yellowed pages, stopping at an entry dated October 14, 1972: *Shift 2 — Return Drift 4 — Subterranean void encountered at 42 yards. Acoustic resonance logged at 45 Hz. Excavation suspended pending geological review.*

"They knew the void was there," Garside muttered, his calloused finger tapping the ink entry. "They poured thirty tons of slag into the drift and laid the boiler slab right on top of it. They thought if you bury something in concrete, it stops breathing."

He closed the book with a heavy thump that raised a small cloud of coal dust from the workbench.

Mr. Garside unrolled an old linen map of the Thorne Colliery underground workings across his workbench, weighing the corners down with four brass pipe fittings.

The map was dated 1965, drawn in intricate black and red ink showing the labyrinthine network of coal faces, return drifts, and haulage roadways extending three miles beneath the parish. A thick red cross marked the location of the Ashfield Comprehensive foundation raft, situated directly above the intersection of the No. 4 return drift and the Barnsley seam fault plane.

"The miners called this section the Sump," Garside said, his calloused finger tracing the red cross. "Every time it rained heavy on the moors, the water drained through the fault into the return drift. It's a natural syphon, Sadie. It pulls at everything above it."

In the basement workshop, Mr. Garside stood by the vice, cleaning the threads of a heavy pipe union with a wire brush.

"The 1912 explosion took forty-two men in the No. 4 drift," Garside said, his voice low and steady against the hum of the boiler pump. "They never recovered the bodies from the lower headings. The Coal Board just sealed the drift with brick dams and flooded the shaft to stop the fire. When they excavated the foundations for this school in seventy-one, the bulldozers broke right into the return airway."

He set the wire brush on the bench and looked at Sadie through the dim yellow light.

"That wasn't just air coming out of that hole, Sadie. It was seventy years of unspent time. The pressure of two thousand men working in the dark, trapped under four hundred feet of sandstone. When they poured the concrete raft, they built a chimney right over the fracture."

He picked up his mug of tea, took a slow sip, and set it back on the timber.

"The yesterday school isn't an accident, lass. It's an open wound in the rock. It bleeds into the present every time someone brings their hatred down those basement stairs."

In the basement workshop, Mr. Garside set his mug of tea on the timber bench and looked at the foundation wall behind Boiler Three.

"My Jenny died of an enlarged heart when she was twenty-two," Garside said quietly, his voice low and steady. "The coroner in Doncaster said it was a congenital defect in the mitral valve. But I saw her medical charts, Sadie. Her heart was beating at eighty-four beats a minute, exactly seventy-two hours out of sync with her respiration. Her body was living in one day, and her pulse was ticking in another."

He picked up an old brass plumb bob from the tool tray and swung it on its braided cord.

"The splay takes your pulse, lass. You spend too much time listening to the pipes down here, your blood starts running on colliery time."

Mr. Garside took an old tin of tobacco from his pocket, opened the lid, and pinched a small wad of dark shag between his thumb and forefinger.

"The miners had a saying down at Ashfield Main," Garside said, his voice raspy with boiler smoke. "They said you can dig forty yards into the coal, but the coal is always digging forty yards into you. The earth doesn't let you borrow its dark for nothing, Sadie. It always wants a piece of what you brought down with you."

He struck a match on the side of his bench, the yellow flare illuminating the deep lines around his gray eyes.

In the basement workshop, Mr. Garside took a heavy steel caliper from his tool rack and adjusted the knurled brass thumb screw.

"The miners had a rule down the pit," Garside said, his voice quiet in the warm cellar. "You never went down the heading with a grudge in your pocket. If two men had a row in the pithead baths, the deputy kept them on the surface until they shook hands. Malice in the dark brings the roof down, Sadie. It's like firedamp; you can't smell it until the spark hits the gas."

Mr. Garside picked up his tin of tobacco, tucked it into his boiler suit pocket, and picked up his heavy rubber torch.

"Come on, lass," Garside said gruffly, nodding toward the concrete stairs. "Before Henderson starts sending search parties down the heating ducts. You've got an English lesson in five minutes, and you don't want another yellow slip on your pastoral file."

Sadie set her empty tea mug on the timber bench, thanked him under her breath, and climbed the stairs into the corridor.

"The crowd you saw on Monday? They're not shadows anymore, lass. They're trying to pull the living school down into the coal seam to keep them company in the dark."

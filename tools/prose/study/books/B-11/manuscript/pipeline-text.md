# Chapter 1: The Ham Sandwich

The space behind Boiler Three measured three feet wide, choked with yellow glass-wool lagging and smelling of cooked oil. Sadie Bell sat with her spine pressed against the warm brickwork of the ventilation chimney, her knees pulled tight beneath the hem of her bottle-green school skirt. In the dark between the return pipes, the temperature stayed close to ninety degrees all through October. It made her school tights itch around her calves, but nobody came down the iron stairs after the bell rang for lunch.

She peeled back the silver foil of her packed lunch. Her mother had made the sandwich at six that morning before the bus to the distribution depot in Thorne. It was two slices of white supermarket loaf, a scrape of pale margarine that had melted into grease along the crusts, and a single folded square of processed ham with watery edges. The bread was squashed flat from four hours in the bottom of her rucksack, compressed beneath the dead weight of an Atlas of the British Isles and a broken plastic geometry set.

She ate in small, methodical bites, chewing until each mouthful turned to paste.

Through the concrete floor above, the midday rush sounded like gravel rattling inside a cement mixer. Three hundred pairs of hard-soled shoes pounded along the English corridor. Lockers banged shut on their thin steel latches; the squeal of rubber plimsolls from the lower gym drifted down through the air duct. Whenever someone kicked the heavy double doors at the foot of the west stairwell, the four-inch cast-iron gas main vibrating against the rafters gave off a dull, ringing chime that Sadie felt in the back of her molars.

Her left knee still throbbed with a dull ache beneath her wool tights. The bruise had turned the dark, mottled color of an overripe plum. Yesterday morning at break, Chloe Rimmer had caught her by the water fountain outside Room 11. It had not been a fight; Sadie did not fight. Chloe had simply walked past with Jessica Mills and Courtney Vance, swung her heavy sports holdall with a casual flick of her wrist, and clipped Sadie's kneecap against the tiled corner of the wall. Sadie had gone down on both hands, her palms skidding across the grit on the linoleum, while Chloe laughed without turning round.

The memory tasted like copper on the back of her tongue.

Before fourth period, Sadie had spent morning break huddled by the damp brickwork of the bicycle sheds, watching the rain pool in the oily hollows of the tarmac. The school day in Ashfield always began with that gray, clinging dampness that seeped through cheap school blazers and made wool smell like wet dog. In the cloakrooms, two dozen girls from Form 8A had crowded around the cracked wall mirrors, spraying apple-scented body mist until the air choked your lungs. Sadie had kept her eyes fixed on the cracked tiles beneath the sinks, counting the blue plastic pushpins embedded in the noticeboard until the five-minute warning bell released her into the corridor crush.

Her mother's voice still echoed in her head from that morning's kitchen breakfast. "Keep your chin down, Sadie love," her mother had said, scraping margarine onto toast with a blunt table knife while the kettle rattled on the gas ring. "Don't go giving them reasons to look at you. People round here have got eyes like rooks; you show a frayed cuff and they peck you bald."

Sadie had nodded, pulling her sleeves over her knuckles.

At home in the evenings, there was never anyone to tell. Her mother returned from the frozen packaging line at quarter past six, her fingers red and swollen from handling chilled poultry trays, her eyes glazed with the dull, gray stupor that came from eight hours under sodium tubes. They ate microwaved baked potatoes on plastic trays in front of the television, watching game shows with the volume turned down so the neighbors through the thin party wall would not complain about the noise. Her father's empty corduroy armchair still stood in the corner beside the electric fire, its headrest dented where his neck had rested for eleven years before he packed his tools and drove south to Northampton.

Sadie chewed the last crust of the bread and swallowed. The dough stuck in her gullet; she had to wait three seconds for her throat muscles to force it down into her chest. She had left her water bottle in her form room locker because the lock was sticking again, jammed with folded bus tickets that someone had pushed into the keyhole with the point of a compass.

Behind Boiler Three, the gas burner ignited with a low, coughing roar. A blue tongue of flame flared behind the mica inspection window, casting long, barred shadows across the concrete floor. The heat washed over her face in a dry wave, carrying the sour reek of scorched pipe dust and the damp coal dust that still coated the cellar walls from thirty years before.

She balled the tinfoil into a tight silver pellet, small enough to hide in the change pocket of her blazer.

Her gaze drifted to the narrow recess behind the header tank. The gap back there was framed by two vertical four-inch hot water risers that had lost their asbestos wrapping in jagged patches, leaving the gray fibrous padding hanging down like sheep fleece snagged on wire fencing. Behind the risers, where the original Victorian brick foundation met the 1972 cinder-block footings of the extension, an iron plate was set into the masonry.

Sadie had noticed it three days ago, assuming it was an old flue clean-out hatch, sealed up and rusted shut when the county council switched the boilers from coal to gas.

Now, as the blue flare of the burner flickered across the brickwork, she noticed the hatch was clean. The four edges of the iron frame had been scraped free of lime wash, leaving bright silver scratches against the dark iron. There was no padlock on the hasp. Instead, a heavy drop-latch made of forged iron, counterweighted with an iron ball the size of a plum, rested in a grooved keeper.

She reached out with two fingers. The iron was cold. It made no sense; six inches away, the hot water riser was hot enough to scorch wool, but the metal of the hatch felt as though it had been pulled from an icebox.

Her hand retreated into her cuff. Her heart hammered against her ribs with an irregular, heavy beat, like a pigeon fluttering inside an overturned coal scuttle. She leaned forward again, gripped the cold iron ball in her palm, and lifted.

The latch cleared the lip of the keeper with a dull clink. Cold air spilled out from the seam, smelling of ancient floor wax and cold plaster.

The boiler room floor was laid with engineering pavers that had absorbed half a century of mineral oil and coal dust.

Near the drain in the center of the pit, where the wastewater from the blow-down valves trickled into a cast-iron sump, the tiles had settled into a sunken bowl two inches deep. In the wet weeks of November, water seeped up through the expansion joint, forming a dark, oily film that reflected the yellow ceiling bulbs like a greasy mirror.

Sadie touched the seam of her green blazer where the shoulder wool was thinning.

Her mother had bought the blazer secondhand from the parish charity shop behind the bus station in August, paying five pounds for the stiff, scratchy garment. The sleeves had been hemmed with crooked black thread by the previous owner, leaving two faint creases where the fabric had been let down for an older boy. Sadie had spent her first Sunday of the autumn term picking at the stitches with a pair of nail clippers, trying to smooth the creases with a damp tea towel and an old flatiron on the kitchen board.

In the quiet between Boiler Three and the brick chimney, the smell of damp wool was comforting.



Sadie leaned back against the warm brick of the chimney, her fingers tracing the rough mortar joints.

She remembered the Saturday morning in August when her father had packed his steel cantilever toolbox into the boot of his Vauxhall Cavalier. The car had stood on the cracked asphalt outside their terrace, its exhaust pipe puffing blue smoke into the damp drizzle. Her father had worn his heavy oilskin donkey jacket, the shoulders stiff with coal grease from his years on the surface plant at Thorne.

"There's no work left on the Don, Sadie," he had said, not looking at her face as he slammed the boot lid down. "The council's shutting the depot. A man's got to eat, hasn't he? Northampton's got logistics hubs springing up along the bypass like mushrooms."

Her mother had stood on the front doorstep with her arms folded inside her apron, her slippers wet from the morning rain. She hadn't shouted. She hadn't cried. She had simply stood there like a post set in concrete while the blue Cavalier backed down the street, turned the corner by the railway bridge, and vanished toward the motorway.

In the three months since, her mother had barely spoken sixty words an evening. They lived in the council house like two strangers billeted together during an evacuation, their lives regulated by the drone of the television and the timer on the electric immersion heater.

Sadie looked at the small iron hatch set into the foundation brickwork.

The red oxide primer was cool beneath her fingertips. In this narrow space behind Boiler Three, the silence was different from the silence of her mother's house. It was a dense, physical hush that seemed to hold its breath, waiting for someone to touch the brass pivot pin.

Sadie pulled her knees up to her chin, watching the blue flicker of the burner through the mica window.

The space beneath the three boilers was a network of narrow brick trenches, laid out in 1972 to carry steam returns and blow-down lines to the central sump. In the winter of her first year at Ashfield, an older boy had hidden down here for three days during a police search, sleeping on a pile of mineral wool lagging and drinking water from the condensate cock. Sadie had heard the story from a cleaner in the cloakrooms; people in Ashfield remembered every minor act of truancy and rebellion like parish folklore, passing the details along the bus queues.

She checked the pockets of her green blazer. Beside the squashed tinfoil pellet from her sandwich lay a worn copper coin—a two-pence piece minted in 1982—and a plastic library card with a bent corner.

She took the two-pence coin and scraped it against the foundation footing.

The copper produced a faint, greasy streak across the red engineering brick. Even the clay down here was saturated with mineral oil from the old colliery winches that had operated on this site before the schools reorganization. Everything in the parish was built upon thirty feet of compressed coal slack and sandstone ballast.

Sadie looked at the four-inch return main running along the ceiling above Boiler Three. The pipe was lagged with gray hair felt bound with copper wire, its surface coated in forty years of soot and dried spiderwebs. Whenever the heating circulating pumps kicked on in the central plant room, the iron pipe gave a low, shuddering groan that resonated through the concrete slab into her teeth.

She remembered finding an old brass valve wheel half-buried in the coal dust near the sump pump during her first week at the school. It had been cast with raised lettering: *GLENFIELD & KENNEDY — KILMARNOCK — 1958*. She had cleaned the brass with a wire brush from Garside's bench until the letters shone like gold, then hidden it in the gap between the boiler casing and the foundation wall where nobody could touch it.

In the quiet recess between Boiler Three and the brick footing, Sadie watched a small spider crawl along the cold mortar seam.

The spider was gray, its legs moving with that cautious, hesitating gait that insects used when exploring cold stone. Sadie did not disturb it. She pulled her knees tighter beneath her bottle-green skirt, watching the insect disappear into a hairline crack between two engineering bricks. In this basement, even the spiders lived in the dark, surviving on the dust that sifted through the floorboards from the classrooms above.

Nobody came looking for her here. Mr. Henderson stayed in his warm pastoral office, monitoring behavioral logs and entering attendance metrics, while Chloe Rimmer and her friends ruled the humanities corridor with their strawberry sprays and snapping plastic hair clips. Down here, thirty feet beneath the concrete, there were only the cast-iron pipes, the hum of the circulating pump, and the cold iron hatch resting silently in the brickwork.

# Chapter 2: The Dining Hall Gauntlet

The smell of the dining hall on a wet Tuesday hit the throat like hot bleach and old cabbage.

Sadie stood in the doorway by the fire exit, her canvas rucksack held tight against her stomach with both arms. Two hundred students were wedged along the gray folding tables, their knees bumping under the plastic laminates, their wet blazers steaming in the humid roar of the room. The queue for the hot meal counter stretched all the way back to the double doors: three dozen Year 7 boys jostling for rectangular slabs of cheese pizza while Mrs. Clough, behind the aluminum sneeze guard, slapped scoops of yellow instant mash onto divided plastic trays with a wet thwack.

Mr. Henderson stood upon the stage steps, his voice barking across the din. He held a blue plastic clipboard under his elbow and a bamboo travel mug in his right hand. The silicone seal around the lid had split, leaving a brown smear of milky coffee down the side of his thumb.

"Keep moving, Year Eight," Henderson called out, waving his clipboard toward the tray return racks. "Trays to the washer window before the ten-past bell, or whole tables stay behind for detention. That means you, Ryan."

Sadie had four minutes before the bell to reach the west stairs without crossing the central aisle, but the narrow lane between the bench ends was jammed with wet parkas and sports bags.

She took three steps forward, keeping her head down, her eyes fixed on the scuffed gray floor tiles.

"Well look who crawled out of the drain," a voice said.

Chloe Rimmer sat on the corner of the end table, her gray uniform skirt rolled three times at the waistband to bring the hem four inches above her knees. Her blazer was draped over the back of her plastic chair, revealing a tight white cotton shirt with the collar turned up. Between her manicured fingers she twirled a bright pink mechanical pencil, tapping the eraser against the plastic edge of a half-empty tray.

Jessica Mills sat beside her, chewing on a strip of strawberry bubblegum, while Courtney Vance guarded the gap between their bench and the tray return stack.

"Where is your lunch, Bell?" Chloe asked. She spoke without shouting, yet her voice had that carrying, flat northern sharpness that cut straight through the clatter of cutlery and plastic beakers. "Did your mum forget to buy bread again, or did you eat it out of the bin behind the kitchen?"

"Leave it, Chloe," Sadie muttered. Her voice emerged too quiet, muffled against the canvas of her bag.

"What did you say?" Chloe stood up. She was four inches taller than Sadie, her shoulders squared under the white shirt. She smelled of artificial strawberry lip-gloss and the sweet coconut mist she sprayed in the girls' toilets after second period. "Look at me when I speak to you. You got no manners?"

"I am going to the library," Sadie said, trying to squeeze through the eighteen-inch space between Courtney's chair and the waste bin.

Courtney shifted her hips, blocking the passage with her knee. "Library is shut, Bell. Year Eleven exams."

Sadie tried to turn back toward the fire exit, but two Year 9 boys carrying full trays of curry sauce pushed past her, shoving her sideways against the edge of Chloe's table. Her canvas rucksack slipped down her forearm, its brass zip gaping open where the pull-tab had broken off three months ago.

Chloe looked down into the open bag. Her eyebrows twitched with sudden delight.

"Oh, look at this," Chloe laughed, turning to Jessica. "She has got her geography book in here. Look at the corners. All chewed up."

"Give it back," Sadie said, reaching for the bag.

Chloe moved faster. Her fingers hooked into the strap of the rucksack, dragging it onto the table surface. With her other hand, she picked up a small, open carton of Cravendale chocolate milk from Jessica’s tray. It was three-quarters full, lukewarm and skinning over at the spout.

"You look thirsty, Sadie," Chloe said, her smile wide and cold. "You look like you need some proper calcium. That is what Henderson says in assembly, isn't it? Healthy bones."

She tipped the carton upside down directly over the open mouth of the rucksack.

The brown, syrupy liquid poured in a thick cord over Sadie's lined exercise books, soaking through the blue cardboard cover of her atlas, flooding the fabric pencil case with its chewed biros and broken eraser. It pooled at the bottom of the canvas, dripping through the seam onto Sadie's school shoes with a soft, heavy patter.

Jessica shrieked with laughter, clapping both hands over her mouth. At the next table, four boys turned round, banging their plastic spoons against their trays in rhythm.

A chorus of jeers rose from the benches.

Sadie stood frozen. A drop of chocolate milk had splashed onto the white collar of her shirt, soaking into the cotton fibers like a brown tear. The smell was suffocating—sweet, rancid dairy and cocoa powder.

From the far end of the hall, Mr. Henderson blew his plastic whistle. The sharp, piercing trill silenced the immediate circle of tables for three seconds.

"Right, that is enough noise on Table Four," Henderson called out, not looking up from his clipboard. "Clear your rubbish. The bell is going in two minutes."

Chloe dropped the empty milk carton onto the tray with a soft plop. She wiped her thumb against her paper napkin, looked Sadie straight in the eyes, and smiled.

"Clumsy," Chloe whispered. "You really ought to zip your bag, Bell."

Sadie grabbed the wet canvas straps, hauled the dripping rucksack to her chest, and ran for the fire door.

The disabled toilet beside the music block was the only room in the west wing without a frosted glass panel in the door.

Sadie slipped inside, turning the heavy brass thumb-turn until the deadbolt clicked into the strike plate. She leaned against the cold porcelain basin, her chest heaving, the stench of sour chocolate milk rising from her blazer lapels in humid waves.

Her hands were shaking so hard she could barely turn the chrome cross-head tap.

The water spluttered from the nozzle in a freezing, chlorinated jet that splashed over her wrists. She took a wad of coarse brown paper towels from the plastic dispenser and soaked them under the flow. The paper turned to wet brown sludge in her fingers, tearing into soggy strips as she scrubbed at the dark stains on her green wool skirt.

The milk had penetrated the fabric completely. Where the dairy fat had dried into the weave, the wool had stiffened into a brittle, crusty board that smelled like vomit.

She looked up into the mirror above the basin.

The glass was scratched with compass points carved into the silver backing. In the cracked reflection, Sadie saw her own face staring back: pale skin, red eyes, a smudge of gray soot across her right cheekbone, and her brown hair escaping from its scrunchie in wild, wet wisps.

From the corridor outside, the heavy thud of soft-soled shoes approached the door. A hand rattled the handle twice with an impatient, aggressive jerk.

"Someone in here?" Courtney Vance's voice called out through the timber. "Jessica, the disabled door is locked. Bell is probably in there crying her eyes out."

A chorus of shrill laughter echoed down the corridor, followed by the clatter of lockers being slammed shut.

Sadie held her breath, her back pressed against the tiled wall, until the footsteps faded toward the lower gym. She crumpled the wet paper towels into the metal bin, pulled her damp rucksack onto her shoulders, and slipped out into the empty hallway.

The west corridor outside the dining hall was jammed with Year 7 and Year 8 students trying to escape the rain.

The air smelled of wet duffle coats, damp trainers, and the vinegar that seeped from the cafeteria chip counter. Along the wall, the lockers were painted a chipped institutional teal that showed scratches of gray undercoat beneath the enamel. Every locker door was dented where boys had kicked them shut with steel-toed boots.

Sadie squeezed past the queue by the library entrance, her head down, her rucksack clutched to her stomach.

Near the trophy cabinet, Courtney Vance and Jessica Mills were leaning against the glass case, watching the corridor crowd with sharp, predatory eyes. The trophy cabinet held thirty years of faded silver-plated cups won by Ashfield teams in the 1980s. The velvet lining behind the glass had faded to the color of wet sand, covered in a fine layer of gray dust that had seeped through the sliding wooden doors.

"There she goes," Courtney said, her voice cutting through the noise of the hallway. "Look at the back of her skirt. She has got chocolate milk right down to her socks."

Jessica snorted, blowing a pink bubblegum sphere that popped with a wet snap against her lips. "Smells like a dairy farm. I bet her mother does not even own a washing machine."

Sadie kept walking. She did not turn her head.

She had learned in infant school that answering back only prolonged the game. If you shouted, they laughed; if you cried, they mocked your tears; if you ran, they chased you down the stairs like terriers after a rabbit in a drain. The only defense was absolute, wooden silence—to turn your face into blank plaster and let the insults slide off your skin like water off greasy linoleum.

Inside the west stairwell, two older girls from Year 11 were smoking menthol cigarettes behind the fire-hose reel, their knees propped against the cast-iron risers. The smoke hung in pale blue layers beneath the ceiling, smelling of mint, burning paper, and damp nylon coats. They didn't look at Sadie as she squeezed past, their conversation continuing in low, urgent murmurs about a boy who worked the weekend shift at the petrol station on Thorne bypass.

Sadie held her breath, pressing her shoulder against the damp brickwork to avoid brushing their elbows. The stairwell smelled of fifty years of institution: floor sweeping compound, copper pipe water, and the sour vinegar fumes that drifted up from the kitchen larders below.

In the crowded corridor outside the dining hall, the prefects in their maroon blazers were trying to direct the queue of Year 7 pupils toward the water station.

"Single file against the lockers!" a tall boy shouted, his voice cracking with adolescent strain. "Keep moving! Anyone caught pushing will lose their library privilege for a fortnight!"

Sadie squeezed past the queue, her wet canvas rucksack held against her stomach.

The smell of the dining hall had followed her into the corridor: hot frying fat, vinegar, and the damp wool of two hundred rain-soaked parkas drying on the cloakroom hooks. On the wall beside the trophy cabinet, someone had taped a handwritten poster for the school production of *The Crucible*, its edges torn where boys had picked at the Sellotape during break.

Near the library entrance, Courtney Vance and Jessica Mills were leaning against the radiator, watching the hallway crowd.

"Look at her hair," Courtney whispered, pointing a manicured finger toward a Year 7 girl with crooked plaits. "She looks like she fell out of a hedge backwards. I bet her mum buys her uniform off the market stall."

Jessica giggled, blowing a pink bubblegum sphere that popped against her teeth. "Did you see Chloe dump the milk on Sadie Bell? Her face went completely red like a beetroot. She looked like she was going to wet herself."

Sadie walked past them with her chin down, her sneakers squeaking over the wet vinyl tiles.

In the girls' cloakroom beside the disabled toilet, two Year 8 girls were re-applying strawberry lip gloss in front of the chipped mirror.

"Chloe told Courtney that Sadie Bell doesn't even have a shower in her house," one girl whispered, blotting her lips on a paper towel. "Said they have to boil the kettle on the gas stove just to wash their hair. That's why she always smells like fried bread."

The other girl laughed, capping her gloss tube. "Her mum works nights at the Thorne turkey plant. My brother says the whole street down by the canal smells like chicken feathers."

Sadie stood behind the partition door, her fingernails pressing into the fabric of her damp satchel until her knuckles ached.

She waited until the swing doors clicked shut and the sound of their rubber trainers faded down the corridor before stepping out into the empty room. She looked at her reflection in the cracked mirror: a pale, narrow face framed by damp dark hair, two dark circles beneath her eyes, and a smear of yellow pipe dust along her jaw.

In the west stairwell, Sadie stood behind the fire doors for two minutes, listening to the roar of the school above.

Through the wired glass panel, she could see the entrance to the lower library. Mr. Vance, the headteacher, was walking down the corridor in his dark charcoal suit, accompanied by two school governors carrying leather briefcases. They walked with that slow, measured authority of adults who owned the bricks and mortar, their voices discussing heating oil allocations and roof asphalt grants.

Neither of them looked toward the basement stairs. To the authorities who ran Ashfield Comprehensive, the space below the concrete floor was merely an engineering conduit, a dark cellar of pipes and pumps that kept the radiators warm during the November frosts.

Sadie walked past the English department noticeboards where Mrs. Appleby had pinned the autumn reading list.

The typed sheets of paper were curling at the corners, the names of novels printed in purple stencil ink: *A Kestrel for a Knave*, *The Machine Gunners*, *Lord of the Flies*. Next to the reading list, someone had pinned a newspaper clipping about the closure of the railway locomotive works in Doncaster, its photograph showing three middle-aged men in cloth caps standing outside the locked depot gates.

In the queue for the hot meal counter, two dinner ladies were wiping down the stainless steel food warmer with yellow dishcloths.

"Look at that steam off the cabbage," Mrs. Clough muttered, adjusting her white hairnet with the back of her wrist. "The thermostat on the serving well has been sticking since Monday. If the council doesn't send an engineer before Friday, we'll be serving cold beans to Year Nine."

Sadie moved past them, keeping her head low, her canvas rucksack held against her ribs.

She pushed through the fire doors onto the west basement stairs, the heavy rubber draft excluder scraping the concrete with a long, dull sigh.

# Chapter 3: The Rusty Latch

The chocolate milk had seeped through the bottom of the rucksack and soaked the wool of Sadie's skirt against her left thigh. It felt cold and greasy, sticking to her skin through her tights like pond slime.

She knelt on the dry concrete behind Boiler Three, her breathing coming in harsh, ragged hitches. The cellar smelled of sulfur, gas fumes, and the sweet, sick reek of the milk that was already beginning to sour in the ninety-degree heat of the boiler casing. She took out her exercise books one by one. The blue cover of the atlas was ruined, warped into damp gray cardboard that tore when she touched the corner. Her geography homework on the coastal erosion of Holderness was a smear of running blue ink across lined margins.

She threw the papers into the coal dust by the wall.

The silence of the basement pressed in on her ears. Down here, thirty feet below the ground, the sound of the school bells was reduced to a faint, metallic vibration that rattled through the overhead heating pipes. The two-fifteen bell had already gone; third period had started. By now, Miss Finch would be calling the register in Room 14, recording an orange letter against Sadie's name on the interactive whiteboard. In forty minutes, Mr. Henderson's pastoral portal would generate an automated text message to her mother's mobile phone.

Sadie knew her mother would not see it until evening. Her mother was stacking cardboard cartons in a refrigerated warehouse in Thorne; phones stayed locked in metal lockers until the shift ended.

She wiped her sticky fingers on the dry hem of her slip, turned around on her knees, and looked at the iron hatch behind the header tank.

The four-inch hot water riser beside it clanked three times as the thermostatic valve opened, sending a rush of scalding water through the radiator circuit of the science block above. The air around the pipe shimmered with rising heat waves.

Yet the iron hatch remained matte and frosted, as though a layer of invisible rime coated the forged plate.

She reached behind the pipe. The iron counterweight ball was roughly cast, pitted with small air pockets from the foundry mold. When she closed her fingers around it, the cold bit into her palm with the sharp sting of dry ice. She braced her left boot against the bottom flange of the boiler and hauled upward.

The mechanism was counterbalanced with surprising smoothness. There was no screech of rusted iron, no shower of red dust. The pivot turned inside its brass bushing with a faint, oily whisper, lifting the heavy iron tongue three inches out of its keeper.

The door did not swing outward into the boiler room. It pulled inward, opening away from her into the dark.

Sadie held her breath. She had expected to see the foundation earth—the damp yellow clay and crushed shale that the school was built upon, or perhaps an abandoned coal chute filled with rubble and discarded fluorescent tubes.

Instead, the opening revealed a clean, rectangular doorway four feet high and two feet wide.

Beyond the threshold lay a corridor.

The light inside was a strange, flat amber, like the color of weak tea held up against a window on an overcast afternoon. The floor was laid with nine-inch vinyl composite tiles, speckled gray and off-white, identical to the flooring in the main humanities corridor on the first floor. Along the left-hand wall ran an enameled dado rail painted institutional teal, slightly chipped at the corner where a cleaning trolley had struck the plaster.

Sadie leaned forward until her forehead crossed the line of the brick wall.

The air was completely different. The sweltering, oily heat of the boiler room vanished, replaced by an absolute, dry chill that smelled of floor polish, paper dust, and carbolic soap. There was no sound at all. No hum of air vents, no boiler burner, no distant thud of footsteps from the floor above.

It was the silence of a building locked up for the summer holidays.

On the wall ten feet into the corridor, mounted at eye level, was a red plastic fire alarm call point. Beside it hung an acrylic sign: *HUMANITIES CORRIDOR — ROOMS 11 TO 18.*

Sadie stared at the letters. She knew that sign. She passed it every single morning on her way to registration. But this was the basement, thirty feet below the grass of the playing fields, buried beneath six feet of reinforced concrete footings.

She put both hands flat on the cold linoleum threshold and crawled through.

The threshold was framed in heavy red engineering brick, laid in English bond with black lime mortar that had hardened into stone.

Sadie crouched on all fours, her knees resting on the dry concrete of the boiler cellar, her hands flat against the cold composite floor of the amber hallway. The temperature differential made the hairs on her arms stand up beneath her sleeves; her left cheek was warm from the radiating metal of Boiler Three, while her right cheek was chilled by the dead air of the replica corridor.

She looked down at the threshold line.

The joint between the two floors was clean, separated by a quarter-inch brass expansion strip set flush into the tiles. On the cellar side, the concrete was stained with black oil droplets and swept with gray sawdust compound. On the other side, the gray-speckled vinyl composite tiles were polished to a dull, matte sheen, clean of dust and grit.

She reached into her blazer pocket and took out a five-pence coin.

She laid the silver coin directly across the brass strip, half in the cellar, half in the hallway.

The coin sat perfectly flat. After ten seconds, she touched the cellar half with her index finger; it was lukewarm. She touched the other half; the metal felt like an ice cube from the freezer.

She pushed herself forward with her toes.

Her entire body slipped through the rectangular opening. The smell of the boiler room—the gas, the sulfur, the damp coal—cut off instantly, as cleanly as if a heavy velvet curtain had dropped behind her back.

The amber light filled her vision. It was a flat, sourceless illumination that cast no distinct shadows, making the corridor look like a photograph printed in sepia ink on cheap sugar paper.

Before Sadie crawled behind the boiler casing, she paused by the rusted workbench where Mr. Garside kept his maintenance sundries. A heavy cast-iron vice was bolted to the timber slab, its jaws gripping a severed length of two-inch copper piping green with verdigris. Beneath the bench, three wooden fruit crates held dozens of discarded brass fittings, oxidized lead washers, and empty tins of petroleum grease that had dried into brown rubber. The basement air smelled of forty years of machine maintenance: graphite powder, cutting oil, and the sharp chemical tang of swarfega hand cleaner.

She ran her fingertips along the cold edge of the bench. A framed health and safety certificate from the National Coal Board hung crooked above the light switch, its glass cracked across the signature of the district inspector. The date on the yellowed parchment read November 1974. Everything down here belonged to an era when coal was king, when two thousand men walked down the pit road every morning with metal tally tokens in their waistcoat pockets.

Sadie examined the brick piers that supported the foundation chimney of Boiler Three. The red Staffordshire engineering bricks were laid in English bond, their joints mortared with gray sulfur cement that had turned black from decades of flue dust. In the narrow recess between the chimney footing and the ash pit, someone had scratched a miner's pick emblem into the brick face with a nail, accompanied by the initials *J.G. 1974*.

She rubbed her thumb over the carved pick. It was Garside's mark, left there when he was a young fitter working the night shifts for the county council. The past down here wasn't a dead memory; it was an accumulation of physical labor, recorded in iron and brick beneath the running feet of two thousand living children.

She stood upright, smoothed her skirt, and took her first step down the hall.

# Chapter 4: The Silent Hallway

The iron hatch did not click shut behind her, but the moment Sadie’s boots cleared the threshold, the temperature dropped five degrees.

She stood up slowly, ducking her head until she was certain the ceiling cleared her scalp. The corridor was seven feet high, lined with standard acoustic ceiling tiles held in a white aluminum grid. Three of the tiles near the center were water-stained, marked with yellow, kidney-shaped rings where an upstairs radiator had leaked two winters ago.

She turned in a full circle.

Behind her, the opening in the wall was not an iron door. It was an ordinary fire exit door framed in painted pine, propped open three inches by a red rubber wedge. Through the gap, she could see the glowing blue eye of Boiler Three's pilot flame, flickering like a small gas jet in the dark cellar thirty feet away.

She turned back to the corridor. The light had no source she could identify. The fluorescent strip lights overhead were unlit, their glass tubes gray and dead inside their metal housings, yet the entire hallway was filled with that pale, sourceless amber gloom. It cast no sharp shadows; when she raised her hand, her fingers left only a soft, blurred gray smudge against the teal paint of the dado rail.

She took four steps forward.

Her school shoes were leather-look synthetic lace-ups with thick rubber soles. In the real school, they squeaked loudly on the polished linoleum, announcing her arrival at every corner.

Here, they made no sound at all.

She lifted her foot and brought the heel down hard against the tile. Nothing. No thud, no click, no vibration through the floorboards. The impact was deadened instantly, swallowed by the cold air like a stone dropped into a barrel of flour.

She stopped outside Room 12.

The door was pine with a square panel of wire-reinforced Georgian glass. Through the diamond mesh, she could see the history classroom: five rows of two-person desks with blue plastic chairs, the pull-down map of Europe during the Napoleonic Wars hanging slightly crooked from its brass hook, and Mr. Finch's battered oak desk by the radiator.

On the front desk, an open ring binder lay beside an empty plastic pencil case.

Sadie moved past Room 12 toward the central foyer where the corridor joined the main stairwell. The noticeboards along the right wall were covered in green felt, pinned with blue and yellow notices. She leaned close to read them.

*YEAR 8 NETBALL TRIALS — THURSDAY 17 OCTOBER.*
*ALL PUPILS MUST WEAR CLEAN WHITE TRAINERS.*

She looked at the corner of the sheet. The drawing pin had a cracked yellow plastic head; Sadie remembered seeing it on Monday morning when she had stood by the board trying to hide from Courtney Vance.

She walked out into the foyer.

Above the library doors, the electric school clock hung inside its square steel cage. The black minute hand pointed straight at the nine; the hour hand sat midway between the twelve and the one.

Twelve-forty-five.

That was lunch hour. The middle of lunch hour.

She walked up to the double doors of Room 14, the English room where Miss Finch taught Year 8. The door was propped open with a wooden block. Sadie stood on the threshold, her fingers resting lightly against the pine doorframe.

Inside, the classroom was not empty.

Sitting in the third row, in the seat next to the radiator where the pipe always leaked, was a girl wearing a bottle-green uniform jumper and a white collared shirt. Her brown hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail with a fraying black scrunchie. Her shoulders were hunched forward over a lined exercise book; her left hand was tucked under her thigh while her right hand held a blue Bic biro between two ink-stained fingers.

The girl was not moving. Her chest did not rise or fall; her hair did not stir.

Sadie walked three paces down the aisle, her throat tightening until she could barely draw breath through her nose.

She looked down at the girl’s face.

The girl was twelve years old, with pale skin, a small scar through her left eyebrow from a fall off a tricycle in infant school, and a dark, mottled bruise on her left kneecap visible through her wool tights.

Sadie was looking at herself.

The classroom door of Room 13, where Mr. Finch taught history, stood slightly ajar.

Sadie pushed the wood with one finger. The hinges moved with absolute silence, turning on greased steel pins without the faintest squeak. She stepped inside, her rubber soles soundless on the green linoleum.

Thirty-two desks were arranged in neat rows facing the blackboard.

At the front of the room, on the third desk in the center row, lay an open exercise book. The handwriting was neat, written in black fountain pen: *The Causes of the Industrial Revolution in the West Riding of Yorkshire.* At the bottom of the page, a drop of dried blue ink formed a raised bump on the paper.

Sadie touched the ink drop. It was completely hard, dry and cold as glass.

She walked to the window.

The glass in the steel-framed casement was cold against her palms. Outside, the view of the school playing fields was sharp and clear, bathed in the desaturated gray light of Monday midday. Two hundred yards away, by the perimeter fence where the birch trees bordered the railway line, a magpie was caught in mid-flight between two branches.

Its black-and-white wings were spread wide, tilted at an angle of twenty degrees against the breeze.

The bird was not falling. It hung suspended in the gray air like an insect trapped in amber, five feet above the chain-link fence, frozen between one wing-beat and the next.

Sadie watched it for sixty seconds.

The feathers did not ruffle in the wind. The leaves on the birch branch below remained rigid, their pointed tips angled toward the canal bridge.

Sadie stepped out of Room 11 and walked ten yards down the silent hallway to the biology store room.

The frosted glass door was half unlatched. Inside, four wooden cabinets stood against the rear brickwork, their shelves packed with glass jars containing sheep lungs, pickled dogfish, and wax models of human cell structures. Everything was frozen in perfect, amber clarity. On the teacher's prep bench, a half-empty mug of instant coffee sat beside an open register, a thin white skin across the brown liquid, completely undisturbed by mold or evaporation. Beside the mug, an electric kettle was plugged into the wall, its rocker switch depressed in the 'on' position, yet the red indicator lamp did not glow.

She touched the plastic casing of the kettle. It was cold as marble. In this duplicate wing, energy didn't flow through wires; things simply occupied space without combustion, without current, without the friction that wore out living gears.

Sadie walked past the geography department book store on the ground floor of the duplicate school.

Through the glass panel, fifty copies of the *Modern School Atlas* sat stacked on a wooden shelf, their blue buckram spines identical to the one ruined in her satchel. She reached through the door, lifted the top volume from the pile, and weighed it in her palm. The paper inside was dense and cold, smelling of fresh lithographic ink from thirty years ago.

When she flipped the pages, the colored relief map of South Yorkshire showed the railway lines and collieries of 1975: Thorne, Hatfield, Brodsworth, and Ashfield Main, their pithead icons printed in sharp black squares across the green contours of the Don valley.

She turned away from the window, a cold shiver running across her ribs, and walked toward Room 14.

# Chapter 5: The Pastoral Incident Log

In the pastoral office on the first floor of the real school, the radiator clanked twice and hissed a plume of wet steam from its bleeder screw.

Mr. Henderson clicked the scroll wheel of his mouse, dragging the cursor down the blue interface of the school’s management portal. His screen was divided into three panels: attendance flags on the left, behavioral incident logs in the center, and the pastoral communication log on the right. A small yellow warning icon was blinking beside the entry for 14:15.

*Bell, Sadie (Year 8, Form 8H) — Period 3 Attendance: N (No reason provided).*

He rubbed the bridge of his nose where the plastic pads of his reading glasses had dug two red ruts into the skin. On the desk beside his Dell keyboard sat a half-eaten tuna sandwich in a clear plastic triangle, a stack of twenty-four unread progress reports, and his bamboo travel mug. The coffee inside was cold, covered in a pale, greasy film.

He clicked the icon. The dialog box expanded, displaying Sadie’s behavioral history for the autumn term:

*08/09 — Late registration (5 mins). Logged by tutor.*
*19/09 — Missing PE kit (trainers). Detention issued.*
*02/10 — Found in out-of-bounds area (boiler stairs). Verbal warning.*
*14/10 — Period 3 truancy flagged by ClassCharts automated sweep.*

There was a soft knock at the frosted glass panel of the office door.

"Enter," Henderson said without looking up from the monitor.

The door swung inward. Sadie stood on the industrial cord carpet, her head bowed, her wet rucksack clutched in both hands like a shield. She had tried to wipe the front of her skirt with paper towels from the girls' toilets, but the chocolate milk had dried into a stiff, pale smear across the dark wool, giving off a sharp, vinegar scent that filled the small office in three seconds.

Henderson took off his glasses, folded the wire arms, and laid them on the corner of his desk.

"Shut the door, Sadie," he said. He picked up his bamboo mug, swirled the cold liquid, and set it down without drinking. "Sit down."

Sadie sat on the low plastic chair opposite the desk. She kept her rucksack on her knees, her fingers hooked through the canvas seams.

"Do you know what time it is?" Henderson asked.

"Ten to three, sir."

"It is eight minutes to three. Miss Finch tells me you were absent from English. No note from the medical room, no signing-out slip from the front office. Where were you?"

"I was in the basement, sir."

Henderson sighed. It was a long, dry exhalation that smelled of stale menthol lozenges and black coffee. He picked up a red dry-erase marker and began tapping the flat end against his desk blotter in a slow cadence.

"Sadie, we had this conversation two weeks ago. The basement boiler room is strictly out of bounds to pupils. It is a health and safety risk. Mr. Garside has complained twice about students leaving rubbish behind the fuel tanks. If you have an issue with the dining hall, you use the library. We have a supervised quiet room for Year Eight pupils during the lunch break."

"The library was locked, sir," Sadie said. Her voice remained low, flat, devoid of inflection. "They were doing GCSE speaking exams."

"Then you go to the lower hall. You do not hide in boiler rooms." He leaned forward, resting his forearms on the edge of the desk. His gaze dropped to the stiff, brown stain on the hem of her skirt. "And what on earth have you done to your uniform? Is that chocolate milk?"

Sadie bit the inside of her lower lip. She could taste the salt and copper on the tip of her tongue.

"I dropped it, sir."

"You dropped it all over your rucksack and your skirt?"

"My hands were slippery."

Henderson looked at her for four long seconds. His eyes flicked to the incident panel on his screen, where Chloe Rimmer’s pastoral profile sat three tabs over: *Grade A target student, netball captain, zero behavioral points.*

"Sadie," he said, his tone softening into the smooth, rehearsed cadence of a secondary school pastoral training module. "Year Eight can be a difficult transition. Friendship groups shift. Girls who got on well in Year Seven sometimes find they have different interests. That is a normal part of growing up. But running away to hide in maintenance areas and being careless with your belongings will not help you settle."

"Yes, sir."

"I am recording this as an unauthorized absence. One behavior point. If it happens again before half-term, it will be an automatic Friday detention. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Go to the washroom, clean yourself up as best you can, and go straight to Form 8H for final registration. I do not want to see you on the truant report tomorrow morning."

Sadie stood up, her knees cracking in the quiet office. She took her rucksack by the straps, turned to the door, and walked out into the corridor without another word.

Behind her, Henderson clicked his mouse twice, confirmed the single behavioral penalty on the portal, and reached for his tuna sandwich.

Mr. Henderson's office was lined with gray filing cabinets, each drawer labelled with red plastic Dymo tape: *YEAR 8 ATTENDANCE 2024-2025*, *EXCLUSION LOGS*, *PASTORAL INTERVENTIONS*.

On the wall behind his swivel chair hung a large white dry-erase board divided into thirty numbered rows. Each row carried the name of a student flagged for academic or behavioral review. Sadie's name was in row seven, marked with two blue magnets indicating attendance alerts.

"We have target percentages from the department, Sadie," Henderson said, his eyes returning to his computer monitor. "Ashfield cannot afford an attendance drop below ninety-two percent this term. If inspectors come in November and see unauthorized absences clustered on Friday afternoons and Tuesday periods, it affects our pastoral grading."

He tapped a key on his keyboard. The laser printer on the low filing shelf whirred into life, spitting out a single sheet of paper with a sharp, mechanical clack.

"This is an attendance contract," Henderson said, sliding the warm sheet across the desk blotter toward her. "It requires your signature and your mother's signature by Friday morning. It states that any future absence must be certified by a doctor's note or an official letter from a hospital clinic."

Sadie looked down at the paper. Her mother worked six days a week from seven to four; taking three hours off to sit in the walk-in center in Doncaster for an attendance note meant losing forty pounds in shift pay.

"My mother is working, sir," Sadie said quietly.

In group exercises, subject routinely isolates herself at the periphery of the workspace, declining verbal interaction with peers.

"Then she signs it in the evening," Henderson replied, his tone final and flat. "Take the form, Sadie. Registration has finished."

# Chapter 6: The Chemistry of Lagging

On Tuesday lunch, Sadie returned to the boiler room.

She carried a six-inch steel ruler taken from her form room’s communal stationery box and a digital kitchen timer she had taken from her mother’s cutlery drawer that morning.

The boiler room was hotter than the day before. The external temperature outside was eleven degrees, with a driving northern drizzle that had turned the grass of the rugby pitch into brown porridge. Inside the cellar, Boiler Three’s burner was firing at full output, its green metal casing radiating a dry, choking heat that made the dust on the floor smell like baked flour.

Sadie slipped behind the lagged return pipes. She knelt by the header tank, set her rucksack on a dry cinder block, and examined the iron hatch.

The drop-latch was exactly as she had left it on Monday afternoon: unhasped, the forged iron arm resting loosely in the groove. The metal felt just as bitterly cold as before, drawing the warmth from her fingers through her woolen gloves.

She opened the door six inches and leaned her face into the gap.

The threshold was a razor line.

On the boiler room side, the air was thirty-four degrees Celsius, humid with pipe steam and thick with suspended soot motes that danced in the blue beam of the pilot flame.

One inch past the iron frame, the temperature plunged to sixteen degrees. The air was absolutely still.

She reached out with the steel ruler. She held it horizontally across the threshold, half in the cellar, half in the amber hallway. When she pulled it back after ten seconds, the cellar end was warm to the touch, while the end that had entered the corridor was covered in a thin, microscopic film of condensation that smelled faintly of carbolic soap.

She took out the digital timer. The small LCD screen showed four zeros: *00:00.*

She pressed the start button. The seconds began ticking upward: *01, 02, 03...*

She set the timer on the floor five inches inside the amber corridor, pulled her hands back across the threshold, and sat on her heels to watch.

The numbers continued to advance: *14, 15, 16...*

Time inside the duplicate school was moving forward at the same speed as the school outside. It was not frozen in a single millisecond; the digital quartz crystal was counting sixty seconds to the minute.

She took a pinch of dried coal dust from the cellar floor between her thumb and forefinger. She held her hand directly over the threshold, leaning her wrist into the amber corridor, and opened her fingers.

In the boiler room, dust fell in a drifting cloud, carried sideways by the convective draft of the hot pipes.

Inside the amber corridor, the black grains fell straight down like lead shot. There was no air movement. No thermal currents, no ventilation draft from the ceiling grilles. The black specks struck the composite floor tile with absolute precision, forming a neat, circular mound two inches wide.

She crawled through the opening and stood up.

The hallway was identical to yesterday: the same tea-colored light, the same total absence of sound. She walked to the noticeboard outside Room 11.

Yesterday, when she had looked at the board, the paper cross-country sign-up sheet had been pinned at three corners, the bottom right corner curling upward like a dry leaf.

She looked at it now. The curl had not moved. It was held in the same three-millimeter curve, perfectly rigid in the still air.

She took the steel ruler and tapped the plastic frame of the noticeboard.

The ruler struck the plastic with a faint, hollow clink that sounded as though it were being muffled through three layers of wet wool. There was no resonance. The sound did not travel down the hallway; two feet to her left, she could barely hear the tap.

She walked twenty yards toward the science block, checking the classroom doors.

Room 15 was the chemistry laboratory. Through the glass panel, she saw the benches of dark teak, the gas taps with their yellow brass nozzles, and the white ceramic sinks.

On the third bench, frozen in mid-stride between two rows of stools, stood Mr. Henderson.

He wore his white laboratory coat over his gray suit trousers. In his left hand he held an open glass beaker; in his right hand, a glass stirring rod was poised two inches above the liquid. His mouth was open, his lips shaped around a word that had stopped in his throat twenty-four hours ago.

On the blackboard behind him, written in yellow chalk with heavy strokes, was the title of Monday’s Year 8 lesson:

*ACIDS AND BASES — MONDAY 14 OCTOBER.*

Sadie stood in the silent doorway, watching him. She felt the cold linoleum beneath her thin soles.

The date on the board was Monday. Outside in the rain, it was Tuesday lunch.

The school behind the iron hatch was not a duplicate. It was yesterday.

The chemistry lab benches were made of solid English teak, dark as bitter chocolate, treated with paraffin wax to resist concentrated acids.

Sadie walked down the aisle between benches two and three, her fingers hovering an inch above the gas taps. Each tap was cast from solid yellow brass, fitted with a ribbed hose nozzle and a lever handle painted red. In the real school, three of the taps always hissed with a faint leak of coal gas that made the back row smell of rotten cabbage.

Here, there was no smell of gas. The air held only the cold, sterile tang of copper sulfate crystals and powdered sulfur.

She stopped beside Mr. Henderson's high wooden demonstration stool.

The teacher stood in his frozen stride, his white coat flared slightly at the hip as if caught by a sudden draft that had ceased twenty-four hours ago. Sadie looked closely at his spectacles. Behind the wire frames, his brown eyes were dull, completely flat, showing neither curiosity nor anger. The tiny red veins at the corners of his eyes were frozen in microscopic detail, like red ink drawn on lard.

On the demonstration bench beside his open beaker lay his pocket diary.

It was a small, black faux-leather book with a ribbon marker. The page was open to Monday 14 October. Written across the lines in Henderson's precise, cramped handwriting were his notes for the morning:

*08:45 — Form registration. Check Tyler's absence.*
*10:15 — Year 8 Science. Neutralization reactions.*
*12:30 — Lunch duty: Dining Hall Table 4.*
*14:00 — Department meeting: Key Stage 3 budget cuts.*

Sadie touched the paper. The vellum page was cold as slate, giving off no scent of ink.

She looked at the date again. Monday 14 October.

Leaving the cloakroom, Sadie walked twenty yards down the corridor to the lower library annexe.

The room smelled of dry paper, floor polish, and old binding glue. Thirty oak desks stood in neat rows, each holding a stack of returned geography textbooks. Above the librarian's counter, a brass pendulum clock hung against the pine paneling. Its eight-inch brass pendulum was frozen three inches to the left of the center line, halted in mid-swing as if welded into place by invisible solder.

Sadie picked up an uncatalogued paperback novel from the trolley. When she opened the cover, the spine cracked with a dry, stiff sound, but the paper pages felt cold and dense, resisting her thumb like thin sheets of mica. On page forty-four, a pressed yellow sycamore leaf sat between two paragraphs of text, its dried veins intact, preserved without fading or decay.

Outside Room 15, Sadie stopped by the glass trophy display case in the lower humanities wing.

Inside the cabinet, three silver-plated athletic cups from the South Yorkshire Schools League sat on green velvet pads, their handles tarnished with brown silver sulfide. Beside the cups, a black-and-white photograph showed the Ashfield Comprehensive girls' netball team of 1988, their hair teased into perms, their green cotton bibs bearing the school crest.

Sadie touched the glass pane with the tip of her forefinger. The glass was so cold that a faint circle of frost blossomed beneath her skin, disappearing the moment she withdrew her hand.

In the real school, thirty feet above her head, it was Tuesday lunch. The students in the dining hall were eating instant mash and fish fingers, shouting over the rattle of cutlery, while down here, Monday stood locked in silent amber.

# Chapter 7: The Red Pencil on the Desk

Sadie stepped across the threshold of Room 14, her hand resting against the pine doorframe to steady herself.

The classroom was suspended in amber stillness. The air had the dry, mineral taste of crushed blackboard chalk and old exercise books left in a closed cupboard over the weekend. Through the high, steel-framed windows on the north wall, the view showed the school playing fields—not under today’s driving October sleet, but bathed in Monday’s gray, overcast midday light. The bare birch trees at the boundary fence were completely still, their yellow leaves frozen in mid-sway against the leaden sky.

There were twenty-six pupils in the room.

They sat at their double desks in five rows, their postures locked in the mundane, slumping boredom of Monday period four.

In the front row, Ryan Bailey was caught halfway through a yawn, his jaw unhinged, his eyes squeezed shut into two pink folds. Beside him, Jessica Mills had her chin propped in the palm of her left hand, her mouth open in a vacant, dead stare that looked toward the whiteboard without seeing it.

Miss Finch stood at the front, her back half-turned to the class. Her white arm was raised toward the interactive whiteboard, her black digital stylus touching the glass screen three inches below a bullet point about *The Iron Man*. A loop of her gray cardigan had snagged on the corner of the wooden lectern, held taut like a tiny wire snare.

Sadie walked slowly down the center aisle.

The silence was heavier than the cellar. It pressed against her eardrums with an icy, physical weight, like diving to the bottom of the deep end at the municipal baths. None of the figures blinked. None of them drew breath. When she passed close to Ryan, she held her hand three inches from his open mouth; there was no warmth, no moisture, no faint puff of exhalation.

She stopped at the third desk from the back on the window side.

Yesterday’s Sadie sat there.

The sight made the small hairs on the back of Sadie's neck prickle with cold terror. She leaned down until her cheek was six inches from her own frozen ear.

Yesterday’s Sadie was chewing on the plastic end of a blue biro. Her brown eyes were dull, staring down at a blank page in her English notebook where she had written only the date—*Monday 14th October*—underlined with two shaky pencil lines. Her collar was slightly twisted, tucked into the neck of her bottle-green jumper on the right side.

Sadie reached out with one trembling finger. She touched the woolen shoulder of the frozen girl’s jumper.

The wool was stiff, like cloth soaked in starch and dried in a freezer. It gave under her finger with a faint, brittle resistance, but the figure beneath did not shift by a millimeter. There was no heartbeat under the ribs. It felt like touching a statue carved from compressed tallow and wool.

Sadie pulled her hand back. She turned toward the desk in front.

Chloe Rimmer sat there.

Chloe was leaning sideways toward Jessica’s desk, her lips pursed in that sharp, mocking sneer she always wore when Miss Finch looked at the board. Her blonde hair fell in perfectly straight sheets across the shoulders of her blazer, held back at the temple by a pair of glittery pink hair clips.

On the corner of Chloe's desk lay her stationery.

There was an enameled tin pencil case with a silver unicorn on the lid, two highlighters, an eraser shaped like a macaron, and a bright red mechanical pencil with a gold-plated pocket clip.

Sadie looked at the red pencil.

It was an expensive one, bought from the department store in Sheffield where Chloe's mother went on Saturdays. It took 0.5-millimeter polymer lead that clicked forward with a tiny, precise snap every time Chloe thumbed the brass button. All morning during English, Chloe had been clicking that button right beside Sadie’s ear, laughing whenever Sadie flinched.

Sadie reached down.

Her fingers closed around the barrel of the red pencil.

The plastic was cold as slate, but it lifted from the laminate surface easily. There was no resistance, no suction. When she picked it up, it left no mark on the desk.

Sadie held the pencil in both hands, her thumbs pressed together against the center of the red casing, right above the gold brand lettering.

She thought of the chocolate milk dripping into her rucksack. She thought of the soggy, ruined pages of her Holderness erosion project. She thought of the laugh in Chloe’s throat by the tray return racks.

Her jaw clamped shut until her molars ground together.

She drove her thumbs forward.

The plastic snapped with a sharp crack that echoed down the silent aisle like a pistol shot.

The red barrel fractured clean across the brass threads. The tiny steel internal spring popped out, pinging off the edge of Chloe's desk and tumbling into the aisle, while the three spare sticks of lead inside the reservoir shattered into gray powder between Sadie's fingers.

Sadie dropped the two broken halves of the casing onto Chloe’s open exercise book.

They lay across the lined paper, jagged and ruined, beside a neat sentence written in rounded handwriting: *Chapter One introduces the monster as an outsider.*

Sadie stood in the aisle, her chest heaving, the broken spring lying by the toe of her synthetic boot.

She waited for someone to scream. She waited for Miss Finch to turn round from the board, for Henderson to burst through the door with his blue clipboard, for Ryan to snap his mouth shut and point at her.

Nothing moved.

Miss Finch remained frozen with her stylus three inches from the screen. Chloe’s sneer remained locked in profile. The spring lay motionless on the gray linoleum.

Sadie wiped the gray graphite powder from her thumb onto her skirt, turned around, and walked quickly out of the room.

The enameled tin pencil case on Chloe's desk was painted with a silver unicorn leaping over a rainbow.

Sadie looked at the highlighters inside: pink, pastel yellow, mint green, and lavender, all bought from the Japanese stationery shop in the Meadowhall shopping center. Chloe had lined them up by shade, their white plastic barrels pristine, untouched by the dirt that coated ordinary school pens.

Sadie's thumbs pressed against the red mechanical pencil.

She remembered the afternoon in September when Chloe had snatched her geography exercise book from her desk and dropped it out the second-floor window into the stinging nettles behind the boiler house. Sadie had spent forty minutes after school climbing through the brambles in her tights, her legs covered in white welts that burned for three days, while Chloe and Jessica watched from the staff car park, laughing through the open window of Chloe's mother's car.

She remembered the whisper in the cloakroom: *Sadie Bell's dad ran away because he could not stand looking at her face.*

Her knuckles turned white.

The red plastic barrel groaned under the force of her thumbs.

The internal threads sheared first, a microscopic failure inside the brass ferrule, followed an instant later by the loud, explosive crack of the outer casing.

When the two pieces fell onto Chloe's exercise book, the turquoise ink of the sentence was obscured by gray graphite powder.

Sadie did not feel excitement. She felt only a cold, heavy satisfaction, like an iron bolt sliding into a greased mortise lock.

Before entering Room 14, Sadie stood in the corridor observing the student work displayed on the cork boards outside the art department. Dozens of watercolor studies of the cooling towers at Thorpe Marsh hung in rows, painted by Year 9 pupils three years ago. The paper had buckled from damp autumn air, but in this hallway, the water stains were sharp and unbleached.

Through the glass panel of Room 13 next door, Mr. Finch's lower chemistry set was frozen in mid-demonstration. A glass flask of copper sulfate sat on a cast-iron tripod, its blue crystals suspended halfway to the bottom of the water column. The blue flame of the Bunsen burner beneath the mesh was rigid, a three-inch cone of cobalt light that emitted no heat, smelling faintly of cold methane that had lost its pressure.

Sadie paused, listening to her own shallow breathing. The stillness was thick as pond water.

Sadie stood in the aisle of Room 14, looking down at Chloe Rimmer's desk.

The desktop was covered in small, carved inscriptions: initials intertwined with plus signs, compass-drawn circles, and the logo of the rock band Queen scratched into the varnish with a ballpoint tip. On Chloe's open exercise book, the title of the lesson was written in pink gel pen, the letters shaded with tiny diagonal cross-hatches: *Imagery in Ted Hughes's 'The Thought-Fox'*.

Beside the book lay the two halves of the fractured Rotring pencil, the jagged aluminum edges gleaming in the flat amber light of the strip tubes.

Sadie touched the circular eraser cap of the pencil. It was made of white rubber, completely clean, never used to correct a single mistake. Chloe never corrected her work; she simply scribbled through any errors with three sharp strokes of her pen and turned the page.

In the second row, Jessica Mills remained frozen mid-laugh, her teeth catching the light with a dull, silver gleam from her wire braces. Behind Jessica, thirty other children sat suspended in their wooden desks, their blazers unmoving, their pens poised above lined pages that would never be turned in this replica world.

Sadie felt the strange, intoxicating warmth of absolute control settle into her chest.

In the living school, Chloe was an untouchable tyrant, surrounded by sycophants and protected by her brother's reputation. Here, in the quiet amber gloom, Chloe was merely a mannequin made of cold wax, completely vulnerable to the pressure of Sadie's thumbs.

Sadie walked along the rows of wooden desks in Room 14, her rubber shoes making no sound on the green linoleum.

At Bench 3, Ryan Fletcher sat frozen with a green plastic protractor held against his cheek, his eyes locked on the corner of the blackboard. Beside him, an open notebook was covered in pencil doodles of motorcycle engines and football crests, the lead smudged along the margin where his wrist had rested.

She looked toward the high windows.

Outside, the playing field was frozen in Monday morning's gray drizzle. Along the boundary fence, a flock of five rooks hung suspended six feet above the hawthorn hedge, their black wings spread wide against the zinc clouds, motionless as iron weathercocks bolted to a roof.

She reached down and touched the edge of Chloe's desk. The polished timber felt completely cold, devoid of the living warmth that usually radiated from the furniture in a crowded classroom.

Sadie looked at the teacher's podium at the front of Room 14.

Mr. Henderson's brown corduroy jacket hung stiffly from his shoulders, the elbows patched with dark suede ovals that were worn thin along the outer seams. On the corner of his desk sat a ceramic mug bearing the logo of the National Union of Teachers, its interior stained with dark rings of stewed tea that had dried forty years ago.

Beside the mug, an open red exercise book showed an essay on William Blake's *Songs of Innocence*, marked in red biro with neat, pedantic corrections in the margin: *Explain the symbolic function of the chimney sweeper's soot.*

Sadie pulled her fingers back into her cuffs, stepping quietly between the frozen desks toward the door.

# Chapter 9: The Realization of Power

The change did not move backward. It waited.

It waited twenty-four hours, coiled inside the fabric of the building like a spring under tension, and then released itself into today’s reality at the exact minute she had enacted it.

She had snapped the pencil at ten-fifteen on Monday in the duplicate room.

The pencil had snapped at ten-fifteen on Tuesday in Chloe's hand.

She took the flattened spring from her pocket and rolled it between her thumb and forefinger.

The rules were establishing themselves in her mind, clear and immutable as the laws of chemistry:

First: the duplicate school was not a parallel universe; it was yesterday's Ashfield, operating exactly twenty-four hours in the past.

Second: whatever physical damage was inflicted inside the duplicate school did not alter the past; yesterday remained as it had been lived.

Third: the physical consequence waited for twenty-four hours to elapse, manifesting in today's real world at the exact minute and second of the original act.

She looked down at the bike sheds where Chloe Rimmer was still pacing back and forth, waving her arms, complaining to anyone who would listen.

Sadie's lips parted in a cold, silent breath.

She had endured six months of being hunted through corridors, of having her bags emptied into recycling bins, of being tripped on concrete stairs while other girls laughed.

Now she had the key to the foundation.

Sadie walked past the railway coal siding on her way home, her hands tucked deep inside her blazer pockets.

The rusty iron hopper wagons stood on the overgrown sidings, their black steel sides stenciled with faded white lettering: *NCB SOUTH YORKSHIRE AREA*. Between the rotting wooden sleepers, wild rosebay willowherb had seeded in thick, brown thickets, rattling in the cold autumn breeze.

She looked at the high brick chimney of Ashfield Comprehensive rising above the slate roofs of the terrace houses.

The causal link between yesterday and today was an invisible lever. A child with a spanner or a pair of pliers in the replica corridors could bring down the whole school, brick by brick, without anyone ever seeing her touch a single bolt in the living world.

She turned from the railing, walked down the galvanized steel fire stairs, and headed for the west entrance.

# Chapter 10: The Caretaker's Warning

# Chapter 01

Two hundred and fourteen objects waited in the reclassification queue, and eleven of them required a judgement.

Ilsa worked the top of the list first, the way the procedure said to, because the procedure had been written by someone who had never had to clear a queue before a client call. The other two hundred and three required only a signature. She signed those in batches of twenty, reading the summary line, checking the epoch against the last observation, and moving on. The clock in the catalogue bay ran four minutes fast. She had stopped correcting for it years ago.

The floor at Panamint was cold in the mornings. The air handling ran at a fixed rate because the dish motors downstream of the wall did not like a pressure swing, and the result was a draught along the floor that moved the loose corners of the printouts she still kept. Her desk had two monitors, a terminal she was not supposed to use for anything but catalogue work, and a cup of coffee that had been hot at 06:10 and was now a different substance.

Object 214 arrived at 06:38. K-7741. The summary line said *spent upper stage, breakup debris, no further action*. She opened the file.

The record was thin in a way she associated with bought data. No international designator. A K-number assigned internally in 2021 when Kestrel had purchased a block of observations from a defunct provider, and the observations had come with a note that said *provenance incomplete, do not certify*. Somebody had certified them anyway, because the catalogue was a product and a product needed rows. The object had been tracked for four years and had never been assigned a priority above the screening floor. It had a radar cross-section consistent with a booster. It had a drag response that was, on the face of the summary, consistent with a booster.

She looked at the epoch. The last observation was eleven hours old, which was normal for a low-priority object.

The reclassification form had six fields and a free-text box. She filled the first five from the record: object identifier, previous classification, new classification, authority, reason code. The authority field defaulted to her own name. The reason code had a drop-down with nine options, and the one she needed was the second: *catalogue hygiene, client request*.

She did not choose it. She chose the fourth, *routine review*, because there had been no client request that she knew of, and because the fourth option did not generate a notification.

The free-text box accepted two hundred characters. She wrote: *Booster-class RCS, drag consistent with hollow stage. No designator. Hygiene reclassification. No further action.* The counter said 148. She read it back once.

Then she signed it, because it was 06:52 and the queue would not clear itself and the object had been in the catalogue for four years without anybody dying.

The queue ticked down to 213.

Femi arrived at seven with a paper cup and a question about the covariance tool. He was twenty-six and dressed like a man who had read an article about dressing for the job you wanted. He asked whether the new version of the tool was reporting in the same frame as the old one, because he had run the same object through both and got two different numbers.

"Different to what?"

"Four decimals."

"Then it's the tool."

"It's meant to be the same tool," he said.

"Then it's the same tool and one of them is rounding differently." She turned back to her screen. He stood there long enough that she looked up again. "Femi. The queue is two hundred and thirteen. If you want to spend the morning on four decimals, spend it, but put it in a ticket so it's somebody's problem."

He went away. She heard him say something to Hal at the far end of the floor, and Hal's laugh, which was mostly a cough.

At 08:00 the daily stand-up happened in the corner with the broken projector. Priya ran it standing up, which was supposed to make it short, and which made it eleven minutes instead of twenty. She talked about the Vantage renewal. She used the word *hygiene* twice. Ilsa, who had used the same word in a form at 06:52, felt the small unpleasantness of hearing her own vocabulary in someone else's mouth.

Afterwards Priya stopped her by the coffee machine.

"You cleared the queue."

"Two hundred and thirteen."

"Good. I need you on the account review next week. Vantage wants to see how we handle a client-requested reclassification, end to end, and you're the only one who can walk them through it without reading from the slide."

"I can do that."

"Start from an object with a boring history. Not a fragment cloud. They get nervous."

Ilsa said she would. Priya went back towards her office, then turned.

"The four-decimal thing. Is it real?"

"Probably rounding."

"Make it not real by Friday," Priya said, and smiled, and was gone.

Ilsa stood by the coffee machine for a moment. Through the window at the end of the bay, the first dish was turning to its morning position, a slow mechanical patience that never failed to make her feel that the building was doing something honest. The dish was pointed at a piece of sky where nothing had happened yet. It would point there for six hours and record whatever was there, which was mostly nothing, which was the job.

She went back to her desk and pulled the approach plates out of her bag. She had bought three more at the weekend, from a dealer in Reno who did not ask why a woman in her thirties wanted instrument procedures for airports in Norway. She filed them by ICAO code. ENGM went between ENBR and ENVA. The missed approach for the second runway required a turn to a holding fix she could not have found on a map.

She put them away and opened the next queue. It had begun to fill again already, because the sky does not stop producing objects for a procedural deadline.

At 07:35 the duty officer came round with a tablet and a face that meant a client call.

"Vantage wants a walkthrough of the reclassification queue," he said. "Not the product. The queue. They've got an audit committee and the committee has questions about how objects get from the raw catalogue into the customer view."

"Who's asking?"

"A woman called Halloran. She's the committee's technical adviser. She wants to see a live queue, not a slide."

"Live queues are boring. Two hundred objects, eleven judgements, a lot of signatures."

"Boring is what they want. They've had enough excitement." He glanced at her screen. "They've asked for you specifically. Priya put your name forward."

Ilsa looked at the queue. It had refilled to two hundred and twenty-six overnight, because the fence had been recalibrated and the recalibration had produced a crop of new detections that were mostly noise.

"Fine. When?"

"Thursday. Two hours. They'll bring their own auditor."

"An auditor with a checklist."

"An auditor with a checklist and a plane to catch," the duty officer said, and went away.

She spent the next twenty minutes doing the thing she did before any client walkthrough, which was to walk the process herself as though she were a stranger. The queue sorted by priority, then by age. The priority was a number between one and five, and the number was computed from a formula that nobody in the room had written and everybody in the room trusted. The age was the time since the last observation. The top of the list was priority one, which meant a crewed asset or an asset of strategic value, and there were never more than three of those, and they were always handled by the duty officer.

The middle of the list was where the work was. Priority three and four, objects that could matter if they were something else. The queue asked for a judgement, and the judgement was a classification, and the classification was a field with nine options.

She opened the procedure for the ninth option, *unknown — pending assessment*, and read the note attached to it. The note said that the option should be used sparingly, because an unknown object generated a conjunction row for every crewed asset in its shell, and four hundred unknown objects would make the product unusable.

The note had been written in 2021. She had written it, in the first year of the contract, when the product was young and the fear was false alarms.

She read her own note twice. Then she closed it and went back to the queue, and worked the next object, which was a piece of a Chinese upper stage that had been in the catalogue for nine years and had been classified as debris since the day it was detected, and which was, she knew from the drag response, exactly what the catalogue said it was.

At 08:05 the client liaison sent an email confirming Thursday, with an agenda attached. The agenda had four items and a request for a demonstration of a reclassification from start to finish.

Ilsa replied that she could demonstrate the workflow but not the specific object, because a live demonstration would require a live case, and a live case was not something you could schedule.

The liaison replied that the committee would accept a historical example.

She looked at the queue. There were eleven objects requiring a judgement, and one of them had a mass anomaly she had not yet looked at, and the anomaly was sitting in a field the summary did not display.

She opened a new email and typed: *Thursday is fine. I'll walk the committee through the 2024 hygiene review and the subsequent correction.* Then she deleted the sentence, because the 2024 review was not a demonstration she could give to the client who had commissioned it.

She typed: *Thursday is fine. I'll walk the committee through the standard workflow and a historical case from 2021.*

The queue was a screen and the screen was a list, and the list was the product's whole argument.

Ilsa had learned to read it the way a pilot reads an approach plate: not the numbers first, but the shape. Two hundred and twenty-six rows, sorted by priority then age. Eleven rows with a judgement pending. Forty-one rows with a covariance that had grown since the last epoch, which meant the object had not been observed recently, which meant a dish had been tasked elsewhere.

The covariance column was the one that mattered and the one nobody watched. A small covariance meant a good orbit and a confident classification. A large covariance meant the object might be somewhere else, and an object that might be somewhere else was an object that could be anything.

She filtered to large covariance. Nine rows. She opened the first.

The first was a spent stage from a launch four years ago, with a covariance that had grown because the stage had been tumbling and the tumbling changed its drag. The classification was right and the covariance was honest, and the row was a good row.

The second was a fragment from a breakup in 2019. Same story.

The third was K-7741.

She sat with it for a moment. The covariance was large because the object had last been observed eleven days ago, and the observation gap was explained in the log by a tasking conflict, and the tasking conflict was explained by a note that said *dish six reassigned, client request*.

She looked at the note for a while. A client request was a normal thing. Clients requested observations all the time; the requests went into a queue and the queue was worked in priority order, and a client request could move a row up or down that queue.

She opened the tasking history for the eleven days. The history was a list of every time the object had been scheduled and every time it had been displaced. It had been scheduled four times and displaced four times.

The displacements had three reasons between them: *higher priority object*, *dish maintenance*, and *client request*.

The client request was the one that had happened twice.

She wrote the pattern in her notebook: *object scheduled 4, displaced 4, twice by client request, covariance now 4x nominal.*

Then she sat back and looked at the queue, which was a screen and a list and a product, and she thought about the fact that the product's whole argument was that every row on it had been looked at by somebody, and that the looking was the thing the client paid for, and that a row displaced four times had been looked at by nobody at all.

At 08:50 the duty officer came back with the tablet.

"Vantage confirmed Thursday. Two hours. They want the walkthrough in the catalogue bay, not the conference room."

"Fine."

"They also want to see a live reclassification. I told them we'd do a historical one."

"You told them right."

He looked at her screen. The K-7741 row was open, and the anomaly was in the field below the fold.

"That's the one Priya's been asking about," he said.

"Has she?"

"She asked me on Monday whether the object was in the queue. I said it was. She said to keep it in the queue." He shrugged. "That's all she said."

Ilsa looked at the row. Keep it in the queue. The phrase could mean anything: keep working it, keep it visible, keep it out of the summary.

She wrote the phrase down, because a sentence from a manager is a fact about the week, and the week was going to need facts.

At 09:10 she pulled K-7741's raw returns, because she had written *drag consistent with hollow stage* in a box, and because she had learned, eight years ago, that *consistent with* was a phrase that could be true and still be the last thing anybody read.
# Chapter 02

The drag coefficient was the first thing that did not fit, and it did not fit by enough to be interesting.

A spent upper stage is a cylinder with a lot of empty volume and a large area facing the direction of travel. When the atmosphere at four hundred kilometres thins and thickens with the solar cycle, the stage responds to it like a sail. Its orbit decays in a curve. The curve is not smooth, because the sun is not smooth, but over four years it has a shape, and the shape is a signature. K-7741's curve was too flat.

Ilsa pulled the raw radar returns for the last two hundred days. The station kept them for eighteen months, then archived them to a tape library that nobody had migrated since the cloud contract went to review. The returns came back as a table: time, range, range-rate, angle, signal strength. She sorted by time and began to compute.

The drag response of an object is a function of its area-to-mass ratio. The area can be estimated from the radar cross-section if you accept a large error bar and a shape assumption. The mass cannot be measured at all. It can only be inferred, and the inference is the part of the trade that separates an analyst from a form-filler. A hollow booster of that cross-section should have an area-to-mass ratio in a certain range. K-7741's was a third of the bottom of that range.

She checked her arithmetic twice. Then she checked the calibration of the radar that had produced the returns, because a gain error would produce exactly this result, and a gain error was the boring explanation, and boring explanations were usually right.

The calibration log for that dish showed nothing unusual. The gain had drifted by 0.2 decibels over the period, which was inside tolerance and had been corrected in software. The returns were good.

She leaned back. The floor was emptying for lunch. Hal was still at the far end, eating a sandwich over his keyboard with the care of a man who had dropped one there before.

"Hal."

He looked up.

"Does a mass anomaly ring anything for you? An object in a booster-class catalogue entry with an A-over-m a third of what it should be."

"Rings what?"

"That it's not a booster."

Hal chewed. "Or it's a booster with something in it."

"That's the same thing."

"No it isn't." He wiped his fingers on a napkin that had come from a drawer. "A booster that's been used as a booster is empty. A booster that's been used as a shipping container is not. You'd get a higher mass, not a lower one."

"Lower means lighter. Lighter than hollow."

"Then it's not a cylinder," Hal said. "Or your cross-section's wrong. What's it tumbling at?"

She did not know. The returns were range and range-rate; a tumble showed up as a modulation in the signal strength, and she had not looked at signal strength, because signal strength was not a field the form asked for.

She looked at it now. The returns had a periodicity she had filtered out as noise: a rise and fall every forty-one seconds, clean enough to be a rotation, dirty enough to be a body with an uneven shape. A booster tumbling at forty-one seconds was possible. A booster tumbling at forty-one seconds for four years, without the tumble decaying, was not.

She wrote the number down. Then she opened the object's history and looked for every field the record had, including the ones the summary did not show.

There was a field called *mass estimate, kg*, and it was populated. Nine hundred and twelve. She stared at it for a while. The summary line had not shown it because the summary line showed six fields, and mass estimate was the seventh. Nine hundred and twelve kilograms for an object with a booster's cross-section and a third of a booster's drag response.

She pulled the object's launch history. There was none. Not *unclear*, not *provisional*: the field was empty, and the record had been migrated twice, and both migrations had preserved the emptiness as a deliberate null rather than filling it with an unknown.

She wrote in the notes field: *mass anomaly, unresolved. A/m inconsistent with hollow stage. 912 kg estimate. Tumble 41 s, undamped.*

The form did not have a code for that. It had nine reason codes for reclassification and none of them meant *this object is not what the catalogue says it is*. She saved the note as a draft and left the reclassification as it was, finished, signed, closed.

At 13:20 she went to find Priya and found the office empty, with the paper diary open on the desk to a page with two entries in the same handwriting: *Vantage review — Thursday* and *Ilsa — reclass process*.

She stood in the doorway for a moment. Then she went back to her desk and opened the raw returns again, and this time she did the thing she had been avoiding since 09:10, which was to fit an orbit instead of trusting the one in the catalogue.

The catalogue orbit had an apogee of 412 kilometres and a perigee of 398. It had been fitted six weeks ago. The raw returns from the last two weeks put the apogee at 404 and falling. The perigee was holding. That was the shape of a circularising manoeuvre, or of a body that had been given a push.

She wrote the new elements by hand on the whiteboard she was not supposed to use, because the whiteboard was for the stand-up and the stand-up was for the account.

Hal came back from lunch at 12:50 with a paper bag and the specific air of a man who had been thinking about something while eating.

"You said a third of the expected A-over-m," he said.

"Give or take."

"Give or take what?"

"Twenty per cent, depending on the shape assumption."

Hal sat down on the corner of her desk, which was the thing he did instead of hovering. "So the object is either heavier than a booster or smaller than one. If it's smaller, the cross-section's wrong, and if the cross-section's wrong then the fence gain is off, and if the gain is off then every object in that band has the same error."

"The gain's been checked."

"The gain's been checked by the software that applies the correction." He said it without emphasis. "I'm not saying it's wrong. I'm saying the check is the same instrument. You want a second opinion, you get it from a different dish."

She looked at the clock. It was 12:55 and the afternoon shift was coming in, and the dish farm's schedule had a gap at 14:10 while dish seven was off for a receiver swap.

"Dish seven's down for maintenance."

"Dish seven's down for a receiver swap, which takes forty minutes, and the swap is done by a contractor who owes me a favour." He stood. "I'll ask him to point it at your object for one pass before he pulls the receiver. If the gain's the same, the object's real. If it isn't, you've got a maintenance problem and I've got a contractor who's going to be very apologetic."

She wrote the object's elements on a sticky note and gave it to him. He folded it into his shirt pocket without looking at it.

At 14:20 he called her extension.

"Dish seven saw it," he said. "Signal strength's within half a decibel of dish six. Gain's fine. Your object's real."

"And the mass?"

"And the mass is whatever the drag says it is, which is nine hundred and something, which is not a booster." He paused. "There's one more thing. Dish seven's got a different filter chain, and it sees a modulation dish six doesn't. Your object's flashing. Not like a tumbler. Like a beacon."

"A beacon."

"Once every forty-one seconds, two milliseconds wide." He said it slowly. "That's a strobe, or a corner reflector catching the sun, or a piece of polished glass. It's not a booster."

She sat with the phone against her ear and looked at the mass anomaly on her screen, and the field that had no code.

"Hal. Could it be a calibration target?"

"It could be anything," he said. "That's the problem with a real object. It doesn't come with a label."

At 15:10 Priya's assistant emailed the whole group with the Vantage review agenda. Ilsa read it standing up, and then she went back to her desk and opened the object's provenance record, and looked at the note that said *seller declined to provide a launch record*, and thought about the phrase *declined to provide* and what it implied about the conversation in which somebody had asked.

Then she sat down and looked at the ellipse for a long time.
# Chapter 03

She fitted the orbit at 11:20 the next morning and did not believe it until 11:52.

The fit itself was ordinary work. You take the returns, you weight them by the quality of the angle and the range, you solve for the state vector, you propagate. The software did the solving. What the software could not do was decide which returns to trust, and Ilsa spent the first half hour throwing out three that had come from a pass with a poor geometric dilution of precision. What was left produced a solution with a covariance ellipse she could draw.

She drew it on the whiteboard, because the whiteboard was the only surface in the bay large enough to hold the shell.

The ellipse was long and thin and tilted. It intersected the altitude band occupied by Meridian Station at two points, one on the ascending leg and one on the descending leg. The station's own covariance, published in the conjunction summary she could see as a customer of her own company, was a much smaller ellipse, and it sat inside the band like a stone in a river.

The two ellipses did not touch. They did not have to. The conjunction assessment did not use the drawn ellipses; it used the probability that the two objects would occupy the same volume at the same time, integrated over the uncertainty, and the number that came out of that integration was a function of time. At the current epoch it was small. At the epoch plus fifty-eight hours it was not small.

Fifty-eight hours. She checked the propagation twice. The fragment's perigee was falling, and the station's orbit was precessing, and the two motions were going to bring them into the same place at the same time with a probability that would cross the company's own action threshold somewhere in the next two and a half days.

She stood back from the whiteboard and looked at the numbers she had written in the corner, the ones she had not shown anyone. *A/m 0.011. m 912. Δv history: 3 manoeuvres, last 9 days ago.* A dead booster did not have a manoeuvre history. The record said it did not have one. The data said it did.

The whiteboard also had, in the bottom left corner, a date from 2018 in her own handwriting, which she had not wiped because the cleaner was instructed not to touch the board and nobody else used it.

2018 had been a spent upper stage with a bad area-to-mass ratio and a reclassification that said *breakup debris, no further action*. She had signed it on a Friday at 17:40, nine minutes before a deadline, on the basis of a cross-section and a drag response that were consistent with a booster, because they were a booster. The object had been a booster. It had also been a booster that was going to shed a panel, which nobody could have known from a cross-section, and which the analysis had not predicted because the analysis had not been asked to predict it.

The panel had come off six weeks later and crossed the orbit of a station with four people aboard. It had missed the pressurised volume by eleven metres. It had taken a solar array with it on the way past.

Nobody died. The program that owned the array was cancelled the following year for reasons that had nothing to do with the panel, which did not stop the panel from being the thing she thought about at three in the morning.

She wiped the date off the board with the side of her hand.

The action threshold was a number in a document. The document said that any conjunction with a probability above one in ten thousand within seventy-two hours was to be raised as a watch item, and any above one in a thousand was to be raised as a warning, and a warning generated a manoeuvre recommendation for the station operator. The station operator was a customer. The customer paid for the warning, which meant the warning was a product, which meant that raising one was not an entirely internal act.

She ran the probability at epoch plus fifty-eight hours. It came back one in four hundred.

She ran it again with the covariance inflated by twenty per cent, which was the conservative thing to do when the object had a manoeuvre history the catalogue did not acknowledge. One in ninety.

She sat down. The floor was quiet. Somewhere behind her a printer produced a page and stopped.

There were two ways to raise a warning. The first was through the catalogue, which required the object's classification to support it, and K-7741 was classified as breakup debris with no further action, a classification she had signed herself, in a form, at 06:52, with a reason code.

The second was an analyst-initiated flag, which bypassed the classification and went directly to the duty officer, and which required the analyst to state, in writing, that the catalogue record was wrong.

She opened the form for the second and looked at the field labelled *basis for override*. It was a free-text box with a character limit of four hundred, and beneath it, in small type, a line that said *analyst overrides are audited monthly*.

She typed: *Object mass estimate 912 kg is inconsistent with its catalogue classification. Object exhibits undamped 41 s tumble and a three-event manoeuvre history not present in the record. Propagated conjunction with Meridian Station at epoch +58h, Pc 1:400 nominal, 1:90 inflated. Request watch item and independent fit.*

She read it back. Then she saved it as a draft, because the duty officer was not on shift until 14:00, and because she wanted to look at the orbit one more time before she put her name on a document that said the catalogue was wrong.

At 13:10, Priya sent an email to the whole catalogue group. The subject line was *Vantage account review — agenda*. The first item was *reclassification workflow, end to end*.

Ilsa read it twice. Then she went back to the draft and added a sentence to the end of the basis field: *Recommend the object be re-fitted from raw returns before the review; current elements are six weeks old.*

She had been in the room for the 2018 inquiry, and the room had been smaller than she expected.

It was a conference room on the second floor with a table that seated eight and eleven chairs around it, and the extra chairs had been brought in from the canteen. The inquiry was internal, which meant it was a conversation with minutes, and the minutes were taken by a woman from legal who typed faster than anybody spoke.

Ilsa had been asked to explain the reclassification. She had brought the file and the cross-section analysis and the drag response, and she had put them on the table in the order she had used them, and she had walked the room through nine minutes of work that had been done at 17:40 on a Friday.

The chair of the inquiry was a man from the parent company who had flown in for the day. He had asked three questions.

The first was whether she had followed the procedure. She had.

The second was whether the procedure required a second signature for a reclassification of that class. It did not. The class was below the threshold for a second signature.

The third was whether she had considered the object's history. She had considered the four years of observations in the file, and the history was consistent with a spent stage, and she had said so.

The chair had thanked her and moved to the next item, and the inquiry had concluded eleven days later with a finding that the classification had been made in accordance with procedure and that the procedure should be reviewed. The review had produced a new threshold for second signatures, which had been implemented six months later, and which did not apply retrospectively.

The panel that came off the station's array had been six weeks after the reclassification. The inquiry had been three months after that, which meant the review of the procedure had happened after the consequence, which was the usual order.

What she remembered about the room was not the questions. It was the water pitcher. It was a glass pitcher with a metal lid that did not seal, and every time somebody poured, a little water ran down the outside and made a ring on the table, and the ring had been there for the whole two hours, and nobody had wiped it.

She had looked at the ring while the chair asked his questions, and she had thought about how the water had got there, and how it would dry, and how the table would be wiped at the end of the day by somebody who had not been in the room.

The whiteboard in the catalogue bay still had the 2018 date in the corner, in her handwriting, because she had written it there during the inquiry prep and had never wiped it. It was a reminder of a thing she did not need reminding about, and she had kept it for eight years, and she had never told anybody why.

She wiped it off with the side of her hand at 11:58, and the marker came away in a grey smear, and she looked at the smear for a moment before she found the solvent.

She saved it again. The character count was 392.
# Chapter 04

There were four registries that mattered, and Ilsa had access to three of them.

The first was the United Nations launch register, which was a database maintained by an office in Vienna and populated by states on a voluntary basis, with a lag that could be measured in months and an honesty that could be measured in nothing at all. The second was the national notification system of the launching state, which in practice meant the United States, Russia, China, India, Japan and a handful of others, each with its own definition of what constituted a launch requiring notification. The third was the insurers' launch register, which was commercial and reliable and covered only payloads that somebody had insured.

The fourth was Kestrel's own purchase log, which recorded the provenance of every observation block the company had ever bought, and which Ilsa could not open because it was part of the contract file.

She started with the insurers, because insurers do not insure things they have not been told about. K-7741's epoch put its launch eighteen months ago, in a window of nine days. She pulled every launch insured in that window and compared the orbital planes. Forty-one launches. None matched. A launch into an unlogged plane was not impossible; it was expensive, and it was the kind of thing that showed up in the reinsurance market as an unexplained loss three years later.

She moved to the UN register. Two hundred and six notifications in the window, most of them for satellites, a few for cargo. The planes did not match. She widened the window to thirty days. Still nothing. She widened it to ninety and found a launch from a sea platform, notified by a flag-of-convenience state, carrying a communications test payload. The plane was close but not the same, and the mass was wrong, and the notification had been filed eleven days after the launch, which was late even by the standards of the register.

She wrote the notification number on a sticky note and put it on the edge of her monitor, where she kept the things she did not yet know how to use.

Then she went to find Hal.

The radar room was two doors down and four degrees colder. Hal kept it that way because the equipment ran hot and because he liked to wear a cardigan to work without comment. He was at the console with his back to the door, watching a track that was doing nothing.

"K-7741," she said.

"I saw the note."

"What did you see eighteen months ago?"

He did not turn around. The track on his screen crawled a centimetre and stopped.

"Who told you eighteen months?"

"The epoch. Give or take."

"So you did the arithmetic." He turned. His face was the face of a man deciding whether to be tired. "There was a track. February, the year before last. Nineteen-something degrees elevation, coming up out of the south-west, not in the tasking list. I asked the duty officer and he said it was a calibration target for the new fence."

"Was it?"

"It was a real object. Calibration targets are real objects; that's the point of them. They put a corner reflector on a spent stage and track it to check the gain. This one didn't have a corner reflector. It had a manoeuvre."

"You saw a manoeuvre."

"I saw a track that came back three nights running with a different period. That's a manoeuvre or a bad fit. I asked, and I was told to log it as a calibration target and not to task it again."

Ilsa sat down on the edge of the desk, which was not allowed, and Hal did not tell her not to.

"Did you log it?"

"I logged it as a calibration target." He reached into the drawer under the console and took out a notebook. It was a pocket notebook with a cardboard cover, the kind sold in hardware shops.

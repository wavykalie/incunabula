# Chapter 05

Priya's office had a window that looked at the dish farm and a chair for visitors that was lower than her own, which Ilsa had always assumed was accidental until she sat in it.

"Close the door," Priya said.

Ilsa closed it. The air handling was quieter in here. On the desk, the paper diary lay open beside a laptop showing the same week in a calendar application, two records of one life.

"I've had a note from the duty officer," Priya said. "He says you have a draft override waiting."

"I have a draft."

"Show me."

Ilsa had printed it. She put the page on the desk and watched Priya read it, which took less time than it had taken to write.

"The mass estimate is nine hundred and twelve," Priya said.

"Yes."

"And the classification is breakup debris."

"Yes."

"Which you signed this morning."

"At 06:52."

Priya set the page down and squared it with the edge of the desk, which was a thing she did when she was deciding how to say something.

"Data quality is a product feature, Ilsa. That's not a euphemism, it's the business. We sell a catalogue. The catalogue has rows with confidence values attached. When a row's confidence value is wrong, we fix the row. We don't issue an override that says the catalogue is lying, because our customers buy the catalogue precisely so they don't have to hear that."

"The confidence value isn't wrong. The classification is."

"The classification is a field. The field says debris. The mass estimate is another field, and it has a confidence interval on it, and I'd bet you a week's salary that interval is wide enough to swallow nine hundred and twelve kilograms."

"It's not. I checked."

"Then check it again." Priya said it without raising her voice. "You've had this file for four hours. You found a number that surprised you, and you've built a story on top of it, and the story has a station in it because a station makes it urgent. I've watched you do this before. You did it in 2018."

The room was quiet. Ilsa looked at the diary on the desk and read the two entries upside down.

"That was a different file," she said.

"It was the same instinct. You were right in 2018, as it happens. You were right and it cost us a client and a year of my life. Being right is not free."

"I'm not asking the company to pay for it. I'm asking for an independent fit."

"Which is an override."

"Which is a procedure. The procedure exists. It's in the manual. The duty officer can commission an independent fit on an analyst's written basis."

"The duty officer can." Priya turned the page around and pushed it back across the desk. "The duty officer works for me. He'll commission your fit when I tell him to, and I'll tell him to when the file is in a shape that doesn't read like an accusation."

"What shape is that?"

"A data-quality note." Priya opened the laptop, typed for a moment, and turned the screen. "There's a form. It's called a sensor artefact report. It says the returns are inconsistent, the object needs recalibration, and the classification is provisional pending review. It doesn't say the catalogue is wrong. It says the sensor is tired."

"The sensor isn't tired."

"The sensor is always tired. That's what sensors are." Priya's voice was reasonable, which was worse than anger. "File the artefact report. I'll approve the independent fit myself, off the back of it, and you'll have your answer by tomorrow morning. If the fit says what you think it says, we'll have a conversation about the classification with the client's technical people, in a room, with the lights on. That's how this gets fixed."

"And if the fit says it tomorrow morning, and the station needs a decision tonight?"

"The station has a screening volume and a conjunction summary. The summary is generated from the catalogue. If the catalogue has a provisional object in it, the summary will include it."

"At what priority?"

Priya looked at her for a moment.

"At the priority the artefact report assigns," she said. "Which is what you write in the field."

Ilsa took the page back. The artefact form was on the screen. It had a field for the object, a field for the sensor, a field for the observed inconsistency, and a drop-down for severity with three options: low, medium, high.

"High gets a screening flag," Ilsa said.

"High gets a screening flag," Priya agreed.

"And a screening flag generates a watch item."

"A screening flag generates a watch item if the object is classified as a payload or an unknown." Priya said it gently. "It's classified as debris. Debris doesn't generate watch items, because if it did, the customers would get four hundred of them a day."

Ilsa sat with that.

"File the report," Priya said. "Severity high. I'll approve the fit. We'll have the answer in the morning, and if the answer is what you think it is, then we'll reclassify the object and the watch item will follow automatically, and nobody will have been accused of anything. That's not a cover-up, Ilsa. That's sequencing."

She picked up her pen and wrote something in the paper diary, and Ilsa saw that it was the same appointment she had already typed.

"Fine," Ilsa said. "High."

"Thank you."

Ilsa stood. At the door she turned.

"The 2018 file. You said it cost a client."

"It did."

"Which one?"

Priya looked up from the diary, and for a moment her face was not managerial at all.

On her way out she passed the duty office and Voss was in the doorway with a mug in his hand.

"Priya talked to you?"

"She did."

"Then you know the instruction's real." He stepped aside to let her pass, then followed her halfway down the corridor. "I want you to understand something about it. It wasn't written by a bad person. It was written by a woman who'd spent four months dealing with objects that turned out to be calibration targets, and who'd been asked by the client, twice, why her team kept raising alarms about nothing."

"Who was she?"

"She left in 2023. She works for an insurer now." Voss drank from the mug. "The instruction solved her problem. It probably solved the client's problem. It just didn't solve the problem of an object that's actually there."

Ilsa stopped at the stairwell door.

"Voss. If I file the override and it goes up the chain and the object turns out to be a piece of a stage, what happens to me?"

"You get a note on your file and a conversation with Priya."

"And if I don't file it and the object turns out to be real?"

Voss looked at his mug for a moment.

"Then nothing happens to you at all," he said. "That's the thing about not filing. The person who doesn't file is never wrong, because there's no document that says they were."

He went back to the duty office. Ilsa stood at the stairwell door with her hand on the bar, and then she pushed it open and went down, and the stairwell was cold and smelled of concrete, and her footsteps made a sound that the architects had not intended.

"Vantage," she said. "They were the operator of the station with the array."

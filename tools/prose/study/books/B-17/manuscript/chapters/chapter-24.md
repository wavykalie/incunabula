# Chapter 24

The first crossing came at 13:52 with fourteen minutes of warning, and the warning came from a radar fence that was not Kestrel's.

The fence was a US Space Force asset that published an unclassified conjunction bulletin for crewed objects as a public service, and it used its own catalogue, which was not commercial and which had been tracking K-7741 since the object's first orbit because the fence tracked everything above ten centimetres in low orbit regardless of what anybody's catalogue said. The bulletin had gone out at 13:38. It listed object 58991, which was K-7741's number in a catalogue that was not Kestrel's, and it gave a miss distance of 380 metres and a probability of one in forty.

Kestrel's summary, generated at 12:00, listed no tracked objects.

Ilsa saw the bulletin because she had subscribed to it on Wednesday, using her personal email, on a public mailing list, because it was the one source of truth in the entire affair that did not belong to her employer.

She called the station's ground contact at 13:41 and got a voicemail.

She called the number on the back of Grant's card at 13:42 and got Grant, who said he had seen the bulletin, and that the flight director had seen the bulletin, and that the flight director was on the phone with the operator's duty manager.

She called Priya at 13:44 and got Priya's assistant, who said Priya was in a meeting.

At 13:47 she sent an email to the duty officer, the flight director, the operator's duty manager, and Priya, with the subject line *K-7741 — public conjunction bulletin — 380 m*, and a body that contained the bulletin number, the time, the probability, and the sentence *this object is in your catalogue as debris and it is four hundred metres from your station*.

At 13:49 the duty officer replied, to all, with a single line: *received, assessing.*

At 13:50 the station's commander came onto the public voice loop, which was monitored by hobbyists and by at least one journalist, and said: "Meridian, ground. We have an unlisted object in the screening volume. Request immediate clarification of catalogue status."

At 13:51 the flight director authorised a hold-attitude, no-burn posture, because a burn at that range with an uncertain state vector was worse than no burn, and because the station's own radar could see the object now, and the object was passing.

At 13:52 the object went past.

---

On the station, Ada Reyes watched it through the cupola with her hands on the handrail.

It was not visible as an object. It was visible as a moving star, and then as nothing, and the nothing lasted longer than she expected, and then the station's structure transmitted a small tick through the handrail, which was either the object's wake, or a micrometeoroid, or a pump.

"Closest approach," Marek said from the node. "Three hundred and eighty metres. No contact indication. No pressure change."

"Hold," Ada said.

They held for four minutes. The station did what it always did, which was nothing, at speed.

"Stand down from the hold," Ada said. "June, get me the ground. Marek, walk the truss. Bex, check the array currents."

Marek went out at 14:20 and came back at 15:05 with a photograph of the port truss and a number. A panel on the second bay had a hole in it eleven millimetres across, with a crater lip and a spray of shattered silicon, and the array's current was down four per cent.

"Micrometeoroid," he said. "Or a fragment. Something small and fast. It's not the object; the object was three hundred and eighty metres away."

"Could it be a piece of the object?"

"It could be a piece of anything."

Ada looked at the photograph. The hole was a clean puncture with a bright rim, the kind of thing that happened once every three years and was always written up as a micrometeoroid because there was never any way to prove otherwise.

She keyed the microphone.

"Ground, Meridian. We have a penetrator on the port truss, second bay. Array's down four per cent. No pressure loss. Requesting a full conjunction review and the raw data for the object that just passed us."

"Copy, Meridian. Stand by."

"Ground, I've been standing by since thirteen fifty."

There was a pause on the loop, and then the flight director's voice, which was a different voice, and which said: "Meridian, we're working it."

The bulletin arrived on the station at 13:40 and June read it aloud, which was the procedure for a priority warning.

"Object five-eight-nine-nine-one. Time of closest approach thirteen fifty-two. Miss distance three hundred and eighty metres. Probability of collision one in forty. Radial uncertainty ninety metres. Action: manoeuvre recommended."

Ada was in the node with the checklist in her hand.

"Where's the object now?"

"Forty-one minutes behind us in the same plane. It's below us by two kilometres and closing."

"Which means it's been below us for a while."

"Three days," June said. "The bulletin has a history. It's been screened since Tuesday."

Ada looked at the tablet. Three days. The summary had said no tracked objects for three days, and the bulletin had said the object existed for three days, and the two statements had been in the same building, on the same screens, at the same time.

"Get the ground," she said.

The ground came up in ninety seconds, which was fast, and the voice was Phil Okonjo's.

"I've seen the bulletin," he said. "I've also seen the provider's summary. They disagree."

"They do."

"The provider's summary is our contracted source. The bulletin is a public good-samaritan product. If I act on the bulletin and the provider says the object doesn't exist, I'm acting outside my contract."

"You're acting inside your flight rules."

"The flight rules say I act on a conjunction warning. The question is which warning counts." Phil's voice was tight. "Ada, I'm not going to pretend this is a technical problem. It's a liability problem. If I burn and the object's a ghost, the burn is unauthorised. If I don't burn and the object's real, the station is at risk and the decision is mine."

"Then make it."

"I'm making it. I'm filing for a waiver on the basis of the bulletin, and I'm telling the insurer that the provider's product and the bulletin disagree, and I'm asking them for a decision within the hour."

"That's an hour we may not have."

"That's the hour we have."

Ada looked at the window. The earth was below, and the terminator was a line of light, and somewhere below and behind the station there was an object moving at seven and a half kilometres a second in the same plane.

"Phil. I'm going to configure the station for a burn."

"That's my call."

"It's my crew." She said it quietly. "You can file your waiver. I'm going to have the vehicle ready to fly, and if the waiver doesn't come, I'm going to ask you to give me the authority anyway, and if you don't, I'm going to do it and explain it afterwards."

The line was quiet for four seconds.

"Configure the station," Phil said. "I'll have the waiver in forty minutes."

---

The burn was configured at 13:58, six minutes after the closest approach, which meant the manoeuvre happened after the object had already passed.

The station's track crossed the object's track at 13:52. The miss distance was three hundred and eighty metres, which at orbital velocity was eleven milliseconds. The crew did not feel anything, because there was nothing to feel: the object went past below the truss at a relative speed of eleven kilometres a second, and the closest approach was within the noise of the tracking.

The burn at 13:58 was for the next crossing, which was nine days away, and which the bulletin had not mentioned because the bulletin only covered the next forty-eight hours.

Ada released the key. She floated in the node with the photograph on the tablet and the station turning around her, and she thought about the fourteen minutes, and about the fact that the warning had come from a public bulletin rather than from the company that was paid to provide one.

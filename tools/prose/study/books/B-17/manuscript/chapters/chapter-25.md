# Chapter 25

The platform was registered in a jurisdiction whose maritime registry consisted of a post office box and a fee schedule, and the company that owned it had one director.

Ilsa found the registration through the launch notification, which listed the platform's name and the state that had notified the launch. The state's registry was online, searchable, and updated quarterly. The platform was called *Aster Minor*. It had been registered eleven years ago, re-registered twice, and its registered owner was a company called Pellow Maritime Holdings.

Pellow had one director, a woman named Marta Pellow, and an address that was a mail drop in the same city as the registry.

Ilsa searched for Marta Pellow. She found a LinkedIn profile with no photograph and a job history that listed three companies, all of them with the same registered address, all of them dissolved. She found a corporate filing that listed the directors of one of the dissolved companies, and among them was a name she recognised from Vantage's board page: an assistant to a board member, who had left Vantage two years ago.

She sat back. It was not proof. It was a name on a filing, and names on filings are how money moves, and money moving is not a crime.

She wrote it down anyway: *Aster Minor — Pellow Maritime — director's co-filer was Vantage board assistant (left 2023).*

Then she opened the launch notification again and read the vehicle description. *Small lift, solid.* A solid rocket motor launched from a platform at sea. There were perhaps six companies in the world that did that commercially, and three of them had launch records that were public.

She checked all three. None had a launch in the window that matched the plane.

A solid motor launched from a platform was a specific capability. It was also a capability that left a specific signature: a plume of aluminium oxide particles in the upper atmosphere, which showed up in the climate-monitoring data as a transient aerosol layer, which was published by a scientific consortium every month.

She downloaded the aerosol data for the month and looked at the layer over the Indian Ocean.

There was a transient at the right latitude, three days after the notification date, with an amplitude consistent with a small solid motor. The consortium had flagged it as *unidentified, possibly volcanic*.

She photographed the screen. Then she went to find Femi.

He was at his desk with the water bottle and a spreadsheet of variable names, and he looked up as she approached and closed the spreadsheet, which meant he had been doing something he did not want her to see.

"The platform," she said. "I've got a registration and an aerosol signature. I need you to check the insurer's launch register against the notification date and tell me if any insured payload went up within a week of it."

He did not ask why. He opened the register and searched, and it took four minutes, and he found nothing.

"Nothing," he said. "Not within a week, not within a month."

"Then nobody insured it."

"Nobody insured it, or the insurer doesn't know it happened." He looked at her. "Which is the same thing from our side, except for the part where an uninsured launch is a launch somebody didn't want on a piece of paper."

She nodded. The catalogue floor was loud with the afternoon shift, and somewhere a phone was ringing with the specific persistence of a call nobody wanted to answer.

"Femi. The second object. The one with no catalogue number."

"I saw the folder."

"If it's a companion, it went up on the same launch. That means two objects, one insured, one not, or neither insured, or one of them is the payload and the other is the thing that flies the payload."

"Or the second one is the part that comes back," Femi said.

She looked at him.

"Every test has a part that comes back," he said. "You launch a thing, you test it, you bring it down. If you don't want anybody to see the test, you bring it down somewhere nobody's looking."

"Or you leave it up and call it debris."

The registry clerk answered on the second ring and had the tone of a person who had answered the same question a thousand times.

"Pellow Maritime Holdings. Yes. It's on the register."

"Can you tell me the vessel's particulars?"

"Name, flag, tonnage, registered owner. That's the public record."

"Does the public record include the vessel's position?"

"It includes the registered port. The position is not part of the register."

"Does the register record a change of ownership?"

"It records a change when the owner files it. Owners file within thirty days. Some owners file within three hundred."

Ilsa asked for the particulars and wrote them down. The vessel was registered as a general cargo ship, fourteen years old, nine hundred gross tonnes, flagged to the same state as the platform. The registered port was a city with a container terminal and a shipyard.

"Has the vessel been inspected?"

"Port state control inspections are a different register. The maritime authority keeps them, and they're public, but they're not this desk."

"Can you transfer me?"

"I can give you the number."

She took the number and called it, and the inspection register was an online database with a search field, and the vessel had been inspected twice in five years, both times in the same port, both times with three deficiencies and no detentions.

The deficiencies were: fire main pressure, lifeboat davit, and charts not corrected.

She wrote them down. A cargo ship with a bad fire main and an uncorrected chart was a ship that was not being maintained for cargo.

---

The AIS record was the thing that made the shape clear.

AIS was the automatic identification system that every ship above a certain size was required to carry: a transponder that broadcast the vessel's identity, position, course, and speed. The broadcasts were collected by shore stations and satellites and were, in principle, public.

The vessel's AIS history had a gap.

The gap was eleven days long and it began four days before the launch window and ended three days after. In the gap, the vessel's last broadcast was at the edge of the coverage area, and the next broadcast was four hundred nautical miles away, and the vessel's reported position on either side of the gap was consistent with a track that went through the launch point.

The gap was not proof. Ships lost AIS coverage in the open ocean, and a vessel that turned its transponder off was committing an offence in most jurisdictions but not in all of them, and the state of registry was one of the ones where the offence required a prosecutor who cared.

But the gap was the same shape as the launch notification's delay. Eleven days of silence, eleven days of delay, and a launch that had happened in the silence.

Ilsa wrote on the page: *AIS gap 11 days. Launch inside gap. Notification filed after gap closed. The silence is the paperwork.*

Then she looked at the vessel's particulars again. Nine hundred gross tonnes. A general cargo ship that size could carry a launch container on deck, and a launch container could carry a rocket, and a rocket could put nine hundred kilograms into a sixty-one degree orbit if the rocket was small and the payload was patient.

She sat back. The chain now had a hull in it, and the hull had a name, and the name had a director, and the director had an address that was a mail drop.

At 18:40 she searched the director's name in the corporate registry of the state, and found four companies, all with the same address, all registered in the same year, all with the same director.

Four shells. One hull. One launch.

The vessel's crew list was not public, but the vessel's port calls were, and the port calls told a story that the registration did not.

Ilsa pulled the port call record from the maritime authority's database. The vessel had called at four ports in five years. Three were in the same region, one was a shipyard. The intervals between the calls were long, and the cargo manifests were generic: *project cargo*, *machinery*, *steel*.

Then she pulled the vessel's draught. A cargo ship's draught told you how loaded it was. The vessel's draught on arrival at the shipyard had been 4.1 metres, and on departure 5.6.

One and a half metres of draught was about nine hundred tonnes of cargo.

She sat with that. Nine hundred tonnes was a lot of project cargo, and a launch container with a rocket and a payload was perhaps forty tonnes, and forty tonnes did not change a draught by a metre and a half.

So the vessel had loaded something heavy at the shipyard, and the something was not the rocket.

She looked at the shipyard's public records. The shipyard built barges and repaired fishing vessels. It did not build launch platforms.

She looked at the vessel's AIS gap again. Eleven days, beginning four days before the launch window. The gap began after the shipyard departure and ended before the first port call in the region.

A vessel that loads nine hundred tonnes at a shipyard, leaves, goes dark for eleven days, launches something, and then calls at a port with a normal draught is a vessel that has delivered its cargo. The nine hundred tonnes had been left somewhere.

She wrote: *ballast? or a platform component?*

The platform. *Aster Minor* was a semi-submersible, and a semi-submersible could be assembled at sea from modules, and a module was heavy, and a module could be carried as deck cargo by a ship with a crane.

She searched the shipyard's records for the vessel's departure and found a note in a local newspaper archive: *Pellow vessel departs with deck cargo for an offshore project.*

The photograph with the article showed the vessel's deck. The deck had a rectangular structure on it that was taller than the wheelhouse, and the structure had a crane on top, and the crane was a launch crane.

She looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then she saved it, and put it in the folder, and wrote on the printout: *the platform was assembled at sea. The ship carried the module. The module had a crane. The crane is the launch capability.*

At 19:20 she called Femi and read him the draught numbers, and he was quiet for a moment.

"You're saying they built the platform at sea."

"I'm saying the module was carried to sea and assembled there, and the assembly explains the AIS gap, and the gap explains why no port ever saw a launch."

"That's a supply chain."

"That's a supply chain with one ship, one shipyard, and one director," Ilsa said. "Which is the smallest supply chain that can put nine hundred kilograms into a sixty-one degree orbit without anybody noticing."

"Or you leave it up and call it debris," he agreed.

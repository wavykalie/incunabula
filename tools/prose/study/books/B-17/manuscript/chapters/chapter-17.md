# Chapter 17

Hal took his pension two years early on the Thursday, and the reason was a calibration log.

The log was a single page with eleven entries and a signature block, and it recorded the gain values for the fence on three nights in February, the year before last. The values were plausible. The entries were in a hand that was not Hal's, although the signature at the bottom was, and the signature had been copied from an old shift roster.

He brought it to Ilsa at 16:20 and put it on her desk.

"Look at the third entry," he said.

She looked. The third entry was the night of the track he had been told not to log. The gain value was 0.8 decibels higher than the calibration record in the system, and the difference was exactly the amount that would make a bright return look like a calibration target's corner reflector.

"It's a fudge," he said. "Somebody made the return fit the story."

"Who?"

"Doesn't matter who. It's my signature." He sat down in the chair beside her desk, which he had never done. "They want me to sign a re-issued copy. The system had a data loss, they say. The original's gone, they say. Sign the re-issued copy and the record's whole again."

"And if you don't?"

"Then I'm a radar operator who won't sign a calibration log, which is a disciplinary matter, and I'm sixty-three, and my pension is calculated on the last three years' average, and a disciplinary finding removes the discretionary element."

She looked at the page. The signature was a good copy. Not perfect — the W in Wexler had a loop that Hal's did not — but good enough for a system that did not look.

"You could refuse," she said.

"I could."

"And they'd dismiss you, and you'd fight it, and the fight would take two years, and you'd win or you wouldn't."

"That's about the size of it."

"Or you sign the copy and take the pension."

"Or I sign the copy and take the pension." He said it without any particular emphasis. "I've been at Panamint for thirty-one years. I've seen four owners. Every one of them has asked me for something before they left, and every time I've done the arithmetic and decided it wasn't worth it. This is the first time the arithmetic has come out the other way."

"What's different?"

He looked at her.

"You are," he said. "Not because you're brave. Because you're going to be here after I've gone, and if I sign the copy, then the only person who remembers what the fence actually recorded is you, and you'll have a signed page that says the fence recorded something else."

He took the notebook out of his jacket and put it on the desk. It was the same pocket notebook, and it was fuller than it had been, and there was a rubber band around it.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "Every track that mattered and a lot that didn't. The system's got eighteen months. This has got the rest."

"I can't take this."

"You can't refuse it. It's not a gift, it's evidence, and evidence doesn't belong to the person who wrote it down." He stood. "The page I gave you is in there, and the second night, and the third night, and the azimuths, and the signal strength. There's also a list at the back of every object I ever saw manoeuvre that the catalogue said was debris. It's a short list. You're on it twice."

He walked to the door. At the door he stopped.

"One thing," he said. "When they ask you who gave you the notebook, tell them I did. Don't protect me. If I'm going to be a coward about the log, I'm not going to be a coward about this."

The page had been printed on the office printer, which meant it had a job number in the footer.

Ilsa found the job number at 17:10 by opening the printer's web interface, which was a thing anybody in the building could do and nobody did. The interface kept thirty days of jobs: user, document name, pages, time.

The log for the day the calibration sheet was printed had four jobs. Three were rosters. The fourth was a document called *feb_gain_final_v3.docx* printed at 08:41 by a user account that belonged to the calibration team's shared login.

"Shared login," Hal said when she told him. "That's eleven people."

"Eleven people and a password on a sticky note."

"Twelve, if you count the contractor who cleans the keyboards."

She looked at the sheet again. The signature at the bottom was Hal's, and the signature had been copied from a shift roster from 2019, which was the year Hal had signed a roster for the last time before the system moved to badges.

"How do you know it's from the roster?"

"Because I don't sign like that anymore." He pointed at the H. "The crossbar. I broke my wrist in 2020 and the crossbar's been flat since. This one's sloped. That's the 2019 hand."

"You can see that?"

"I've been looking at my own signature for forty years." He put the page down. "Somebody wanted a calibration record that showed the fence was fine on the nights in question. They printed it on the shared login, they copied my signature off a roster, and they filed it in the calibration folder where an auditor would look."

"Who'd want that?"

"Whoever was on shift when the fence drifted." He looked at her. "The gain drifted for three nights in February. If the gain drifted, the mass estimates from those nights are wrong by up to fifteen per cent. If the mass estimates are wrong, the anomaly you found might be an artefact. And if the anomaly is an artefact, the object's a spent stage and everybody can go back to sleep."

Ilsa sat with that.

"Except the object manoeuvred," she said.

"Except the object manoeuvred." Hal nodded slowly. "Which means the anomaly isn't an artefact, and the calibration sheet is a lie about a thing that didn't matter, written by somebody who was covering a drift that turned out not to be the problem."

"Why would somebody do that?"

"Because in February the drift was the problem. In February the drift was the thing that would have got somebody written up. The object wasn't in anybody's mind in February." He tapped the page. "That's the thing about covering up a small error. You don't know what the big error is going to be."

---

He took his pension the next morning. The form was two pages and he filled it in at his desk with a pen, and he signed it with the flat crossbar, and he put it in an envelope and walked it to human resources himself.

Ilsa met him at the lift.

"You don't have to do this."

"I've got forty-one years and a notebook," Hal said. "If I stay, the sheet becomes a disciplinary matter and the notebook becomes evidence in a case about a signature. If I go, the sheet is a page in a file and the notebook is a thing an old man kept."

"That's not justice."

"No," he said. "It's arithmetic."

The lift came. He got in and pressed the button for the ground floor and looked at her through the closing doors.

"Get the object right," he said. "That's the only part of this that anybody will remember."

Then he went to the car park, and his badge did not work at the barrier, and he had to walk round, which took him four minutes in the wind, and Ilsa watched him from the third-floor window until he reached the gate.

# Chapter 31

She spent the hour between eight and nine building three models, because three was the number of payloads consistent with the data and she wanted to see them side by side.

The first was a tungsten rod. A kinetic penetrator, unpowered after release, dense enough that its area-to-mass ratio was tiny. It would tumble slowly, or not at all. It would not have a manoeuvre history, because a rod does not manoeuvre after release. It would not need a window.

She ruled it out in four minutes. The tumble period was wrong, the specular glint was wrong, and the three manoeuvres were impossible.

The second was a grappling vehicle. A small bus with a manoeuvring kit and an inspection payload, designed to approach a satellite, look at it, and either service it or take it apart. It would have a body, a panel, a window if it carried an optical sensor, and a manoeuvre history. It would be nine hundred kilograms if it carried enough propellant to do the approach three times.

The third was a dummy mass. A test article, released from a platform, with a manoeuvring kit and a beacon, designed to validate a delivery system without delivering anything. It would have the same physical signature as the second, and it would explain the second object: a target for the test.

She built the models in the spreadsheet, with the observed values in one column and the predicted values in three.

Tumble period. The rod predicted forty-one seconds only if it had been released with exactly that rotation. The grappler predicted forty-one seconds if its solar panel was asymmetric. The dummy predicted anything.

Specular glint. The rod predicted none. The grappler predicted a flat optical window. The dummy predicted a corner reflector, which would be brighter and more regular than what she had seen.

Mass. The rod predicted eight hundred to a thousand kilograms. The grappler predicted seven hundred to a thousand. The dummy predicted four hundred to six hundred.

The observed mass was nine hundred and twelve, with an uncertainty of a hundred and forty. All three fitted.

Delta-v history. The rod predicted zero manoeuvres. The grappler predicted up to five. The dummy predicted up to three.

The observed history was three.

The tumble ruled out the rod. The delta-v ruled out the dummy, because a test article that manoeuvres three times is not testing a delivery, it is testing a vehicle.

That left the grappler. A nine-hundred-kilogram vehicle with a manoeuvring kit, an optical sensor, and a window, in an orbit that crossed a crewed station at a time of its choosing.

She sat back and looked at the spreadsheet. The conclusion was not proof; it was the shape of the evidence, and the shape pointed at a machine built to approach something.

The question that followed was the one she had been avoiding all week, and she made herself write it down: *if it is a grappler, what is it for?*

Three answers. A servicing vehicle, legitimate, unlogged because the launch was unlogged. An inspector, semi-legitimate, unlogged because the client did not want the inspection known. A weapon, illegitimate, unlogged because the launch was the weapon's first act.

The first answer required the launch to be a secret for commercial reasons. The second required a client with something to hide. The third required a client with something to hide and a target.

She looked at the station's orbit on the other monitor. Meridian was a commercial platform, leased to three tenants, insured by a company that had just reopened a case. Its destruction would cost the operator a station, the tenants their experiments, the insurer a payout, and the six crew their lives.

It would also remove a witness. Meridian's sensors recorded everything in its shell, including objects that were not in anybody's catalogue. A station that had been watching an unlisted object for four days was a station that could testify.

She wrote that down too, and then she sat in the kitchen with her hands flat on the table, because the third answer had just become the most likely one.

At 20:10 her phone rang. It was Priya.

"The client has responded," Priya said. "They've instructed us to produce the raw data. All of it. They've also instructed us to reclassify the object as an unknown payload, effective immediately, and to issue a warning."

"Why?"

"Because the insurer's request put them in a position where withholding would be visible," Priya said. "And because somebody at Vantage has finally done the arithmetic on what a station costs."

She built a fourth model at 21:40, after the third one and before she admitted to herself what she was doing.

The fourth model was not a payload but a sequence.

She wrote it as a timeline with the observed events in one column and the modelled events in another:

Day minus 547: launch from a sea platform, unlogged.
Day minus 541: notification filed, eleven days late, payload mass understated.
Day minus 400 to day minus 30: no manoeuvres. Object coasts, tumbles, is catalogued as debris.
Day minus 9: manoeuvre one, 0.35 metres per second, phasing.
Day minus 4: manoeuvre two, 0.4 metres per second, alignment.
Day minus 1: manoeuvre three, 0.5 metres per second, approach geometry.
Day zero: crossing, 380 metres, and then a manoeuvre to a holding orbit.

The sequence had a property that none of the models had. It had a rehearsal.

The first manoeuvre had put the object into a phasing that would bring it to the station's shell. The second had aligned the crossing. The third had set the approach. Each of the three had been small, and each of the three had been consistent with a vehicle that was refining an approach it had already planned.

A debris object did not refine an approach. A servicing vehicle refined an approach to a satellite it had been contracted to service, and it filed a notification, and it did not hide in a catalogue for eighteen months.

So the fourth model was a vehicle with a purpose, and the purpose had been chosen before the launch, because the phasing that produced the crossing had begun nine days before the crossing and the launch had been eighteen months before that.

She sat with the timeline. Then she wrote the sentence she had been avoiding: *the object was launched to do this, and the thing it was launched to do required it to be invisible until the last nine days.*

She called Priya at 21:50. Priya answered on the third ring and sounded awake.

"I've built a sequence," Ilsa said. "It's the models plus the timeline. It says the object was launched for this."

"Launched for what?"

"To approach Meridian. To be invisible until it was close. To manoeuvre into a geometry where the station's own screening would miss it." She looked at the page. "And then to pass at three hundred and eighty metres and go to a holding orbit."

Priya was quiet for a while.

"You understand what you're saying."

"I'm saying the object was aimed."

"You're saying somebody aimed it." Priya's voice was careful. "Those are different sentences, and the second one is a sentence that requires a person, and a person requires a jurisdiction, and a jurisdiction requires evidence that survives a lawyer."

"The timeline survives a lawyer. Every entry has a source."

"The timeline survives. The word *aimed* doesn't." Priya breathed out. "Write it as a sequence. Don't write the word. Let the reader arrive at it and the reader will defend it, because it will be the reader's conclusion and not yours."

"That's cynical."

"That's litigation." A pause. "And Ilsa—send me the timeline before you send it anywhere else. Not for approval. So that when the client asks me whether I knew, I can say I saw it on Thursday night and I told you to file it."

She called a man called Sorensen at 22:10, because he had flown a servicing vehicle for eleven years and because he owed her a favour from a conference in 2019.

Sorensen was retired and lived in a house with a wood stove and answered his phone at ten at night without complaint.

"A grappler," he said. "You're asking me what a nine-hundred-kilogram vehicle with a window and a manoeuvring kit is for."

"I'm asking what it could be for."

"Three things. Servicing, inspection, and removal." He said the words the way a man reads a list he has written himself. "Servicing is the legitimate one. You approach a satellite, you dock or you don't, you replace a box or you refuel it. It requires a client, a contract, and a lot of paperwork. Inspection is the middle one. You approach, you look, you go away. It requires a client and less paperwork, because nothing touches."

"And removal?"

"Removal is the one nobody puts on a slide. You approach, you grab, you push." A pause. "Or you don't grab. You just get close enough that the other side has to decide whether you're going to."

"That's a threat."

"That's a presence." The stove clicked. "A grappler doesn't need to be a weapon. It needs to be able to be one. The ambiguity is the product. If you can dock with a satellite, you can also nudge it. If you can nudge it, you can move it into a decaying orbit, and you can do it slowly enough that nobody can prove it was you."

Ilsa sat with the phone against her ear.

"In eighteen months of ops," she said, "what would you do with a vehicle like that?"

"Rehearse. You'd approach your own target, or a dead satellite you'd been given, and you'd do it four or five times until the approach was routine. Then you'd do a demonstration, somewhere visible, on something that mattered, without touching it."

"Why without touching?"

"Because touching is a war and not touching is a message." Sorensen breathed out. "The best demonstration is a near miss on a crewed station. Nobody dies, everybody sees, and the object goes home. That's not a weapon test. That's a capability announcement."

She wrote it down, and then she sat in the kitchen with the phone on the table and the notebook open, and she read what she had written.

*Capability announcement.*

The phrase was worse than *weapon*, because a weapon could be intercepted and a capability could only be answered with a capability. A message could not be shot down.

---

At 22:40 she did the delta-v budget on the back of an envelope, which was the only paper left in the house that was not already in the folder.

A vehicle in a sixty-one degree orbit with a manoeuvring kit. Three manoeuvres of about 0.4 metres per second. A holding orbit above the station. A disposal burn to lower the object's perigee by eighteen kilometres, which at that altitude was about 9 metres per second.

Total: 10.2 metres per second of delta-v over eighteen months, plus margin, plus the approach at the end.

A nine-hundred-kilogram vehicle with a storable propellant and a specific impulse of 220 seconds needed about 42 kilograms of propellant for that budget, plus residuals, plus a reserve.

Forty-two kilograms was a tank the size of a suitcase.

She looked at the envelope. A suitcase of propellant was enough to approach a station, miss it by three hundred and eighty metres, go to a holding orbit, and bring a nine-hundred-kilogram object down on command.

That was the number that made the whole thing real. It was not a rocket. It was not a missile. It was a machine the size of a car with a suitcase of fuel, and it had done everything the book had been arguing about with a budget smaller than a delivery van's.

The warning went out at 20:22, thirty-eight minutes before the crossing.

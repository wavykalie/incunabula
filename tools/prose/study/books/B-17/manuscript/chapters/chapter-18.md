# Chapter 18

The launch took her four hours to reconstruct, and when she had it she wished she had taken longer.

She started from the orbit, because the orbit was the only thing she trusted. A fragment with a mass of nine hundred kilograms and a manoeuvre history had to have been delivered by something. The delivery did not have to be a rocket in the ordinary sense; it could have been a hosted payload released from a larger satellite, or a stage that had been left in a transfer orbit and then nudged. But the object's plane was inclined sixty-one degrees, which was an unusual inclination for a commercial launch and a common one for a particular kind of reconnaissance orbit, and the plane had not changed in four years.

She built a table of every launch in the window that had gone to sixty-one degrees. There were six. Five had payloads that were still tracked and accounted for. The sixth was the notification she had found on the first day: a communications test payload, launched from a sea platform, notified eleven days late by a flag-of-convenience state.

She pulled the notification. It listed a launch vehicle as *small lift, solid*, a payload mass as *approximately 400 kg*, and an operator as a company with an address in a jurisdiction she had to look up. The notification had a note that said the launch had been conducted *for a commercial client under a confidentiality agreement*.

Four hundred kilograms. K-7741 was nine hundred and twelve. The difference was five hundred and twelve kilograms, which was a lot of propellant and a manoeuvring kit, or a second payload, or both.

She went back to the object's manoeuvre history. Three events, reconstructed from the change in the orbit's shape. The first, two years ago, had raised the apogee by eleven kilometres. The second, fourteen months ago, had circularised the orbit. The third, nine days ago, had lowered the perigee by nine kilometres.

Lowering the perigee by nine kilometres, on an object whose perigee was already inside the altitude band of a crewed station, was not a manoeuvre that improved the object's situation. It shortened its life. It brought it down sooner.

Unless the point was to bring it down somewhere particular.

She sat back. The catalogue floor was quiet; it was 07:40 and most of the night shift had gone home and the day shift had not arrived. The coffee machine was doing its periodic flush, which sounded like a small animal in a pipe.

She opened the station's published orbital data, which was a commercial product and which she was entitled to see because Kestrel sold it. Meridian's orbit was a 51.6-degree inclination, 408 by 415 kilometres. K-7741's new perigee was 409 kilometres. The two planes were not the same plane; they crossed at an angle of nine and a half degrees, twice per orbit.

A crossing geometry was not a collision geometry. Two objects in crossing planes passed through each other's altitude band twice per orbit, and the probability of a conjunction depended on the timing, and the timing depended on the phasing, and the phasing was the one thing the object's manoeuvre had changed.

She computed the phasing for the next four days. The third manoeuvre had moved the object's argument of perigee by a fraction of a degree, which moved the crossing point by eleven kilometres along the station's track, which brought the two crossing points into alignment on Friday night.

Nine days ago, somebody had adjusted the phasing of a nine-hundred-kilogram object so that its orbit crossed a crewed station's orbit at the same time as the station.

She looked at the numbers for a long time. Then she wrote in the notebook: *not a decay. a delivery.*

At 08:10 Femi came in and found her at the whiteboard with the two orbits drawn on it, the crossing points marked, and the phasing equation written in the corner in a hand that had gone small.

"That's not a coincidence," he said.

"No."

"Nine days ago." He looked at the board. "That's the same week the review was commissioned. The hygiene review. The one with the portal."

"It's the same week."

"Who commissions a review and moves an object in the same week?"

She put the marker down.

The launch plume was in a weather satellite image, and the image was in a public archive, and the archive had a search function that nobody used because it had been built for meteorologists.

Ilsa found it on the third attempt. The search was by date and by region, and the region was a box in the Indian Ocean, and the date was eleven days before the notification. The image was a mosaic of four passes, stitched by the weather agency's processing chain, and the plume was a smudge that ran north-east from a point in the box for two hundred kilometres.

She downloaded the mosaic and opened it in the analysis tool and measured the smudge.

The smudge was an aerosol transient: a cloud of particles that had been injected into the stratosphere by a rocket exhaust and had spread along the wind field. Weather agencies saw them regularly and mostly ignored them, because a rocket plume looked like a thin cirrus and cirrus was the background.

The transient was nine hours old in the image. The wind field at that altitude was a known quantity, and the wind field put the source six hundred kilometres west of the smudge's leading edge, which put the source at a point in the ocean that had no islands and no shipping lane.

She cross-referenced the point with the maritime registry. The platform's registered position at the time of the launch was a circle with a radius of fifty nautical miles, and the circle contained the point.

She sat back. The chain was now complete in the way that chains are complete in an audit: not proof, but a sequence in which every link was independently observed.

Launch. Plume. Platform. Registration. Notification. Object. Orbit. Mass. Manoeuvre.

Nine links. Each of them in a different system, each of them kept by a different organisation, none of them designed to be joined to the others.

She wrote the sequence on a fresh page of the notebook, with the link and the source and the time, and then she sat looking at it for a while.

The thing that struck her was not that the chain existed. It was that the chain had existed since the day of the launch, in public archives and registries and weather mosaics, and nobody had joined it, because joining it was nobody's job.

The catalogue's job was to track objects. The registry's job was to record notifications. The weather agency's job was to forecast. The insurer's job was to price risk. Each of them held a link. None of them held the chain.

She thought about the phrase *the difference between debris and a weapon*, which she had been carrying since the first day, and about Hal's notebook, and about the fact that the only place the chain existed was on a page in front of her, in her handwriting, at 21:40 on a Wednesday.

At 22:10 she photographed the page and emailed it to herself and to Femi, and then she put the notebook in her bag and the bag by the door, and she went to bed, and she did not sleep.

"Somebody who wants the review to conclude before the object arrives," she said.

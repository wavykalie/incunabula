# Chapter 28

Ada Reyes used her commander's override at 16:20 and got Priya Raghunathan on the line in ninety seconds, which was faster than the ground had answered any of her previous calls.

"Ms Raghunathan. This is Ada Reyes, commander of Meridian Station."

"I know who you are, Commander. I'm sorry we're speaking under these circumstances."

"We're speaking because your company's summary told me there was nothing in the screening volume and a public bulletin told me there was an object at three hundred and eighty metres. I want the raw conjunction data for that object. Not the summary. The data."

There was a pause on the loop. Ada could hear the ground room behind Priya: a fan, a voice, the click of a mouse.

"The raw data is commercially sensitive," Priya said.

"The object passed my station at three hundred and eighty metres and put a hole in my truss."

"I understand. I'm not disputing the pass. I'm telling you that the raw data belongs to the client contract, and that releasing it to a third party requires the client's consent."

"I'm not a third party. I'm the operator of the asset your product is supposed to protect."

"You're the operator of the asset. The client is the operator's parent company." Priya said it carefully, the way a person says a thing they have said before. "Meridian Station is owned by Vantage Launch Systems and leased to three tenants. Your contract is with Vantage. Our contract is with Vantage. The data flows to Vantage, and Vantage decides what its operators receive."

"Then Vantage can decide now. I'm asking you to call them."

"I've called them. I called them at two o'clock, when the bulletin went out."

"And?"

"And they're assessing." Priya's voice was steady. "Commander, I want to be straight with you, because you're going to find this out anyway and I'd rather you heard it from me. The object is in our catalogue as debris. The classification was made under a client instruction in 2024, and the client has been notified of the discrepancy, and the discrepancy is under review. Until the review concludes, my company's position is that the catalogue is correct as filed."

"Your catalogue is wrong."

"My catalogue is as instructed." A pause. "That's not the same as wrong, legally. It's the same as wrong, operationally. I know that. I'm not going to pretend I don't."

Ada looked at the photograph of the hole on the tablet strapped to her thigh. Eleven millimetres. A clean puncture with a bright rim.

"Who computed the conjunction?" she asked.

"The what?"

"The probability. The one in three hundred. Somebody computed it. I want their name."

"Why?"

"Because I want to talk to the person who did the arithmetic, not the person who owns the contract."

The loop was quiet for a moment. Ada heard Priya breathe.

"Ilsa Merrick," Priya said. "She's an analyst. She filed the audit. She's also the analyst who signed the reclassification in 2024, and the one who signed it again this week."

"She signed it twice?"

"Twice. The first time on a day she wasn't in the building. The second time because the file said debris and she believed the file." Priya's voice had something in it that Ada could not identify. "She's the one who found the mass anomaly. She's the reason there's a review at all. She's also, on paper, the person who classified the object as debris two days before it nearly hit you."

Ada looked at the station around her: the handrail, the hatch, the checklist Velcroed to the wall, the six names on the duty roster.

"Is she good?" Ada asked.

"She's the best analyst I have."

"Then why are you telling me her name?"

"Because you asked," Priya said. "And because in about four hours somebody is going to need a name to put in a report, and I'd rather it was the name of the person who did the work than the name of the person who signed the instruction."

The loop clicked. Priya was gone.

Ada called the crew together at 16:40, in the node, with the hatch to the lab module closed because the lab module's fan made a noise that got into the recordings.

"Here's what I know," she said. "An object passed us at three hundred and eighty metres at thirteen fifty-two. The bulletin gave us fourteen minutes. The provider's summary said there was nothing in the screening volume. The provider's analyst has told us, on the loop, that the summary is generated from a classification and the classification is wrong."

"How wrong?" Marek asked.

"Wrong enough that the object manoeuvred three times in nine days and the catalogue still calls it debris."

Sam Iyer had his arms folded. "Is there going to be another one?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"The object's in a holding orbit above us and in our plane. It can come down whenever it wants. The next natural crossing is in nine days, and we've burned to move five kilometres off that track. The burn used a third of the contingency."

"That's a decision the ground made," Marek said.

"That's a decision I made and the ground ratified forty minutes later." Ada looked at him. "I want that on the record and I want it in the log with my name on it, because if the board asks why a third of the contingency went on a one-in-forty, the answer is that the commander of the station made a call."

Nobody said anything. The station hummed. Somewhere in the lab module a pump cycled.

"Second thing," Ada said. "The water ration stays. The array is at ninety-six per cent and the penetrator's assessed as a micrometeoroid, and the assessment is probably right, and I'm not going to spend the next nine days arguing about it. The ration stays because the recycler's at eighty-one and because I'd rather be thirsty than wrong."

"That's a false choice," Katya said.

"It's a real choice and I've made it."

"Then log it as a choice."

"I'm logging it as a choice." Ada turned to June. "Third thing. From now on, every pass, you read the bulletin out loud in the node, and you read the provider's summary right after it, and if they disagree you say the word *disagree* and I come to the node."

"That's an hour a day of reading."

"It's an hour a day."

June nodded and wrote it down, and the meeting ended, and the crew went back to their tasks: Katya to the crystal, Marek to the recycler, Sam to the medical log, June to the comms bay.

Ada stayed in the node. She looked at the earth through the cupola, at the terminator, at the lights of a city she could not name coming on in the dark.

She had been a commander for two years. She had commanded through a depressurisation drill, a failed gyro, and a resupply that had arrived nine days late. She had never commanded through a thing that somebody else had aimed.

---

The second call to the ground came at 17:10 and it was not Priya. It was a man from the operator's government affairs office, and he wanted to know what the crew had been told.

"The crew have been told the facts," Ada said.

"Which facts?"

"That an object passed at three hundred and eighty metres, that the provider's summary was wrong, and that there will be a statement from the operator at eighteen hundred."

"There won't be a statement at eighteen hundred."

"Then there will be a statement from the crew."

"That's not your call."

"It's my crew." Ada said it quietly. "You can have the statement I write or the statement six people give to their families on the next video call. I'd rather it was the one I write."

Ada floated in the node with the handset against her ear and the station humming around her, and she thought about the sentence *the person who signed the instruction*, and about the fact that Priya had not said whose instruction it was.

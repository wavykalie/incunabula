# Chapter 13

Grant Iwasaki arrived at 10:00 with a leather folder and a lanyard that said VISITOR in a font that had been chosen to be friendly.

Ilsa met him in the small conference room, which had a table, four chairs, and a whiteboard with the ghost of a previous meeting on it. He put the folder on the table and aligned it with the edge, which she recognised as a habit she had.

"Ms Merrick. Thank you for making time."

"I didn't have much choice. You're the client."

"I'm the client's compliance officer, which is a different thing. The client is a company. I'm the person the company sends when it wants to be sure it has done what it said."

He sat. He was fifty-five and had the specific calm of a man who had spent twenty years being careful about paper.

"You filed a sensor artefact report," he said. "On an object called K-7741. Severity high."

"Yes."

"And an independent fit was commissioned, and it agreed with you. I've read both."

"Then you know the classification is wrong."

"I know the fit is good." He opened the folder and took out a single page, which he turned to face her. It was a form. "This is a data-quality submission. It goes through the client portal, into the hygiene queue, and it is reviewed by the technical team within five business days. It asks for the object, the observed inconsistency, the supporting data, and the submitter's assessment. It doesn't ask for a reclassification. It asks for a review."

"Five business days is a week."

"It is."

"The conjunction is in forty-eight hours."

"I'm aware of the conjunction." He said it evenly. "I'm also aware that the conjunction is computed from a fit that the catalogue does not accept, which means that from the client's perspective there is no conjunction, there is an analyst's concern. The portal is how a concern becomes a review. On the record."

"The record is the problem."

"The record is the only thing that survives." He said it without heat, and for a moment he looked tired. "Ms Merrick, I have read your file. I know about the 2018 reclassification. I know that you were right and that it cost my company a station's array and cost your company a contract. I am not here to tell you that you were wrong. I'm here to tell you that the record of a thing being raised correctly is worth more, in the long run, than the thing being fixed quickly by somebody who then has to explain why they did it."

"And in the short run?"

"In the short run, the portal takes five days."

She looked at the form. It was well designed, in the way that forms are well designed: every field had a purpose, and the purpose was to make the person filling it in accept the pace of the organisation.

"What happens if I submit it and the review finds what I found?"

"Then the classification is corrected, and the correction propagates to the conjunction product, and the station gets a warning. That is the system working."

"What happens if the station needs a warning before the review completes?"

"Then the duty officer notifies the technical contact directly. It's in the standing instruction, the paragraph at the end." He said it before she could, which meant he had read the same paragraph she had. "That provision exists precisely for this. It requires the duty officer to make a judgement, and it protects him if he makes it."

"It protects him."

"It protects the record of his judgement." Grant closed the folder. "I'm not going to pretend that's the same as protecting the station. But it's the mechanism we have, and it works more often than the alternative, which is people behaving courageously and nobody being able to prove it afterwards."

He stood. At the door he took a card out of his jacket and put it on the table. It was a plain card with his name, his title, an email address, and a switchboard number.

"Submit the form," he said. "Today. I'll mark it for the fast queue, which is a thing I can do and which is not in the manual."

"And if the portal is slow?"

He looked at the card.

"Then use the number on the back."

He left. Ilsa turned the card over. On the back, in blue ink, in handwriting that was small and careful, there was a mobile number and no name.

Grant Iwasaki had brought his own pen, which was a detail Ilsa noticed because it was the same pen she used: a black gel pen with a fine tip that smudged if you were left-handed.

"The form is designed to be completed in nine minutes," he said. "That's not an accident. The technical team found that submissions longer than a page were read less carefully than submissions that fit on a page."

"So the form is a filter."

"The form is a shape. Every organisation has a shape it can receive." He turned the page. "You'd be surprised how many people submit a document instead of a form and then complain that the document wasn't read."

"I'm not complaining. I'm telling you the form takes five days."

"The form takes five business days. The review takes as long as it takes." He looked at her. "You want me to move it faster. I can move it into the fast queue. The fast queue is for submissions that involve a crewed asset or an active conjunction. It's a judgement call, and I'm allowed to make it."

"Then make it."

"I already have." He slid a printout across the table. It was the form, stamped with a reference and a queue code. "You're in the fast queue. The technical officer will pick it up tomorrow morning."

"Tomorrow morning is Thursday."

"Tomorrow morning is Thursday."

"And the conjunction is Friday."

"And the conjunction is Friday," Grant said. "Which is why I'm here in person instead of sending an email."

He sat back. The conference room was warm, and outside the window a dish was turning, and the light on the table was the flat overhead kind.

"Can I ask you something that isn't about the form?" he said.

"Yes."

"The 2024 appendix. You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Your signature is on it."

"Yes."

"Do you remember signing it?"

She looked at him. He was fifty-five and careful, and the question was the first one anybody had asked her that was not about the procedure.

"No," she said. "I remember the day. I was in Fresno. I remember the room and the table and the social worker's folder. I don't remember signing."

Grant nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something.

"I signed a launch licensing file in 2004," he said. "I was twenty-nine. The file had a footnote that I didn't write, and the footnote said the payload mass was within the declared envelope, and the declared envelope was wrong. I signed it because the file was on my desk and the deadline was Friday."

"What happened?"

"Nothing. The launch went up, the payload was heavier than declared, the orbit was lower, and the satellite lasted four years instead of seven. The insurer paid. Nobody was hurt." He turned his pen over. "I've spent twenty years being careful because of a footnote I didn't write. That's not a confession. It's a qualification. I know what it looks like when a signature is the only thing that survives."

"And?"

"And I'm telling you that the appendix is going to be the thing that survives from 2024, and that the question will be whether you signed it, and that the answer will be a log entry that says you did." He put the pen down. "If you want a different answer, you need a different record. I can't give you one. I can tell you where to look."

"Where?"

"The signature service keeps a session log. Not the location. The IP address and the browser. If the signature was applied from a terminal in this building while you were in Fresno, the log will show it."

"Who administers the signature service?"

"Your company's IT. Which means the log is in a system your company controls."

"Then it can be edited."

"It can be edited," Grant agreed. "But an edit leaves an edit. That's the thing about systems that log everything. The log of the log is usually the one nobody remembers to clean."

The form had eleven boxes and Ilsa filled it in at the conference table while Grant watched, which was a thing he had asked for and a thing she had not expected.

"Box four is the object's designation," he said. "The designation as it appears in your catalogue, not the designation you think it should have."

"K-7741."

"Box five is the classification at the time of the observation. Debris."

"Debris."

"Box six is what you believe the classification should be. There's a closed list."

The closed list had nine options: spent stage, fragmentation debris, payload, unknown payload, active payload, controlled object, natural object, calibration target, other.

She read the list twice. The word *other* was the ninth option and had a note that said *describe in box seven*.

"I'd use unknown payload," she said.

"That's option four. Option four generates a bulletin entry. Option nine generates a review."

"I'd like the bulletin entry."

"Then use option four." Grant made a note. "Box seven is your evidence. It's four lines. Not four pages. Four lines."

She wrote four lines:

*Mass 912 kg from drag, 3 independent fits. Tumble 41.2 s. Three manoeuvres in nine days, 0.35/0.4/0.5 m/s. Launch reconstruction: sea platform, late notification, payload declared 400 kg.*

Grant read them and nodded slowly.

"That's a good box seven," he said. "It's the first good box seven I've seen this year."

"Why?"

"Because it's numbers." He put the form down. "Most analysts write a paragraph of concern. The panel reads a paragraph of concern as an opinion. A number is not an opinion, even when it's wrong."

"And if a number is wrong?"

"Then it's wrong in a way that can be checked." He tapped the box. "That's the whole of my job. I don't decide whether an object is dangerous. I decide whether the numbers are checkable. If they are, the object goes into the queue and the queue decides."

Ilsa looked at the form. Eleven boxes, four lines of evidence, and a queue that decided.

"Who's the technical officer who'll pick it up?"

"Her name's Okafor. She's careful and she's slow and she's been doing it for six years." Grant paused. "She'll want the raw returns. Not the summary. The returns."

"I've got the returns."

"Then send them with the form. Not after. With." He stood. "The form's the door. The returns are what makes the door stay open."

---

At the lift he stopped.

"One more thing that isn't on the form. The 2024 appendix. If you find the session log, don't send it to the panel. Send it to your own legal department first."

"Why?"

"Because a session log is evidence of an act, and an act has a person, and a person has a defence." He pressed the button. "The panel can't do anything with an act. Your legal department can, and the regulator can, and the insurer can. The panel can only change a classification."

"So I should change the classification and let the act go."

"You should change the classification, and put the act in front of the people who can act on it, in the order that makes each of them responsible for their own part." The lift came. "That's not justice either. It's the only machine that moves."

She put the card in the folder with the photographs, and the provenance note, and the printed conjunction summary, and then she opened the portal and began to fill in the form.

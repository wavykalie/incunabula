# Chapter 07

Hal's rule was not written down anywhere, which was why he could explain it in eleven words.

"The catalogue is a product," he said. "A product has a shape."

They were in the radar room at 21:40, because Ilsa had stayed and Hal had the night shift and neither of them had said why. The dish outside was pointed at a low elevation and the console showed a track that was doing nothing, which was most tracks.

"That's Priya's line," Ilsa said.

"Priya's line is longer. Mine's the same line without the salary." He poured coffee from a flask into a cup that had a chip out of the rim and did not offer her one, because he had offered once in 2019 and she had said no, and he had never asked again. "A product has a shape. The customer wants a catalogue that is complete and calm. Complete means every object has a row. Calm means the rows don't wake anybody up at night. Those two things are in tension, and the shape is where the tension gets resolved."

"And the resolution is debris."

"The resolution is whatever classification keeps the row complete and the customer calm." He drank. "Debris is a good resolution. Debris is nobody's fault. A booster that came apart is an act of physics. A payload that nobody declared is an act of somebody."

Ilsa looked at the console. The track crawled.

"The manual says an analyst can override."

"The manual says an analyst can request an independent fit. The manual also says the request goes to the duty officer, and the duty officer's performance review has a line in it about client escalations. You've read the manual. You know what it says. What you don't know is that the manual was written by a committee that had a client on it."

"You're telling me the procedure is the cover."

"I'm telling you the procedure is the procedure. The cover is what happens when the procedure is followed correctly by everybody who wants to keep their job." He put the cup down. "That's not the same as a conspiracy. It's worse, in a way. A conspiracy has meetings you can find."

Ilsa took out the page he had given her and put it on the desk between them. Azimuth 214, elevation 19, 02:41, and under it, in pencil, *came back 8 min early. then nothing*.

"You wrote this from memory."

"I wrote it the night it happened. That's the point of the notebook." He tapped the drawer. "The system keeps eighteen months. The notebook keeps thirty-one years. When the system loses something, the notebook is the only thing that says it existed."

"Why keep it?"

"Because the dish doesn't lie, and the system does." He said it flatly, the way he said most things. "The dish gives you a number. The system gives you a record of a number. Those are different objects. People get confused about that, mostly people who've never had to explain a gap in a track to somebody who wanted the gap to be a calibration target."

Ilsa turned the page over. On the back, in the same pencil, there was a number she had not noticed: 3.4.

"What's this?"

"Signal strength at closest approach. In the units we used before the upgrade." He shrugged. "I wrote it because it was high. A calibration target with a corner reflector comes back bright. That one came back bright and then it came back less bright, three nights later, and then it didn't come back."

"Less bright means what?"

"Means the reflector wasn't pointed at us any more." He looked at her. "Or means there wasn't a reflector. Means there was a body, and the body turned."

The air handling hummed. On the console the track did nothing, patiently, the way the sky does.

"Hal."

"Mm."

"If I file this, and it goes where I think it's going to go, they'll ask who gave me the page."

"They'll ask," he agreed. "And I'll say I gave it to you because you asked, and because I'm sixty-three and my pension is already calculated, and because somebody should have asked in 2025."

He picked up his cup and drank, and then he said the thing that she would remember later, when everything had gone the way it went.

The notebook had a story in it that Hal told her without opening the book, because he had told it before and because the telling was the point.

"1997," he said. "I was on the night shift at the old site, before the dishes. We had a fence and a plot and a logbook, and the logbook was paper because the computer was a terminal that talked to a mainframe in Denver and the mainframe went down every Tuesday."

"What happened in 1997?"

"A track came up out of the south that wasn't in the tasking list. Not a satellite. It was slow, and it was bright, and it stayed up for four minutes and then it went behind the horizon." He drank his coffee. "I called the duty officer. The duty officer called the customer. The customer said it was a re-entry of a spent stage and not to worry about it."

"And?"

"And two days later a piece of it came down in a field in Colorado. A farmer found it. It was a fuel tank with a Cyrillic stamp on it." He shrugged. "The customer had been wrong. Not lying. Wrong. They'd looked at the plot and decided it was a re-entry because a re-entry was the thing that made sense, and they'd told me not to worry, and I'd written it in the logbook and gone home."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing. What was there to do? The piece came down in a field and nobody was hurt and the farmer got a visit from two men in a car who took the tank away and gave him a receipt." He put the cup down. "But I wrote it in the logbook. Time, azimuth, duration, and the words *customer says re-entry*. And when the men in the car came, the logbook was the only record that said the thing had been seen before it came down."

Ilsa looked at the notebook on the desk between them.

"So the notebook is a habit."

"The notebook is a habit and a pension and a way of sleeping." He tapped it. "You want to know why I keep it? Because in 1997 the logbook was the only thing that survived the mainframe. Not because I was brave. Because paper doesn't have a migration."

She turned the page. The entries were not all interesting. There were tracks that had turned out to be aircraft, tracks that had turned out to be weather balloons, tracks that had turned out to be nothing at all. Hal had written them all down, in pencil, in the same hand, with the same four fields.

"Why keep the boring ones?"

"Because the boring ones are the control." He said it as though it were obvious. "If you only write down the ones that matter, then in twenty years nobody can tell whether the notebook is a record or a story. If you write down all of them, the boring ones prove you weren't selecting."

She looked at the page. At the top there was a date from 1997, and the entry said *az 197, el 14, 04:12, 3m, customer says re-entry*. At the bottom of the page there was a date from last year, and the entry said *az 214, el 19, 02:41, 8m, customer says calibration*.

The two entries were twenty-six years apart and the same sentence.

"That's the thing I wanted you to see," Hal said. "It's the same sentence. It's always the same sentence. The customer says something and the operator writes it down and the object does what it does."

"And nobody reads the notebook."

"Nobody reads the notebook." He took it back and put it in the drawer. "Until somebody does."

The tasking list for the night was pinned to the wall above the scheduler's console, and it was printed on paper because the scheduler did not trust the screen.

Ilsa read it at 19:40 while the scheduler was on the phone.

The list had forty-one objects and eleven dishes and four hours, and the arithmetic of it was visible in the margins. Dish six had a gap at 02:00 because the scheduler had decided the object it was tracking would be below the horizon and the next object was not due until 03:10. Dish seven was down for maintenance. Dish nine was tasked to a client's urgent request, which meant a satellite operator wanted to know where its own satellite was.

The list was the catalogue's real shape. Objects entered the summary through the list, and the list was finite, and the finiteness was the thing nobody outside the building understood.

"You can't track everything," the scheduler said when he got off the phone. "That's the first thing they teach you and the first thing the clients refuse to believe. There are eleven dishes and four hours and forty-one objects, and if you give one object more time you take it from another."

"Who decides?"

"The duty officer decides. I execute." He tapped the list. "But the duty officer decides under a contract that says which objects are priority. Priority one gets tracked every pass. Priority two gets tracked twice a night. Priority four gets tracked when there's a gap."

"And if a priority four object turns out to matter?"

"Then somebody changes its priority." He looked at her. "That's the whole system. Somebody changes the priority, and the priority changes the tracking, and the tracking changes the covariance, and the covariance changes the classification. It all runs downhill from a number that somebody typed."

She looked at the list. K-7741 was on it, in the priority four block, with a scheduled pass at 02:00 and a note that said *if gap*.

"Who typed four?"

"The catalogue. The catalogue assigns the priority from the classification. Debris is four."

"So the classification is the priority and the priority is the tracking and the tracking is the covariance and the covariance is the classification."

"That's a loop," the scheduler said. "I've been telling people it's a loop for nine years. Nobody wants to hear it, because a loop means you can't fix it in one place."

---

At 02:00 the gap came and dish six was pointed at K-7741, and the object was where the orbit said it would be, and the covariance shrank, and the classification stayed debris.

Ilsa watched the trace come in on the console. The signal was good. The object was a clean return with a specular glint, and the glint was the kind of detail that would have been a clue if anybody had been looking for clues, and the system logged it as a photometric artefact because the system had a field for photometric artefacts and no field for windows.

She wrote in the notebook: *02:00 obs good. glint. logged as artefact. loop closes.*

Then she went to the canteen and got a coffee and sat with it, and the building hummed, and outside the dishes turned in the dark, and the number that somebody had typed in 2021 was still running everything.

"You've got a station in the path and a product with a shape. I know which one I'd rather be wrong about."

# Chapter 21

She wrote the audit on Friday morning, between 04:10 and 07:30, at the kitchen table, with the folder open on one side and the notebook on the other.

It was forty-one pages. She had never written anything that long that was not a catalogue procedure, and she found that the structure came easily, because a catalogue procedure and an audit are the same shape: what was observed, what was expected, what was done, what should have been done, and who is responsible for the difference.

Section 1 was the object. Mass estimate, area-to-mass ratio, tumble period, specular signature, manoeuvre history, reconstructed launch, the missing designator, the provenance note that said the seller declined to provide a launch record.

Section 2 was the classification. The 2024 hygiene review, Appendix 4, the recommendation of *payload, unknown*, the disposition of *breakup debris, no further action*, the signature and the date, the fact that she had been in Fresno, the access log entry at 02:11 on 17 October, the four-minute session.

Section 3 was the conjunction. The fit, the covariance, the probability, the screening volume definition, the priority threshold, the standing instruction, the downgrade, the summary that said no tracked objects.

Section 4 was the contract. Schedule 4, clause 4.3, clause 4.6, the liability cap, the client direction of 12 March 2024, the fact that the person who signed it was the person who was now reviewing the portal submission.

Section 5 was the part she had rewritten four times. It was titled *Assessment*, and it said, in five paragraphs, that the object was a manoeuvring spacecraft, that its orbit had been adjusted nine days ago to align a crossing with a crewed station, that the catalogue had been instructed to describe it as debris, that the instruction had been implemented in the ordinary course by people who had done their jobs correctly, and that the station was receiving a conjunction summary that was accurate and useless.

She did not name anybody as having done anything wrong. She named what had been done, by whom, on what date, under which clause. The wrongness was for somebody else to find.

At 07:30 she read it through once. Then she made a decision that she had been putting off since Wednesday, which was where to send it.

The insurer was the obvious route. Meridian's operator insured the station, and the insurer had a contractual right to the raw conjunction data, and an insurer's interest in a warning was not the same as a provider's interest in a product. The insurer had an analyst who read appendices, or it did not.

The regulator was the other route. Filing with the regulator made the document a regulatory record, which meant it could not be withdrawn, and which also meant it could not be quietly handled by a technical officer in five business days.

She did both, at 09:00, from her own account, with the audit attached as a single PDF and a covering note that said: *Attached is an audit of object K-7741 and its treatment in the Kestrel catalogue. The object is on a conjunction trajectory with Meridian Station. The catalogue describes it as debris. I am the analyst who signed the reclassification in 2024 and the reclassification this week. I am available to answer questions.*

The regulator's portal accepted the filing and returned a case number. The insurer's portal accepted the filing and returned a different case number, and then a message that said the submission had been classified as a *complaint*, because the portal classified everything that came from outside the claims process as a complaint, and complaints were handled by a different team.

She sat at the kitchen table with the two case numbers on the screen and the coffee going cold, and she felt the specific flatness of having done the thing and discovering that the thing had a queue of its own.

Section 6 was the 2024 appendix, and she wrote it last and worst.

The problem was that the section had two subjects. The first was the appendix itself: a signature, a date, and a paragraph in a quarterly report that had classified an object as debris. The second was the fact that she could not remember signing it.

She wrote the first subject in the flat voice of an analyst. Signature. Date. The report's purpose, which was to demonstrate that the hygiene review had been completed for the quarter. The appendix's language, which repeated the standing instruction almost word for word.

Then she wrote the second subject, and the sentences came out wrong. *I do not recall signing the appendix. The date coincides with a day on which I was not in the building. I have no record of the session.*

She read it back and could hear the review panel's response: an analyst who does not remember signing a document is an analyst who cannot be relied on to tell the difference between what happened and what she would like to have happened.

So she rewrote it as a discrepancy rather than a denial.

*The appendix carries my signature and the date 14 March 2024. The signature service records the session's capture time as 11:41 on 14 March. On 14 March I was at a care assessment in Fresno, two hundred and forty kilometres from the building, between 11:00 and 12:30. I have a printed agenda from the assessment which is time-stamped by the home's office system. The discrepancy is not explained in any record I can access.*

That was better. It described two records and the gap between them, and it did not ask the reader to believe her.

At 06:20 Femi came in with two coffees and sat down opposite her without asking.

"Which section are you on?"

"Six."

"Six is the appendix."

"Six is the appendix."

He looked at the page over the rim of his cup.

"Are you going to put the access log in?"

"The 02:11 session?"

"Yes."

She put her pen down.

"If I put it in, the audit says that somebody used my credentials at two in the morning to clear the object's watch item. That's an allegation against a person I can't identify, in a document that will be read by a regulator and a client and possibly a court."

"If you leave it out, the audit says the watch item was cleared and doesn't say how."

"The audit says the watch item was cleared by a session using my credentials, and that the session took place at a time when I was not in the building." She turned the page. "That's what section 5 says. I wrote it that way because it's true and because it doesn't accuse anybody."

Femi read section 5 slowly.

"It accuses the session."

"The session doesn't have a lawyer."

"No," he said. "But the person who ran it does, and the first thing the lawyer will say is that the credentials were yours, and the second thing will be that the analyst is the only person who could have used them, and the third thing will be that the analyst has a reason to invent a mystery."

"Then the audit needs a record that isn't mine." She looked at him. "The camera. The export request went in on Tuesday. If the footage exists, the audit can cite it. If it doesn't, the audit cites the request and the absence of the footage, and the absence is itself a fact."

Femi sat back.

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I've thought about nothing else for four days."

He nodded, and drank his coffee, and looked out of the window at the light coming up over the plateau.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I think you should file it."

"I'm going to file it."

"I know. I'm saying I think you should." He put the cup down. "I've been in this building for two years and I've never seen anybody file anything. Everybody talks about filing. Nobody files. The system's designed so that talking about it is enough."

Ilsa looked at the page. Section 6 needed one more sentence and she had been avoiding it.

She wrote: *A signature is not a memory. The record should be able to tell the difference.*

Section 7 was the recommendations, and it was the section she rewrote four times.

The first version had six recommendations. The second had four. The third had two and a paragraph explaining why the other four were outside the analyst's authority.

The fourth version had one, and the one was a sentence: *the warning threshold should not depend on a classification that the client can instruct.*

She read it back and could hear the objection before it was made: the whole product depends on classifications, and the client pays for the product, and a threshold that does not depend on the product is a threshold that cannot be sold.

So she wrote the recommendation as a procedure change rather than a principle.

*Recommendation 1. Where an object has been observed to change its orbit, the warning threshold should be applied to the observed behaviour rather than to the classification. The change is mechanical: a manoeuvre flag in the screening input, set by the analyst, clearing the classification filter for that object only.*

That was defensible. It was small. It did not require anybody to admit that the instruction was wrong; it required the system to notice a manoeuvre.

She read it again and added a second paragraph, because the first one was too clean.

*The change would not have prevented the 2024 classification. It would have prevented the 2026 conjunctions from being screened out, because the object would have carried a flag from its first manoeuvre. The cost is one extra row per manoeuvring object per pass.*

One extra row. There were perhaps forty manoeuvring objects in the shell. Forty rows per pass, eleven passes a day, four hundred and forty rows a day, on a product that currently produced two hundred.

She did the arithmetic twice and wrote it down. The product would grow by a factor of three, and the product's growth was the client's cost, and the client's cost was the reason the instruction existed in the first place.

At 07:10 she made a decision, which was to put the arithmetic in the recommendation rather than in an appendix. A recommendation without its cost was a wish, and the review panel had read a hundred wishes.

She wrote the cost into the third paragraph and then sat back and looked at the page.

Forty-one pages. Six sections of evidence, one section of recommendation, and one appendix of sources that ran to nine pages because every number in the audit had a file behind it.

She printed it at 07:20. The printer was in the corridor and it took four minutes and made a noise that the night shift had learned to ignore. When the pages came out she stacked them on the table and put a binder clip on the corner and wrote the date on the first page with a pen, because a date in ink was a thing a reader could not edit.

Then she made a second copy, and a third, and put one in the folder and one in her bag and one in the drawer of the desk in the spare room, which was the only piece of furniture in the house that locked.

Then she opened the promotion folder, took out the agreement, wrote *declined* across the signature page in pencil, photographed it, and put it back in her bag.

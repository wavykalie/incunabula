# Chapter 02

The drag coefficient was the first thing that did not fit, and it did not fit by enough to be interesting.

A spent upper stage is a cylinder with a lot of empty volume and a large area facing the direction of travel. When the atmosphere at four hundred kilometres thins and thickens with the solar cycle, the stage responds to it like a sail. Its orbit decays in a curve. The curve is not smooth, because the sun is not smooth, but over four years it has a shape, and the shape is a signature. K-7741's curve was too flat.

Ilsa pulled the raw radar returns for the last two hundred days. The station kept them for eighteen months, then archived them to a tape library that nobody had migrated since the cloud contract went to review. The returns came back as a table: time, range, range-rate, angle, signal strength. She sorted by time and began to compute.

The drag response of an object is a function of its area-to-mass ratio. The area can be estimated from the radar cross-section if you accept a large error bar and a shape assumption. The mass cannot be measured at all. It can only be inferred, and the inference is the part of the trade that separates an analyst from a form-filler. A hollow booster of that cross-section should have an area-to-mass ratio in a certain range. K-7741's was a third of the bottom of that range.

She checked her arithmetic twice. Then she checked the calibration of the radar that had produced the returns, because a gain error would produce exactly this result, and a gain error was the boring explanation, and boring explanations were usually right.

The calibration log for that dish showed nothing unusual. The gain had drifted by 0.2 decibels over the period, which was inside tolerance and had been corrected in software. The returns were good.

She leaned back. The floor was emptying for lunch. Hal was still at the far end, eating a sandwich over his keyboard with the care of a man who had dropped one there before.

"Hal."

He looked up.

"Does a mass anomaly ring anything for you? An object in a booster-class catalogue entry with an A-over-m a third of what it should be."

"Rings what?"

"That it's not a booster."

Hal chewed. "Or it's a booster with something in it."

"That's the same thing."

"No it isn't." He wiped his fingers on a napkin that had come from a drawer. "A booster that's been used as a booster is empty. A booster that's been used as a shipping container is not. You'd get a higher mass, not a lower one."

"Lower means lighter. Lighter than hollow."

"Then it's not a cylinder," Hal said. "Or your cross-section's wrong. What's it tumbling at?"

She did not know. The returns were range and range-rate; a tumble showed up as a modulation in the signal strength, and she had not looked at signal strength, because signal strength was not a field the form asked for.

She looked at it now. The returns had a periodicity she had filtered out as noise: a rise and fall every forty-one seconds, clean enough to be a rotation, dirty enough to be a body with an uneven shape. A booster tumbling at forty-one seconds was possible. A booster tumbling at forty-one seconds for four years, without the tumble decaying, was not.

She wrote the number down. Then she opened the object's history and looked for every field the record had, including the ones the summary did not show.

There was a field called *mass estimate, kg*, and it was populated. Nine hundred and twelve. She stared at it for a while. The summary line had not shown it because the summary line showed six fields, and mass estimate was the seventh. Nine hundred and twelve kilograms for an object with a booster's cross-section and a third of a booster's drag response.

She pulled the object's launch history. There was none. Not *unclear*, not *provisional*: the field was empty, and the record had been migrated twice, and both migrations had preserved the emptiness as a deliberate null rather than filling it with an unknown.

She wrote in the notes field: *mass anomaly, unresolved. A/m inconsistent with hollow stage. 912 kg estimate. Tumble 41 s, undamped.*

The form did not have a code for that. It had nine reason codes for reclassification and none of them meant *this object is not what the catalogue says it is*. She saved the note as a draft and left the reclassification as it was, finished, signed, closed.

At 13:20 she went to find Priya and found the office empty, with the paper diary open on the desk to a page with two entries in the same handwriting: *Vantage review — Thursday* and *Ilsa — reclass process*.

She stood in the doorway for a moment. Then she went back to her desk and opened the raw returns again, and this time she did the thing she had been avoiding since 09:10, which was to fit an orbit instead of trusting the one in the catalogue.

The catalogue orbit had an apogee of 412 kilometres and a perigee of 398. It had been fitted six weeks ago. The raw returns from the last two weeks put the apogee at 404 and falling. The perigee was holding. That was the shape of a circularising manoeuvre, or of a body that had been given a push.

She wrote the new elements by hand on the whiteboard she was not supposed to use, because the whiteboard was for the stand-up and the stand-up was for the account.

Hal came back from lunch at 12:50 with a paper bag and the specific air of a man who had been thinking about something while eating.

"You said a third of the expected A-over-m," he said.

"Give or take."

"Give or take what?"

"Twenty per cent, depending on the shape assumption."

Hal sat down on the corner of her desk, which was the thing he did instead of hovering. "So the object is either heavier than a booster or smaller than one. If it's smaller, the cross-section's wrong, and if the cross-section's wrong then the fence gain is off, and if the gain is off then every object in that band has the same error."

"The gain's been checked."

"The gain's been checked by the software that applies the correction." He said it without emphasis. "I'm not saying it's wrong. I'm saying the check is the same instrument. You want a second opinion, you get it from a different dish."

She looked at the clock. It was 12:55 and the afternoon shift was coming in, and the dish farm's schedule had a gap at 14:10 while dish seven was off for a receiver swap.

"Dish seven's down for maintenance."

"Dish seven's down for a receiver swap, which takes forty minutes, and the swap is done by a contractor who owes me a favour." He stood. "I'll ask him to point it at your object for one pass before he pulls the receiver. If the gain's the same, the object's real. If it isn't, you've got a maintenance problem and I've got a contractor who's going to be very apologetic."

She wrote the object's elements on a sticky note and gave it to him. He folded it into his shirt pocket without looking at it.

At 14:20 he called her extension.

"Dish seven saw it," he said. "Signal strength's within half a decibel of dish six. Gain's fine. Your object's real."

"And the mass?"

"And the mass is whatever the drag says it is, which is nine hundred and something, which is not a booster." He paused. "There's one more thing. Dish seven's got a different filter chain, and it sees a modulation dish six doesn't. Your object's flashing. Not like a tumbler. Like a beacon."

"A beacon."

"Once every forty-one seconds, two milliseconds wide." He said it slowly. "That's a strobe, or a corner reflector catching the sun, or a piece of polished glass. It's not a booster."

She sat with the phone against her ear and looked at the mass anomaly on her screen, and the field that had no code.

"Hal. Could it be a calibration target?"

"It could be anything," he said. "That's the problem with a real object. It doesn't come with a label."

At 15:10 Priya's assistant emailed the whole group with the Vantage review agenda. Ilsa read it standing up, and then she went back to her desk and opened the object's provenance record, and looked at the note that said *seller declined to provide a launch record*, and thought about the phrase *declined to provide* and what it implied about the conversation in which somebody had asked.

Then she sat down and looked at the ellipse for a long time.

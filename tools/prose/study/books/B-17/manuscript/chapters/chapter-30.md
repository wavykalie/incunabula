# Chapter 30

Femi's copy reached the insurer's technical team at 17:40 on Friday, and the insurer reopened the case at 18:05.

He told her afterwards, on the phone, in a voice that was trying to be steady and not managing it.

"I didn't send it to the complaint desk. I sent it to the technical address on the claims file, the one for conjunction disputes. There's a form. I filled in the form."

"Femi."

"I know. I know I'm on a visa. I know what the policy says about data leaving the building. I sent it anyway, because you filed your audit and they put you on leave and the station still doesn't have a warning, and I thought—" He stopped.

"You thought what?"

"I thought somebody should do the next thing."

She was in her car in the car park with the engine off and the phone against her ear, and she looked at the third-floor windows, which were lit, and at the dish farm beyond them, which was turning.

"What did they say?"

"They said the case is reopened. They said the technical team has reviewed the covariance and the fit and they've asked Kestrel for the raw data under Schedule 4, clause 5."

"What's clause 5?"

"Clause 5 says the provider will make raw data available to the insurer on request where a conjunction with an insured asset is in dispute. It's in the contract. I found it this afternoon."

"Who at Kestrel gets the request?"

"The duty officer, the account lead, and legal. And the client's technical contact, as a courtesy."

"Priya will see it."

"Priya will see it in about four minutes," Femi said.

The request arrived at 18:12. Ilsa knew because Femi forwarded it to her personal email, which was against policy, and because Priya called her at 18:20.

"You're on leave," Priya said.

"I know."

"Your junior analyst has just sent the insurer a package that includes raw radar returns, an independent fit, and a copy of your audit, and the insurer has responded with a formal request under clause 5, which gives us twenty-four hours to produce the data or explain in writing why we won't."

"I know."

"The explanation in writing goes to the insurer, to the regulator, and to the client. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means the client has to decide whether to instruct us to withhold data from its own insurer."

"It means the client has to decide, in writing, by tomorrow evening, whether to be the party that withheld the conjunction data." Priya's voice was tight. "Which is a decision that will be read by a regulator and, eventually, by a court. It is the first decision in this entire affair that the client cannot make quietly."

Ilsa sat in the car with the phone against her ear and the windows fogging.

"Priya. Did you know about the second manoeuvre?"

"What second manoeuvre?"

"Nine days ago. The phasing change. The one that aligned the crossing."

The line was quiet for a long moment.

"No," Priya said. "I knew about the reclassification. I knew about the instruction. I did not know that the object had manoeuvred."

"Do you believe me?"

"I believe the fit. The fit is good." A breath. "Ilsa, I'm going to ask you something and I need you to answer it as an analyst, not as a person who has been wronged. If the object manoeuvred nine days ago, and the manoeuvre aligned a crossing with Meridian, what is the most likely explanation?"

"Somebody wanted the object to arrive at a particular time."

"Or?"

"Or somebody wanted the object to arrive at a particular place, and the time followed."

"Which is worse?"

"The second," Ilsa said. "The second means the target was chosen."

Priya was quiet. In the background, the ground room hummed.

"Go home," she said at last. "Stay on leave. Keep your phone on. And Ilsa—"

"Yes."

"Don't send anything else. Not to the insurer, not to the regulator, not to the station. Whatever happens in the next four hours, it has to happen through the process, or the process will be the story and the object won't be."

She hung up.

Ilsa sat in the car for a while. Then she drove home, and at 19:10 she opened her laptop and found that the regulator had acknowledged her filing, and that the acknowledgement contained a case number, a reviewer's name, and a line that said *response requested within thirty days*.

Femi was in the car park when she got back, standing beside his car with his phone in his hand and his jacket done up wrong.

"Compliance wants to see me at eight," he said.

"About the package?"

"About an unauthorised transfer of client data to a third party." He said the words the way a man says words he has been rehearsing. "That's the phrase in the email. It doesn't say insurer. It says third party."

Ilsa looked at him. He was twenty-six and his shoes were wet, and the car park light made everybody look like they had been up for a day too long.

"Who's the compliance officer?"

"A woman called Okafor. She's new. She came from a bank." He put the phone in his pocket. "I've looked her up. She's been here four months. She's got a checklist and a queue and she's measured on how many cases she closes."

"Then she'll close it."

"She'll close it," Femi said. "That's what I'm afraid of."

They stood in the car park. The dish farm was behind the building and the dishes were dark shapes against a sky that had gone the colour of a road.

"Femi. Listen to me. When you go in there, you tell the truth in the order it happened. The package was sent because the raw data showed a mass anomaly and a manoeuvre history and the summary didn't. You sent it to the technical address on the claims file, which is the address the contract designates for technical data. You did not send it to the press. You did not send it to a competitor. You sent it to the party that insures the asset at risk."

"That's not what the policy says."

"The policy says client data can't leave the building without authorisation. The contract says the provider must produce raw data to the insurer on request. The request came four hours after the package. So the policy is true and the contract is true, and the thing that decides which one applies is the order of events."

"So I should say it was a pre-emptive response to a request that hadn't been made."

"You should say what happened."

He looked at her.

"They're going to ask me why I did it."

"Then tell them."

"And if I say because you asked me to?"

"I didn't ask you to. I asked you to check the fit." She said it quietly. "If they ask whether I knew, say yes. If they ask whether I directed it, say no, because I didn't."

"But I did it because of you."

"You did it because the data was wrong and you're an engineer," Ilsa said. "That's the true version, and it's also the version that survives a compliance interview, because it's the only version where you're not a person who took a risk for a colleague. You're a person who found a defect and reported it."

Femi stood there for a moment. Then he laughed, once, without any humour in it.

"You know what's funny? I've been in this building for two years and nobody's ever asked me to check anything. Nobody's ever given me a fit to look at. I've been maintaining a pipeline." He looked at the building. "And the first time somebody gives me something real, it turns into this."

"It was already this," Ilsa said. "You just hadn't seen it."

Okafor's finding came on the Friday afternoon, in a document of two pages, and the finding was that the transfer had been a technical disclosure made in good faith to a party with a contractual right to the data.

Ilsa read it in the car park on her phone while Femi read his own copy in his car with the door open.

"That's the good outcome," she said through the gap.

"That's the outcome where I keep my job." He looked up. "The finding also says the disclosure should have been made through the account manager and not directly to the claims address."

"Which is a process finding."

"Which is a written note in my file that says I didn't follow the process." He put the phone on the passenger seat. "I've been here two years and my file's got one note in it and the note says I did the right thing the wrong way."

"That's most files."

"That's most files." He laughed, once, without much in it. "My mother's going to ask me what happened and I'm going to say I got a note in my file and she's going to say what does that mean and I'm going to say it means I'm still employed."

Ilsa leaned against the car. The dish farm was behind them and the dishes were turning and the light was the flat afternoon kind that made everything look like a photograph of a place rather than the place.

"Femi. There's a thing I need to tell you and I'd rather tell you now than have you find out from an email."

He looked at her.

"The insurer's statement has my name on it and it describes the object as a vehicle. The company's position is that the classification was corrected and the addendum is an analyst's assessment. Those two things are going to be in the same regulator file, and the file is going to be read by people who will want to know which one is right."

"And which one is right?"

"Both of them, in the sense that matters to a lawyer. The company corrected a classification. The analyst described a vehicle. The correction doesn't require the description and the description doesn't accuse anybody."

"That's the sentence you've been practising."

"That's the sentence I've been practising." She looked at the ground. "It's also true."

Femi sat in the driver's seat with the door open and his feet on the tarmac.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" he said. "The pipeline. I've spent two years maintaining a pipeline that turns observations into a summary. It's a good pipeline. It does what it says. And the thing it says is that the summary is the product, and the product is what the client buys, and the client buys it because the client wants to know what's in the sky."

"And?"

"And the pipeline never asks whether the client wants to know what's in the sky or whether the client wants a document that says the sky is empty." He looked at her. "I've been maintaining a machine that can't tell the difference."

---

At 17:10 she went back inside and cleared her desk, which was a thing she had not done in eight years, because the supervision note meant her console access was read-only and the read-only console was a console she did not need to sit at.

She took the notebook, the folder, the pen, and the mug with the chip in the rim. She left the plant, which had been dead since March, and the printout of the 2021 procedure, which was pinned to the partition and had been for two years.

At the door she stopped and looked back at the desk. It was a desk in a room with eleven other desks and a window that looked at the dish farm, and she had spent a third of her waking life at it, and the thing she felt was not grief and not relief. It was the specific flatness of a room that had stopped being hers.

Then she went down in the lift and out through the car park and drove home with the radio off.

Thirty days. The object would arrive in four hours.

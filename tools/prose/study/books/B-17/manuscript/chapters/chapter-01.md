# Chapter 01

Two hundred and fourteen objects waited in the reclassification queue, and eleven of them required a judgement.

Ilsa worked the top of the list first, the way the procedure said to, because the procedure had been written by someone who had never had to clear a queue before a client call. The other two hundred and three required only a signature. She signed those in batches of twenty, reading the summary line, checking the epoch against the last observation, and moving on. The clock in the catalogue bay ran four minutes fast. She had stopped correcting for it years ago.

The floor at Panamint was cold in the mornings. The air handling ran at a fixed rate because the dish motors downstream of the wall did not like a pressure swing, and the result was a draught along the floor that moved the loose corners of the printouts she still kept. Her desk had two monitors, a terminal she was not supposed to use for anything but catalogue work, and a cup of coffee that had been hot at 06:10 and was now a different substance.

Object 214 arrived at 06:38. K-7741. The summary line said *spent upper stage, breakup debris, no further action*. She opened the file.

The record was thin in a way she associated with bought data. No international designator. A K-number assigned internally in 2021 when Kestrel had purchased a block of observations from a defunct provider, and the observations had come with a note that said *provenance incomplete, do not certify*. Somebody had certified them anyway, because the catalogue was a product and a product needed rows. The object had been tracked for four years and had never been assigned a priority above the screening floor. It had a radar cross-section consistent with a booster. It had a drag response that was, on the face of the summary, consistent with a booster.

She looked at the epoch. The last observation was eleven hours old, which was normal for a low-priority object.

The reclassification form had six fields and a free-text box. She filled the first five from the record: object identifier, previous classification, new classification, authority, reason code. The authority field defaulted to her own name. The reason code had a drop-down with nine options, and the one she needed was the second: *catalogue hygiene, client request*.

She did not choose it. She chose the fourth, *routine review*, because there had been no client request that she knew of, and because the fourth option did not generate a notification.

The free-text box accepted two hundred characters. She wrote: *Booster-class RCS, drag consistent with hollow stage. No designator. Hygiene reclassification. No further action.* The counter said 148. She read it back once.

Then she signed it, because it was 06:52 and the queue would not clear itself and the object had been in the catalogue for four years without anybody dying.

The queue ticked down to 213.

Femi arrived at seven with a paper cup and a question about the covariance tool. He was twenty-six and dressed like a man who had read an article about dressing for the job you wanted. He asked whether the new version of the tool was reporting in the same frame as the old one, because he had run the same object through both and got two different numbers.

"Different to what?"

"Four decimals."

"Then it's the tool."

"It's meant to be the same tool," he said.

"Then it's the same tool and one of them is rounding differently." She turned back to her screen. He stood there long enough that she looked up again. "Femi. The queue is two hundred and thirteen. If you want to spend the morning on four decimals, spend it, but put it in a ticket so it's somebody's problem."

He went away. She heard him say something to Hal at the far end of the floor, and Hal's laugh, which was mostly a cough.

At 08:00 the daily stand-up happened in the corner with the broken projector. Priya ran it standing up, which was supposed to make it short, and which made it eleven minutes instead of twenty. She talked about the Vantage renewal. She used the word *hygiene* twice. Ilsa, who had used the same word in a form at 06:52, felt the small unpleasantness of hearing her own vocabulary in someone else's mouth.

Afterwards Priya stopped her by the coffee machine.

"You cleared the queue."

"Two hundred and thirteen."

"Good. I need you on the account review next week. Vantage wants to see how we handle a client-requested reclassification, end to end, and you're the only one who can walk them through it without reading from the slide."

"I can do that."

"Start from an object with a boring history. Not a fragment cloud. They get nervous."

Ilsa said she would. Priya went back towards her office, then turned.

"The four-decimal thing. Is it real?"

"Probably rounding."

"Make it not real by Friday," Priya said, and smiled, and was gone.

Ilsa stood by the coffee machine for a moment. Through the window at the end of the bay, the first dish was turning to its morning position, a slow mechanical patience that never failed to make her feel that the building was doing something honest. The dish was pointed at a piece of sky where nothing had happened yet. It would point there for six hours and record whatever was there, which was mostly nothing, which was the job.

She went back to her desk and pulled the approach plates out of her bag. She had bought three more at the weekend, from a dealer in Reno who did not ask why a woman in her thirties wanted instrument procedures for airports in Norway. She filed them by ICAO code. ENGM went between ENBR and ENVA. The missed approach for the second runway required a turn to a holding fix she could not have found on a map.

She put them away and opened the next queue. It had begun to fill again already, because the sky does not stop producing objects for a procedural deadline.

At 07:35 the duty officer came round with a tablet and a face that meant a client call.

"Vantage wants a walkthrough of the reclassification queue," he said. "Not the product. The queue. They've got an audit committee and the committee has questions about how objects get from the raw catalogue into the customer view."

"Who's asking?"

"A woman called Halloran. She's the committee's technical adviser. She wants to see a live queue, not a slide."

"Live queues are boring. Two hundred objects, eleven judgements, a lot of signatures."

"Boring is what they want. They've had enough excitement." He glanced at her screen. "They've asked for you specifically. Priya put your name forward."

Ilsa looked at the queue. It had refilled to two hundred and twenty-six overnight, because the fence had been recalibrated and the recalibration had produced a crop of new detections that were mostly noise.

"Fine. When?"

"Thursday. Two hours. They'll bring their own auditor."

"An auditor with a checklist."

"An auditor with a checklist and a plane to catch," the duty officer said, and went away.

She spent the next twenty minutes doing the thing she did before any client walkthrough, which was to walk the process herself as though she were a stranger. The queue sorted by priority, then by age. The priority was a number between one and five, and the number was computed from a formula that nobody in the room had written and everybody in the room trusted. The age was the time since the last observation. The top of the list was priority one, which meant a crewed asset or an asset of strategic value, and there were never more than three of those, and they were always handled by the duty officer.

The middle of the list was where the work was. Priority three and four, objects that could matter if they were something else. The queue asked for a judgement, and the judgement was a classification, and the classification was a field with nine options.

She opened the procedure for the ninth option, *unknown — pending assessment*, and read the note attached to it. The note said that the option should be used sparingly, because an unknown object generated a conjunction row for every crewed asset in its shell, and four hundred unknown objects would make the product unusable.

The note had been written in 2021. She had written it, in the first year of the contract, when the product was young and the fear was false alarms.

She read her own note twice. Then she closed it and went back to the queue, and worked the next object, which was a piece of a Chinese upper stage that had been in the catalogue for nine years and had been classified as debris since the day it was detected, and which was, she knew from the drag response, exactly what the catalogue said it was.

At 08:05 the client liaison sent an email confirming Thursday, with an agenda attached. The agenda had four items and a request for a demonstration of a reclassification from start to finish.

Ilsa replied that she could demonstrate the workflow but not the specific object, because a live demonstration would require a live case, and a live case was not something you could schedule.

The liaison replied that the committee would accept a historical example.

She looked at the queue. There were eleven objects requiring a judgement, and one of them had a mass anomaly she had not yet looked at, and the anomaly was sitting in a field the summary did not display.

She opened a new email and typed: *Thursday is fine. I'll walk the committee through the 2024 hygiene review and the subsequent correction.* Then she deleted the sentence, because the 2024 review was not a demonstration she could give to the client who had commissioned it.

She typed: *Thursday is fine. I'll walk the committee through the standard workflow and a historical case from 2021.*

The queue was a screen and the screen was a list, and the list was the product's whole argument.

Ilsa had learned to read it the way a pilot reads an approach plate: not the numbers first, but the shape. Two hundred and twenty-six rows, sorted by priority then age. Eleven rows with a judgement pending. Forty-one rows with a covariance that had grown since the last epoch, which meant the object had not been observed recently, which meant a dish had been tasked elsewhere.

The covariance column was the one that mattered and the one nobody watched. A small covariance meant a good orbit and a confident classification. A large covariance meant the object might be somewhere else, and an object that might be somewhere else was an object that could be anything.

She filtered to large covariance. Nine rows. She opened the first.

The first was a spent stage from a launch four years ago, with a covariance that had grown because the stage had been tumbling and the tumbling changed its drag. The classification was right and the covariance was honest, and the row was a good row.

The second was a fragment from a breakup in 2019. Same story.

The third was K-7741.

She sat with it for a moment. The covariance was large because the object had last been observed eleven days ago, and the observation gap was explained in the log by a tasking conflict, and the tasking conflict was explained by a note that said *dish six reassigned, client request*.

She looked at the note for a while. A client request was a normal thing. Clients requested observations all the time; the requests went into a queue and the queue was worked in priority order, and a client request could move a row up or down that queue.

She opened the tasking history for the eleven days. The history was a list of every time the object had been scheduled and every time it had been displaced. It had been scheduled four times and displaced four times.

The displacements had three reasons between them: *higher priority object*, *dish maintenance*, and *client request*.

The client request was the one that had happened twice.

She wrote the pattern in her notebook: *object scheduled 4, displaced 4, twice by client request, covariance now 4x nominal.*

Then she sat back and looked at the queue, which was a screen and a list and a product, and she thought about the fact that the product's whole argument was that every row on it had been looked at by somebody, and that the looking was the thing the client paid for, and that a row displaced four times had been looked at by nobody at all.

At 08:50 the duty officer came back with the tablet.

"Vantage confirmed Thursday. Two hours. They want the walkthrough in the catalogue bay, not the conference room."

"Fine."

"They also want to see a live reclassification. I told them we'd do a historical one."

"You told them right."

He looked at her screen. The K-7741 row was open, and the anomaly was in the field below the fold.

"That's the one Priya's been asking about," he said.

"Has she?"

"She asked me on Monday whether the object was in the queue. I said it was. She said to keep it in the queue." He shrugged. "That's all she said."

Ilsa looked at the row. Keep it in the queue. The phrase could mean anything: keep working it, keep it visible, keep it out of the summary.

She wrote the phrase down, because a sentence from a manager is a fact about the week, and the week was going to need facts.

At 09:10 she pulled K-7741's raw returns, because she had written *drag consistent with hollow stage* in a box, and because she had learned, eight years ago, that *consistent with* was a phrase that could be true and still be the last thing anybody read.

# Chapter 04

There were four registries that mattered, and Ilsa had access to three of them.

The first was the United Nations launch register, which was a database maintained by an office in Vienna and populated by states on a voluntary basis, with a lag that could be measured in months and an honesty that could be measured in nothing at all. The second was the national notification system of the launching state, which in practice meant the United States, Russia, China, India, Japan and a handful of others, each with its own definition of what constituted a launch requiring notification. The third was the insurers' launch register, which was commercial and reliable and covered only payloads that somebody had insured.

The fourth was Kestrel's own purchase log, which recorded the provenance of every observation block the company had ever bought, and which Ilsa could not open because it was part of the contract file.

She started with the insurers, because insurers do not insure things they have not been told about. K-7741's epoch put its launch eighteen months ago, in a window of nine days. She pulled every launch insured in that window and compared the orbital planes. Forty-one launches. None matched. A launch into an unlogged plane was not impossible; it was expensive, and it was the kind of thing that showed up in the reinsurance market as an unexplained loss three years later.

She moved to the UN register. Two hundred and six notifications in the window, most of them for satellites, a few for cargo. The planes did not match. She widened the window to thirty days. Still nothing. She widened it to ninety and found a launch from a sea platform, notified by a flag-of-convenience state, carrying a communications test payload. The plane was close but not the same, and the mass was wrong, and the notification had been filed eleven days after the launch, which was late even by the standards of the register.

She wrote the notification number on a sticky note and put it on the edge of her monitor, where she kept the things she did not yet know how to use.

Then she went to find Hal.

The radar room was two doors down and four degrees colder. Hal kept it that way because the equipment ran hot and because he liked to wear a cardigan to work without comment. He was at the console with his back to the door, watching a track that was doing nothing.

"K-7741," she said.

"I saw the note."

"What did you see eighteen months ago?"

He did not turn around. The track on his screen crawled a centimetre and stopped.

"Who told you eighteen months?"

"The epoch. Give or take."

"So you did the arithmetic." He turned. His face was the face of a man deciding whether to be tired. "There was a track. February, the year before last. Nineteen-something degrees elevation, coming up out of the south-west, not in the tasking list. I asked the duty officer and he said it was a calibration target for the new fence."

"Was it?"

"It was a real object. Calibration targets are real objects; that's the point of them. They put a corner reflector on a spent stage and track it to check the gain. This one didn't have a corner reflector. It had a manoeuvre."

"You saw a manoeuvre."

"I saw a track that came back three nights running with a different period. That's a manoeuvre or a bad fit. I asked, and I was told to log it as a calibration target and not to task it again."

Ilsa sat down on the edge of the desk, which was not allowed, and Hal did not tell her not to.

"Did you log it?"

"I logged it as a calibration target." He reached into the drawer under the console and took out a notebook. It was a pocket notebook with a cardboard cover, the kind sold in hardware shops. "I also wrote the time and the azimuth in here, because I'm old and I don't trust the system to keep anything past the next migration."

He flipped through it. The pages were covered in pencil, in a hand that had been trained to write legibly on a moving ship. He found a page, looked at it, and did not tear it out.

"Azimuth 214, elevation 19, 02:41. That's the first night. Second night it came back eight minutes early. Third night it didn't come back at all, and the fence had a gap in the tasking list where it should have been."

"Why didn't you escalate?"

"To who?" He said it without heat. "I'm a radar operator on a lease. The fence belongs to the customer. The tasking list belongs to the customer. I wrote it down and I went home."

She looked at the notebook. "Can I have the page?"

"Not yet." He put it back in the drawer. "First you tell me what's in the file that made you come down here at lunchtime."

She told him. The mass. The tumble. The manoeuvre history that the catalogue did not have. The conjunction probability at fifty-eight hours, rising. Meridian Station's orbit, and the six people in it.

Hal listened with his hands flat on the console, which was how he listened to anything that mattered.

"Fifty-eight hours," he said.

"Give or take the solar forecast. If the atmosphere inflates, it's less."

He nodded slowly. Then he did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the whiteboard, the one that put the two dates together: the track he had been told not to log, and the epoch of the object she had reclassified at 06:52.

"Fifty-six hours from now," he said, "that thing is going to be inside the screening volume for Meridian, and the catalogue is going to say there's nothing there."

"Fifty-six from the last epoch."

"Same thing, near enough." He opened the drawer again. "They told me it was a calibration target. I wrote the time down because I'm old, not because I'm brave."

The UN register had a contact address, and the contact address had a phone number, and the phone number was answered by a man who introduced himself as the duty officer for notifications and who was, by the sound of him, in a room with a lot of paper.

"I'm calling about a notification," Ilsa said. "A launch from a sea platform, eighteen months ago. The notification was filed eleven days late by a flag state."

"Which flag state?"

She told him.

"Hold on." The sound of paper. "We've got the file. It's a short file."

"Does it say why it was late?"

"It says the notification was received after the launch and that the delay was due to administrative processing."

"Does it say who the operator was?"

"It lists a company. Pellow Maritime Holdings."

"And the payload?"

"Communications test payload, approximately four hundred kilograms." He read it the way a man reads a form he has read before. "That's what it says. I can't tell you whether it's true."

"Do you get many of those?"

"Late notifications? A few a year. Most of them are small operators who don't have a compliance officer and file when their lawyer tells them to." He paused. "The ones from sea platforms are always late. There's no port state to nag them."

She asked whether the register had ever received a follow-up, a correction, a note of any kind, for that notification. He checked, and said no, and then he said something that she wrote down.

"The register is a notification system," he said. "It records that a state said a launch happened. It doesn't verify the launch, and it doesn't track the objects. If the state says four hundred kilograms and the object is nine hundred, the register will never know."

"Who would know?"

"Whoever tracks the object." He said it without irony. "That's you, isn't it."

---

The insurers' register was easier and worse. It was a commercial database with an API and a subscription, and Kestrel had a subscription, and the subscription covered launches but not payloads, which meant she could see that a launch had been insured and not what it carried.

She searched the window. Forty-one launches, each with a launch date, a vehicle, a site, and a policy number. None of them had a site at sea.

She widened the search to the whole quarter. Fifty-eight launches. Still no sea site.

She searched for the platform by name. Nothing. The insurers' register did not know that *Aster Minor* existed.

She sat back. A launch from a sea platform, uninsured, unnotified for eleven days, with a payload mass that was less than half of what the object's drag implied. Every registry she could reach had a hole in it, and the holes were in different places, and the object had gone through all of them.

She wrote on the sticky note: *no registry covers a launch that nobody insured and nobody verified.* Then she put the sticky note on the monitor with the others, and looked at the row of yellow squares, which was becoming a kind of map.

He tore the page out and held it out to her, and she took it, and the paper was warm from the drawer.

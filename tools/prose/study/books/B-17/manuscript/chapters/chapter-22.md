# Chapter 22

The insurer's analyst called at 11:20 and said his name was Peter and that he had eleven minutes.

"I've read the summary page," he said. "I haven't read the forty pages."

"Then read the summary page and ask me questions."

"I'm asking." There was the sound of a keyboard. "You say the object is a spacecraft. The catalogue says debris. Your own company's catalogue."

"Yes."

"And you signed the reclassification."

"Twice. Once in 2024, on a day I wasn't in the building. Once this week, because the file said debris and I didn't look at the mass field until the next morning."

"So you're the person who made the error."

"I'm the person whose name is on it."

Peter was quiet for a moment. "That's not the same thing."

"No."

"Okay. The summary says the probability is one in three hundred. My threshold for action on a crewed asset is one in ten thousand. One in three hundred clears it by a factor of thirty. Why is this a complaint and not a claim?"

"Because there hasn't been a loss."

"Yet."

"Yet."

"Right." Another keyboard. "Here's my problem, Ms Merrick. I can open a file. I can notify the operator. I can require the operator to demonstrate that it has assessed the conjunction. What I can't do is require the operator to manoeuvre, because the operator's propellant is not my asset and the manoeuvre is an operational decision. And I can't require your company to reclassify, because your company's contract says the classification is the client's instruction."

"I know what the contract says."

"Then you know what I can do." He said it without unkindness. "I can make the operator answer a question in writing by Monday. That's the whole of my authority."

"Monday is after Friday."

"Friday's the conjunction."

"Yes."

"Then Monday is too late," Peter said, "and we both know it, and I'm going to open the file anyway because the file is the thing that protects the operator's position if something happens."

"The operator's position."

"The operator's legal position. Yes." He paused. "I'm sorry. I'm not being callous. I have four hundred files. This one has a crewed asset and a probability that clears the threshold and a provider that says debris, and the only lever I have is a written question with a deadline. If you want a lever that works before Friday, it isn't in my office."

"Where is it?"

"In the operator's waiver process. A station can manoeuvre for debris avoidance on the operator's authority, without the insurer, if the operator's flight rules say so. Your company sells the operator the warning that triggers the rule. No warning, no trigger."

"So it comes back to the warning."

"It always comes back to the warning." He sounded tired. "That's not cynicism. That's the design. The warning is the product. Everything downstream of it is somebody else's procedure."

Ilsa looked at the audit on the table, forty-one pages, and thought about the four hours she had spent rewriting the summary page.

"Peter."

"Mm."

"The summary page. Was it any good?"

"It was clear," he said. "I read it in eleven minutes and I knew what you wanted me to do. That's a good summary page."

"Then why isn't it enough?"

Peter's eleventh minute ran to thirty-four, and in the twenty-third minute he asked the question that mattered.

"If this object is a weapon," he said, "what does that change for us?"

The line was quiet. Ilsa could hear a keyboard.

"It changes the loss model," she said. "A debris strike is an accident. A deliberate approach is an act, and an act has an actor, and an actor has insurance or a state behind it."

"Or neither."

"Or neither."

"Then the loss model doesn't change. The recovery model changes." The keyboard stopped. "We insure the station. If the station is damaged by an object, we pay the operator and we subrogate against whoever's liable. If the object belongs to nobody, we pay and we recover nothing. If the object belongs to a state, we pay and we spend four years in a treaty claim. If the object belongs to a company, we pay and we sue a company that has one director and a mail drop."

"You've done this before."

"I've done this for nineteen years. The object's ownership is the whole of the case. Everything else is the size of the cheque."

Ilsa looked at the notebook. The chain was on the page in front of her, nine links long.

"The platform is registered to a company with one director," she said. "The company's address is a mail drop. The launch was notified late by a flag state. The payload mass in the notification is less than half of what the drag implies."

"So the company that owns the platform is a shell."

"It's a shell with a vessel and a launch capability."

"Shells with vessels are common," Peter said. "Shells with launch capability are not." He was quiet for a moment. "Ms Merrick, I'm going to tell you something about how this works on my side. When a claim has an actor, the first thing we do is send a request for information to the actor's insurer. If the actor has no insurer, we send it to the state of registry. If the state of registry doesn't answer, we send it to the state's mission in New York, and the mission sends it to a ministry, and the ministry sends it back to the mission, and after eighteen months we have a file that says the state is considering its position."

"And then?"

"And then we reserve. We put a number on the claim that's large enough to cover the loss and small enough to survive an audit, and we wait."

"What's the number for a station?"

"The station's insured value is on the policy. The number I'm allowed to discuss is the reserve, and the reserve is not the value." He paused. "But I'll tell you what the reserve does. It changes the behaviour of everybody in the chain. A reserve is a statement that the insurer believes the loss is possible. When a reserve goes up, the operator's board asks questions. When the board asks questions, the operator's ground team gets a budget. When the ground team gets a budget, it can afford to manoeuvre."

"You're saying the reserve is the thing that buys the fuel."

"I'm saying the reserve is the thing that makes the fuel look cheap."

Ilsa wrote that down.

"One more question," she said. "If I file a report that says the object is a vehicle, does that help or hurt the claim?"

"It helps the claim and hurts you," Peter said. "A report that says the object is a vehicle is evidence that the risk was knowable. Knowable risk is the thing we price. It's also the thing a regulator uses to ask why nobody acted."

"And the company?"

"The company will say the report is one analyst's opinion."

"And you'll say?"

"I'll say the analyst sent me raw data and a fit and a chain of custody, and that the company's own contract required it to produce the data on request." Another pause. "I've been doing this for nineteen years, Ms Merrick. I've never had an analyst send me a chain. I've had a hundred analysts send me conclusions. The chain is the thing I can use."

The claim file arrived at 12:40 as an attachment with eleven pages and a schedule, and the schedule was the page that mattered.

Ilsa read it on her phone in the corridor outside the conference room, because the conference room was booked for a client call and she had twenty minutes.

The schedule was the station's insurance programme. There were four layers. The first was the operator's own retention, which was a figure large enough to matter to the operator and small enough to be paid without a board meeting. The second was the primary market, which was three syndicates and a company. The third was a layer that started where the second stopped. The fourth was a layer that existed mostly on paper and was funded by a reinsurance treaty.

Peter had written in the margin of the third page: *the object's owner is not insured by us. The station is. Subrogation runs against the actor, not the asset.*

She read that twice and then read the subrogation clause, which was four lines and said that the insurer was subrogated to the insured's rights of recovery against any third party.

Rights of recovery against a third party. The third party was a company with one director and a mail drop and a vessel that had gone dark for eleven days.

She called Peter at 12:55.

"The schedule's clear," she said. "The reserve is a claim on the second and third layers, and the subrogation runs at a company that has no assets in any jurisdiction that will answer a letter."

"That's the shape."

"So the reserve buys nothing. The station manoeuvres, the claim is paid, and the recovery is a file that gets closed after four years."

"The recovery is a file that gets closed," Peter said. "But that's not what the reserve is for."

"You said it was for the manoeuvre."

"I said it makes the fuel look cheap. It does that by changing what the operator's board can see. A board can see a reserve. A board cannot see a risk." He paused. "The reserve is a way of putting a number on something that would otherwise be a paragraph in a risk report."

Ilsa looked at the corridor. A man from finance went past with a trolley and said good afternoon to nobody in particular.

"Peter. What do you need from me to raise it?"

"Four things. A written description of the object. A statement of the basis for the description. The raw data, or a path to it. And your name."

"My name."

"Your name, your role, and a sentence that says the description is your professional judgement and not the company's position." He said it evenly. "I'm not asking you to sign away your job. I'm asking you to sign something that says the judgement is yours, because a reserve has to be attached to a judgement, and a judgement that belongs to nobody cannot be raised."

"And if the company later says the judgement was wrong?"

"Then the judgement was wrong, and the reserve was still properly raised, because it was raised on the evidence that existed." A pause. "That's the difference between an act and an error. An act has an author. An error has a process. I can price an act. I can't price a process."

Ilsa stood in the corridor with the phone against her ear.

"Send me the form," she said.

---

The form was one page. It had four fields and a signature block and a sentence at the bottom that said *this statement is made for the purposes of insurance assessment and may be disclosed to a regulator*.

She filled it in at her desk, in the open, with the door to the corridor ajar, which was a thing she did deliberately and would not have been able to explain if asked.

Field one: *A nine-hundred-kilogram vehicle in a sixty-one degree orbit, catalogued as K-7741, with a mass inconsistent with its declared launch mass by a factor of 2.2 and a manoeuvre history of three events in nine days.*

Field two: *Basis: three independent drag fits, radar cross-section history, three orbital determinations consistent to 0.4 metres per second, and a reconstructed launch from a sea platform whose notification was eleven days late.*

Field three: *Path: books/K-7741/raw/ — eleven files, checksums attached.*

Field four: *Additional: a second object observed by two sensors on the same night and dropped by both, currently uncatalogued.*

She read it back. Field four was the sentence that would be quoted, and she knew it, and she left it in.

Then she signed it and scanned it and sent it at 13:20, and copied it to the folder, and wrote in the notebook: *13:20 — statement of judgement filed with insurer. Field four is the one that will travel.*

"Because I'm not the person who decides," he said. "I'm the person who opens the file."

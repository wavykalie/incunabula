# Chapter 08

Meridian Station crossed the Panamint horizon at 07:20, which meant Ilsa was at her desk with the tracking schedule open and a cup of coffee she had forgotten.

The pass lasted eleven minutes. The station came up out of the south-west, bright enough to be a morning star if you were standing outside with the right eyes, and the dish tracked it with a small mechanical whine that she could feel through the floor. The station's transponder reported its state vector, its propellant margin, its attitude, and a conjunction summary generated by Kestrel's own product and uplinked on the previous pass.

The conjunction summary listed nothing.

Ilsa read it twice. The summary was a table with columns for object, time of closest approach, miss distance, and probability. The table had a header and no rows. At the bottom there was a line that said *screening volume: 25 km radial, 50 km along-track, 50 km cross-track*, and a line that said *no tracked objects within screening volume for the next 72 hours*.

She looked at the clock. Fifty-six hours to the conjunction, and the summary said seventy-two.

The summary was not lying. It was answering a different question. The screening volume was defined around tracked objects above a priority threshold, and K-7741 was below the threshold because it was debris, and debris did not generate a row because four hundred rows a day would destroy the product. The summary was correct, and the station was being told there was nothing there by a document that had been designed to be correct.

She picked up the phone and put it down. There was no number to call that would not go through Priya.

---

On the station, the morning began with the water ration.

Ada Reyes listened to Bex Oulton explain the conservation schedule while she repaired a watch. The watch was a 1970s manual-wind with a bent balance staff, and she had been repairing it for three weeks, which was long enough that the crew had stopped asking about it. The bench was bolted to the lab wall. The screwdriver was magnetised. The watch was not going to work.

"Two litres a day for drinking and food," Bex said. "No showers. Wet wipes. The recycler's running at eighty per cent and Marek says the membrane's due."

"Marek says the membrane's been due since March."

"Marek says it's due now."

Ada put the watch down. "How long?"

"Eleven days at this rate. Eight if we lose another cell."

"Then we're fine. Tell them two litres and no showers, and tell them it's a drill, because it is."

Bex left. Ada picked up the watch again and did not work on it.

The station was a commercial platform leased to three tenants and operated by Vantage Launch Systems, which meant that the ground did not belong to her, the schedule did not belong to her, and the water did. She commanded six people and a pressurised volume, and the limits of her authority were written in a document she had read eleven times. She could refuse an unsafe instruction. She could not refuse a lawful one. The document did not define lawful.

At 07:31 the pass began. June Park was on comms, and the loop was quiet, and the conjunction summary came down with the rest of the traffic. Ada read it on the tablet strapped to her thigh while she floated in the node.

*No tracked objects within screening volume for the next 72 hours.*

She looked at the number for a while. Then she opened the previous day's summary and compared them. They were identical, which was normal, and the format was identical, which was also normal.

"June. When did the summary change format?"

"It didn't."

"The header. It used to say *screening volume 25/50/50* on one line. Now it's on two."

June checked. "It's a software release. Ground pushed it Tuesday."

"Did they say why?"

"They never say why."

Ada put the tablet away. The station turned, and the light through the cupola moved across the handrail in a way that she had stopped noticing in her second month and had started noticing again in her fourth year.

At the end of the pass she keyed the microphone.

"Meridian, ground. Summary received, no tracked objects, all nominal."

"Copy, Meridian. All nominal."

She released the key. The word sat in the module with her, weightless and stupid, and she went back to the watch.

---

At Panamint, Ilsa watched the pass end on the tracking schedule: the elevation counting down, the signal strength dropping, the dish beginning its slow turn to the next task. The station's state vector stopped updating.

She opened the sensor artefact report she had filed and read the acknowledgement. It said the report had been received and assigned to the duty officer, and that the severity had been set to high, and that an independent fit had been commissioned at 16:40 the previous day.

Then she opened the conjunction summary that had gone up on the pass, the one that said no tracked objects, and she printed it, and she put it in the folder with the photographs from the records room.

The station's morning had a shape, and the shape was the same every day, which was the point of it.

Ada woke at 05:30 station time, which was a number that had been chosen by the operator to align with a ground shift in a country she had never visited. She did fifteen minutes of resistance exercise on the treadmill that was bolted to the floor of the lab module, and then she checked the station's status on the tablet, which took four minutes if nothing had happened and forty if something had.

Nothing had happened. The cabin pressure was 101.3 kilopascals. The oxygen partial pressure was 21.4. The carbon dioxide scrubber was on its second bed. The attitude was stable, the arrays were tracking, the propellant margin was 1.9 metres per second, and the water recycler was at eighty-one per cent efficiency, which was a number Marek had been watching for a month.

She floated to the galley and made coffee, which on the station meant a bag with a tube and a valve, and drank it while Bex read the handover.

The handover was the same every day. The station was a machine that required a list, and the list was read aloud by one crew member to another, and the reading aloud was the check, because a list that is only read silently is a list that gets skimmed.

"Water ration in effect," Bex said. "Two litres per person per day. No showers. Wet wipes. The recycler's at eighty-one. Marek's written a note about the membrane."

"Tell Marek the membrane is on the next resupply."

"Marek says the next resupply is eleven weeks away."

"Tell Marek I know."

Bex read the rest of the list. The experiment rack in the lab module was at temperature. The crystal-growth experiment was in its fourth week and the samples were viable. The comms schedule had four passes. The medical check was at 09:00. The exercise rota had been updated because Katya had swapped her slot with Sam.

Ada listened to all of it, and signed the tablet at the end, and the day began.

---

Katya Belov was in the lab module when Ada came through at 09:20, and she was looking at a rack of sample cassettes with the expression of a woman who had been looking at them for four weeks.

"Anything?"

"The third cassette has a defect," Katya said. "A bubble. Two millimetres. It's within tolerance, but it changes the growth interface."

"Can you fix it?"

"I can rotate the cassette and restart the run. It costs six days." She did not look up. "The experiment is worth more than the station. That's not a figure of speech. The insurer's schedule values the rack at eleven million, and the station's hull is insured for less."

"The station has six people in it."

"The station has six people in it," Katya agreed, "and the rack has a crystal that three companies have paid for, and the two facts are not on the same schedule."

Ada looked at the cassettes. They were small and grey and ordinary, and the crystal inside the third one was growing at a rate that had been specified by a contract.

"Rotate it," she said. "Restart the run. Log the six days."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Log it."

---

At 11:00 Sam Iyer did the medical check, which was a blood pressure cuff and a questionnaire and a conversation that Ada suspected was the actual point of the check.

"How are you sleeping?"

"Six hours."

"Broken?"

"Twice."

"That's the water ration," Sam said. "Everybody's waking up. It's the dehydration and the schedule." He wrapped the cuff around her arm. "You're also the commander, which means you're carrying the schedule in your head. That's a known cause of waking up twice."

"The schedule is fine."

"The schedule is a list of things that can go wrong." He pumped the cuff. "You want my honest medical opinion? The station is safer than any place I've worked. The risk here is not the hull. It's the ground telling us everything is nominal while we do the arithmetic ourselves."

Ada looked at him. Sam was fifty-two and had been a flight surgeon for eleven years and had a way of saying true things in a tone that made them sound like small talk.

"The summary's accurate," she said.

"The summary's accurate and useless," Sam said. "That's what I'd put in the log if anybody asked me."

He took the cuff off and wrote the numbers down, and Ada went back to the node, and the station turned, and the sun came over the horizon at 11:41 and filled the cupola with a light that made everybody stop talking for a moment, which was the one thing on the schedule that happened every day and was never written down.

The water recycler was Marek's machine, and Marek talked about it the way other people talked about a relative who was ill.

"It's at eighty-one per cent," he said. "It was at eighty-six in January. The membrane's fouling and the fouling is cumulative, and the replacement membrane is on a resupply that's eleven weeks away."

"Can you clean it?"

"I can back-flush it. That buys two per cent. I've back-flushed it twice this month." He was floating by the rack with a tablet in one hand. "The ration is two litres a person. That's drinking and food prep. No showers. The wipes are counted."

"Are the crew all right?"

"The crew are thirsty and pretending not to be." Marek looked at the tablet. "Katya's the one to watch. She's running an experiment that needs her to be sharp, and she's not drinking enough because she doesn't want to use the ration before the evening."

"Then tell her to drink."

"I've told her. She's a principal investigator. She hears advice as data." He shrugged. "I've written it in the medical log. Sam's seen it."

Ada looked at the rack. The recycler was a grey box with a pump and a membrane and a display that showed a number, and the number was eighty-one, and the number was the reason six people were counting wipes.

"All right," she said. "New rule. The ration is two litres and the two litres are a floor, not a ceiling. If anybody's urine colour is off, they drink and they log it and I'll answer to the ground."

"That's not what the ration says."

"The ration is a planning figure. I'm the commander and I'm telling you the two litres is a floor." Ada looked at him. "Put it in the log with my name on it."

Marek wrote it down.

---

At 12:40 June brought the screening annex to the node, which was a document Ada had read twice and had never needed.

"Section four," June said. "Screening volume. It says the provider screens a volume around the station that extends thirty kilometres along the velocity vector, twenty kilometres radial, and five kilometres cross-track."

"Which is a box."

"Which is a box, and it's a box because a sphere would cost more." June turned the tablet. "The box is generated from the station's state vector, and the state vector has an error, and the error is included in the box by making it bigger. That's why the box is thirty by twenty by five."

"And the object came from outside the box?"

"The object was inside the box for the four days it was in the shell, and the summary said there was nothing in the box, because the summary only lists objects above priority four." June paused. "So the box was screened and the screening found nothing, and the nothing was true in the sense that the provider's product only counts what it counts."

Ada looked at the annex. It was written in the flat prose of a technical schedule, and every paragraph was true, and the truth was the problem.

"Log it," she said. "Both the box and the priority. With the words *screened and missed*."

The folder was getting thick. It was the only thing in the building that was getting thicker.

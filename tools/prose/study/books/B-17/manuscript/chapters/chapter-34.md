# Chapter 34

The second object manoeuvred at 23:40, and the manoeuvre was small enough that the public feed did not flag it.

Ilsa saw it because she had left a script running on her laptop, a comparator that pulled the object's state vector every ten minutes and printed the delta between the predicted and observed positions. The script had been running since Wednesday. It was the kind of thing Femi would have written with better variable names, and she had written it at one in the morning with the names she used in her own notebook.

At 23:40 the residual jumped from eleven metres to two hundred and forty. She sat up in bed and pulled the laptop onto her knees and ran the fit.

The second object had raised its perigee by eighteen kilometres. The change was consistent with a burn of 0.2 metres per second, applied along the velocity vector, and it put the object into an orbit that was slightly higher and slightly slower than the first object's.

Slightly slower meant it would fall behind. In four days it would be twelve minutes behind the first object instead of forty-one.

She sat in the dark bedroom with the laptop and the numbers.

A dead object did not manoeuvre. A piece of a rocket did not manoeuvre. A test target did not manoeuvre unless somebody was flying it, and the somebody had just adjusted its phasing to match the vehicle it had been launched with.

She wrote in the notebook: *second object active. burn 0.2 m/s, perigee +18 km, phasing to trail first by 12 min.*

Then she sat with the pen in her hand and thought about what a two-vehicle system was for.

A vehicle and a target. A vehicle and a witness. A vehicle and a second weapon. A vehicle and a tank of propellant that could be transferred. A vehicle and a relay that could talk to a ground station the first vehicle could not see.

She did not know which. She knew that the second object had been invisible to the catalogue for eighteen months, that it had no number, that the only record of its existence was a pencil note in a notebook and a single radar hit that the fence had discarded, and that it had just made a decision.

She thought about the phrase *the difference between debris and a weapon*, which had been in her head since Wednesday, and which she had used in the briefing in Colorado Springs and in the audit and in her own notebook. The phrase had assumed that the difference was a property of the object. The manoeuvre made it a property of the behaviour: a thing that decided where to go was not debris, whatever its mass or its cross-section or its catalogue entry.

At 23:55 she called Femi, because he was awake, and because there was nobody else she could call at that hour who would not be frightened by the numbers.

"The second one moved," she said.

"I know. I've got the same script." He sounded like a man who had been staring at a screen for six hours. "It's phasing to the first one."

"Yes."

"Which means they're a pair."

"Which means they're a pair."

"Are you going to file it?"

She looked at the laptop. The residual was steady now, the new orbit established, the object coasting.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The regulator opens at nine. The operator's duty desk is staffed all night, but a filing at midnight will be read on Monday. A filing at nine will be read at nine."

"That's very procedural of you."

"I've had a long week of learning how procedure works."

He laughed, once, without much in it.

"Ilsa. If they're a pair, and the first one is the vehicle, then the second one is the thing the vehicle is for. And if the second one just manoeuvred to match the first one's phasing, then the vehicle isn't done."

"No."

"So what do we do?"

She looked at the window. The sky over the plateau was clear, and the station was somewhere above it, moving at seven and a half kilometres a second, with six people in it and a hole in the truss and a water ration.

"We file," she said. "And we keep watching. And we don't tell the station that the thing that missed them is still up there with a friend, because the station has enough to do."

"That's not a plan."

Femi had found the fence's discarded hit on Wednesday and had not told her until Saturday, because he had wanted to be sure.

"It's in the raw returns," he said on the phone at 00:20. "The fence discarded it on the same night the radar room dropped the track. Two systems, same object, eleven minutes apart, both dropped."

"Both dropped by an operator instruction?"

"The fence's note says *uncorrelated, below reporting threshold, close track*. The threshold is a signal-to-noise number. The object was below it for the fence because the fence's antenna is smaller." He paused. "But the fence's archive keeps the return even when it closes the track. That's the difference between the fence and us. The fence keeps everything and reports some of it. We keep some of it and report everything."

Ilsa sat up in bed with the laptop on her knees.

"Can you get the return?"

"I already have it. I pulled it on Wednesday and I've been checking it against the tape ever since." The sound of a keyboard. "The return's consistent with a nine-hundred-kilogram object at that altitude and that geometry. The covariance's big, because it's one return. But the position agrees with the tape to within four kilometres, and four kilometres at that range is the same object."

"So the second object was seen by two independent sensors on the same night and dropped by both."

"Seen, logged, and dropped," Femi said. "That's the part that bothers me. One drop is a coincidence. Two drops in eleven minutes is a policy."

"Or a coincidence twice."

"Or a coincidence twice." He was quiet for a moment. "Ilsa, I've been thinking about the phrase *below reporting threshold*. It's a real phrase. It means the return wasn't strong enough to be worth a report. But the threshold is set by the operator, and the operator can set it wherever it wants, and the reason the threshold is where it is, is that a lower threshold produces more reports and more reports cost more money."

"So the threshold is an economic decision."

"The threshold is an economic decision that has been described as a technical one." Another pause. "That's what I keep finding. Every layer of this is a decision that's been dressed as a fact."

---

Ilsa looked at the window. The sky was clear and the station was somewhere above it, and the second object was somewhere above the station, in an orbit that had been adjusted twice in four days.

"Femi. Send me the fence return. Not the summary. The return."

"I'll send it now."

"And Femi—don't put it in the shared drive."

"I know."

"Put it in the folder."

"There's a folder?"

"There's a folder," Ilsa said. "It's on my personal account and it's got everything in it, and if this goes the way I think it's going to go, the folder is going to be the only complete record of the week."

"That's not a secure place for client data."

"No," she said. "It's not. But it's a place that exists, and the shared drive is a place that can be edited."

"No," she said. "It's what we have."

# Chapter 36

The catalogue correction went out on Monday at 11:20, and it was attributed to an internal review.

Ilsa saw it because she still had the alert, and because the alert had not been turned off when her access was suspended. The correction changed K-7741's classification from *breakup debris* to *unknown payload*, changed its priority from 4 to 1, and added a note: *classification corrected following internal review; conjunction warning issued 20:22 Friday; operator manoeuvre executed 20:40 Friday.*

Her name was not on the correction. The reviewer field said *catalogue operations*.

She read it three times. Then she sat back and looked at the kitchen, which was full of morning light and had a stack of approach plates on the end of the table and a folder that no longer closed.

The correction was what she had asked for on Tuesday. It had taken six days, a near miss, a hole in a truss, an insurer's clause, a regulator's receipt, and a client's arithmetic. It had arrived as a line in a log with no author.

She opened the station's public status page. The page said the station was nominal, the array was at ninety-six per cent, and the water ration was in effect. It said the penetrator on the truss had been assessed as a micrometeoroid. It did not mention the object.

She opened the regulator's portal. The case was open, the supplement was logged, and the status said *under assessment*.

She opened the insurer's portal. The ticket was open and assigned to a team.

None of the records said what she knew. They said what had been filed.

At noon Priya called.

"Have you seen the correction?"

"Yes."

"Then you know it doesn't have your name on it."

"I noticed."

"That was my decision." Priya's voice was flat. "The correction is a catalogue action. If it carried an analyst's name, it would be read as an admission that the analyst's audit was right and the company was wrong, and the company cannot make that admission while the regulator's case is open."

"The company can't admit it."

"The company can correct the record without characterising it. That's what the note does. It says the classification was corrected. It doesn't say why, and it doesn't say who."

"It says internal review."

"It says internal review, which is true."

Ilsa looked at the alert on the screen. The object was now priority 1, which meant it generated a row in every conjunction summary for every crewed asset in its shell. The station would see it on the next pass. The row would say *unknown payload*, and the crew would read it, and the word would be there.

"The station gets the row now," she said.

"The station gets the row. Meridian gets a warning on every pass until the object is out of the shell. So do eleven other crewed assets." Priya paused. "It's the outcome you wanted."

"It's the outcome I filed for."

"Yes." Another pause. "Ilsa, the review of your classification activity has concluded. The finding is that you followed the policy. The special project leave is lifted, effective tomorrow. Your access is restored, with a note on the account."

"A note."

"A note that says classification activity is subject to second-signature review for ninety days. It's a supervision condition, not a disciplinary finding."

"So I come back, and somebody checks my work."

"Somebody checks your work." Priya's voice was quiet. "That's the best outcome available. It's also, if you think about it, what you've been asking for since Tuesday: a second signature on every classification."

Ilsa looked at the folder on the table. Forty-one pages, eleven pages, photographs, a torn page from a notebook, a business card with a number on the back.

"That's not what I was asking for," she said.

The client's technical contact was called Haddad and she came to the building on Monday afternoon with two colleagues and a folder that she did not open.

"I've read the correction," she said. "I've also read the addendum. I want to understand how a catalogue entry can be wrong for eighteen months and then be corrected in a single line with no explanation."

"The explanation is in the addendum," Ilsa said.

"The addendum is your document. I'm asking about the correction."

The correction was a single line. Ilsa had read it eleven times.

"The correction is a catalogue action," she said. "The catalogue action changes the classification. The reason is in the review file, and the review file is with legal."

"Which means it's privileged."

"Which means it's with legal."

Haddad looked at her for a moment. She was fifty and precise and she had the manner of a person who had spent her career being the only person in the room who had read the annex.

"Ms Merrick. I'm going to tell you what my problem is, and it isn't the object. My problem is that I have a board meeting on Wednesday and I have to explain why the station received a conjunction warning from a public bulletin before it received one from the company it pays for conjunction warnings."

"The bulletin was faster because it uses its own catalogue."

"The bulletin was faster because our provider's catalogue said the object was debris." Haddad said it without heat. "That's the sentence I have to say to the board. I'd like to be able to say it with a document that explains how it happened, and the document I have is a one-line correction and a forty-one-page audit written by an analyst who has been placed on leave."

"I'm back from leave."

"I know. I read that too."

Ilsa sat back. The conference room was warm and the light was flat and Haddad's colleagues had not said a word.

"What would you like me to do?"

"I'd like you to tell me what you think happened, in your own words, for the record. Not the audit. The audit is a document. I want the version you'd tell a colleague."

Ilsa thought about it. The version she would tell a colleague was shorter than the audit and less careful.

"An object was launched from a platform that nobody registered properly. It was delivered into an orbit that a commercial catalogue had been told to classify as debris. It manoeuvred three times and it passed the station at three hundred and eighty metres. The company's product didn't warn because the product's warning threshold depends on a classification, and the classification was wrong, and the reason the classification was wrong is a standing instruction from 2021 that was applied to a case it was never written for."

Haddad wrote for a while.

"That's four sentences," she said.

"The audit is forty-one pages because each sentence needs a source."

"The board will read the four sentences." Haddad closed her folder. "Can I quote them?"

"You can quote them and attribute them. I'd rather they were attributed than paraphrased."

"They'll be attributed." Haddad stood. "One more thing. The board is going to ask whether we should keep buying the product. I'm going to tell them yes, with conditions. One of the conditions is that the provider's catalogue corrections are notified to us within twenty-four hours, in writing, with a reason. The other is that the analyst who found this is named in the file."

"You don't need to do that."

"I don't need to. I want to." Haddad looked at her. "I've been on the other side of a correction before. The person who finds it usually doesn't survive it. I'd like this one to be different, and I can only make it different in the small part of the world I control."

The industry newsletter ran the correction on Tuesday morning with a headline that said *Catalogue operator reclassifies object after station manoeuvre*.

The newsletter was read by about four thousand people and quoted by two of them. The first quote was in a trade blog, which said the reclassification showed that "the screening system works when the data is right". The second was in a competitor's marketing email, which said that "independent multi-source tracking is the only defence against catalogue error".

Ilsa read both. Then she read the newsletter's own paragraph, which was three sentences and did not mention her name.

Femi sent her the link at 09:40 with a message that said *they've made it about the system*.

She wrote back: *it is about the system. That's the finding.*

He replied: *it's about a person who spent four days being told she was wrong.*

She did not answer that, because he was right and because being right about it did not change what the newsletter could print.

---

At 11:00 Priya called, and the call was short.

"The client has published its own statement," she said. "It says the station manoeuvred on a conjunction warning and that the warning was issued after a reclassification. It doesn't say who reclassified."

"I saw."

"The board asked Haddad whether the company should keep the contract. She said yes, with the two conditions." Priya paused. "The first condition is notification of corrections within twenty-four hours. The second is that the analyst is named in the file. The company has agreed to the first and is considering the second."

"Considering."

"Considering. The legal view is that naming an analyst in a client-facing document creates a duty of care to that analyst's judgements, which is a liability the company doesn't currently carry."

"So the company would rather not name me."

"The company would rather not carry the liability." Priya's voice was flat. "I've told them what I think. I've told them that the file already names you, in the audit and in the addendum and in the insurer's ticket, and that refusing to name you in the client's copy would be a distinction that nobody outside the building would understand."

"And?"

"And they're considering."

Ilsa looked at the window. The dish farm was turning. The morning light was the flat kind that made the dishes look like paper cutouts.

"Priya. What happens to the audit now?"

"The audit goes to the regulator as a filing. The regulator reads it and decides whether to extend the scope to the second object. The panel sits in three weeks." A pause. "And the company has asked me to tell you that your return to the catalogue is conditional on the supervision note, which we discussed."

"I know."

"I'm sorry it's not better."

"It's the outcome that exists," Ilsa said. "I've spent a week learning the difference between the outcome that exists and the outcome that should."

---

The competitor's marketing email arrived at 14:20 and was forwarded around the building by three people before it reached her.

It had a graph in it. The graph showed two curves: catalogue-tracked objects and independently tracked objects, and the independent curve was above the catalogue curve by a margin that had been chosen to look like a gap and was, in fact, a difference in counting.

Ilsa looked at the graph for a while. Then she wrote a note to the product team that said: *the graph compares our catalogue to their catalogue, not to the sky. The sky is the only thing that doesn't have a marketing department.*

The product team did not reply. The note went into the folder with everything else.

"I know," Priya said. "But it's what you got."

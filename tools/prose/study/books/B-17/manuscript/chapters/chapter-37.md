# Chapter 37

The room had four walls, two chairs, a table, and a phone that did not ring.

Ilsa sat in one chair. The other was empty. On the table there was a glass of water she had not touched and a folder she had brought and not opened.

She had been asked to come at ten. It was 10:20. The door was closed. The room had no window, and the light was the flat kind that came from a panel in the ceiling, and the air was the recycled air of a building that had sealed itself against the plateau.

She counted the corners. Four. She counted the legs of the table. Four. She counted the tiles in the ceiling, which took longer, because the grid was interrupted by a light fitting and a vent.

At 10:26 the door opened and three people came in: Cole from human resources, the man from legal whose name she had not caught, and a woman she had not seen before, who introduced herself as the company's external counsel and sat down without shaking hands.

"Thank you for coming in," Cole said. "This is a conversation about the terms of your return."

"My return is tomorrow."

"Your return is conditional on the terms." Cole opened a folder of her own. "The company is prepared to offer you a settlement in relation to the events of the past week. The settlement includes a payment, a reference, and a mutual non-disparagement agreement. In exchange, you would withdraw the regulatory filing and the insurer's complaint, and confirm that your classification activity was undertaken in good faith and in accordance with policy."

Ilsa looked at the folder. It had three tabs, the way Cole's documents always had three tabs.

"And if I don't?"

"Then you return tomorrow under the supervision condition, and the regulatory case continues, and the company responds to it in the ordinary course." Cole said it evenly. "The settlement is not a threat. It's an option. It exists because the company would prefer to close this without a regulatory finding, and because a regulatory finding would take a year and cost more than the settlement."

"What would the regulatory finding say?"

The external counsel spoke. "That depends on the evidence. On the present record, the finding would likely be that the company's classification process was followed, that the catalogue was corrected, and that the conjunction warning was issued before the crossing. The finding would likely note the delay between the artefact report and the warning, and the circumstances of the 2024 signature. It would not likely find misconduct, because the conduct was authorised by a client instruction and implemented under a contract clause."

"And the 02:11 access?"

"The access log shows a session under your credentials. There is no evidence of who was at the terminal. The company's position is that the credentials were yours and the session is therefore yours to explain."

Ilsa looked at the woman for a moment. Then she looked at the water glass.

"I'm not going to withdraw the filing," she said.

Cole did not react. The external counsel wrote something down.

"The payment is not conditional on the withdrawal," Cole said. "It's conditional on the non-disparagement. The withdrawal is a separate term. You can take the payment and keep the filing."

"Then what does the reference say?"

"The reference says you were employed as an analyst and that you left by mutual agreement."

"I haven't agreed to leave."

"You'd be agreeing to the terms." Cole set down her pen. "Ms Merrick, I want to be clear about what happens if you return tomorrow without the settlement. You return to a supervised account, on a project that has been reassigned, with a regulatory case open and a client who has been told that your audit is the reason the insurer reopened its file. You will be isolated. Not by policy. By the ordinary social physics of a company where everybody knows."

"And if I take the settlement?"

"Then you leave with a payment, a reference, and no case."

Ilsa sat with that. The room was quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall a printer ran, and stopped, and ran again.

"The filing stays," she said. "And I'll take the reference. And the payment."

"And the non-disparagement?"

"No."

Cole looked at the external counsel, who looked at the table and then nodded, once.

"Then we'll draft it without the non-disparagement," Cole said. "The payment will be smaller."

"That's fine."

Cole closed her folder. At the door she turned.

"For what it's worth, I think you're making a mistake. The settlement was the thing that let everybody walk away."

Cole had brought a second folder, and the second folder was the one with the numbers in it.

"The payment is eleven months' salary," she said. "That's the standard band for a departure by mutual agreement after eight years. The reference is in the third tab. The non-disparagement is in the fourth, and if you decline it, the payment reduces to seven months."

"Why seven?"

"Because the company's risk is different." Cole said it without embarrassment. "A departing employee who can speak freely is a departing employee who might speak. The payment reflects the risk."

"The payment reflects the silence."

"The payment reflects the risk," Cole said again. "You can call it silence. The contract calls it a release."

Ilsa looked at the folder. Eleven months was a number that would cover the care home's increase and the mortgage for a year, and would leave nothing for the year after.

"The appendix," she said. "If I sign the release, does the company admit the signature was applied by somebody else?"

"The company doesn't have a position on the signature."

"The company has a log that says the session came from this building."

"The company has a log that says the session used your credentials." Cole's pen was on the table. "Ms Merrick, I'll be direct, because we're past the point where indirectness helps. The company cannot admit that a signature was applied by an employee without the signatory's knowledge, because that admission would mean every signature in the catalogue is suspect. The catalogue is the product. If the signatures are suspect, the product is worthless."

"So the company would rather have one suspect analyst than a suspect product."

"The company would rather have a product." Cole looked at her. "That's not a defence. It's a description of an institution."

Ilsa sat with it. The room was quiet and the printer beyond the wall ran and stopped.

"Then I'll take the reference and the payment," she said, "and I won't sign the release, and I'll accept the seven months."

Cole wrote it down.

"And the filing stays," Ilsa said.

"The filing stays." Cole closed her folder. "For what it's worth, I think you're going to win the filing and lose the year. The regulator will find the instruction was applied outside its terms, and the company will pay a penalty, and the penalty will be smaller than the legal fees, and nobody will be named. And you'll be the analyst who filed, which in this industry is a thing that follows you."

"I know."

"You know, and you're doing it anyway."

"I've been the analyst who signed the appendix," Ilsa said. "I've had that for eight months. I'd rather be the other one."

---

Anna was waiting in the car park, leaning on the car with her arms folded, and she straightened when Ilsa came out.

"Well?"

"Seven months, a reference, no release, and the filing stays."

Anna looked at her for a moment. Then she nodded slowly.

"That's a good outcome."

"It's a smaller number than the first one."

"It's the number with your name still on the filing." Anna opened the car door. "I've done eleven of these. The ones who take the money and sign the release are fine for about a year. Then they start writing letters to regulators at two in the morning, and the letters are worse than the filing would have been, because by then they've got nothing but the letter."

"And the ones who file?"

"The ones who file are tired for two years and then they're something else." Anna started the engine. "Get in. I'll buy you a bad dinner."

"I know," Ilsa said. "I've been walking away since Tuesday. I'd like to stop."

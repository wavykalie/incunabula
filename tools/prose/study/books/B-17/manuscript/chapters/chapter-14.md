# Chapter 14

The meeting was scheduled for forty minutes and had six people in it, and it produced one action item, which was assigned to Ilsa.

The room was the big conference room on the second floor, the one with the screen that worked. Priya chaired. The duty officer, Voss, was there with his laptop. A woman from legal whose name Ilsa did not catch. Two people from the account team, one of whom spent the meeting answering messages. And Ilsa.

The agenda was on the screen:

1. K-7741 — status
2. Conjunction screening — process
3. Vantage review — readiness
4. AOB

Priya took item one.

"Ilsa has filed a sensor artefact report and an independent fit has confirmed her elements. The object is provisionally an unknown with a mass anomaly. The classification remains debris pending client review through the hygiene portal. The watch item is routine. That's the status."

"Routine is a distribution label," Ilsa said. "It doesn't change the probability."

"It changes the distribution list," Voss said, without looking up from his laptop. "Which is the only thing a label does."

"Then the label is the problem."

"The label is the instruction." Voss looked up. He was forty-one and had the flat affect of a man who had been on the night shift for six years. "Standing Instruction 7 says objects from the 2021 purchase without a launch record default to routine pending review. K-7741 has no launch record. I applied the instruction. If I hadn't, I'd be the one explaining why a debris object generated a client notification at two in the morning."

"Unless it's not debris."

"Then the review will say so, and I'll have applied the instruction correctly and the review will change the outcome. That's what the instruction is for." He said it without malice. "You want me to break the instruction because you're right. I want the review to say you're right, so that when I act, the record says I acted on a finding."

"Which takes five days."

"Which takes five days."

The woman from legal said, "Is there a crewed asset in the screening volume?" and everyone looked at Ilsa.

"Meridian Station," she said. "Six crew. The probability at closest approach is one in three hundred nominal, one in seventy with the covariance inflated."

"And the screening summary?"

"Has no rows. Because the catalogue says debris."

Legal wrote something down. Priya said, "Thank you," in the tone that moves a meeting along.

Item two was process. Voss explained the screening volume, the priority thresholds, the four hundred objects a day, the client complaints in 2023, the reason the thresholds were where they were. It was a good explanation. Ilsa had heard it before and had given a version of it herself.

Item three was the Vantage review. Priya walked through the agenda. Ilsa was down to present the reclassification workflow end to end. The account team asked whether the client would see the K-7741 file, and Priya said the review would cover the workflow, not individual objects, and that the hygiene portal submission would be handled separately.

Item four was AOB, and there was none.

The meeting had taken thirty-one minutes. Voss closed his laptop and left first. Legal followed. The account team stayed to talk to Priya about the review, and Ilsa gathered her papers.

"Ilsa." Priya, from the head of the table. "The action item."

"What action item?"

"The one I'm about to write in the minutes." Priya looked at her. "Confirm the artefact. By tomorrow noon. That's the wording I'm going to use, and I want you to know it before it's in the record."

"Confirm it how?"

"Confirm it with the portal submission. The submission is the confirmation. Once it's in, the object is officially under review, and the review has a date, and the date is after the conjunction, and everybody in this room has done their job according to the instruction." She closed her folder. "That's what I can give you. It's not what you want. It's what exists."

She left.

The woman from legal was called Bhatt and she asked the only question in the meeting that nobody had prepared for.

"If the object is a spacecraft," she said, "who owns it?"

The room was quiet. Voss looked at his laptop. Priya looked at the table.

"Unknown," Ilsa said.

"Unknown is not an answer that survives a deposition." Bhatt turned a page. "If the object is owned by a state, we have a treaty problem. If it's owned by a company, we have a tort problem. If it's owned by nobody, we have a salvage problem. The classification affects which problem we have."

"The classification is debris."

"The classification is debris, and the object manoeuvres, and those two facts are going to be in the same paragraph of somebody's filing." Bhatt looked at Ilsa. "I'm not asking you to solve it. I'm asking you to tell me what you know."

Ilsa told her. The mass, the tumble, the reconstructed launch, the platform, the registration, the aerosol transient, the three manoeuvres, the phasing change. She said it in the order she would have put it in an audit, which was the only order that kept it honest.

Bhatt wrote for a while after she finished.

"The platform," she said. "Registered in a flag state."

"Yes."

"And the state notified the launch late."

"Yes."

"Then the state has assumed responsibility for the launch under the registration convention, which means the object has a state of registry, which means it has an owner for treaty purposes." Bhatt looked up. "That's not good news for the state. It means the state is liable for damage caused by its space object, regardless of who built it."

"Liable to whom?"

"To the injured party. In this case, the station's operator. And the operator's insurer." She closed her folder. "If I were advising the state, I'd tell it to deny that the object is its space object. If I were advising the platform, I'd tell it to keep the registration quiet. If I were advising this company, I'd tell it to make sure its records show that it raised the concern before the conjunction, which is what your audit does."

"So the audit is a legal document."

"The audit is evidence. The legal document is the filing." Bhatt stood. "Keep filing. Every day. A filing a day is a record. A record a day is a defence."

She left. Priya stayed.

"She's not wrong," Priya said.

"She's not wrong about the records."

"She's not wrong about the object, either." Priya looked at the window. "If it's a state asset, we're in a treaty dispute. If it's a company asset, we're in a tort. Either way, the thing that decides which one is a document that says what we knew and when."

"And if the document says we knew on Tuesday and did nothing until Friday?"

"Then the document says we knew on Tuesday and did nothing until Friday," Priya said. "That's why I want the record complete. Not to protect the company. To protect the person who kept it."

Ilsa sat in the conference room with the agenda still on the screen and the ghost of the previous meeting underneath it, and she took out her notebook and wrote the date and the time and the six names, and beside Voss's name she wrote *instruction applied correctly*, and beside Priya's name she wrote *sequencing*, and beside her own name she wrote nothing at all.

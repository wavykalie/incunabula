# Chapter 06

The records room at Panamint was a converted equipment bay with no windows and a dehumidifier that ran all year. It held the paper that the company was required to keep and the paper it had chosen to keep, in grey boxes on metal shelving, labelled by year and function.

Ilsa signed the access sheet at 15:10. The sheet was a legal requirement and nobody had looked at it in three years; the last three lines were in her own handwriting.

The 2024 catalogue-hygiene study was in box 24-C, under *Client Reviews*. It was a hundred and forty pages, bound with a plastic comb, and it had been written by a consultancy that no longer existed. The cover page carried the Vantage logo and a line that said *prepared for Vantage Launch Systems under the catalogue hygiene programme*.

She read the executive summary standing up. It said the catalogue contained an estimated four hundred objects whose classification was inconsistent with their observed behaviour, that the inconsistency was concentrated in objects acquired through data purchases, and that the recommended remedy was a programme of individual re-reviews with client participation.

Then she read the appendix.

Appendix 4 was a table of forty-one objects. Each row had a catalogue number, an observed inconsistency, a recommended classification, and a disposition. K-7741 was row nineteen. The observed inconsistency was *mass estimate exceeds booster-class expectation by a factor of 2.4*. The recommended classification was *payload, unknown*.

The disposition column said *reclassified as breakup debris, no further action*.

The signature block at the end of the appendix had two names. The first was Priya Raghunathan. The second was Ilsa Merrick.

She looked at it for a long time. The signature was hers. It had the small backward loop on the M that she had tried to train out of her hand and never had. The date beside it was 14 March 2024.

On 14 March 2024 she had been in Fresno, at a care home, for an assessment meeting about her mother. She remembered the room: a low table, a social worker with a folder, the smell of disinfectant and something sweeter underneath it. She had driven back that evening and had not come to the office.

She turned the page. The appendix had a second signature block, for the client. It was signed by a Vantage compliance officer whose name she did not recognise, and dated the same day.

She sat down on the step stool and read the appendix again from the top, which is what she did when a document was telling her something she did not want to hear. The recommendation was *payload, unknown*. The disposition was debris. The signature was hers. The date was a day she had been in another city.

There were three possible explanations. The first was that she had signed it remotely, which was possible, because the company used an electronic signature service for client documents and the service logged the session, not the location. The second was that somebody had used her credentials, which was also possible, because the credentials were a password and a token and the token was kept in a drawer. The third was that she had signed it and forgotten, which was the explanation she trusted least and feared most, because she had been tired that year in a way that had made whole weeks go missing.

She took out her phone and looked at the calendar for March 2024. The assessment was there, at 11:00, with a note that said *take the discharge summary*. She had taken it. The drive back was two hundred and forty kilometres and she had stopped at a service station and bought a coffee she did not drink.

The electronic signature service kept an audit log. She could request it. The request would be visible to whoever administered the service, which was Priya's assistant, which meant it would be visible to Priya.

She put the phone away.

The dehumidifier clicked on. The room was cold, and the cold was the dry cold of a machine that was doing its job.

She turned to the front of the report and read the commissioning letter. It was addressed to the consultancy, signed by Priya, and it specified the scope: *a hygiene review of objects acquired through the 2021 data purchase, with particular attention to objects that may have been misclassified at acquisition*. The fee was listed. So was a clause that said the review was advisory and that no finding would be acted upon without a separate client instruction.

The separate client instruction was in the box. It was a single page, on Vantage letterhead, dated 12 March 2024. It said that Vantage had reviewed the consultancy's draft findings and directed that objects without a confirmed launch record be reclassified to debris pending further information, in order to avoid *unnecessary alarm in the conjunction product*.

The signature on the instruction was the compliance officer's. The name under it was G. Iwasaki.

Ilsa read the instruction twice. Then she took out her phone and photographed it, and the appendix, and the signature block, and the commissioning letter, and the page with the advisory clause. The phone made a small sound for each photograph, which in the quiet room sounded like something being counted.

She put the report back in the box, and the box back on the shelf, and she signed out on the access sheet at 15:52, and the signature she wrote on the sheet was the same one that was on the appendix, with the backward loop on the M.

The care home was called Meadowbrook and had a sign with a willow on it, and the willow was the only thing about the place that was not beige.

Ilsa had driven up on 14 March 2024 for the quarterly assessment, which was a meeting where three professionals sat around a low table and described her mother's decline in the passive voice. Her mother had been in the room for the first ten minutes and had then been taken to lunch, and the rest of the meeting had been about her.

The social worker's name was Denise. She had a folder with a tab for each resident and a way of turning pages that made the meeting feel like it was going somewhere. The assessment said that her mother's short-term memory had deteriorated, that she was no longer able to manage her own medication, and that the care plan should be reviewed.

Ilsa had asked what the review would change.

Denise had said that it would change the level of support and the fee.

The fee was the part of the meeting that Ilsa remembered most clearly, because it had been the only part with a number in it, and because the number had been presented with an apology and accepted without one.

Her mother had come back from lunch at the end of the meeting and had looked at Ilsa with the polite attention of a woman being introduced to somebody at a party.

"Are you here about the room?" she had asked.

"No, Mum. I'm here about you."

"The room's very small," her mother had said. "They say it's temporary."

Ilsa had said it was temporary. She had said it every visit for two years. Her mother had nodded and looked out of the window at the willow, and the conversation had moved on to the weather, which was the only subject that survived the meetings intact.

On the drive back she had stopped at a service station and bought a coffee she did not drink. She had sat in the car park with the cup in the holder and the engine off, and she had thought about the fact that her mother had asked about the room and not about her, and that the question had been the same question she had asked in 2019, and that the sameness was the disease and not the room.

She had got home at 21:40. She had not opened her laptop. She had gone to bed, and in the morning she had gone to work, and the work had included an appendix that carried her signature and a date she had spent two hundred and forty kilometres away from the building.

---

The records room was cold and the dehumidifier clicked off, and Ilsa sat on the step stool with the report open on her knees and did the arithmetic of that day again.

Leave at 06:00. Two hundred and forty kilometres. Arrive at 10:40. Meeting at 11:00. Lunch at 12:30. Drive back at 15:00. Home at 18:50. Asleep by 22:00.

There was no window in that day for a signature. There was no laptop in the car, and the car had no charger, and the phone had been in her bag with the battery at nine per cent because she had forgotten to charge it the night before.

She remembered the battery because she had wanted to call the home from the service station and had decided not to, to save the power.

She closed the report. The signature was hers. The date was not. Both of those facts were true, and the system that held them did not have a field for the difference.

Then she went back to her desk and opened the sensor artefact report, and set the severity to high, and filed it.

# Chapter 16

Schedule 4 was eleven pages, and Ilsa read it twice before she understood that the first reading had been a waste of time.

The access came through at 14:55, two hours after the meeting, with a notification that said *approved by P. Raghunathan* and a line that said *reason: analyst review, object K-7741*. She opened it at her desk with the folder beside her and a fresh page of the notebook, because she had learned to take notes on documents she might later have to describe from memory.

Schedule 4 was titled *Hygiene Reviews and Classification Services*. It was written in the flat, careful prose of a contract drafted by two legal teams who had agreed to disagree about almost everything and had compromised on ambiguity.

Clause 4.1 said that the provider would conduct hygiene reviews on request, at the rates set out in Annex C.

Clause 4.2 said that a hygiene review would produce findings and recommendations, and that the findings would be delivered to the client's technical contact.

Clause 4.3 said that the findings were advisory, and that no finding would be acted upon by the provider without a written instruction from the client.

Clause 4.4 said that the client could issue a written instruction at any time, and that the provider would implement it within five business days.

Clause 4.5 said that the provider would maintain a record of every instruction and every implementation, and that the record would be available to the client on request.

Clause 4.6 said that the provider's liability for any loss arising from a classification, a reclassification, or a failure to warn was limited to the value of the subscription under Annex A.

Clause 4.7 said that the client acknowledged that the provider's catalogue was a commercial product and that the client's use of it was at the client's own risk.

Ilsa read clause 4.3 three times. *Advisory.* The word was doing a lot of work. It meant that the consultancy's finding — *payload, unknown* — was not a finding the provider was obliged to act on. It meant that the client's instruction — reclassify to debris — was the operative act, and that the provider had implemented a client instruction in the ordinary course. It meant that every person in the chain had done exactly what the contract said they should do.

She turned to Annex A. The subscription value for Meridian Station's operator was listed as a figure with six digits before the decimal point. She looked at it for a while. Then she turned to Annex C and found the rate for a hygiene review, which was a fraction of the subscription value, and the rate for an independent fit, which was smaller still.

The arithmetic was on the page. The cost of a warning was not on the page, because the cost of a warning was not a contract term. The cost of a warning was a manoeuvre, and a manoeuvre was propellant, and propellant was allocated quarterly by the operator, and the operator was the client.

She sat back and looked at the ceiling. The catalogue bay had a suspended ceiling with tiles that had water stains in the corner where the air handling condensated. She had looked at those stains for eight years and had never once thought about them.

Clause 4.6 was the one she kept coming back to. *Limited to the value of the subscription.* If Kestrel failed to warn, and the station was damaged, Kestrel's exposure was the subscription. Not the station. Not the crew. The subscription. Somebody had negotiated that clause, and somebody had signed it, and the somebody was not in the room and probably did not think of themselves as having done anything wrong.

She wrote in the notebook: *4.3 advisory — client instruction operative. 4.6 cap = subscription. The contract prices the warning, not the consequence.*

Then she closed the document and opened the hygiene portal, where her submission was still sitting in draft, because she had not pressed submit.

The form had a field for *supporting data* and a field for *assessment*, and a checkbox that said *I confirm that the information provided is accurate to the best of my knowledge*. She looked at the checkbox for a long time.

At 15:40 her phone rang. It was an internal extension, and the display said *P. Raghunathan*.

"You've read it," Priya said.

"Yes."

"Then you understand why I approved it."

"I understand what it says."

"Good. That's all I wanted." A pause. "The submission. Have you pressed submit?"

"Not yet."

"Press it before you go home. If it's in the queue tonight, the review clock starts tomorrow, and the review will be assigned to a technical officer who is not me and not Grant, and that's the best I can do for you."

"Priya."

"Mm."

"Clause 4.6."

The line was quiet for a moment.

"I know what 4.6 says," Priya said. "I negotiated it. It's the reason we have a company."

She hung up.

Clause 9.4 was the one she photographed first.

*The provider shall exercise reasonable skill and care in the performance of the services. The provider does not warrant the accuracy of any classification, and the customer acknowledges that classifications are judgements made on the basis of available data.*

She read it twice. Then she read 9.5, which said that the provider's obligation to warn arose when an object had been classified at priority two or above and the conjunction probability exceeded one in a thousand.

The obligation to warn was conditional on the classification. The classification was a judgement. The judgement was not warranted. The chain was complete, and each link was reasonable, and the whole of it meant that the company's duty to warn began only after somebody inside the company had decided the object was worth warning about.

She wrote in the notebook: *9.5 makes the warning depend on the classification; 9.4 makes the classification unwarranted. The duty is therefore a duty to be right about a judgement nobody guarantees.*

Then she read Schedule 4 for the second time, looking for the things that were not there. There was no clause about the client's power to instruct a classification. There was no clause about who owned the provenance record. There was no clause about what happened when an analyst believed a classification was wrong.

The gap was not an oversight. It was the shape of the contract: the client bought a service, the provider exercised judgement, and the question of what happened when the judgement and the instruction disagreed had been left to the people in the building.

She called Femi at 15:40.

"Schedule 4," she said. "Clause 9.4 and 9.5. Read them and tell me what you think."

She heard him scrolling.

"Nine four says we don't warrant the classification," he said. "Nine five says we warn at priority two."

"Yes."

"Then the object was priority four and we didn't warn, and both of those clauses say we did what we promised."

"That's the company's reading."

"What's yours?"

"Mine is that 9.4 is about a judgement made on available data, and the data available included a mass anomaly that the system did not display, a provenance note that said the seller declined to provide a launch record, and a fit that three people had checked." She looked at the window. "The classification wasn't made on available data. It was made on displayed data. Those aren't the same thing, and the contract doesn't have a clause for the difference."

Femi was quiet for a moment.

"You're saying the contract assumes the system shows you what it knows."

"I'm saying the contract was written by people who believed the software."

"That's a very expensive sentence."

"It's a free sentence," Ilsa said. "The expensive one is the one the insurer will write."

---

At 16:20 she went up to the fourth floor and stood in the corridor outside Priya's office for a minute before she knocked. Through the glass she could see Priya on the phone, standing, with one hand on the windowsill, and the posture was the posture of a woman being told something she did not want to hear.

Priya saw her and held up one finger. Ilsa waited. The corridor was carpeted and quiet, and the light came through the glass in a way that made the office look like an aquarium.

Priya put the phone down and opened the door.

"That was the client," she said. "They've heard from the regulator."

"Already?"

"The regulator doesn't wait for the filing to be complete. It reads the filing and it calls the party named in it." Priya went back to her desk. "They've asked the client to confirm that the object was classified as debris on the client's instruction. The client has asked me how they should answer."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them to answer truthfully." Priya sat down. "Which means the client is now considering whether the instruction was a mistake, and the answer to that question will determine whether the instruction was authorised by somebody senior enough to have made it."

"And if it wasn't?"

"Then the client has a governance problem, and we have a client who will need somebody to blame." Priya looked at her. "I'm telling you this because you're going to be in the room when it happens, and because I'd rather you heard it from me than from a lawyer."

Ilsa sat with the phone in her hand. Then she pressed submit, and the portal returned a reference number, and the reference number was the first thing in three days that had not been a description of a problem but a record that the problem existed.

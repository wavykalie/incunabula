# Chapter 27

The briefing was in a room with no windows on the second floor of a building in Colorado Springs, and the man who ran it introduced himself by a first name and a job title that did not appear on any organisation chart Ilsa had ever seen.

"Call me Doug," he said. "I'm the desk for orbital anomalies. That's not the real name of the desk, but it's the name we use when somebody from industry asks."

There were four of them in the room: Ilsa, Doug, a woman from the legal office who took notes, and a man from the space surveillance centre who had introduced himself as a lieutenant colonel and had not spoken since.

Ilsa had been invited because she had filed with the regulator, and the regulator had passed the filing to the authority that owned the desk, and the authority had decided that a conversation was cheaper than a formal response.

"You've said the object is a spacecraft," Doug said. "Walk me through that."

She walked him through it. The mass, the tumble, the specular glint, the reconstructed launch, the three manoeuvres, the phasing adjustment nine days ago. She put the prints on the table as she went: the dish trace, the orbit fit, the aerosol transient, the registration, the timeline.

Doug listened without interrupting. The lieutenant colonel looked at the prints in the order she laid them out, and his face did not change.

When she finished, Doug said, "What do you want us to do?"

"I want you to classify it."

"Classify it as what?"

"A weapon. Or a hazard. Or an unauthorised manoeuvring object. Any of those would trigger a different set of procedures."

Doug looked at the lieutenant colonel, who looked at the table.

"Ms Merrick," Doug said, "the authority's definition of a weapon in orbit requires a payload function. Something that can damage or destroy another object. We can see that your object manoeuvres. We can see it has a mass consistent with a manoeuvring kit. What we can't see is a function. It could be an inspector. It could be a servicing vehicle. It could be a test article with a grappling mechanism that has never been used. All of those manoeuvre. None of them are weapons."

"A nine-hundred-kilogram object with a grappling mechanism is a weapon if it decides to be."

"That's an argument. It's not a classification." Doug spread his hands. "I'm not being obtuse. I'm telling you what the definition is. If I classify an object as a weapon without a function, I've made a political statement, and political statements get reviewed, and reviews take longer than Friday."

"Then classify it as a hazard."

"A hazard is a conjunction problem. The conjunction is on Friday. The hazard procedures are the warning procedures, and the warning procedures belong to your company."

"The warning procedures are the product."

"Which is why we're having this conversation." He leaned back. "Let me tell you what I can do. I can note your concern in the anomaly log. I can ask the surveillance centre to add the object to the crewed-asset screening bulletin as an unclassified object with an unknown function. That's a real change: it moves the object from your catalogue into ours, and our bulletin goes to every crewed operator, including Meridian, without going through your product."

"That's what I want."

"It's not what you asked for. It's better, in one way, and worse in another. Better because the station gets the number from a source that isn't your company. Worse because the number is a probability, and the bulletin is advisory, and the operator still has to decide to burn."

"Then make the bulletin a requirement."

"I can't. The bulletin is a public service. Requirements are for licensed launches." He paused. "Your object isn't a licensed launch, as far as we can tell. That's actually the most interesting thing you've brought us."

The lieutenant colonel spoke for the first time.

"Unlicensed launch is a treaty matter," he said. "If the platform was registered to a state that is a party to the registration convention, and the launch was not notified, that's a breach. Breaches are handled through diplomatic channels. Diplomatic channels are slow."

"How slow?"

"Months. Sometimes years. Sometimes never."

Ilsa looked at the prints on the table. The dish trace. The orbit fit. The aerosol transient. Nine days of work, and the answer was that the object was interesting, and the interesting thing was the paperwork.

"The bulletin," she said. "You'll add it today?"

"I'll request it today. It'll go out on the next screening cycle, which is eighteen hundred UTC."

That was four hours before the crossing. Four hours of warning from a source the station might actually believe.

"Thank you," she said.

Doug walked her to the door. In the corridor, out of earshot of the legal officer, he said, "For what it's worth, I think you're right about what it is. I've seen three of these in eleven years. The ones that manoeuvre are never debris."

"Then why not classify it?"

"Because the moment I call it a weapon, the question becomes who launched it, and the answer to that question is above my pay grade, and the people above my pay grade have a different set of priorities than the station does." He held the door. "I'm sorry. That's the honest version."

The lieutenant colonel spoke for the first time at the forty-minute mark, and he spoke in the flat cadence of a man reading from a document he had memorised.

"The object you've described is consistent with a manoeuvrable payload in a sixty-one degree orbit," he said. "That orbit is not unusual. Sixty-one degrees is a common inclination for certain classes of satellite. What's unusual is the absence of a designator and the manoeuvre history."

"Unusual how?" Ilsa asked.

"Objects manoeuvre. Satellites manoeuvre constantly. What they don't do is manoeuvre without an operator who files a notification, and what they don't do is change their orbit after a conjunction with a crewed asset without telling anybody." He looked at her. "The manoeuvre after the crossing is the part that concerns us."

"Why?"

"Because it's the part that looks like a test." He put his pen down. "A vehicle approaches a station, passes it, and then changes orbit to a holding position above it. That's not a mistake. That's a sequence."

Doug cut in. "What the colonel is saying is that the desk's interest is not the object's origin. It's the object's behaviour."

"The origin matters for attribution," the colonel said. "Behaviour matters for warning."

Ilsa looked at the two of them. The room had no windows and a projector and a table with four chairs, and the legal officer was taking notes in a hand that never stopped.

"Can you put the object on a bulletin?" she asked.

"The object is already on a bulletin," the colonel said. "It's on the unclassified conjunction bulletin as a catalogue object. It's not on the crewed-asset screening bulletin, because that bulletin is for objects above a certain mass and a certain priority, and the object's priority is set by the commercial catalogue."

"The commercial catalogue says debris."

"The commercial catalogue says debris, and the bulletin takes the catalogue's priority, and the catalogue's priority is four." He said it without any particular feeling. "The system is federated. Each part trusts the part before it."

"Then the bulletin is only as good as the catalogue."

"The bulletin is exactly as good as the catalogue," the colonel said. "That's not a flaw in the bulletin."

Doug leaned back. "What the colonel is too polite to say is that the bulletin can't fix a catalogue that's been told to say debris."

"I'm not too polite," the colonel said. "I'm precise. The bulletin can't fix it. Neither can the desk. Neither can this meeting. The only thing that can fix it is a record that says the classification was wrong, filed by the organisation that made it."

Ilsa looked at the table.

"That's what I'm trying to produce."

"Then produce it," the colonel said. "And when you do, send it to the desk and to the bulletin's duty officer and to your own client, in that order. The order matters. The desk first means the bulletin can act. The client last means the client can't suppress it before it moves."

---

On the way out Doug walked her to the lift.

"He's not wrong about the order," he said. "He's also not going to say the thing that matters, which is that the desk has been watching that platform for eleven months and hasn't been able to do anything with it, because a platform that registers late isn't a violation and a launch that isn't notified isn't a launch the desk can act on."

"So the desk knew."

"The desk suspected. There's a difference, and the difference is a document." He pressed the lift button. "You've got the document. That's why I asked you to come."

The legal officer asked the only question that required Ilsa to think about her own position.

"Ms Merrick, are you here as an analyst or as a witness?"

"I'm here because I was asked to brief the desk."

"That's not an answer." The woman's pen was still. "If you're an analyst, this meeting is technical and the notes are internal. If you're a witness, the notes are evidence and you should have been advised."

Doug intervened. "She's here as an analyst. The notes are internal."

"Then I'll write that down, and I'll write down that I advised the desk that the analyst's material may later be evidence." The pen moved. "That's for the file, not for the room."

Ilsa looked at the two of them. The room was warm and the projector was on and the slide on the screen was her own figure, the perigee staircase that she had drawn on a laptop at two in the morning.

"I don't mind being a witness," she said.

"That's not yours to decide," the legal officer said, without unkindness. "It's the desk's, and mine, and eventually a court's. What's yours is to be accurate about what you saw and clear about what you inferred."

"Then let me be clear. I saw a mass anomaly and a manoeuvre history. I inferred a vehicle. I inferred an operator. I inferred a purpose. The first is a measurement, the second is a fit, and the third is a story."

"And the story?"

"The story is consistent with the measurements and with the platform and with the launch. It is not proved by them."

The lieutenant colonel nodded, once, which was the first thing he had done that looked like approval.

"That's the correct way to say it," he said. "Most people come in here with a story and dress it as a measurement. You've come in with a measurement and dressed the story separately. That's why the desk will act on it."

Doug looked at the screen. "What do you want the desk to do?"

"Put the object on the crewed-asset bulletin. Tell the operator. Tell the insurer if the operator won't." She paused. "And put the second object on as an uncorrelated track, because the second object is the one that can still manoeuvre."

"The second object has one radar return and a pencil note."

"The second object has one radar return, a pencil note, and a burn at 23:40 that changed its phasing to match the first." She looked at the room. "That's three facts. Three facts is a track."

The colonel wrote something. "Three facts is a track," he said. "I'll take it to the bulletin duty officer tonight."

She went down in the lift with the prints under her arm and the honest version ringing in her ears.

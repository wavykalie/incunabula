# Chapter 32

The liability cap was on page nine of the contract, in a clause that had been negotiated by two legal teams and summarised in a single sentence: *the provider's aggregate liability under this agreement shall not exceed the value of the subscription.*

Ilsa had read it on Thursday and had understood it as an abstraction. On Friday night, with the warning issued and the crossing thirty minutes away, she read it again and understood it as a number.

The subscription value for Meridian's operator was on the same page, in Annex A: a six-figure sum that covered the conjunction product, the screening service, and the technical support line. The station was worth two orders of magnitude more. The crew were not on the page at all.

She sat at the kitchen table with the contract open and the notebook beside it and did the arithmetic three ways.

The first way: if Kestrel failed to warn and the station was damaged, Kestrel paid the subscription. The company would survive. The analyst who signed the classification would not, professionally, but the company would.

The second way: if Kestrel warned and the operator manoeuvred, the operator spent propellant that was charged against the next quarter. The operator would bear that cost, not Kestrel.

The third way: if Kestrel warned and the operator did not manoeuvre, and the station was damaged, the operator's insurer would pay, and the insurer would subrogate against Kestrel, and Kestrel's liability would be capped at the subscription.

The clause made the company's exposure small in every branch. The only branch in which the exposure was large was the branch where somebody at Kestrel had knowingly withheld a warning, and even then the cap applied unless the conduct was found to be in bad faith.

She looked at the word *knowingly* for a while.

Then she took out her phone and photographed page nine, and Annex A, and the clause, and the signature page with Priya's name and the client's countersignature, and she put the photographs in the folder with the rest.

The folder was now full. She had to press it closed with the flat of her hand.

At 20:35 the station's public loop came alive. Ada Reyes, on the voice circuit, reading a statement that had been drafted by the operator's ground team and approved by somebody in an office.

"Meridian, all stations. We have received a conjunction warning from our provider for object K-7741, classified unknown payload, probability one in seventy, time of closest approach twenty-one hundred. We are configured for a debris avoidance manoeuvre. We will execute a burn of zero point three five metres per second at twenty-forty. Crew is secure. This is a declared manoeuvre under the operator's flight rules."

The loop clicked off.

Ilsa sat in her kitchen with the phone in her hand and the folder under her palm, and she listened to the silence of a house at night, and she thought about the word *declared*.

The burn was declared. The form had been filed. The warning existed. Somewhere in the last four hours, the machinery had turned, and the thing she had been pushing against had moved, and it had moved because an insurer had asked a question and a client had done the arithmetic.

She had not moved it. The audit had not moved it. The regulator had not moved it. The thing that moved it was a clause in a contract that made withholding visible.

Bhatt called at 19:50 and asked whether she had read clause 9.4.

"I've read it."

"Then you know the cap applies unless the conduct is found to be in bad faith." Bhatt's voice had the flatness of a lawyer working through a list. "I want to explain what bad faith means in this context, because it's not what people think it means."

"Go on."

"Bad faith is not negligence. It's not carelessness. It's not even knowing that a thing is risky and doing it anyway. Bad faith is a conscious decision to do something the person knew was wrong, for a reason the person knew was improper."

"That's a high bar."

"It's a high bar and it's designed to be. The cap exists because the company sells judgement, and judgement is sometimes wrong, and if every wrong judgement were actionable the company couldn't sell judgement at all." She paused. "The cap is the price of the product."

"And if somebody inside the company cleared a watch item at two in the morning to keep an object classified?"

"Then that is not a wrong judgement. That's an act." Bhatt said the word carefully. "The cap protects judgement. It doesn't protect acts. If you can show an act, the cap doesn't apply, and the company's exposure becomes the loss, not the subscription."

Ilsa sat with the phone against her ear.

"How would somebody show an act?"

"A record of the act. A log. A camera. A witness. Something that says a person did a thing, rather than a process producing an outcome." Bhatt paused. "That's why the distinction between a bad classification and a cleared watch item matters. A bad classification is a judgement. A cleared watch item is a keystroke."

"And a keystroke is an act."

"A keystroke is an act," Bhatt said. "And an act is what a regulator is looking for, because an act can be attributed to a person, and a person can be disciplined, and discipline is the thing regulators produce."

---

After the call Ilsa sat at the kitchen table with the contract open at page nine and the notebook beside it, and she wrote a sentence she did not like.

*A company that caps its liability for judgement has an interest in describing every failure as judgement.*

She read it back. It was true. It was also the kind of sentence that, written in an audit, would be read as an allegation, and she was not in the business of allegations. She was in the business of records.

So she crossed it out and wrote instead: *9.4 caps liability for classification. The cap does not apply to acts. The 02:11 session is an act if it can be attributed to a person.*

Then she closed the notebook and went to bed, and at 03:40 she woke up with the sentence in her head that she had crossed out, and she lay in the dark and thought about how many sentences like it were in the system, waiting.

She wrote that down, because it was the truest sentence she had written all week, and then she closed the notebook and put on her coat and drove to the station, because the car park badge still worked and she wanted to see the pass on a screen that was not her laptop.

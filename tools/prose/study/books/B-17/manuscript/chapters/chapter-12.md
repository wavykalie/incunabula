# Chapter 12

The dish farm at three in the morning was a set of white shapes against a sky with too many stars in it, and the only sound was the wind and the motors.

Ilsa had a flask of coffee, a laptop, and Hal's authorisation to borrow dish four for a seventeen-minute window that had been booked as maintenance. Dish four was the oldest of the nine, a nine-metre with a receiver that had been upgraded twice and a mount that had been welded once, and it made a noise when it slewed that the newer dishes did not.

Hal was in the control room with the door open so he could hear her if she shouted. The control room was a Portakabin with a heater that worked and a kettle that did not.

"Elevation's coming up," he said over the intercom. "You've got a clean sky and a bad moon. If it flares, you'll see it."

The object came over the horizon at 03:11. Ilsa watched the signal strength on the laptop: a flat line, then a bump, then a trace that rose and fell with the slow regularity of something turning in the sun.

"There," she said.

"I see it."

The trace had a period. She timed it by hand first, counting the peaks against the clock in the corner of the screen, and got forty-one seconds. Then she ran the autocorrelation and got forty-one point two, which was the same number to the precision the data supported.

A tumbling object with a regular specular flash was not a random shape. A booster tumbling end over end gave a double peak, because the ends were broader than the middle. A flat panel gave a sharp peak and a long trough. This gave one sharp peak, then a broad shoulder, then nothing, repeating.

"That's a panel and a body," Hal said. "You've got a flat thing and a lump."

"Which is a solar array."

"Which is a solar array, or a radiator, or a door." He paused. "Boosters don't have doors."

She let the pass run. At 03:19 the trace changed: the peak narrowed, and the shoulder disappeared, and for six seconds the signal strength went up by a factor of four.

"Glint," Hal said. "That's a specular return off something flat and shiny. You get that off a mirror, or a polished panel, or a window."

"A window."

"A window on a spacecraft. Big one." He said it the way he said most things, as a fact that had arrived without any obligation to be pleasant.

The signal dropped back. The pass ended at 03:28, and the dish slewed away with its old mount complaining.

Ilsa sat in the dark with the laptop on her knees and the data in front of her. Forty-one-second tumble. A flat panel. A body. A specular glint that implied a surface flat to within a fraction of the wavelength, which implied manufacture.

Nine hundred and twelve kilograms. A booster's cross-section. No launch record. A manoeuvre history the catalogue did not have.

She had spent four years in a job where the answer to almost every question was *a spent stage* or *a fragment* or *a piece of a fragment*. The vocabulary of the trade was a vocabulary of things that had stopped working. K-7741 had not stopped working. It was turning, in the sun, with a window on it.

"Hal."

"Mm."

"It's a spacecraft."

The intercom was quiet for a moment.

"Say it again when you're inside," he said. "I want to see your face when you say it."

She packed the laptop and walked back across the apron. The wind was coming off the plateau and it smelled of dust and cold metal. Above the dish farm the sky was doing what it always did, which was nothing, at a scale that made the nothing look deliberate.

Inside, Hal had made tea, which he did not drink, and set it in front of her.

"Right," he said. "Say it."

"It's a spacecraft. It's manoeuvring. It's in a decay path that intersects Meridian's orbit, and the catalogue says it's debris because I signed a form that says it's debris."

"And?"

"And the form was wrong, and I knew it was wrong when I signed it, because the mass was in the file and I didn't look at the file."

Hal nodded slowly. He picked up the tea and put it down again without drinking.

"That's the first honest thing you've said all night," he said. "Now say the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The thing you've been not saying since you walked in."

She looked at the screen. The trace was still there, the peak and the shoulder, repeating, patient.

"If it's a spacecraft," she said, "then somebody launched it, and somebody is flying it, and somebody decided that the catalogue should say it was a piece of a rocket."

"Yes."

"And the somebody who decided is the client."

Hal looked at her for a long moment. The heater clicked off, and the Portakabin was quiet.

The contractor who had swapped the receiver was called Ruiz, and he came out to the dish at 03:40 because Hal had called him and because Hal was the only person at Panamint who had ever helped him when a mount seized in a windstorm.

Ruiz was forty and had a van with a ladder on it and the specific competence of a man who fixed things that could not be brought indoors.

"Receiver's swapped," he said. "The old one's on the bench. It was drifting on the high band. That's why dish seven was off the tasking list."

"How much was it drifting?"

"Point four decibels over six hours. Enough to matter if you're doing photometry. Not enough to matter if you're tracking a satellite." He looked at the laptop. "You're doing photometry."

"I'm doing a mass estimate."

"Then you care about the drift." He wiped his hands on a rag. "I can leave the new receiver on your object for another two passes if you want. It's a good receiver. It was going to be a spare."

"Hal said you owed him a favour."

"Hal helped me when the mount seized and I was going to lose the contract." Ruiz shrugged. "That's not a favour, that's a debt. A favour's when you do something for somebody who might do something for you. A debt's when you do something because you already got paid."

He went back to the van and came out with a thermos and two plastic cups, and they stood on the apron in the cold and drank tea that was too strong.

"You're the one who found the mass anomaly," Ruiz said.

"I'm the one who looked at the field."

"Same thing." He looked up at the dish. "I've been out here eleven years. I've fixed every one of these. You know what I've never seen? An object that changed its orbit without a manoeuvre. Not once. The sky's full of junk and the junk does what the drag tells it to do."

"And this one?"

"This one does what somebody tells it to do." He drank. "That's not a mass anomaly. That's a pilot."

Ilsa looked at the dish. It was pointed at a patch of sky where the object was, or had been, or would be in nine minutes.

"The pilot's flying it somewhere," she said.

"The pilot's flying it somewhere," Ruiz agreed. "And whoever's paying for the fuel knows where."

---

The pass came at 03:51. The new receiver gave a cleaner trace than dish six, and the specular glint was sharper, and the period was forty-one point two seconds, and the signal strength was a factor of nine above the background.

Ruiz watched the trace over her shoulder and said nothing. When the pass ended he took the cups and the thermos back to the van.

"You want the next pass?"

"Yes."

"Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow."

He got in the van and drove off across the apron, and his headlights made the dishes into a row of white shapes that turned and turned.

"Now you're doing the job," he said.

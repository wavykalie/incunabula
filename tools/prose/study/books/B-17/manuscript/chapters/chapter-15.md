# Chapter 15

June Park called the ground at 11:40 station time because the conjunction summary had changed format, and the call reached Ilsa because the switchboard routed technical queries to the catalogue group and Ilsa was the only one at her desk.

"Meridian Station, comms. I'm looking for the catalogue desk."

"This is the catalogue desk."

"Who am I speaking to?"

"Ilsa Merrick. I'm an analyst."

There was a pause, and the hiss of the loop.

"Ms Merrick, this is June Park on Meridian. We've had a change in our conjunction summary format and I'm trying to find out whether it's a software release or something else. Ground said it was a release. I'm asking the catalogue because the catalogue is where the numbers come from."

"It's a release. The header formatting changed on Tuesday."

"Right." Another pause. "Can I ask you a different question?"

"Yes."

"The summary says no tracked objects within the screening volume for seventy-two hours. Is that a statement about the catalogue, or a statement about the sky?"

Ilsa looked at her screen. The question was the one she had been asking for three days, and it had arrived from four hundred kilometres up, in a voice that sounded young and tired.

"It's a statement about the catalogue," she said.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it lists objects above a priority threshold. Objects below the threshold aren't listed. They're still there."

The loop was quiet for a moment. Ilsa heard something in the background, a fan or a pump, and a voice calling a name.

"How many objects are below the threshold?"

"At any given time, a few thousand."

"And the screening volume?"

"Twenty-five kilometres radial, fifty along-track, fifty cross-track."

"So the summary could say nothing, and there could be something."

"The summary is accurate. It's just answering a narrower question than the one you're asking."

"That's a very careful sentence," June said.

"It's the true one."

"Okay." A breath. "Okay. Thank you. I have to go, we're coming up on a handover."

"June."

"Yes?"

"Look up the object K-7741."

The loop clicked. The pass was ending; the carrier dropped for a second and came back.

"Say again?"

"K-7741. Look it up. It's in the catalogue. It's classified as debris."

"Ms Merrick, I don't have catalogue access from—"

The carrier dropped. The pass ended at 11:44, and the loop went to static, and Ilsa sat with the handset against her ear for a moment before she put it down.

---

On the station, June Park floated in the comms bay with the headset around her neck and the handover checklist on the screen in front of her.

She had two minutes before the next item on the checklist. She used them to open the station's copy of the conjunction product, which was a read-only mirror updated on every pass, and to type K-7741 into the search field.

The search returned a row: *K-7741, debris, priority 4, no further action, last epoch 11 hours.*

She looked at it for a moment. Then she opened the station's copy of the screening volume calculation, which was a document she had never opened, and read the definition of priority 4.

Priority 4 meant *below screening threshold; not included in conjunction summaries.*

June Park told Ada about the call at the handover, because the handover was where things got said.

"She asked me to look up an object," June said. "K-7741."

Ada looked up from the checklist. "Why?"

"She said it's in the catalogue as debris and it's in our screening volume."

"The summary says no tracked objects."

"The summary says no tracked objects above the threshold." June held up her tablet. "I looked up the threshold. Priority four and below aren't included. K-7741 is priority four."

Ada took the tablet. The row was there, exactly as June described: a catalogue number, a classification, a priority, and a last epoch.

"Who was the analyst?"

"Merrick. Ilsa Merrick."

"Did she say anything else?"

"She said the summary was answering a narrower question than the one I was asking." June paused. "And then the pass ended."

Ada looked at the row for a while. Then she handed the tablet back.

"Pull the raw screening data for the next pass," she said. "Not the summary. The data."

"The station doesn't get raw data. We get the product."

"Then ask for it. On the loop. In writing."

June nodded and went to the comms bay, and Ada floated in the node with the checklist in her hand and the water ration in her head, and she thought about the sentence *a narrower question*.

---

The request went out on the next pass at 14:20. It was a standard form, which meant it was a text message on a loop, and it said: *Meridian requests raw screening data for object K-7741, priority four, per operator technical annex 7.3.*

The reply came back at 14:26: *Request received. Raw data is commercially sensitive. Refer to operator.*

"Refer to operator," June read. "We are the operator."

"We're the tenant," Ada said. "The operator is the company that owns the hull. We're the crew that lives in it."

"That's a distinction that works in a contract and not in a vacuum."

"It's the distinction we have."

Ada went to the lab module and looked at the station's own radar, which was a small X-band unit for rendezvous and docking, with a range of thirty kilometres and a field of view that was mostly forward. It could not see an object that had already passed.

She stood at the window with her hand on the frame and looked at the earth, which was doing its slow rotation under the station, and at the terminator, which was a line of light moving across the Pacific.

"June," she said over the intercom. "Log the request and the reply. Both times. Put them in the station log with the word *refused*."

"It wasn't refused. It was referred."

"Put the word *refused* and then the word *referred* in brackets. Let the reader decide."

She closed the document. The handover checklist was waiting. She opened the next item, which was the water ration, and she read it, and she signed it, and then she went back to the search field and typed the number again, because she wanted to be sure she had typed it correctly the first time.

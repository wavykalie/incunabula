# Chapter 40

Three months later she was working at a company that tracked objects for insurers, in a building with a view of a river instead of a dish farm, and the queue opened at 08:00 every morning.

The company was smaller than Kestrel and older, and its catalogue had been built from insurance claims rather than launches, which meant it was full of objects that had done something expensive. It had no conjunction product. It sold assessments. The assessments were read by people who decided whether to pay.

Her title was senior analyst. There was no supervision condition on her account. There was a second signature on every classification, but that was the company's policy for everyone, and nobody had added a note to her file.

She had been there for six weeks. She had classified four hundred and twelve objects. She had found two mass anomalies and had reported both, and both had been resolved as sensor calibration, and she had accepted the resolutions because the evidence supported them.

On the Tuesday morning in the third month, the queue had two hundred and twelve objects in it, and eleven of them required a judgement.

She worked the top of the list first, the way the procedure said to. The clock in the bay ran two minutes slow, and she had not corrected for it, because she had learned that a clock is a fact about a building and not a fact about the sky.

At 08:40 she reached object 187. The designation was a claim number, not a catalogue number, because the company's catalogue was organised by loss. The object was a fragment in a decaying orbit, catalogued as a separation ring, and its drag response was wrong.

She opened the file. Mass estimate, four hundred and ten kilograms. Area-to-mass ratio, a third of the expected value for a ring. Tumble, no data. Last observation, nine days old.

She sat with it for a moment. Then she opened the raw returns, and pulled the last two hundred days, and ran the fit, which took eleven minutes.

The fit came back with a mass of nine hundred kilograms and a manoeuvre history of two events.

She looked at the number for a long time. The building hummed. The river was doing what rivers do. Somewhere above her, in a shell that was getting more crowded every year, an object with no launch record was turning in the sun.

She did not sign the classification.

She opened a new file, and she typed the object's number at the top of it, and under the number she wrote the date and the time and the words *mass anomaly, unresolved*, and then she saved it and went to find her supervisor.

Her supervisor's name was Ruth and she had been at the company for fourteen years and she believed in the second signature the way some people believed in exercise.

"Nine hundred kilograms," Ruth said, looking at the screen. "The ring's declared at four hundred and ten."

"That's the declared mass in the claim file. The drag says nine hundred."

"The drag says nine hundred if the area's right. Is the area right?"

"The area's from the fragment's orientation model, which is from the separation event, which is from the launch provider's data."

"And the launch provider's data is what the claim was priced on." Ruth sat back. "So you're saying the claim was priced on a mass that's wrong by a factor of two."

"I'm saying the object's mass is inconsistent with the declared mass, and the inconsistency has a manoeuvre history attached."

Ruth looked at the screen for a while.

"Show me the manoeuvres."

She showed her. The two events, the residuals, the fits, the covariance.

Ruth read them carefully, which was the thing Ilsa had not expected, and which was the reason she had gone to find her instead of filing the anomaly and moving on.

"These are good fits," Ruth said.

"They're consistent."

"They're good." Ruth turned from the screen. "I'm going to tell you what happens next, and I want you to listen to the whole of it before you decide whether you've made a mistake.

"First, I take this to the claims team, because the claim is ours and the pricing is ours and a mass error of a factor of two is a material fact. Second, the claims team asks the launch provider for the separation data, and the launch provider says the data is commercially confidential, and the claims team sends a formal request. Third, the formal request goes to the provider's legal team, and the legal team asks us why we want it, and we say because our analyst found a mass anomaly and a manoeuvre history. Fourth, the legal team asks who the analyst is."

"And then?"

"And then somebody in a meeting says your name, and somebody else looks you up, and the first thing they find is a regulatory case in another jurisdiction about a classification you made in 2024." Ruth said it without unkindness. "That's not fair. It's also not avoidable. The question is whether you want your name on this."

Ilsa looked at the screen. The object's designation was a claim number. The mass anomaly was four hundred and ninety kilograms. The manoeuvre history was two events.

"I want my name on it," she said.

"Then I'll put it on it." Ruth picked up her phone. "But I'm going to do one thing first. I'm going to write the referral myself, in my own words, and I'm going to cite your analysis as the basis. That way the first document in the file is mine, and the second document is yours, and nobody can say the analyst went around her supervisor."

"You don't have to do that."

"I know I don't." Ruth dialled. "I've been doing this for fourteen years. I've watched three analysts go around their supervisors. None of them got what they wanted. All of them got a file with their name on it and nobody else's."

Ruth took the referral to the claims team at 09:40, and the meeting was at 14:00, and Ilsa was asked to attend.

The claims team was three people and a wall of screens. The senior one was called Marchetti and he had the manner of a man who had been asked to price things that could not be priced.

"Nine hundred kilograms," he said. "The claim was priced on four hundred and ten. That's a difference of four hundred and ninety kilograms and it changes the debris model, which changes the expected loss, which changes the premium we charged."

"Which means the policy was mispriced."

"Which means the policy was mispriced in one direction and the loss, if it happens, will be larger than the reserve." Marchetti looked at the screen. "We're not the insurer. We're the assessor. Our job is to tell the insurer what the object is and what it's likely to do. If the object is a vehicle with a manoeuvre history, the expected loss isn't a debris strike. It's an approach."

"Which is a different product."

"Which is a product we don't sell." He turned. "Ms Merrick, I've read your file. You found a mass anomaly in an object that had been priced as debris for two years. The question I have is not whether you're right. The question is how many other objects in the book have the same problem."

She had not thought of that, and she should have.

"How many objects are in the book?"

"Four hundred and eleven. Of those, thirty-one have a mass estimate derived from a declared mass rather than a measurement, and eleven of those have a manoeuvre note in the observation log that was never escalated."

"Eleven."

"Eleven." Marchetti looked at her. "The archive project you came from was reconciling the catalogue against the observation logs. The observation logs are the thing that has the manoeuvre notes. If the notes were never escalated, the book has a systematic problem and we've been pricing it for a decade."

Ilsa looked at the wall of screens. Four hundred and eleven objects, thirty-one with a declared mass, eleven with an unescalated note.

"I can re-run the eleven," she said. "The fits take about a day each."

"That's a week."

"A week of my time is cheaper than a mispriced book."

Marchetti looked at Ruth, and Ruth looked at him, and something passed between them that Ilsa could not read.

"Do it," Marchetti said. "And Ms Merrick—when you find the ones you can't resolve, don't file them as anomalies. File them as open questions with a named owner. An anomaly goes to a committee. An open question goes to a person."

---

She started the first fit at 14:40, in the bay with the clock that ran two minutes slow.

The object was a fragment in a sun-synchronous orbit, catalogued as a separation ring, with a declared mass and a drag response that was, when she plotted it, wrong by a factor of two.

She sat with the plot for a moment. Then she wrote the object's number at the top of a new file, and under it the date and the time and the words *open question, owner: Merrick*, and she started the second fit before the first one had finished.

The file was still open on her screen when she left the desk, and the cursor was blinking at the end of the line, and the queue behind it was waiting.

# Chapter 10

The watch item was downgraded at 20:12, four hours and seven minutes after it was raised.

Ilsa saw it because the system sent her a notification, which was the system's way of being helpful. The notification said the watch item had been reviewed and reclassified as a *watch item, routine*, and that the change had been made by the duty officer in accordance with a standing instruction. There was a link to the instruction.

She clicked it.

The instruction was a single page, titled *Catalogue Escalation — Standing Instruction 7*, and it had been issued on 12 March 2024. It said that watch items concerning objects acquired through the 2021 data purchase were to be reviewed by the duty officer before distribution, and that in the absence of a confirmed launch record the default disposition was *routine*, and that client technical contacts were not to be notified until the review was complete.

It was signed by Priya Raghunathan. The countersignature was a client reference number, VL-2024-11, and a date, 12 March 2024.

The same day as the Vantage instruction. The same day, two days before the appendix that carried Ilsa's signature. The document chain was not hidden; it was filed. It was a standing instruction in a system that logged everything, written by a manager who had been careful, and it did exactly what it said it did.

She read the instruction again and found the sentence that mattered. *In the absence of a confirmed launch record.*

K-7741 had no launch record. That was the anomaly, and it was also the trigger. The absence of a launch record was not evidence against the object; it was the condition under which the object's warnings were suppressed. The system had been designed so that the worst case — an object nobody could account for — produced the quietest response.

She called Priya. It was 20:20 and Priya answered on the second ring.

"You've seen the downgrade."

"I've seen it."

"Then you know it's not me. It's the instruction. The instruction was written for a reason, and the reason was that in 2023 we had eleven objects with no launch record and every one of them turned out to be a data artefact. Eleven false alarms in a month, Ilsa. The clients threatened to cancel. The instruction is the thing that kept the product alive."

"One of them isn't an artefact."

"One of them is a mass anomaly with a manoeuvre history, and I've commissioned a second fit, and the second fit agrees with the first. That's why the watch item still exists. It's routine, it's not suppressed. It's in the system."

"It's in the system with a label that says routine. The station gets nothing."

"The station gets a summary. The summary is generated from the catalogue. If you want the catalogue to change, you need the client to agree that the object's classification was wrong, and the client will not agree to that on the basis of an analyst's fit, because an analyst's fit is not a launch record."

"Then get me the launch record."

There was a pause. In the pause Ilsa heard the paper diary, and the pen, and the small sound of a woman deciding.

"Schedule 4," Priya said. "You requested access."

"Yes."

"I'll approve it. Tomorrow morning. Read it. Then come and tell me what you want to do, and I'll tell you what the company can do, and we'll find out whether those are the same thing."

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because it's eight o'clock and I've been in this building since six, and because the instruction says requests are reviewed in two business days, and I'm doing you a favour by not making you wait."

She hung up.

Ilsa sat with the phone in her hand. The notification was still on her screen. The watch item, routine, would be reviewed by the duty officer at the end of the week, which was after the conjunction.

She opened the standing instruction again and read the last paragraph, which she had skipped. It said that in the event of a conjunction with a crewed asset, the duty officer was to notify the client's technical contact directly, *notwithstanding the review status*.

There was a procedure for the thing she was afraid of. It existed. It was written down. It required the duty officer to know that a conjunction existed, and the duty officer knew, because she had told him, because she had filed a sensor artefact report with a severity of high and a basis for override that said *Pc 1:400 nominal, 1:90 inflated*.

The procedure was a chain of people, and every link in it was a person who could decide that the number was not yet a conjunction.

She looked at the clock. Fifty-two hours, eleven minutes.

Voss was in the duty office at 21:00, which was his second shift of the day, and he looked like a man who had been awake since four.

"I downgraded it," he said, before she asked. "You've seen the notification."

"I've seen it."

"Then you know why."

"Tell me anyway."

He turned his chair away from the console. The duty office had four screens and a kettle and a window that looked at the car park.

"The instruction says objects from the 2021 purchase without a launch record default to routine pending review. K-7741 has no launch record. I applied the instruction."

"The object has a mass anomaly and a manoeuvre history. It's not a debris object."

"The instruction doesn't mention mass. It mentions launch record." He said it without heat. "I've read it eleven times tonight. It says what it says. If I'd left the item at high, and the client had seen it, and the object had turned out to be a data artefact, I'd be the duty officer who raised a false alarm on a Friday night and cost the company a client relationship. That's not a hypothetical. That happened in 2023. Three times."

"So the instruction is a scar."

"The instruction is a policy." He picked up a mug and put it down without drinking. "You want to know what I think? I think the instruction is wrong. I think it was written to solve a problem that was real in 2023 and it's been applied to a case that isn't that problem. And I think that if I break it, the review will find that I broke it, and the object will still be in the catalogue, and the next duty officer will have a precedent that says instructions can be ignored when the duty officer has a feeling."

"That's not a feeling. It's a fit."

"It's a fit that nobody has signed except you." He looked at her. "That's the whole thing, isn't it. You're right and I believe you're right and the instruction says what it says. The system is not designed for the case where the analyst is right and the instruction is wrong. It's designed for the case where the instruction is right and the analyst is tired."

Ilsa stood in the doorway. The car park lights were on, and the dish farm was a dark shape beyond them.

"What would change your mind?"

"A second signature on the fit. Not yours. Somebody in the building who'll put their name on the same page." He turned back to the console. "You've got an independent fit from the night analyst. That's a start. Get the night analyst to sign the classification change. If two analysts sign, I can treat it as a review finding rather than an override, and the instruction doesn't cover review findings."

"Who's the night analyst?"

"Balint. He's on at six tomorrow." Voss picked up the mug again. "He's careful. He'll read the whole file. Ask him at six and he'll have an answer by seven."

She turned to go.

"Merrick." She stopped. "For what it's worth, I hope he signs it. I've been the duty officer on this desk for six years and I've never once hoped an analyst was right about something this badly."

Then she did the thing she had been avoiding since the records room, which was to write down, in her own notebook, the names of every person in the chain, and beside each name the date on which they had last had a reason to protect the client.

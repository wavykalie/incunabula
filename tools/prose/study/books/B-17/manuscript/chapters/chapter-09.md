# Chapter 09

The contract file was called *VL-2024-11 Catalogue Services*, and it had a lock icon beside it that Ilsa had never clicked before.

She clicked it at 16:05. The system thought for a moment and returned a page that said *access restricted: client contract*. There was a button labelled *request access*, and a note that said requests were reviewed within two business days.

Two business days was Saturday.

She sat back. The catalogue was hers; the product was hers; the file that said what the product was for belonged to somebody else. She had known this in the abstract, the way she knew the building had a basement. She had never needed the basement.

The access log was visible, because the access log was part of the system and the system was a catalogue of everything, including itself. She opened it out of habit, the way she opened the epoch of any object she was about to work on, and scrolled to the bottom.

*VL-2024-11 — access log — last 90 days.*

There were eleven entries. Ten of them were Priya. The eleventh was her.

*02:11:47, 17 October, Ilsa Merrick, view, session 8841-C.*

She looked at it for a while. On 17 October she had been at home, asleep, because on 18 October she had driven to Fresno for the quarterly assessment and had left at six in the morning. She remembered the drive. She remembered the coffee.

She opened the session record. Session 8841-C had been initiated from a terminal in the catalogue bay, on the third floor, at 02:11. The terminal was the one at the end of the row, the one nobody used, because the chair was broken.

The session had lasted four minutes and eleven seconds. It had opened the contract file, scrolled to a section headed *Schedule 4 — hygiene reviews*, and closed it. There was no download, no print, no screenshot.

Four minutes was enough time to read a clause and decide what to do about it.

She thought about the token in her drawer. The token was a hardware key that generated a six-digit code, and it was required for contract-level access, and it had been in the drawer the whole time. The drawer had a lock. The lock was the kind that opened with a paperclip.

She looked at the timestamp again. 02:11. In chapter nineteen of the records room report, the appendix was dated 14 March 2024. In the access log, three weeks ago, somebody had used her name at two in the morning to read the clause that made the reclassification legal.

She picked up the phone and called the security desk, which was staffed by a contractor who came in four days a week.

"Badge records," she said. "For the third floor, 17 October, between two and three in the morning."

"That's a request. You want me to file a request?"

"I want to know who was in the building."

"The system says who was in the building. It says you were."

"I wasn't."

"Then the system says you were," the contractor said, with the patience of a man who had been told this before about other things.

She hung up.

---

At 16:40 the duty officer came back with the independent fit.

The fit had been run by an analyst on the night shift whose name Ilsa did not know, using the raw returns she had supplied and the same software she had used. It agreed with her to four decimal places on the semi-major axis and to six on the eccentricity. The mass estimate came back at eight hundred and ninety kilograms, with an uncertainty of a hundred and forty.

The duty officer's note said: *fit confirms analyst's elements. Classification not adjusted pending client review. Watch item raised at severity high.*

She read the last sentence three times. A watch item. Severity high. The artefact report had worked.

Then she opened the watch item and read the distribution list. It went to the duty officer, the catalogue group, Priya, and the client's technical contact. It did not go to Meridian Station.

The station received conjunction summaries. Summaries were generated from the catalogue. The catalogue still said debris, and debris did not generate a row, and the watch item was an internal note about a row that had not changed.

She had moved the number from one column to another. The station was still being told there was nothing there.

She looked at the clock. Fifty-three hours. Then she opened the clause she had not been able to read, and typed a request for access to Schedule 4, and gave as her reason: *analyst review, object K-7741, client hygiene provision*. The request went to Priya, because all requests went to Priya, and the system told her it would be reviewed within two business days.

The security contractor's name was Dale, and he had a desk in the lobby with a monitor that showed sixteen camera feeds and a chair that reclined.

Ilsa went down at 16:55 and stood at the desk until he looked up.

"Third floor, 17 October, two in the morning," she said. "I asked earlier. You said the system said I was in the building."

"That's what it said."

"I want the camera."

Dale looked at her for a moment. He was sixty and had the specific patience of a man who had been a guard in a building with a lot of engineers.

"The cameras keep thirty days," he said. "Seventeenth of October is twenty-one days ago. It'll be there."

"Then show me."

He turned the monitor and clicked through a menu that had been designed by somebody who did not expect anyone to use it. The feeds were indexed by hour and camera. Camera 3 was the third-floor corridor, camera 4 was the catalogue bay door, camera 7 was the stairwell.

He loaded the seventeenth of October, 02:00 to 02:30, camera 4.

The footage was grey and silent and ran at four frames a second. The catalogue bay door was closed. At 02:09 a figure came into the frame from the left, wearing a coat, with a hood up. The figure stood at the door for eleven seconds, and then the door opened, and the figure went in.

"That's not me," Ilsa said.

"It's a person in a coat."

"Look at the height." She pointed at the door frame, which had a fire extinguisher bracket at shoulder height. "The bracket's at one point four metres. That person's head is above it. I'm one point six three. That person is one point eight, maybe more."

Dale looked at the frame. Then he looked at her.

"You want me to say the system's wrong."

"I want you to say what the camera shows."

"The camera shows a person." He clicked back. "The badge system shows your card at the door at 02:09. The two systems disagree, or the person had your card."

"Can you export the footage?"

"I can export it to a request. A request goes to the security manager. The security manager's on the fourth floor and he's in meetings until Thursday."

"Then I'll make a request."

"That's the procedure." He turned the monitor back. "For what it's worth, the badge system is the one that gets audited. The camera's the one that gets overwritten. If you want the footage to exist on Thursday, you want it exported today."

"Can you export it today?"

"I can export it today if a manager approves it." He said it without any particular sympathy. "You're not a manager."

She stood at the desk for a moment. The lobby was quiet, and the sixteen feeds showed sixteen versions of a building that was mostly empty.

"Who's the security manager's manager?"

"The facilities director."

"And who's the facilities director's manager?"

"The chief operating officer." Dale looked at her. "You're going to walk up four floors and ask the COO to approve a camera export."

"I'm going to ask."

"Then ask," he said. "But ask today. Tomorrow the footage is twenty-two days old and the buffer's the buffer."

She sat in the broken chair at the end of the row, in the dark, with the terminal's fan running, and thought about the four minutes somebody had spent in her name.

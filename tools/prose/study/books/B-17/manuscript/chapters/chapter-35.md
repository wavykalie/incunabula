# Chapter 35

She filed the supplement at 09:00 on Saturday, from the kitchen table, with the laptop connected to the regulator's portal and the phone on speaker beside it.

The supplement was eleven pages. It added four things to the audit: the post-crossing manoeuvre of the first object, the activation of the second object, the reconstructed two-vehicle system, and the catalogue edit at 04:12 with the observation log that showed dish six had not been tasked.

She filed it as an addendum to the existing case, not as a new complaint, because the portal had a field for addenda and because an addendum to an open case could not be closed without a response.

The portal accepted it and returned a receipt. The receipt had a case number she already knew and a line that said *submitted 09:04:12*.

She sent the same document to the insurer's technical address and to the operator's duty desk, with a covering note that said: *this addendum contains evidence that two objects are manoeuvring in the orbital plane of Meridian Station. The first has been catalogued as debris. The second has no catalogue entry. Both are under active control.*

Then she sat back and looked at the three confirmations on the screen.

The regulator's confirmation was a receipt. The insurer's confirmation was an auto-reply with a ticket number. The operator's confirmation was a read receipt from a duty desk address.

None of them was a person. All three were records.

She thought about Grant Iwasaki, who had said that the record was the only thing that survives, and about Hal, who had said the notebook was the only thing that says it existed, and about Priya, who had said the process would be the story.

At 09:30 her phone rang. It was a number she did not recognise, with a Colorado area code.

"Ms Merrick. This is Doug. We spoke yesterday."

"I remember."

"I've read your supplement. The manoeuvre at twenty-one ten and the second object's burn at twenty-three forty."

"Yes."

"I've asked the surveillance centre to add both objects to the crewed-asset screening bulletin. The first goes on the bulletin today. The second goes on as an uncorrelated track, which means it'll appear as a warning box rather than an object, because we don't have a catalogue entry to attach to it."

"That's fine."

"It's not fine, but it's what we can do." He paused. "I also want to tell you something that isn't for the record. The platform. Aster Minor. We've been watching it for eleven months."

She sat very still.

"You knew."

"We knew there was a platform. We didn't know what it launched. A platform that registers in a flag state and notifies late is not, by itself, illegal, and the desk that watches platforms is a different desk from the desk that watches objects. The two desks do not talk as often as they should."

"And now?"

"Now I've sent the registration and the aerosol transient to the other desk, with your name on the covering note. That's the most useful thing I can do with your name this week."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. It's a transfer of paperwork." He said it without irony. "One more thing. The second object. You've got it phasing to the first. If it's a pair, and the first is a vehicle, the second is either a target or a weapon. If it's a weapon, the target isn't the station."

"What is it?"

"Whatever the first one is pointed at," Doug said. "And the first one just moved above the station, which means it can come down on any satellite in that shell. There are four hundred of them. Most of them are insured."

She looked at the screen. The three confirmations sat in a row, each with a number, each a record.

"So the station was never the target," she said.

The regulator's case officer was called Davies and he called back at 10:15, which was faster than the portal had promised.

"I've read the addendum," he said. "It's unusual."

"Unusual how?"

"It's unusual for an addendum to contain evidence of a second object that isn't in any catalogue." He said it without accusation. "Most addenda are corrections of fact. Yours is a new set of facts."

"The facts were always there. The addendum is the first document that puts them in one place."

"Which is why I'm calling." A pause. "Ms Merrick, I need to tell you how this will be read. The case is about a classification. The classification was made in 2024 and corrected on Monday. The addendum describes events that happened after the correction, including a manoeuvre by the object and the activation of a second object."

"Yes."

"Those events are outside the scope of the case."

"They're outside the scope of the case and inside the scope of the risk."

"That's not a distinction the case file can hold," Davies said. "The case file holds a classification and a correction. If I put a second object in it, the file becomes a different file, and a different file needs a different authority, and the authority for that is not mine."

Ilsa sat with the phone against her ear and looked at the three confirmations on the screen.

"Then what happens to the addendum?"

"The addendum is received. It's placed on the file. It's read by the reviewer and it's summarised in the case note. The case note goes to the panel, and the panel decides whether the scope should be extended."

"How long does that take?"

"The panel sits monthly."

"So a month."

"A month, if the panel takes it up at the next sitting." Davies paused. "I'm telling you this because I'd rather you heard it from me than inferred it. The addendum is a record. It's not a trigger. The trigger is the panel."

"The object is in a holding orbit above a crewed station."

"The station has a bulletin now. The bulletin is a trigger. The bulletin went out on Monday because of the correction, and the correction happened because of your audit." He said it evenly. "You've already pulled the trigger that matters. The addendum is the paperwork that makes sure the trigger is on the record."

Ilsa looked at the folder on the table.

"Can I ask you something that isn't procedural?"

"Yes."

"Does the panel read the addenda?"

"Some of them." He sounded tired. "The ones with eleven pages and four exhibits, usually not. The ones with a summary on the first page and a timeline on the second, usually yes."

"Then I'll send a summary."

"Send a summary," Davies said. "One page. The object, the manoeuvres, the second object, the platform, and what you want the panel to do. If the panel has to work out what you want, it won't."

"The station was the demonstration," Doug said. "The station was the thing that made the catalogue change its mind."

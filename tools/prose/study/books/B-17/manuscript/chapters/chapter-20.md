# Chapter 20

Priya offered her the Vantage account lead on Thursday afternoon, in the same office, with the same chair that was lower than the desk.

"It's a promotion," she said. "More money, a title that means something to clients, and an office with a door."

"And the NDA."

"And the standard client confidentiality agreement, which you've already signed twice in eight years." Priya slid a folder across the desk. "The difference is that this one covers hygiene reviews specifically, because the account lead sits in the room when the reviews are commissioned."

Ilsa did not open the folder.

"You're offering me the job that signs the instructions," she said.

"I'm offering you the job that can refuse to sign them." Priya leaned back. "Think about it. The account lead is the person who tells the client what the catalogue can and can't do. The account lead is in the room when Grant Iwasaki asks for a reclassification. Right now that room has me in it and nobody else. You've spent three days telling me the room needs somebody who thinks like you. This is the door."

"The door with the NDA."

"Every door has an NDA. The question is what's behind it."

Ilsa looked at the folder. It was grey, with the Vantage logo on the cover, and a tab that said *Confidential — Account Lead*.

"What happens to the artefact report if I take it?"

"It becomes a review under the account, which means it gets a date and a technical officer and a conclusion."

"And if I don't take it?"

"Then it stays where it is, which is in the queue, with a date and a technical officer and a conclusion." Priya spread her hands. "The promotion isn't a bribe, Ilsa. It's a job. I'm offering it because you're the best analyst I have and because the account is going to need somebody who can say no to the client without losing the client, and I think you can learn to do that."

"I don't want to learn to do that."

"I know." Priya said it quietly. "That's why I'm asking."

Ilsa picked up the folder and did not open it. Through the window, the dish farm was doing its afternoon turn, nine white shapes moving in a slow choreography that had been designed to avoid collisions and had never failed.

"Does the offer expire?"

"Friday," Priya said. "Close of business."

Friday was the day the object arrived.

"Close of business is five," Ilsa said.

"Close of business is when I leave the building, and tomorrow I'll be here until eight."

Ilsa put the folder down on the desk.

"I'll tell you tomorrow," she said.

"Before eight."

"Before eight."

She went back to her desk. Femi was at his terminal with the water bottle he refilled four times a day, and he looked up as she passed, and she shook her head, once, and he went back to his screen.

The folder sat on the corner of her desk for the rest of the afternoon. She did not open it. At 18:40 she put it in her bag, because leaving it on the desk would have been a statement, and she was not ready to make one.

At home she put it on the kitchen table next to the approach plates, and she made tea, and she sat looking at it while the light went out of the window. Then she opened it.

The agreement was fourteen pages. Clause 9 was the one that mattered. It said that the account lead acknowledged that hygiene reviews were advisory, that client instructions were binding, and that the account lead would not disclose the existence or content of a review to any third party, including a customer of the company, without the client's written consent.

She read clause 9 twice. Then she read the signature page, which had a line for her name and a date and a box for the witness.

The NDA was nine pages, and clause 7 was the one that mattered.

*The employee shall not disclose, whether during or after employment, any information relating to the classification of objects, the existence of objects, or the absence of objects from any catalogue maintained by the company or its clients.*

She read it three times. The third time she read it aloud, quietly, in Priya's office with the door closed.

"The absence of objects from any catalogue," she said. "That's not a confidentiality clause. That's a clause about silence."

"It's a standard clause," Priya said. "It's in every contract we sign with a client. It exists because a client doesn't want its competitors to know what it's tracking."

"It exists because a client doesn't want anybody to know what it's not tracking."

Priya looked at her for a moment.

"Yes," she said. "That's the same sentence with a different emphasis."

"And if I sign it, I can't tell a regulator that an object exists."

"You can tell a regulator anything a regulator compels you to tell. The clause doesn't override law. It overrides conversation."

"And if the regulator doesn't compel me? If the regulator asks a general question and I have to decide whether to volunteer the answer?"

"Then you'd be in breach of the clause, and the company would have a claim against you, and the claim would be expensive and slow and would probably be settled." Priya folded her hands. "Which is why I'm telling you the terms before you decide, instead of after."

"What are the terms?"

"More money. A title. The account. And the clause."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then you keep your job, your title, and your salary, and you don't get the account." Priya said it evenly. "And the audit you're writing stays an internal document."

Ilsa sat back. The chair was lower than the desk and the window was behind Priya and the light was in her eyes.

"The audit is already written," she said. "I've been writing it since Tuesday."

"I know. I've seen the draft in the shared drive." Priya's voice was quiet. "That's why I'm offering you the account. If you take it, the audit goes into a review process that has a legal privilege attached to it, and the review process is the thing that decides what happens to the object. If you don't take it, the audit is a document by an analyst with no account, and the company's first move will be to characterise it."

"Characterise it how?"

"As a draft. As an expression of concern. As the work of somebody who has been under a great deal of personal pressure." Priya looked at her. "I'm not threatening you. I'm describing the machine. You've been in the machine for eight years. You know how it works."

Ilsa looked at the nine pages.

"I need to think about it."

"Take until Friday." Priya stood. "But Ilsa—if you're going to file something, file it before Friday. Because on Friday the machine changes shape, and after that the filing will be read as a response to the machine rather than a description of the object."

She closed the folder and put it back in her bag and did not sleep well.

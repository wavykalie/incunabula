# Chapter 11

Femi ran it twice because that was the instruction, and then he ran it a third time because the first two agreed.

He came to her desk at 08:15 with his laptop open and his badge turned round on its lanyard, which he did when he was nervous.

"Your elements are right," he said. "I used the other package, the one the university gave us, and I got the same orbit to four decimals. The mass is different by eleven per cent but the mass is a guess in both."

"Sit down."

He sat. He was twenty-six and his hands were steady and his voice was not.

"The conjunction at fifty-two hours," he said. "I get one in three hundred nominal, one in seventy with your inflation. That's above the warning threshold."

"Yes."

"The catalogue says debris."

"Yes."

"So the summary won't include it."

"No."

He looked at the laptop, then at her. "I don't understand how this is allowed to happen. There must be a procedure. There's a procedure for everything here. I've read the manual twice."

"There's a procedure." She told him about the standing instruction, and the review status, and the clause that required notification notwithstanding the review, and the duty officer who had already downgraded the item once.

Femi listened. When she finished, he was quiet for a moment.

"Who's the duty officer tonight?"

"Voss. He's on until six."

"And tomorrow?"

"Balint."

"Do they both know?"

"Voss knows. He ran the fit. Balint will read the log when he comes on."

"And the client's technical contact?"

"G. Iwasaki. He's the compliance officer. He's the one who signed the direction in 2024."

Femi nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something he had already worked out and had hoped to be wrong about.

"Can I ask you something that isn't about the object?"

"Yes."

"Is being right a defence? I'm asking because I don't know what the procedure is for this. I've only ever worked where being right was the thing you were paid for."

She thought about 2018, and the panel, and the programme that had been cancelled for unrelated reasons, and the fact that being right had cost Priya a client and had cost her nothing at all except eight years of a particular kind of sleep.

"No," she said. "It's a fact. Facts are useful. They're not a defence."

"Then what is?"

"A record that somebody else can read after you've been fired."

He looked at his laptop. Then he closed it.

"I made a copy," he said. "Last night. The returns, the fit, the draft, the access log entry. It's on a drive at home. I know I'm not supposed to take data off site. I read the policy."

"So did I."

"I'm not going to do anything with it. I just wanted it to exist somewhere that isn't here."

She looked at him for a moment. He was twenty-six, and his visa depended on the job, and he had already decided which of those two facts he was going to live with.

"Keep it," she said. "Don't tell anyone you have it. If nothing happens, delete it in a month and forget you did it."

"And if something happens?"

"Then you'll have the only copy that isn't in a system somebody can edit."

He stood up. At the end of the row he turned back.

"For what it's worth, I checked the 2021 purchase log this morning. It's not in the contract file. It's in the data engineering archive, because the purchase predates the contract. There's a provenance note for every block. The block K-7741 came from has a note that says the seller declined to provide a launch record."

"Does it say why?"

"It says the seller declined to provide a launch record." He shrugged. "That's the whole note. Somebody wrote a sentence that says there's a hole, and then the object went into the catalogue anyway."

He left. Ilsa sat at her desk with the morning queue filling up in front of her, two hundred and eleven objects, eleven of them requiring a judgement, and she did not open it.

The data engineering archive was in the basement, behind a door that said NO ACCESS WITHOUT ESCORT, and the escort was a man named Osei who had been at Kestrel for two years and had never been asked for anything before.

"The purchase log is a table," he said. "It's got a row per block. Block ID, seller, date, volume, and a provenance note. Most of the notes are one line."

He pulled the table up on a terminal that was older than the building. The screen had a slight flicker that he did not seem to notice.

"Here's 2021." He scrolled. "Four hundred and eleven blocks. The seller was a company called Meridian Data Services. They went under in 2022. We bought the blocks in a bankruptcy sale."

"Meridian Data Services."

"Nothing to do with the station. It's a coincidence of names." He said it without hesitation, which meant he had said it before.

Ilsa looked at the rows. Each block had a volume in observations and a provenance note. Most notes said *seller records complete*. Some said *seller records partial*. A handful said *seller declined to provide provenance*.

"Show me the ones that declined."

He filtered. There were eleven. Each had a block ID, a volume, and a note. The note on K-7741's block said: *seller declined to provide launch record. Object included at buyer's instruction.*

"Buyer's instruction," Ilsa said.

"That's the note."

"Whose instruction?"

"The note doesn't say. It's a free-text field." Osei looked at her. "I've been asked about this table twice in two years. Once by an auditor and once by you. The auditor asked whether the volumes reconciled. He didn't ask about the notes."

"Who was the auditor?"

"A woman from the parent company. She had a checklist." He shrugged. "The volumes reconciled."

Ilsa wrote the block ID in her notebook. Then she asked him to export the eleven rows to a file and send it to her, and he said he could not export without a request, and she asked who could approve a request, and he said the data engineering manager, and the data engineering manager was on holiday until Monday.

"Then print it," she said.

"Printing's also a request."

"Osei. You've just shown me a table on a screen. Is there a camera in this room?"

He looked at the ceiling. There was no camera.

"I'm going to write the rows down," she said. "You're going to watch me write them down. That's not an export."

He watched her write. It took four minutes, because there were eleven rows and each row had five fields, and her handwriting was small and careful. When she finished, he looked at the page and then at her.

"You're going to get me fired," he said.

"I'm going to try very hard not to."

"That's not the same as not doing it."

"No," she said. "It's not."

She went back up in the lift with the page in her notebook and the eleven rows in her head, and the one that mattered was the one that said *included at buyer's instruction*, because a buyer's instruction was a decision, and a decision had a person behind it, and the person was somewhere in a system that kept records of instructions.

At her desk she opened the client instruction register, which was a system she had never had a reason to use, and searched for 2021.

There were four instructions. Three were about billing. The fourth was dated 14 September 2021 and said: *Meridian Data Services block K-2021-088 is to be added to the catalogue as a spent upper stage, classification debris, no further action. Provenance not required.*

The instruction was signed by G. Iwasaki.

Instead she opened the provenance note, and printed it, and put it in the folder.

# Chapter 23

The manoeuvre budget was a document with a table in it, and the table said Meridian Station had 0.4 metres per second of propellant available for contingency in the current quarter.

Ilsa obtained it the way she obtained everything that week, which was by asking and being told no and then finding it in a system that had not been designed to keep it secret. The station's operator published a quarterly allocation to its insurers as part of the underwriting file. The file was not confidential to Kestrel; it was confidential to the public, which was a different thing.

She read the table twice. Attitude control: 1.1 metres per second. Orbital maintenance: 0.9. Contingency: 0.4. The contingency line had a footnote that said *debris avoidance manoeuvres require operator waiver and are charged against the following quarter*.

Charged against the following quarter meant that a burn now was a burn not available later, and later was when the station needed to maintain its orbit against the solar cycle. A station that spent its contingency in September was a station that would be lower in March.

She computed the burn. The fragment's miss distance at the crossing point was, at the current phasing, nine hundred metres with a covariance that made the probability one in three hundred. A burn of 0.35 metres per second along the velocity vector, applied fourteen hours before the crossing, would move the station's position at crossing time by about five kilometres. That was enough. It would use most of the contingency and leave 0.05.

The operator was Vantage Launch Systems. The waiver process was in the station's flight rules, which were also in the underwriting file, and which said that a debris avoidance manoeuvre required a conjunction warning from an approved provider at a probability above one in ten thousand, and a recommendation from the flight director, and the operator's authorisation.

The approved provider was Kestrel. The warning was the product. The product said no tracked objects.

She sat at her kitchen table with the three documents open in front of her — the allocation, the flight rules, the standing instruction — and traced the loop with her finger.

Kestrel could not issue a warning because the object was debris. The object was debris because Vantage had instructed a reclassification. The reclassification was implemented under a clause that made the finding advisory. The advisory finding was the one that would have triggered the warning. The warning was what the flight rules required for the burn. The burn was what would save the station.

Every link held. Every link was a document. The loop was not a failure of any one document; it was the documents working exactly as written.

She picked up her phone and called the number on the back of Grant Iwasaki's card.

He answered on the fourth ring.

"Ms Merrick."

"The waiver process. Who authorises it?"

"The flight director, on the operator's behalf. For Meridian, that's a duty flight director in the operations centre. I can give you the name."

"Can you give me the authorisation?"

"I can't authorise a manoeuvre. I'm compliance." A pause. "I can confirm whether a warning exists. That's a factual question, and I'm allowed to answer factual questions."

"There is no warning."

"Then the flight director has nothing to act on." He said it slowly, as though he were writing it down. "Ms Merrick, are you asking me to create a warning?"

"I'm asking you what would make one exist."

"A classification change. Or an operator instruction to treat the object as a hazard notwithstanding its classification. The second one doesn't require the catalogue to change. It requires the operator to decide that the catalogue is not the only source of truth."

"Who can decide that?"

"The flight director can, in an emergency. The emergency has to be declared by the commander on the station or by the operator's duty manager. There's a form."

"There's always a form."

"There's always a form," Grant agreed. "The form is how an emergency becomes a record. Without the form, a courageous decision is an unauthorised burn."

She looked at the clock. It was 13:10 on Friday. The crossing was at 21:00.

The waiver was a form with a checkbox and a signature line, and the signature line belonged to the operator's flight director.

Ilsa found the form in the operator's technical annex, which was a public document, and read the instructions attached to it. A debris avoidance manoeuvre required a waiver when the manoeuvre would consume more than half of the quarter's contingency allocation. The waiver had to be signed by the flight director and countersigned by the operator's insurer.

The countersignature was the interesting part. The insurer had to agree to the burn, which meant the insurer had to be told about the conjunction, which meant the waiver was a notification channel that ran parallel to the provider's warning.

She called the station's flight director at 15:20. His name was Phil Okonjo and he had been in the job for three years and he answered his own phone.

"I've read your email," he said. "You're the analyst from Kestrel."

"I'm the analyst."

"Your company's summary says no tracked objects."

"Your company's summary is generated from our product. Our product is wrong."

"That's a remarkable thing for you to say on a recorded line."

"It's a recorded line because you record it. I'm saying it because it's true."

Phil was quiet for a moment. In the background there was a room: voices, a fan, a printer.

"What do you want me to do?"

"I want you to file a waiver request for a contingency burn."

"On the basis of an object your company says doesn't exist."

"On the basis of an object that a US Space Force bulletin listed at 13:38 with a miss distance of three hundred and eighty metres." She looked at the printout. "That bulletin is public. Your ground team can read it. Your insurer can read it. The waiver doesn't need my company's summary. It needs a reason, and the bulletin is a reason."

"The bulletin's an unclassified product with a one-in-forty probability."

"One in forty is the threshold your own flight rules use for a mandatory manoeuvre."

Phil breathed out. "You've read our flight rules."

"I've read your flight rules, your technical annex, your manoeuvre budget, and your insurance schedule. I've had a lot of time this week."

"Clearly." A pause. "The problem isn't the rules. The problem is the propellant. If I burn a third of the contingency on a one-in-forty, and the object turns out to be a piece of a stage, the next quarter has a third less contingency and the board wants to know why."

"And if you don't burn, and the object hits, the board wants to know why you had a bulletin and didn't act."

"That's the asymmetry," Phil said. "You've put your finger on it. The cost of burning is visible and small. The cost of not burning is invisible and enormous. Organisations are very bad at that trade."

"So burn."

"I can't burn on my own authority. I need the waiver, and the waiver needs the insurer, and the insurer needs a claim reference." He paused. "Which means I need something from Kestrel that says the object exists and the conjunction is real. Not a bulletin. A provider statement."

"Then I'll get you one."

"You're on leave, aren't you."

"I'm on leave," Ilsa said. "I'm also the only person in the building who's read your flight rules."

"Give me the name of the flight director," she said. "And the form number."

# Chapter 33

The burn happened at 20:40 and the pass happened at 21:02, and Ilsa watched both from the car park because the floor was locked to her badge.

She had a laptop on the passenger seat, tethered to her phone, with the public tracking feed open and the station's voice loop on a speaker. The dish farm was behind her, nine white shapes in the dark, and the control room windows were lit, and she could see two people moving inside, which meant the night shift had been reinforced.

At 20:40 the loop carried a countdown in a voice she did not recognise, and then a series of numbers, and then Ada Reyes saying, "Burn complete. Delta-v nominal. Attitude reacquired."

The tracking feed updated eleven seconds later. Meridian's orbit had changed by a fraction of a degree, which on the screen looked like nothing, and which in the math moved the station five kilometres along its track at the crossing point.

At 20:52 the object came over the horizon. The public feed showed it as a diamond on a plot, with a covariance ellipse drawn around it in a translucent grey. The ellipse was small now, because the object had been observed by three sensors in the last four hours, and the smallness was the thing that made the pass legible.

At 20:58 the two markers began to converge. The station's track was a line with a dot on it. The object's track crossed the line at an angle of nine and a half degrees, and the crossing point had been the whole argument, and now the crossing point was empty.

At 21:00 the markers were four kilometres apart.

At 21:01 they were two point one kilometres apart.

At 21:02 they were moving apart.

The loop was quiet. Then Ada Reyes said, "Closest approach, two point one kilometres. No contact indication. No pressure change. All systems nominal."

Somebody in the control room cheered. Ilsa heard it through the glass, muffled, and then the sound of a chair.

She sat in the car with the laptop on the seat and her hands in her lap.

Two point one kilometres. The miss distance had been nine hundred metres at the original phasing. The burn had moved it to two point one kilometres, which was a good result, and which meant the burn had worked, and which meant the warning had worked, and which meant the machinery had done what machinery does when somebody finally turns the handle.

She should have felt something. She felt the way she felt after a fit converged: the flat satisfaction of a number that had stopped moving, and underneath it the knowledge that the number had been moving for four days while she watched.

At 21:10 the tracker feed updated again. The object's orbit had changed.

She sat forward. The diamond had moved off its predicted track by a margin that was visible at the scale of the plot, which meant kilometres, which meant a manoeuvre. The covariance ellipse had rotated.

She pulled the raw feed and computed the delta-v. It came out at 0.6 metres per second, applied along the velocity vector, nine minutes after the crossing.

The object had manoeuvred after the pass. It had not manoeuvred to hit the station; it had manoeuvred to change its orbit after the encounter, which was what a vehicle did when it had completed an approach and was moving to the next objective.

Or when it had completed a test and was going home.

She called Femi. He answered on the first ring.

"Are you seeing this?"

"The manoeuvre. Yes." He sounded awake in a way that meant he had not slept. "It's raising its perigee. It's going up."

"Where?"

"Nowhere good. It's going to a 410 by 430 orbit. That's above the station."

"Above the station and in the same plane."

"Above the station, same plane, and phasing." He paused. "Ilsa, if it's above the station and in the same plane, it can come down whenever it wants."

She looked at the plot. The diamond was moving away from the station's track, slowly, deliberately, the way a thing moves when it has all the time in the world.

"Femi. The second object."

"I've been watching it. It hasn't manoeuvred. It's still in the same plane, forty-one minutes behind the first."

"Which means what?"

"Which means the first one is the vehicle and the second one is the thing it left behind," he said. "Or the second one is the witness and the first one is the weapon, and the weapon has just moved to a holding orbit."

Ilsa sat in the car park with the laptop on the seat and the station's voice loop silent, and she thought about a machine that had all the time in the world and a crew that had a water ration and a hole in the truss.

Through the glass she could see the night shift at work, and the work was mostly waiting.

There were two people at the consoles and a third standing behind them with a headset, and the third was Phil Okonjo, who had come in at 20:30 and had not sat down since. The big screen at the front of the room showed the station's track as a line and the object's track as a line and the crossing point as a small red circle that had been drawn by hand with a marker on a transparency laid over the screen, because the room's display software did not have a function for a cone of uncertainty.

At 20:44 the object's line moved. The movement was a pixel, and the room reacted to it: the person at the left console said something, and Phil leaned forward, and the person at the right console typed.

Ilsa watched through the glass. She could not hear them. The glass was double-glazed and the room was soundproofed against the dish farm's fans, and the effect was like watching a sports broadcast with the sound off.

At 20:51 Phil picked up a phone. He spoke for forty seconds. Then he put the phone down and said something to the room, and both console operators turned to their screens, and the third person — a woman Ilsa did not know — left through the side door and came back with a printer page.

At 20:56 the screen changed. The red circle had a new circle around it, drawn in a different colour, and the two circles overlapped, and the overlap was the region where the object might be.

At 21:00 the object's line crossed the station's line.

The room did not react. The two console operators were looking at their screens, and Phil was looking at the big screen, and the object's marker went past the crossing point and kept going, and the station's marker went past the crossing point and kept going, and the two markers separated.

At 21:02 Phil said something and the room's posture changed. The left operator raised both hands above the console, which was the gesture of a man who had been holding something for a long time and had just put it down.

Ilsa stood in the corridor with her palm flat against the glass.

She had been in the building for eleven hours. She had not eaten since a sandwich at two in the afternoon. Her badge did not open the door to the control room, and she had watched the pass through a window like a visitor, and the thing she felt was not relief and not triumph. It was the flat, specific anger of a person who has spent four days being told that the thing she could see was not there, and who has just watched a room full of people agree with her in a language that did not include her name.

At 21:06 Phil came out.

"Two point one kilometres," he said.

"I saw."

"Your warning made the burn possible. I want you to know that. The waiver needed a provider statement, and the statement came from your audit, and the audit was the thing the insurer read."

"The audit was filed on Monday."

"The audit was filed on Monday and it sat in a queue until Thursday, and on Thursday the insurer reopened the case because your analyst sent them the raw data." He looked at her. "That's the chain. I've been doing this for three years and I've never seen a chain like it."

"What happens now?"

"Now we file the burn report and we wait for the next crossing." He looked at the big screen through the glass. "And we watch that thing up there, because it's still up there, and it's not debris."

The burn's telemetry came down on the next pass at 22:40, and Ilsa read it in the car park with the laptop balanced on the steering wheel.

The telemetry was a table of forty-one rows: time, thrust, attitude, tank pressure, temperature. The burn itself was a single row that said *ignition 20:40:12, cutoff 20:40:29, duration 17 s, delta-v 0.351*.

Seventeen seconds. The station's thrusters had fired for seventeen seconds and moved the station five kilometres along its track, and the fuel for that was 1.9 kilograms of hydrazine, and the cost of that was a line in next quarter's budget that somebody would have to explain.

She looked at the attitude column. The burn had been along the velocity vector to within 0.4 degrees, which was the tolerance in the flight rules, and the flight rules had been written by somebody who had never had to make the decision at 20:40 on a Friday.

Then she did the thing she had been avoiding, which was to compute what would have happened without the burn.

The object's track crossed the station's track at a relative velocity of 11.2 kilometres a second and an angle of 9.5 degrees. Without the burn, the miss distance would have been 380 metres with an uncertainty of 90. At that geometry, a hit would have been a hit on the truss, because the truss was the part of the station that presented the largest cross-section to an object coming from below and behind.

Eleven millimetres of aluminium, at 11.2 kilometres a second, with a nine-hundred-kilogram mass behind it.

She ran the impact model twice. The first run said the truss would be perforated and the station would survive with a depressurisation of one module. The second run, with the object's density set to the upper bound, said the truss would be severed and the station would lose attitude control and the crew would have forty minutes to get to the Soyuz.

She sat in the car with the laptop on the wheel and the model on the screen and the number in front of her, and she thought about the fact that the difference between the two runs was a density assumption that nobody had measured.

At 22:55 her phone rang. It was Femi.

"The burn's in the public log," he said. "The operator filed it at 22:31. It says *debris avoidance manoeuvre, conjunction with object K-7741, waiver granted by insurer*."

"That was fast."

"The insurer granted the waiver at 20:12. The operator filed the burn report at 22:31. The public log took nineteen minutes to publish it." He paused. "Ilsa, the log is public. Anybody can read it. Anybody can see that a station burned for an object that our catalogue calls debris."

"Which means?"

"Which means the correction is going to happen whether the company wants it or not. The log is a record, and the record now says a crewed station manoeuvred for an object that the provider's catalogue classified as debris. That's a contradiction in two public documents, and somebody at the regulator is going to read it on Monday morning."

She looked at the screen. The impact model was still open, and the second run said *truss severed*.

"Femi. Send me the log entry."

"I already did."

She opened her email on the phone and read it. The entry was four lines. *Operator: Meridian Station. Manoeuvre: debris avoidance. Object: K-7741. Note: waiver granted by insurer.*

Four lines. No classification. No mention of the mass anomaly or the manoeuvres or the second object.

"That's not the whole story," she said.

"That's the whole record," Femi said. "The record is four lines, and the four lines are true, and the truth is smaller than the thing."

---

She drove home at 23:20 and sat in the kitchen with the folder and the notebook and the printout of the four lines, and she wrote underneath them: *the record that saves the station is not the record that tells the truth.*

Then she crossed it out, because it was a sentence for a different book, and wrote instead: *the four lines are enough for the regulator to ask a question. The question is the door.*

At 21:10 Phil let her into the control room, which was a thing he had not done for anybody in three years.

"You can stand at the back," he said. "Don't talk. If I ask you a question, answer it in one sentence."

The room was smaller than it looked through the glass. Four consoles, a big screen, a wall of clocks set to three time zones, and a coffee machine that had been switched off because the flight director had a rule about hot liquids during a manoeuvre.

The left console was the tracking desk. The right console was the vehicle desk. The centre console was Phil's, and Phil's console had two screens: one with the station's systems and one with the conjunction.

Ilsa stood at the back with her hands behind her back.

"Two minutes," the tracking operator said. "Object's at two point one kilometres and closing at eleven point two."

"Attitude?"

"Within tolerance. The vehicle's configured for the burn at twenty-forty."

The clock on the wall was two seconds behind the console. She noticed it and then could not stop noticing it.

---

The burn took seventeen seconds and the room was silent for all of them.

There was no noise. That was the thing she had not expected. A station manoeuvring in orbit was a set of numbers on a screen and a low sound in the structure that the microphones picked up as a hum, and the hum was not much louder than the air handling.

"Thrust stable," the vehicle operator said. "Chamber pressure nominal."

"Attitude?"

"Holding within a tenth of a degree."

At nine seconds the tracking operator said, "Object's at one point four."

At twelve seconds: "One point one."

At fourteen seconds: "Eight hundred metres."

Phil said nothing. His hands were flat on the console on either side of the keyboard, which was a posture Ilsa had seen in the radar room and in the duty office and had come to recognise as the posture of a man who had decided not to touch anything.

At seventeen seconds the vehicle operator said, "Cutoff. Delta-v three five one."

The screen changed. The station's track bent, very slightly, and the object's track did not, and the distance between them began to grow in a way that was visible at the scale of the display, which meant it was visible at the scale of kilometres.

"Separation four hundred," the tracking operator said. "Seven hundred. One point one."

The room exhaled. It was not a cheer. It was the sound of eleven people putting something down at the same time.

---

The pass took ninety-four seconds from horizon to horizon, and for most of it nothing happened.

The station went over the Indian Ocean and the object went over the Indian Ocean behind it, and the two tracks separated until the display had to rescale, and then the object was a number in a list again, and the list was a list of things that had not hit the station.

At 21:04 the vehicle operator said, "Object's manoeuvring."

Phil's head came up. "Say again."

"Object's changing its vector. Delta-v approximately zero point four. It's lowering its perigee relative to us."

Ilsa stepped forward before she could stop herself, and Phil held up one hand without looking at her.

"Where's it going?"

"It's not going anywhere. It's staying in the plane and dropping behind." The operator watched the screen. "It's matching our new orbit at a lower altitude. It's going to sit under us."

"Under us how far?"

"Sixteen kilometres and closing to twelve."

Nobody said anything. The big screen had the two tracks on it and the object's track was below the station's track and both were in the same plane and the plane was sixty-one degrees and the two tracks would not cross again for nine days.

Phil turned round.

"Ms Merrick. One sentence."

"It's holding station below us in the same plane, which means it can come up again when it wants to and it can do it without a launch."

Phil looked at her for a moment. Then he turned back to the room.

"All right," he said. "That's the new fact. Nobody leaves this room until the ground has it, the bulletin has it, and the crew has it. In that order. Go."

---

The ground had it at 21:19. The bulletin had it at 21:34. The crew had it at 21:41, when the commander came onto the loop and asked, in a voice that was very level, whether the object was going to come back.

Phil answered.

"Unknown, Commander. It's manoeuvred twice in twenty minutes. The first manoeuvre was a phasing change and the second was a descent. That's a vehicle doing something deliberate, and we can't tell you what."

"Then tell me what you can tell me."

"We can tell you it's not on a collision course with anything for the next nine days. We can tell you the station has four hundred metres per second of contingency left. We can tell you that if it climbs again, we'll see it on the next pass in ninety-one minutes, and if it climbs during a gap, the bulletin will see it from the fence."

"And if nobody sees it?"

"Then the station is where it is and the object is where it is and the next crossing is in nine days, and in nine days we'll have moved twice and you'll have a different geometry."

The commander was quiet for a moment.

"Phil. Did somebody just miss us by three hundred and eighty metres?"

"Yes."

"And now they're parked below us."

"Yes."

"Then I want the next nine days in writing. Every option, every burn, every consequence. Not a summary. The options."

"I'll have it by the next pass."

"Thank you," the commander said, and the loop clicked off.

---

The room emptied slowly. The tracking operator went for coffee. The vehicle operator ran the burn report twice because the first one had a rounding error in the delta-v field. Phil sat at his console with his hands still flat on it.

Ilsa stayed at the back.

"You can go," he said.

"I know."

"The report's going to take two hours."

"I know."

Phil looked up. "You're going to stand there for two hours."

"I'm going to stand here for as long as it takes to write the sentence that says what happened." She took out the notebook. "Because in six weeks somebody in a meeting is going to say the object passed and the station manoeuvred and everything worked, and that sentence is going to be true and it's going to be missing the part where a machine parked below us and waited."

Phil looked at the notebook.

"Write it now," he said. "While you can still hear the room."

She wrote it at the back of the control room, standing up, with the coffee machine off and the clocks two seconds behind.

*21:02 — object passed at 2.1 km.*
*21:04 — object manoeuvred, 0.4 m/s, descended below station, holding at 12 km in plane.*
*The object did not leave. It changed station.*

She read it back. The last sentence was the one that belonged in the audit, and the audit was closed, and the regulator's file was open, and the file had eleven pages of findings and none of them had a sentence like that in it.

"Phil. Does the operator log manoeuvres by other objects?"

"The operator logs what the bulletin gives it. The bulletin logs conjunctions, not manoeuvres."

"So there's no record anywhere that the object parked."

"There's the fence's track, and our radar, and the bulletin's conjunction list." He looked at the screen. "And there's your notebook."

"Then the notebook is the record."

"The notebook is a private record," Phil said. "A private record is a thing a lawyer can call a draft."

"Then I'll file it."

"You'll file it and it'll go in a queue, and the queue is nine days long, and in nine days the geometry's different." He stood up. "File it anyway. I've been in this job three years and the only filings that ever changed anything were the ones that were already in the queue when the thing happened."

---

She left the control room at 22:45 and stood in the corridor with her palm against the glass, and the room behind the glass was lit and empty and the big screen still had the two tracks on it, the station's and the object's, sixteen kilometres apart and both in the same plane.

Then she went down to the car park and sat in the car and did not start the engine, and after a while she took out the notebook and read the three lines again.

*The object did not leave. It changed station.*

She thought about the phrase for a long time. It was the sentence of a person who had spent four days learning that the thing in front of her was not the thing the record said it was, and who had finally arrived at a place where the record could not be made to say it.

At 23:10 she started the engine and drove home, and on the way she passed a lit window in a building where somebody was working late, and she thought, without any particular feeling, that the whole of the week had been about the difference between a record and a thing, and that the difference was a person deciding to write the sentence down.

Then she started the engine and drove home, because there was nothing left to watch.

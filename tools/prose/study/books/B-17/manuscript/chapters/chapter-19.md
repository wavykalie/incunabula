# Chapter 19

The second object was on a page near the back of the notebook, in pencil, under a heading that said *things that came back*.

Hal had listed nine objects. Eight of them had a catalogue number beside them and a note that said *found later* or *turned out to be a tank*. The ninth had no number and a note that said *came back 8 min early, then nothing, Feb, az 214*.

Ilsa read the page three times. Then she went to the radar room, where a contractor she did not know was on the console, and asked for the archive for the three nights in question.

The contractor was young and efficient and found the tapes, which had been migrated to a storage array in 2023 and indexed by date. The tapes held the raw hits for the fence, not the processed tracks, which meant that anything the tracking software had discarded was still there.

She loaded the three nights and filtered by azimuth and elevation. The first night had the track Hal had described: a rising pass, a manoeuvre, a second pass eight minutes early. The second night had the same object at a different phase. The third night had nothing at the expected time.

But the third night had something else, forty-one minutes later, at a lower elevation, moving west.

A single hit, eight seconds of return, no follow-up because the fence's tasking list had moved on. The signal strength was a third of K-7741's, which for a radar return meant either a much smaller body or a much less reflective one.

She ran the range and range-rate through the fit. One hit could not determine an orbit. It could determine a line, and the line passed within four kilometres of K-7741's position at that time.

Four kilometres was close for two unrelated objects. Proof of nothing. A coincidence with a shape.

She sat in the radar room with the contractor pretending not to watch her, and she considered what a second object in the same plane, forty-one minutes behind, would be for.

A companion. A separated piece. A test article released from the same bus. A weapon, a sensor, a decoy, or a panel that had come off at separation and had been tumbling slowly ever since, which was the boring explanation and the one she would have believed on any other day.

The boring explanation did not account for the gap in the fence's tasking list.

She printed the hit and the range and the time. Then she went back to the notebook and found the page, and compared the azimuth Hal had written with the azimuth of the single hit. Four degrees apart, within the uncertainty of a pencil note made at two in the morning eighteen months ago.

The radar archive was stored on tapes, and the tapes were in a cabinet in the radar room, and the cabinet had a lock that had been broken since 2019.

The contractor on the console was called Nia and she was twenty-four and had been on the contract for five months, and she helped Ilsa because the request was interesting and because nobody had asked her to do anything interesting in five months.

"The archive's indexed by night," Nia said. "Each tape's got a header file. If you know the date, I can load it."

"Three nights in February, year before last."

"Which February?"

Ilsa told her. Nia loaded the first tape and the header came up on the console, and the header was a list of tracks with start times and durations and a code for the track's disposition.

The codes were four letters. Nia explained them: ACQ for acquired, TRK for tracked, CLS for closed, DRP for dropped.

"Show me the dropped ones."

There were eleven dropped tracks across the three nights. Ten of them had durations under a minute. The eleventh had a duration of eight minutes and a disposition code that said DRP with a note: *no catalogue match, operator instruction, close track*.

"Operator instruction," Ilsa said.

"That's the code for when the duty officer says stop tracking."

"Who was the duty officer?"

Nia looked at the roster. The duty officer for that night was a name Ilsa did not recognise, and the roster had a note beside the name that said *contract*.

"Contractor," Nia said. "That's why there's no name in the log. Contractors get a code."

"What's the code?"

"Twenty-two."

The track's note had a code too, in a field that said *authorised by*, and the code was twenty-two.

Ilsa wrote it down. Then she asked Nia to export the track's raw returns, and Nia said the export needed a manager, and Ilsa said she knew, and asked her to print the console view instead.

Nia printed it. It was two pages: a plot of the track's position over eight minutes, and a table of the returns with range, azimuth, elevation, and signal strength.

The track had come up in the south, crossed the station's shell, and gone down in the north. It had been slow. It had been bright. And at minute six it had stopped moving relative to the sky, which was the signature of an object that had applied a small thrust.

---

She took the pages back to her desk and put them beside the notebook page with the pencil note, and the two matched: *came back 8 min early, then nothing, Feb, az 214*.

Hal had written the note in pencil in February and had not known what he was looking at. The track had come back early, which meant its orbit had changed, which meant it had manoeuvred.

She called him at home. He answered on the fourth ring.

"The February track," she said. "It's in the tape archive. Eight minutes, dropped by a contractor, code twenty-two. It manoeuvred at minute six."

Hal was quiet for a moment.

"I knew it manoeuvred," he said. "I didn't know it was in the tape."

"It's in the tape."

"Then it's not a pencil note anymore." He breathed out. "Ilsa, do you understand what that means? It means there are two of them. It means the first one isn't the object. It's the delivery."

Code twenty-two was on a roster that the contractor company kept and the client did not.

Ilsa found the roster by asking human resources for the contractor's point of contact and then asking the contractor's point of contact for the shift list, which he sent without hesitation because he had no idea what it was for.

The list had nine names and four shifts. Code twenty-two was assigned to a man called Petrov, who had worked nights for eleven months and had left the contract in March.

She called the number on the roster. It was disconnected.

She called the contractor's office and asked for him, and the woman on the desk said he had moved to a different contract and could not be reached, and that she could take a message.

"Can you tell me which contract?"

"I can't give out that information."

"Can you tell me whether he's still in the country?"

"I really can't give out that information."

Ilsa thanked her and hung up, and sat at her desk with the roster in front of her and the tape printout beside it.

A contractor with a code, a dropped track, and a departure in March. The departure was the part that mattered. Contractors left all the time; the contract was poorly paid and the shifts were bad. But a contractor who dropped a track in February and left in March was a sequence, and sequences were what she had spent the week learning to read.

She called Hal, who was at home and answered on the second ring.

"Petrov," she said. "Do you know him?"

"The name's familiar." A pause. "He was on nights. He kept to himself. He had a folder of printouts he carried around, which was unusual, because everybody else used the console."

"A folder of printouts."

"He said he liked paper. I thought it was a quirk." Hal's voice was careful. "Ilsa, if you're going to ask me whether he was the one who dropped the track, I can't tell you. I can tell you that the track was dropped at 02:41 and Petrov was on the console at 02:41, because the console log has a session for him at 02:38 and the session stayed open until 03:10."

"The console log has sessions?"

"The console log has sessions for contractors. It doesn't have names. It has codes." Hal paused. "You're going to ask me how I know that."

"I'm going to ask you how you know that."

"Because I've been reading the console log for forty-one years and nobody ever asked me what was in it." He said it without pride. "The log records the code, the session, and the keystrokes. Not the text. The count."

"So the log can tell us that a contractor typed something at 02:41."

"The log can tell us that code twenty-two typed nine characters at 02:41, and that the track was dropped at 02:41, and that the disposition note is nine characters long."

Ilsa looked at the printout. *close track* was ten characters with the space. *close* was five.

"Can I get the console log?"

"You can ask the duty officer. He'll say the log is a system record and the system record is retained for ninety days."

"February was ninety days ago."

"February was ninety-one days ago," Hal said. "I checked."

She put the printout in the folder. The folder was now too thick to close properly, and she carried it with both hands the way you carry a book you are afraid of dropping.

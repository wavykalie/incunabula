# Chapter 38

The object came down on a Tuesday in the fourth week, at 04:41 local time, over water three hundred kilometres south-east of the Chagos Bank, and a fishing crew saw it.

Ilsa read about it in a wire report that ran to four paragraphs. The report said a "bright meteor" had been observed by the crew of a longline vessel, that it had broken into three pieces, and that no damage or injury had been reported. It quoted the skipper, who said the light had been "green at the edges and white in the middle, like a flare, but quieter."

She read the report at her kitchen table, where she had been reading things for four weeks, and then she opened the tracking feed and confirmed the time.

The object's perigee had been lowered twice in the preceding ten days, both times by the vehicle, both times by about nine kilometres. The second lowering had put the perigee below the altitude at which the object could survive a pass through the atmosphere. The re-entry had been predicted within a window of eleven minutes, and the actual event had fallen inside the window, and the debris footprint had been over ocean.

Nobody had announced the disposal. The catalogue had simply stopped receiving observations, and after seventy-two hours the object's status had changed to *decayed*, with a note that said *natural decay*.

It had not been natural. A nine-hundred-kilogram object with a manoeuvring vehicle attached had been brought down on purpose, in a place where the pieces would sink, by an operator who had been practising for eighteen months and had finished the exercise.

She closed the laptop and sat for a while.

The first object had been the demonstration. The second object was still up there, in an orbit that crossed the shell of every crewed asset in that inclination, with no catalogue number and no announced purpose. The catalogue correction had made the first object visible; the second object had never been visible at all.

She opened the notebook and turned to the page where she had written the three explanations for a grappler, and read what she had written underneath: *it could be an inspector, or a servicing vehicle, or a weapon, and the difference is a function we cannot see.*

Then she wrote, in the same hand: *the first one came down on command. The second one is still up. The exercise is not finished.*

---

The fishing crew's account was picked up by a space weather blog and then by a news agency and then by a regulator's public log, which is where Ilsa found the second paragraph that mattered.

The regulator's log recorded the re-entry as an *uncontrolled decay of a catalogue object*, which was the standard classification for an object that had not been deorbited under a licensed operation. An uncontrolled decay carried no notification obligation and no liability. A controlled deorbit carried both.

The object had been controlled. The record said uncontrolled. The difference was a word, and the word had been chosen by somebody at Kestrel or at the client or at the regulator, and the choice had been made because a controlled deorbit of an unlogged vehicle would have required somebody to admit that the vehicle existed.

She sat with that for a while. Then she wrote it down, and photographed the log, and put the photograph in the folder, which was now a box file because the folder had split along the spine.

At 07:00 she drove to the office for the first time in four weeks, because her leave had been lifted and the supervision condition had begun, and because she wanted to be at her desk when the next queue opened.

The car park badge worked. The floor badge worked. The terminal at her desk asked for a second signature on her first classification of the day, which was an object with a mass anomaly of four per cent above the expected value for a spent stage.

She looked at the object for a moment. Then she classified it as a spent stage, and the second signature came back in ninety seconds, and the queue ticked down.

At 09:20 the station's next pass came over. The conjunction summary had a row in it now: *K-7741 — decayed — no action*. The row was accurate. The object was gone.

The summary did not have a row for the second object, because the second object was not in the catalogue.

The skipper's name was Anil and he had been fishing the bank for nineteen years, and the interview he gave was eleven minutes long and had been cut to four paragraphs.

He said the light had come from the south-east and had been low, below the horizon line of cloud, and that it had broken into three pieces at a height he could not judge. He said the pieces had gone out one after another, like a hand of cards being folded. He said the sound had arrived forty seconds later, which he knew because he had counted, and that it had been a rumble and then a crack.

The reporter asked whether he had been frightened.

He said he had been on the deck with two of his crew and that the crew had gone below, and that he had stayed because somebody had to watch the gear.

The report ran on a wire service and was picked up by a space weather blog and then by a news agency and then by the regulator's public log, where it was attached to an entry that said *uncontrolled decay of catalogue object K-7741, no notification required.*

Ilsa read the report three times and then went looking for the blog post, which had a comment thread, and the comment thread had a man who said he had seen the same thing from a yacht off Madagascar, and a man who said it was a Starlink deorbit, and a woman who said it was a meteor and that people should stop panicking.

She read the thread for a while. Then she closed it, because the thread was a place where the event was being processed into a story, and the story was already diverging from the record.

The record said uncontrolled. The skipper said three pieces and a rumble. The catalogue said decayed. The vehicle had lowered the object's perigee twice.

She opened the tracking feed and pulled the object's last two weeks of element sets, and plotted the perigee against time, and the plot was a staircase: two steps down, each about nine kilometres, each accompanied by an element set whose covariance was small and whose epoch was fresh.

A natural decay was a curve. The object's perigee was a staircase.

She sat back and looked at the plot. Then she made a screenshot and put it in the box file, and wrote on the printout: *A staircase is a decision. The word in the log is "uncontrolled". The word in the log is wrong.*

---

At noon she called the regulator's case officer, whose name was Davies, and asked whether the case file could be amended to note the discrepancy.

Davies was careful and tired and said that the case file recorded the facts reported by the parties, and that the parties had reported an uncontrolled decay, and that an analyst's disagreement with the characterisation could be submitted as a comment.

"Will the comment be read?"

"The comment will be placed on the file."

"That's not the same as being read."

"No," Davies said. "It isn't."

She submitted the comment at 12:40. It was four hundred words and it had three citations and one sentence that said the perigee history was inconsistent with an uncontrolled decay.

The acknowledgement came back at 12:41 with a reference number.

The regulator's panel sat on the Thursday and issued a summary on the Friday, and the summary was eleven pages long and had one paragraph about the object.

Ilsa read the whole thing at the kitchen table, because the panel's summary was the first document in four weeks that had been written by somebody who had read all the filings.

The summary found four things.

First, that the 2024 reclassification had been made in accordance with the procedure then in force, and that the procedure had been amended in 2025, and that the amendment was not retrospective.

Second, that the standing instruction of 2021 had been applied to an object outside the class it was written for, and that the application had been made by a duty officer acting in good faith on an instruction that did not define the class.

Third, that the catalogue correction of the Monday following the conjunction had been made "promptly and completely", and that the provider had produced raw data on request under its contract with the insurer.

Fourth, that the second object described in the analyst's addendum could not be assessed on the evidence available, and that the provider should be required to report to the panel within ninety days on any object observed to manoeuvre in the shell of a crewed asset.

She read the fourth finding three times.

*Could not be assessed on the evidence available.* The evidence was a radar return, a pencil note, and a burn. The panel had read it and had decided not to decide, and the not-deciding was the most useful thing in the document, because it created a reporting obligation that had not existed before.

She wrote in the notebook: *panel cannot assess, so provider must report. The gap becomes a duty.*

Then she read the penalty. The panel found that the provider had failed to maintain the classification review that its own procedure required, and imposed a penalty of a sum that was smaller than the legal fees and larger than the subscription.

The penalty was published on the regulator's website at 10:00. By 10:30 the trade blog had a post about it. By 11:00 the competitor had a new marketing email.

---

At 16:00 the wire service called her, which was the first time a journalist had called her directly.

"Ms Merrick, I'm doing a follow-up on the re-entry. The skipper's account. Can you confirm that the object was a manoeuvring vehicle rather than debris?"

"I can confirm that the object's perigee history is inconsistent with a natural decay. That's on the record in my filing."

"Can you confirm it was deliberately deorbited?"

"I can confirm that the perigee was lowered twice in ten days by a vehicle that had manoeuvred three times before the conjunction. Whether that constitutes a deliberate deorbit is a characterisation, and I'd rather you made it than me."

"That's a very careful answer."

"It's a careful answer because the record is careful." She paused. "If I characterise it, the characterisation becomes the story and the record becomes a footnote. If you characterise it, the record is the source."

The journalist was quiet for a moment.

"Can I quote that?"

"You can quote the first sentence."

---

She put the phone down and looked at the summary on the table. Eleven pages. Four findings. One paragraph about an object that had come down over water three hundred kilometres south-east of the Chagos Bank and been seen by a man with a longline who had counted to forty before the sound arrived.

She sat at her desk with the summary on the screen and the queue in front of her, and she thought about the green-edged light over the Indian Ocean, and about the skipper who had heard it, and about the fact that the only person who had watched the object arrive was a man with a longline and no idea what he was looking at.

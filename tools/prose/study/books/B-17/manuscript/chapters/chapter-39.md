# Chapter 39

The clearance was revoked on a Thursday, in three stages, and each stage was handled by somebody who was not cruel.

The first stage was the badge. It stopped opening the catalogue floor at 09:00, and the security desk said the access group had been changed, and that the change had been requested by catalogue operations, and that a temporary escort could be arranged.

The second stage was the login. Her account still worked, but the catalogue write function had been moved to a different group, and the group did not include her. The message said *contact your administrator*. Her administrator was Priya's assistant, who was on leave.

The third stage was the parking permit. It was cancelled at 16:00 with an email that said the allocation had been reviewed and that her space had been reassigned, and that she could use the visitor lot at the daily rate.

None of the three messages used the word *revoked*. All three used the word *changed*.

She sat at her desk with the queue open and the second-signature requirement on every classification, and she worked through the afternoon because there was nothing else to do. The queue had two hundred and twelve objects in it. Eleven required a judgement. She made the judgements, and each one was checked by a supervisor in another building, and each check came back within four minutes.

At 17:40 Femi came to her desk. He had been avoiding her since Monday, and he stood at the end of the row with his hands in his pockets.

"They've moved you to the archive project," he said.

"I know."

"That's not an analyst job. That's a filing job."

"It's a job."

He looked at the floor. "I'm sorry."

"For what?"

"For sending the package. If I hadn't sent it, they wouldn't have—"

"They were going to do this anyway," Ilsa said. "The package is the reason the station got a warning. The clearance is the reason I'm on the archive project. Those are two different decisions made by two different groups of people, and one of them is good."

"That's a very reasonable way to put it."

"I've had four weeks to practise."

He nodded. Then he took a folded piece of paper out of his pocket and put it on her desk.

"What's this?"

"A session. I've left a terminal logged in on the archive level. It's my credentials, so it's my risk. It'll stay open until the automatic timeout, which is eleven minutes from now."

She looked at the paper. It had a terminal number and a room number written on it.

"Femi."

"I know what I'm doing."

"You don't. You think you do. You're twenty-six and you've decided to be brave, and the thing about being brave at twenty-six is that it's mostly not knowing what the consequences look like yet."

He met her eyes.

"Then tell me what they look like."

She looked at the paper. Eleven minutes. Enough time to open the archive, pull the 2021 purchase log, and print the provenance note that said the seller declined to provide a launch record. Enough time to copy the file that would prove the catalogue had been told, in writing, that the object had no provenance, and had taken it anyway.

"The consequences look like a disciplinary finding and a visa problem and a year of your life," she said. "And the alternative looks like a file that nobody can find in five years."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she said. "It's a description."

She took the paper, and she went to the archive level, and she logged in with his credentials, and she found the file in four minutes and printed it in two, and she was back at her desk with the pages in her bag before the timeout.

At 18:00 the terminal logged itself out, and the session closed, and somewhere in a system that logged everything, there was a record of eleven minutes of access by an analyst who was not supposed to be there.

The archive project had a desk on the second floor in a room with no windows and eleven filing cabinets, and the work was to reconcile the catalogue's historical entries against the observation logs.

The reconciliation was necessary because the catalogue had been migrated twice, in 2016 and in 2021, and each migration had dropped fields. The fields that had been dropped were not the important ones; they were the marginal ones, the notes, the provenance, the observer's initials. The company had decided to restore them, which meant reading the logs and typing the notes back in.

Ilsa was given cabinets four through seven, which covered 2016 to 2019, and a laptop with no network connection, and a supervisor named Bridget who came by twice a day.

"You'll find things," Bridget said on the first morning. "Everybody finds things. The rule is you write them down in the anomaly log and you don't act on them."

"What's the anomaly log?"

Bridget showed her. It was a spreadsheet with four columns: date, cabinet, entry, description. There were two hundred and eleven rows.

"What happens to the rows?"

"They go to the data governance committee."

"When?"

"Quarterly."

Ilsa looked at the spreadsheet. The most recent row was dated five weeks ago. The oldest was dated two years ago.

"So an anomaly takes two years to reach a committee."

"An anomaly reaches the committee in the quarter after it's logged," Bridget said. "The two-year-old row is an anomaly that the committee has been considering for two years."

"What's the anomaly?"

Bridget scrolled to the row. The description said *object 4471 classified debris in 2017; observation log shows manoeuvre 2017-08-14; no record of review.*

Ilsa read it twice.

"Somebody found this two years ago."

"Somebody found it two years ago and logged it, which is what the procedure requires."

"And it's still classified debris."

"It's still classified debris," Bridget said, "because the committee has not yet made a finding, and the classification can't be changed without a finding."

Ilsa sat with that. The room was quiet. The filing cabinets hummed, or the lights did, or the building did.

"Can I see the observation log for 4471?"

"You can see anything in cabinets four through seven. That's the point of the project." Bridget stood. "But I'll tell you what I tell everybody. The archive is not a place where you fix things. It's a place where things are kept. If you start fixing, you'll be fixing for a year and the cabinets will still be full."

"I'm not going to fix it."

"Good."

"I'm going to read it."

Bridget looked at her for a moment.

"Reading's allowed," she said, and went away.

---

Ilsa found the log entry at 15:20. It was a single line in a handwritten volume, dated 14 August 2017: *4471 observed three passes, range decreasing, then increasing. Not natural.*

The initials beside the line were H. Kessler.

She looked at the initials for a long time. Then she closed the volume and wrote the date in her notebook, and did not add a row to the anomaly log, because the row already existed and had been waiting two years for a committee.

She put the pages in the box file with the rest, and she went home, and she did not sleep.

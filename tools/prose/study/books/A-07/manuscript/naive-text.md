# The Inland Line

### Chapter 1

The rule at the Chancery of the Sea-Mark was three drops of ox-gall to every gill of oak-gall ink, measured with a pewter dropper that had been scored at the rim during the reign of the third chancellor. If an apprentice used two drops, the ink stood high on the calfskin like grease on water, refusing the tooth of the scraper; if she used four, the line crept sideways through the fibers overnight, feathering into the damp salt air until a headland thirty fathoms tall looked like a smear of wet soot.

Maren Brand knew the dropper by its dent. She had dropped it onto the flagged floor of the scriptorium in her second winter, while her fingers were split from the frost, and Master Coram had made her polish the flags with pumice until the copperas stain was gone.

"You draw the Head of Skiffs today," Master Coram said without turning from his desk.

He sat on a three-legged stool raised four inches above the floor on a platform of dry oak planks, so that his wool-slippered feet would not touch the river damp that seeped up through the cellar arches. His spectacles were round, framed in thin horn that smelled of vinegar when his brow grew warm. Across his knees lay the great roll of the Fourth Survey—parchment four feet broad, backed with Dutch linen, its edges bound in green sheep-leather that had faded to the color of pond-weed.

"The Head of Skiffs, sir," Maren said.

"Three fathoms at dead neap," the master said, reciting from the margin of his roll. "The outer ledge carries four feet of drying weed on the seventh moon. The channel between the Cow and the Calf runs north-northwest half-west, soundings in gravel and broken shell. How many strokes to the rood?"

"Seven strokes with the crow-quill," she answered, her hands already moving across the dry linen towel where her tools lay. "Two heavy for the storm-shelf; five light for the sand-run."

"And if the sand-run has shifted?"

"The sand-run does not shift, sir."

"Why does it not shift?"

"Because the Charter does not permit the sand to be uncertain," she said.

Coram dipped his own quill—a heavy grey goose-feather taken from the wing-joint, trimmed square at the nib for engrossing royal charters—into the black pot. "A pert answer is the first mark of a slovenly draughtsman. Go to the bench."

Maren did not take offense. For six years, four months, and nine days, she had stood at the second bench beneath the high, north-facing lancets of the Guildhall of Coastwise Limners and Measurers. The glass was greenish, thick as a knuckle, spun in rounds that left bullseye knots in the center of every pane; when the gulls swept down across the harbour mouth toward the salt-wharves, their wings seemed to buckle and fold through the flaws in the panes, like sails caught in a sudden jibe.

Below the high lancets lay the harbour of Marrow-on-the-Bar.

At low water, the city smelled of three things: salt-cod drying on the pine flakes along the south wall; boiling pine-tar from the careening berths where two Dutch galliots were having their seams caulked; and the heavy, sulfurous stench of the river-silt that accumulated against the harbour boom. The boom was eighty baulks of seasoned fir, chained end to end with iron links as thick as a woman's wrist, swung across the narrows every night at the stroke of nine to keep out the smugglers and the unlicensed coasters from the western bays.

Nothing entered Marrow that was not measured.

The customs reeves measured the timber by the cord and the wine by the tun; the weigh-masters on the South Quay measured the wool-bales with balanced beams of ash-wood; and here, on the high cliff where the Guildhall looked down upon the grey throat of the bay, the Chancery measured the land itself.

Or rather, it measured the edge of the land.

The Guild's charter, granted one hundred and eighty-four years ago under the Great Wax Seal of the Council of the Estates, was seven skins of stout vellum fastened with braided silk. Under Section Four, Subsection Nine, the Guild held the sole right to take, record, engrave, print, and publish the courses, headlands, shoals, sounds, inlets, and anchorages of the province from the Red Head of Orris in the north to the mouth of the Sallow in the south, thirty leagues of granite, shingle, and salt-marsh.

Under Section Four, Subsection Ten, any chart, sea-card, plat, or diagram found in the possession of any mariner, pilot, merchant, or householder that did not bear the Guild's vermilion compass-stamp was declared *erroneous, pernicious, and void*.

And under Section Five, Subsection Three, an erroneous chart was not returned to its maker. It was delivered to the Beadle of the Chancery, carried down the stone steps into the courtyard, and cast into the Kiln.

The Kiln was burning now.

Through the floorboards, Maren could feel the low, steady shudder of its flue. The furnace was built of river-brick lined with Stourbridge clay, fed with dry beech-billets and small sea-coal that burned with a clean, hot, blue flame. Twice a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays at midday, the Assay-Master came down from the Upper Chamber with the condemned sheets: maps made by unfree scribes in the back-lanes; foreign rutters brought in by Baltic skippers whose soundings varied by three cubits from the Guild’s standard; practice sheets from the apprentice benches where a hand had slipped or an ink-blot had fallen across a fairway.

A map that lied, Master Coram often said, was a murderer with a pen. If a skipper set his helm by a chart that placed the reef thirty rods east of its true footing, twenty men went into the surf with their boots full of water and their mouths full of brine. Therefore, the paper must die so that the ship might live.

Maren picked up her ruling pen. It was made of Swedish brass, hinged with a small silver screw that adjusted the nib to the breadth of a hair. She blew through the jaws to clear any fleck of dried gum, then reached for the ink-horn.

On her drafting board lay a sheet of third-grade calfskin, scraped thin with the curved lunellum blade until it was smooth as cold mutton-fat. It was thirty inches across by twenty-four deep. In the center, framed by an oval border of laurel leaves and nautical dividers, lay the Harbour of Marrow: the granite pier-heads, the inner basin, the Customs House with its three chimneys, and the long, curving arm of the Shingles that reached out into the bay like a bent finger.

To the north lay the cliffs: the White Ness, the Red Shelf, the Grey Weirs, and finally the Head of Skiffs, where the coast broken into four iron-stone teeth against which the northern swell broke itself into foam.

She had drawn the Head of Skiffs four hundred times.

Her hand moved without her eyes having to consult the master roll. First the baseline: a faint horizontal line drawn in dry silver-point that would vanish under the ink. Then the compass-rose: sixteen points, the north marked with a gilded fleur-de-lis whose tip pointed two degrees east of true, because eighty years ago the First Surveyor had determined that the needle of the great brass bowl in the watch-tower always leaned that way, and the Guild had never seen fit to contradict the stone.

She dipped the pen. She touched the nib to the thumbnail of her left hand to test the flow. A round, black bead of ink rose, trembling, but did not fall.

She bent her head. Her hair, dark as walnut stain and cut blunt at her shoulders with tailoring shears so that it would not sweep across the wet margins, fell forward against her temples.

*Scratch.*

The brass point met the vellum. The line ran straight for four inches, then angled thirty degrees south-by-west around the Black Ledge. Here the quill must stutter—five tiny dashes to signify the rip-tide that ran over the weeds at half-ebb. Then the double line for the beacon-tower on the Outer Skiff: a square base, an iron lantern-basket, and three rays of red cinnabar ink to show the light.

She had been doing this since she was eighteen.

Her father had been a cooper's trimmer down at the wet docks, a man with salt-rot in his fingernails who could smell the difference between a barrel of tallow from Archangel and one from Bristol with his eyes shut. When he fell between two timber baulks during the spring gales and was crushed before the ropes could be cleared, the parish overseers had looked at his five daughters and his empty cellar. Four of her sisters had gone into the bone-lace manufactory behind the fish-market; Maren, who had spent her childhood drawing the shapes of ships' transoms in charcoal on the cooperage floor with such relentless symmetry that the master cooper had used her boards for templates, was sold into the Chancery for twelve silver groats and a bond of six years' service.

The bond had expired four months ago.

She was still here.

In the Guild of Limners, when an indenture ended, the apprentice did not simply walk through the carved oak doors into the sunlight. She was required to sit the Trial of the Masterwork: to take four skins of royal vellum, twenty books of gold leaf, and six vessels of purified earth-pigments, and draw, from memory and without the aid of a divider or a square, forty leagues of sea-coast with every fathom-line, every lead-mark, and every church spire that served as a sea-mark from the deck of a three-master.

If the Court of Wardens approved the sheet, she was sworn in as a Free Sister of the Roll, given a silver compass to wear upon her bodice, and permitted to sign her own name in the corner of her charts.

If she failed, she remained an unfree journeyman—paid twelve copper pennies a week, fed on pease-pudding and small beer at the common table, and forbidden to own an ink-stone or a ruling pen of her own.

"You are hesitating," Coram said.

He was standing behind her now. He smelled of dried elderberries, which he chewed for his gravel, and old wool that had dried too often near peat-smoke. His hand, dry and spotted with brown freckles like the skin of a brook-trout, rested on the edge of her board.

"The tide-rip at the Black Ledge," she said, her voice quiet. "I was thinking of the depth."

"You are not paid to think of the depth," Coram said mildly. "The soundings were established by Master Varley in the year of the Great Frost. Forty-four feet in the gut; twelve feet on the sand-bar. It was forty-four feet when my grandfather stood at this bench; it will be forty-four feet when your skin is as yellow as an old bladder. Draw the lines as they are laid."

"Yes, sir."

"The Warden of the North Gate comes at two o'clock," Coram added, his spectacles catching the pale grey light from the river. "He wants thirty sheets of the Bight for the herring fleet before the dark moon. If the ink is not dry enough to pounce by noon, you will blow upon it until your lungs are empty."

He walked back to his platform, his slippers scuffing softly against the grit.

Maren did not look up. Her pen moved along the coast: the little tooth of the Herring Spit, the square indent of the Salt-Cellar, the smooth crescent of the Blind Cove where the smugglers used to run brandy before the Guild built the round tower on the ridge.

Every line was fixed. For two hundred miles to the north and two hundred miles to the south, the world was a strip of vellum four inches wide. To the right lay the sea—blue-grey, marked with neat, criss-crossing rhumb-lines in faint vermilion that radiated from the compass-roses like spider-webs over water. To the left lay the inland.

On the Guild's charts, the inland was five inches of blank parchment.

Sometimes, if an apprentice had a light hand and a taste for ornament, she was permitted to draw three or four rounded mounds with brown wash to indicate the hills, or a small cluster of triangular trees to show where the oak-woods began. Beyond that, the skin was left virgin, scrubbed clean with pumice, bare of any town, road, ditch, or boundary.

*Terra Vasta,* the old rolls called it. The Great Waste. The Hinterland.

Officially, it was not the Chancery's affair. The Chancery was established by mariners for mariners; its charter spoke only of the salt-reach, the ebb and flow, the points where a keel might strike or an anchor hold.

Unofficially, every soul in Marrow knew why the inland was white.

The city lived upon the throat. The sea was its meat; the salt was its bread. Every bag of flour, every bolt of frieze, every bar of iron that came into the province had to enter through the river mouth, cross the stone quays of the Bar, and pay forty-two distinct customs duties to the Mayor, the Sheriff, and the Wardens of the Guild. If there were roads through the interior—if a wagon from the southern wool-valleys could roll north to the mining towns without passing under the iron booms of Marrow—the tolls would dry up like ditch-water in August. The great stone houses on the Cliff would fall into ruin, the merchant aldermen would have to sell their velvet gowns to the rag-men, and the Chancery would become nothing more than a cellar where old men remembered the shapes of islands.

The Guild did not merely draw the coast. The Guild guarded the gate.

"No roads," her father had said to her once, leaning over his beer-pot when she was twelve and had asked why the wagons from the farm-country took three weeks to reach the town. "You don't want roads, girl. Roads are for soldiers and tax-gatherers from the capital. The marsh is our friend. The bog keeps the world outside, and the sea brings the silver in."

He had spat into the sawdust then, satisfied with his wisdom.

Maren drew the three cinnabar rays from the lantern on the Outer Skiff. Her pen was steady; her wrist did not tremble. But beneath the ribs of her woollen stays, her heart beat with a small, persistent friction that had nothing to do with the Head of Skiffs.

In her pocket, wrapped in an oil-skin rag to keep it from the river-fog, was a key.

It was not a Guild key; it was a rough iron tongue filed out of a scrap of hoop-iron she had stolen from her father’s shop seven years ago. It fitted the padlock of an oak dower-chest that sat under the rafters in her attic room in the Fish-Gate—a chest that held twelve whole skins of second-grade vellum, six vials of ink made from walnut-hulls and soot, and thirty-four individual sheets that she had been stitching together with waxed hemp thread for the last two years.

It was her own map.

Not of the coast. Of the white space.

At half-past eleven, the great bell in the tower of St. Jude-in-the-Fens began to toll.

It was a heavy, dull sound, muffled by the low ceiling of cloud that hung over the estuary. Three strokes, a pause, then three strokes more: the bell for the Kiln.

The door at the bottom of the scriptorium stairs creaked open. A young clerk in a black frieze coat, his stockings spattered with yellow mud from the streets, entered carrying a wooden tray. On the tray lay seven rolled sheets, each tied with a knot of hemp cord that had been dipped in black wax.

Master Coram did not look up from his desk. "What has the Assay-Master found today, Simon?"

The boy stood with his heels together, his breath smelling of the salt-pork he had eaten for breakfast. "Four from the foreign quarter, sir. Dutch work. A rutter from an Amsterdam ketch that shows three fathoms in the Sallow Gut where the Master of the Rolls says there are only nine feet. Two charts of the West Reach, drawn by an unlicensed scrivener in the Tanneries."

"The scrivener?"

"In the stocks at the Fish-Market, sir. The bailiff broke his pen across his knees."

"And the seventh?"

The boy hesitated, his eyes flicking toward the corner where Maren stood by her board. "A practice sheet, sir. From the lower benches. Master Vane’s boy drew the North Sand with twelve soundings instead of nine."

Coram sighed, a dry, rustling sound like dead leaves stirred by a broom. He laid down his goose-quill, took a pinch of dried elderberries from his pouch, and chewed slowly.

"Call them down," he said.

The scriptorium fell silent. The seven other apprentices—all boys, the youngest twelve, the oldest twenty—laid their pens in their brass trays and stood behind their stools. Maren did not move; she dried her brass nib on her linen towel, slipped the pen into its wooden sheath, and folded her hands before her apron.

They walked in single file down the spiral stairs of the South Tower.

The stone was cold through Maren’s thin leather shoes. The steps were worn into hollows where two centuries of limners had carried ink-horns and slates down to the river; in the curves, the mortar had wept white lime-crusts that tasted of alum if one touched them with a finger.

The courtyard was paved with round river-pebbles that had been set on edge in grey mortar. In the center stood the Kiln.

It was circular, eight feet high, shaped like an inverted pear, with four small iron draught-doors around the base and a short chimney that rose into a square brick hood. The iron doors were glowing a dull cherry-red around their latches. A smell of dry bone and burning paper hung over the stones, so sharp that it made Maren’s eyes water behind her hair.

The Assay-Master stood beside the furnace with his iron tongs in his hand. He was an old man named Hallowell, whose left sleeve was pinned to his waistcoat; he had lost the arm thirty years ago when a survey cutter had been driven onto the rocks of the Skiffs during a winter gale. His face was the color of salted pork, his single eyebrow a thick thatch of grey bristle that shadowed two small, watery blue eyes.

"Master Coram," Hallowell said, touching the brim of his black velvet cap with the tip of his tongs.

"Assay-Master," Coram replied.

The eight apprentices formed a half-circle around the furnace. The wind, blowing straight off the harbour mud, caught the edges of their black wool smocks and fluttered them against their knees.

Hallowell picked up the first roll from the wooden tray. He did not read the title; he did not look at the fine red lines or the delicate gold-leaf compass-roses that showed through the transparent oiled paper. He held it up before the boys.

"The work of Dirck Jansen, master of the ketch *The White Falcon*," Hallowell recited, his voice flat and practiced. "Wherein the Sallow Gut is falsely represented as passable by vessels of ninety tons at half-flood. Being contrary to the Second Roll of King Edward, contrary to the soundings of Master Varley, and dangerous to the shipping of this realm. What says the Chancery?"

"Let it be unmade," Coram said.

"Let it be unmade," the apprentices repeated. The words were a formula, learned by heart on the day an indenture was signed.

Hallowell unhooked the latch of the upper door with his tongs. A blast of clear, white-hot heat leaped out across the pebbles, carrying a handful of grey ash that drifted over Maren’s shoes. He dropped the roll into the mouth.

For three seconds, the paper lay upon the coals, its white skin turning brown, then black, curling at the corners like a dry leaf. The gold leaf on the compass-rose flared once—a brilliant, electric green spark that smelled of melted gum—and then the vellum caught. A sheet of clear orange flame enveloped it, the black wax hissed and ran into the grate, and the roll was gone.

One by one, the four Dutch maps went into the fire.

Then came the scrivener’s work: cheap rag-paper, sized with bone-glue that gave off a foul, greasy smoke as it burned.

Finally, Hallowell picked up the small practice sheet.

A boy of fourteen, standing two paces to Maren’s left, drew in his breath with a sharp, wet sound. His name was Peter; his father was an ironmonger in the High Street who had paid forty silver shillings to place him in the Chancery.

"The work of Peter Vane, second-year apprentice," Hallowell said, his eye fixing on the boy. "Wherein the North Sand is drawn with three unauthorized marks in the fairway, being an error of three hundred yards to the seaward. Is this your work, boy?"

"It is, sir," Peter whispered.

"Speak like a man of the Guild," Coram snapped.

"It is my work, sir," Peter said louder, though his chin was trembling. "The pen slipped on the grease-mark. I tried to scrape it, but the skin was thin—"

"A map does not excuse itself because the calf was greasy," Hallowell said. He dropped the sheet into the furnace.

It burned in an instant—a single puff of white smoke that rose through the brick hood and vanished into the gray sea-fog that was rolling over the roofs of the town.

"Three marks," Coram said to the boy, his voice neither angry nor kind, but cold as a brass rule. "You have two left before your indenture is cancelled. Go to the wash-house and clean the ink-stones until three o'clock. You will eat no bread tonight."

The boy bowed, his face white, and turned toward the back arches.

Maren watched him go. Her fingers, buried deep in the wool pockets of her skirt, closed around the cold iron tongue of her key.

She had made no marks in the fairway. Her copies of the Head of Skiffs were so clean that even the Assay-Master could not tell them from the copper-plate impressions made by the royal engraver in the capital fifty years ago. She was safe. She was twenty-four years old; she had six years of clean ledgers behind her; she was the most useful pair of eyes in the South Tower.

Yet as she looked into the glowing mouth of the Kiln, where the red coals were slowly turning black beneath a crust of white ash, a cold, thin shudder ran through the marrow of her forearms.

She knew what was in the oak chest beneath her pallet.

If Master Coram ever took that key—if Hallowell’s iron tongs ever lifted those thirty-four stitched skins from the oil-cloth—there would be no question of three marks, or bread withheld, or a scolding in the courtyard.

They would take her down to the low-water mark at the mouth of the Bar. They would nail her indenture to a balk of driftwood with an iron spike through the seal, and they would leave her there until the flood-tide came over the flats and the grey water filled her mouth.

"Brand," Coram said, turning his horn-rimmed spectacles toward her.

"Sir?"

"Your board is waiting. The light does not stay for the tide."

"Yes, sir," she said, and followed him back up the spiral stairs.

### Chapter 2

The room where Maren lived was six paces long by four paces wide, tucked under the hip of the roof above the cooperage of Master Gault in the lane called the Gut.

The Gut was the narrowest passage in the Fish-Gate—an alley so dark that even at noon the tallow-candles in the cellar doorways looked like yellow eyes through the fog. The houses leaned toward one another across the stones until their timber jetties almost touched; a man could lean out of Maren’s garret window and shake hands with the journeyman tanner across the way, if either of them had cared for company.

The cooperage below was quiet now. At seven in the evening, the cooper’s boys had banked their wood-fires, washed their arms in the brine-troughs, and gone to the ale-house at the corner.

The only sound was the wind coming off the river, whistling through the gaps in the slate roof, and the regular, wet *clack* of a loose shingle against the chimney-stack.

Maren sat on her three-legged stool with her feet drawn up onto the lower rung to keep them out of the draught that came beneath the door. On her knees was an oak board three feet long, balanced between her apron and the edge of her straw pallet.

She did not burn tallow. Tallow was cheap—the cooper gave her the scrapings from his grease-tubs for nothing—but it smoked with a heavy, greasy soot that settled upon vellum like a grey skin, dulling the cinnabar and turning the white lead yellow.

Instead, she burned wax-ends.

They were the trimmings from the altar-candles of the Church of the Holy Rood, which her sister Clara, who scrubbed the chancel floor twice a week for fourpence, gathered from the dust-bins and brought to her in a greasy handkerchief. They burned with a clear, small, sweet-smelling flame that did not waver unless the window-shutter gave way.

Maren lifted the board.

From beneath the straw pallet, she dragged the chest. It was small, made of green elm-wood that had cracked along the grain, bound with hoops of hoop-iron that had rusted into the timber. She fitted her iron key into the padlock, turned it until the spring gave a dry, reluctant *clink*, and lifted the lid.

Inside lay her world.

There were twelve rolls of second-grade calfskin, four ink-horns stoppered with elder-pith, three hog’s-bristle brushes, and a box of small sea-shells that she used for mixing her pigments: one for vermilion, one for verdigris, one for lampblack, and one for the pale yellow ocher she gathered from the clay-slips at the foot of the Red Shelf.

Beneath the tools lay the Great Plat.

It was not a roll; it was too large to roll without cracking the heavy sizing she had used to join the pieces. It was thirty-four separate panels, each ten inches square, cut from the discarded margins of the Chancery’s great rolls—the strips that the Master limners trimmed off before a chart was sent to the capital to be stamped.

For two years, Maren had gathered these trimmings from the waste-basket beside Master Coram’s desk. She had washed them with warm vinegar to take out the dirt, scraped away the thumb-marks with her curved knife, and stitched them edge-to-edge with waxed silk from her mother’s old sewing-basket.

She laid the sheet upon the board.

It filled the wood from end to end. The candlelight fell upon it, turning the pale vellum to the color of old honey.

On the right-hand margin ran the coast: the same coast she had been drawing all day in the South Tower. The Head of Skiffs; the White Ness; the jagged teeth of the Grey Weirs; the narrow, muddy channel of the Marrow River leading into the harbour basin; the long, protective curved arm of the Shingles; and twenty leagues of salt-marsh running south toward the mouth of the Sallow.

Every headland was in its place. Every sounding was drawn with seven strokes of the crow-quill. Every beacon was marked with three rays of red cinnabar.

It was the Guild's world: complete, absolute, and finished.

Except that on Maren’s map, the coast was only the rim of the dish.

To the west—where the Chancery’s master charts showed only the blank, white *Terra Vasta*—her map was filled with ink.

It was not a secret country; it was merely the country the Guild pretended did not exist.

For six years, Maren had listened to the men who did not come into the scriptorium. She listened to the carters who brought the beech-billets for the Kiln from the woods behind the town; she listened to the charcoal-burners who came down to the South Quay with sacks of black birch-coal on their donkeys; she listened to the drovers who brought the small, shaggy highland cattle down from the northern fens to be slaughtered for the salt-fleet.

A carter knew where the mud was deep enough to drown an ox. A charcoal-burner knew which ridge carried a dry track of chert and flint above the oak-swamps. A drover knew where the water was sweet and where it was bitter with iron-alum.

Whenever a carter spoke of a hill, Maren remembered its name. Whenever a drover cursed the three-mile detour he had to make around the Sallow Marshes, she noted the bearing of the church-tower that showed above the willow-trees.

And slowly, night by night, beneath the smoke-stained rafters of the Gut, she had drawn the hinterland.

Here was the **Great Sough**: four leagues of quaking peat-moss, black as pitch, where nothing grew but cotton-grass and stunted birch.

Here was the **Sallow Basin**: a vast, shallow saucer of grey marl, flooded for eight months of the year by the sluggish, yellow waters of the river, crossed only by the willow-bottoms and the sunken ditches of the eel-catchers.

Here was the **High Scarp**: sixty miles inland, a wall of white limestone that rose three hundred cubits above the fens like the hull of an overturned ship, its top bare of trees, its cliffs broken only by three narrow, rubble-choked ravines known as the Chimneys.

And beyond the High Scarp—at the westernmost corner of her sheet, where the vellum had been trimmed close to the edge to clear a grease-spot—lay a small, square mark.

She had drawn it three months ago, after an evening spent in the tap-room of the *Three Crows*, listening to an old blind drover from the north who had traveled with his father sixty years before the great subsidence drowned the valley.

**Harrow-on-the-Hill**.

The drover had called it *High Harrow*. He said it was an ancient market town built upon a saddle of dry limestone between two crags, where the wool was so fine that the Flemish merchants used to send pack-trains of ninety mules across the mountain passes just to buy the fleeces from the autumn shearing. He said the church had six bells of white bronze that could be heard across three valleys when the wind was from the west; he said there were seven deep quarries of freestone that never cracked in the frost, and five orchards of sour winter-pears whose wine was like cider with an edge of iron.

"Is there a road?" she had asked him, slipping a copper groat into his palm beneath the table.

The old man had laughed—a wet, rattling sound in his throat that smelled of sour beer.

"A road, girl? There was the King's High Track once, before the sea came in at the Sallow mouth and the moss began to grow. My grandfather’s father saw wagons on it—great eight-horse wains with wheels as tall as a man, carrying pig-lead from the hills down to the salt-gate. But that was in the old days, before the Charter. The moss swallowed the stone fifty years before I was dropped. Now there’s nothing between the Bar and Harrow but three fathoms of black mud that would suck a church-steeple down if you dropped it off the causeway. You couldn't drive a hog across the Sough today, not if the King himself stood behind it with a whip."

She had drawn Harrow anyway.

Just a small, square redoubt with four battlements, drawn in lampblack, with a tiny church-tower beside it. Between the Bar and Harrow, she had drawn the Great Sough as it was: an empty, mottled waste of pale grey wash, speckled with tiny horizontal dashes to represent the standing pools of black bog-water.

It was sixty miles of nothing. A barrier of slime and stone that kept the world where the Guild wanted it: upon the salt water.

Maren picked up her bone burnisher—a piece of calf-rib, polished until it felt like glass—and touched the corner of the Great Sough where the vellum had curled slightly from the cold.

Then she stopped.

Her hand froze three inches above the skin.

The candle-flame gave a sudden leap as a gust of air slipped through the roof-slates, throwing her shadow across the board like an arm reaching into the paper.

She leaned down until her cheek was four inches from the vellum.

In the middle of the Great Sough—where three nights ago she had laid down a smooth, even wash of diluted soot to indicate the quaking moss of the Black Carrs—there was a line.

It was not a water-mark. It was not a crack in the sizing.

It was a line of black ink.

It was as fine as a hair from her own head, drawn with an iron pen whose nib was far sharper than the Swedish brass pens in the Chancery scriptorium. It began at the western boundary of the town of Marrow, just beyond the ditch of the South Gate where the tanners emptied their lime-pits.

It did not follow the river. It did not curve along the edges of the gravel-beds where the drovers picked their way from hummock to hummock.

It ran west-northwest. Straight as a plumb-line.

It was two inches long—which, on the scale of her plat, where one thumb-breadth represented a league of three English miles—meant six miles of country.

It cut clean through the heart of the Black Carrs.

Maren did not breathe. She took her magnifying lens—a round pebble of green rock-crystal set in a lead ring, which she had bought from a sailor who claimed to have taken it from a Venetian prize—and held it over the line.

Through the green crystal, the stroke was astonishingly clear.

It was not a single stroke. It was two parallel lines, separated by the breadth of a needle. Between the lines were tiny, microscopic dots, spaced with absolute regularity: four dots to the quarter-inch.

*Chain-marks.*

In the survey of fields, when an engineer laid out a ditch or a garden wall with a surveyor’s chain of sixty-six feet, he marked each chain-length with a pin-prick or a dot of iron-gall ink. It was the universal signature of the land-measurer.

Maren touched the line with the tip of her forefinger.

The ink was dry. It did not smudge; it did not leave the faintest shadow of black grease on her skin. It had sunk deep into the vellum, drinking the sizing just as her own ink did when she boiled it with copperas and beer.

"I didn't draw that," she whispered to the empty rafters.

The words sounded ridiculous, hollow in the cold room.

Of course she hadn't drawn it. She had spent the last three nights copying the North Sand for Master Coram; she hadn't touched the Great Plat since the previous Sunday, when she had spent an hour mending the silk stitching on the lower border. And even if she had walked in her sleep—even if some fever of the brain had driven her to take up her pen in the dark—she could not have drawn a line like that.

No one in the Chancery could draw a line like that.

Master Coram’s hand was heavy, stiff with the gout, running to thick, blunt strokes that ended in blobs of ink; the boys were clumsy, their lines wavering whenever they had to cross an uneven patch in the skin; and her own style was rounded, supple, the line of an illuminator rather than an engineer.

This line was inhumanly rigid. It looked as though it had been scored into the skin with a heated razor.

She sat back on her stool, her knees trembling against the rough oak board.

Could someone have entered the room?

She looked at the door. It was a rough plank door made of green fir, fastened with a simple iron latch and an inside bar of ash-wood. The bar was in its iron staples; it had not been moved. The window-shutter was hooked from within. The lock on her chest had not been forced; there was not a scratch upon the rusty iron hoops.

And who in Marrow would enter an apprentice’s garret to draw six miles of road through a bog where a man could not walk five paces without a pole?

If a thief had found the chest, he would have stolen the vellum to sell to the scriveners in the market; if a Guild officer had found it, he would have called the Beadle, dragged her down to the courtyard, and set the furnace doors open.

No one would sit in an attic by the light of a stolen candle and carefully, exquisitely, lay down twenty-four chain-dots between two parallel ink-lines across a swamp.

Maren picked up her bone scraper.

The scraper had a curved, crescent edge of polished steel, sharp enough to take the beard off a man’s chin without soap. When an error was made on a Guild chart—a sounding in the wrong gutter or an extra reef on the shore—the limner used the scraper to lift the ink from the surface of the vellum before it had time to penetrate the inner membrane.

She rested the steel edge against the beginning of the line, just outside the South Gate.

She held her breath. She drew the blade toward her, light as a feather, keeping the steel flat so that it would not cut the skin.

*Scritch.*

A tiny curl of white powder rose from the vellum: the dried sizing and the chalk-dust with which she had pounced the sheet two years ago.

She blew the powder away.

The line was still there.

She scraped harder. The steel bit deeper into the skin, scraping away the surface fibers until the vellum showed the grey, greasy under-layer of the hide.

The line was still there.

It was not on the vellum. It was *in* it.

It was as if the ink had not been laid upon the surface with a pen, but had grown through the skin from the other side, like the root of an alder-tree pushing through the mud of a ditch.

Maren dropped the scraper onto the board. Her palm was sweating, cold and slick against the wood.

She looked at the western end of the line—the point where the ink stopped, dead in the middle of the Black Carrs, six miles out from the town wall.

The stroke did not end in a rounded pool or a tapered point, as a line does when the draughtsman lifts his pen from the sheet.

It ended squarely, cleanly, as if the pen had been interrupted in the middle of a stroke.

And beside the terminal point, so small that she had to use the green crystal lens to be certain she was not imagining it, was a single character written in red cinnabar:

**+**

A cross. Not a decorative cross, such as an illuminator places at the head of a chapter, but a surveyor’s benchmark: the mark a road-man chisels into a stone or drives into a timber stake to show where the level was taken before the light failed.

Maren looked from the mark to the window.

Through the crack in the timber shutter, she could see the salt-fog moving through the Gut, grey and heavy, blotting out the roofs of the town. Below, in the harbour, a bell struck the third quarter after seven.

Then, from the far side of the river—out beyond the South Gate, out beyond the tanneries, out in the great, dark, level waste where no man lived—she heard a sound.

It was very faint. The wind brought it between the chimneys, thin as the whistle of a plover.

*Thump.*

A pause. Then again:

*Thump.*

It was not the sea. The sea broke on the Skiffs with a long, tearing hiss like gravel dragged down an iron plate.

This was the dull, heavy, rhythmic thud of an iron shoe striking wet timber.

The sound a man makes when he is driving an oak pile into the mud of a bog.

### Chapter 3

The Chancery did not like the rain.

When the wind hung in the east for more than three days, bringing the grey sea-mizzle that the sailors called the *Scotch fog*, the scriptorium grew so damp that the sheets of calfskin relaxed upon their stretchers like wet laundry. The lines drawn on Monday broadened by a tenth of an inch by Wednesday; the sizing turned sticky beneath the palm; and Master Coram’s horn spectacles became so fogged with moisture that he had to wipe them on his woollen skirt every twenty minutes.

"The Dutchmen are grumbling," Warden Kedge said.

He had come into the scriptorium at ten in the morning, his heavy beaver hat dripping onto the floorboards, his cloak of dark green broadcloth smelling of wet horse. He was an alderman as well as a Warden—a man who owned three shares in the salt-wharves and a half-share in the great herring-curing house at the South Quay. His face was round, red, and hard as an iron weight, his lower lip protruding in a perpetual pout of suspicion.

"Let them grumble," Coram said without looking up from his roll. "The Dutch would grumble if the sea were made of gin and the clouds rained silver groats."

"They say the bar has silted," Kedge said, walking toward the high lancets and staring down at the harbour mouth. "A galliot from Enkhuizen went aground on the Shingles at five this morning. Struck her rudder off on the gravel. The master says the channel has moved forty rods to the south."

"The channel has not moved," Coram said flatly. "The channel is where it was in the Third Survey. The master was drunk on juniper-water and did not keep the church-tower in line with the beacon."

"The master had two pilots from the town," Kedge said, his voice dropping an octave. "Both sworn brothers of the Guild. They say the same thing. They say the water was four feet shallower than the chart."

Coram’s hand paused on his goose-quill. For a moment, the only sound in the great room was the scratching of Maren’s brass pen and the dripping of the rain from the lead gutter outside the window.

"The water is the water," Coram said at last. "The chart is the law. If the water does not agree with the chart, the pilots must wait for the spring-tides. We do not alter the plates for every skipper who cannot keep his lead-line wet."

"We may have to alter more than the plates," Kedge muttered.

He turned from the window, his eyes sweeping across the apprentice benches until they settled upon Maren. She did not raise her head; her pen was moving across the rocks of the Grey Weirs, laying down the tiny, crescent-shaped dots that represented the drying weed at low water.

"Is that Brand?" Kedge asked.

"It is," Coram said. "Her indenture is up. She sits the Masterwork in the spring."

Kedge took two steps toward her board. His boots were thick-soled, studded with square-headed iron nails that clicked against the grit on the floor. He leaned over her shoulder, his sour breath—stale ale and pickled oysters—washing across her cheek.

"Clean work," he said, tapping the edge of the vellum with a gloved finger. "A steady hand for a cooper’s brat. But too fine. You draw the cliffs as if they were made of lace, girl. Granite isn't lace. Granite is six thousand tons of cold rock that will take the bottom out of an oak ship before the master can say his prayers."

"Yes, sir," Maren said softly.

"Can you draw a line that doesn't bend?"

She looked up then. His eyes were small, dark, set deep beneath heavy red brows that met over the bridge of his broken nose.

"I draw what the master roll sets out, sir."

"Do you?" He looked at her closely, his gaze dropping to the ink-stains on her fingers, then to the wooden box where her pens lay. "There are stories in the lower town, Brand. Foolish stories. Some of the carters coming out of the West Reach say there are men in the marshes. Men with poles and iron chains."

Coram gave an irritated grunt. "Carters! Carters see ghosts in every peat-hole after three pints of small beer."

"These were not ghosts," Kedge said, not taking his eyes from Maren’s face. "They were driving stakes into the Black Carrs. Ash stakes, four inches thick, with red marks on the crown. Someone is surveying the Sough."

"No one surveys the Sough," Coram said, his voice rising in anger. "The Sough belongs to the Crown, and the Crown has granted the survey of all lands within thirty leagues of the tide to this Chancery. If any man sets an iron chain in that bog without the seal of the Wardens, he is a common trespasser and a wrecker. The sheriff’s men will have him in the cage at the South Gate before sunset."

"The sheriff’s men went out on Tuesday," Kedge said quietly. "They didn't find any men. But they found the stakes."

Coram turned on his stool, his horn spectacles slipping down his nose. "What stakes?"

"Seventy of them," Kedge said. "Set in a line from the Tanners’ Ditch straight toward the high ground at the foot of the Scarp. A line two leagues long. Driven four cubits into the peat, topped with crushed limestone from the upland quarries."

Maren felt the blood leave her lips.

Her fingers tightened around the brass barrel of her pen until the joint of her thumb went white. She kept her eyes fixed upon the Grey Weirs, but the lines on the vellum seemed to waver, breaking into double images that danced before her eyes.

*Two leagues long.*

Last night, when she had blown out her candle, the line on her map had been two inches: six miles. Two leagues.

The mark on the skin had not been a dream. It had not been an old stroke showing through from the hide.

It was the stakes.

"Who paid for the limestone?" Coram demanded, his old voice cracking with indignation. "Limestone does not walk out of the hills on its own feet. To carry seventy horse-loads of stone across the Sough would take forty men and twenty carts."

"No carts," Kedge said. "The carters wouldn't go. They’re afraid of the quag. But the stone is there. Packed around the heads of the stakes like collars. Someone is laying a line, Coram. A line that doesn't come through the North Gate. A line that doesn't pay the bridge-toll. And if a road reaches the limestone scarp..."

He left the sentence unfinished. He didn't need to finish it.

If a road reached the foot of the Scarp, it would find the ravines: the three Chimneys where the old pack-trails used to climb into the high wool-valleys. It would link the interior directly to the sea—not through the harbour of Marrow, with its forty-two customs duties and its iron boom, but through some little, unchartered fishing-cove in the western bays where no King’s officer had ever set his foot.

It was treason against the Charter.

"We will burn it," Coram said, his teeth clicking together. "If any map of that line exists—if any man has set a stroke of ink to paper to record those marks—the Assay-Master will have it in the Kiln before the ink is dry. I will go to the Mayor myself. I will have the Beadle search every scrivener’s shop from the Fish-Gate to the High Dike."

"Search the scriveners if you like," Kedge said, turning toward the stairs. "But look closer to home, too, old man. A line that straight wasn't laid by a ditcher with an alder-pole. It was laid with a cross-staff and a brass divider. It was laid by someone who knows how to keep a back-sight across two leagues of dead water."

He looked at Maren once more—a cold, measuring glance that lingered on her wrists and the small, calloused hollow on the side of her right middle finger where the pen had rested for six years.

Then he went down the stairs, his heavy boots shaking the tower with every step.

***

That night, Maren did not light her candle until the town watch had cried the tenth hour.

The rain had stopped, but the fog was so dense that when she opened the shutter a hand’s breadth, she could not see the timber jetty of the house across the Gut. The air was cold, smelling of the dead weed that the low tide had left stranded upon the mud-banks.

She knelt by her chest.

She did not use the board. She laid the Great Plat directly upon the bare floorboards, weighting the four corners with two flat-irons, an empty ink-pot, and her brass box of sand.

She took out the green crystal lens.

Her hands were so cold that she had to blow upon her fingers before she could hold the glass steady.

The line from the Tanners’ Ditch was there, exactly as it had been the night before: two parallel strokes, the twenty-four chain-dots, and the small red benchmark at the sixth mile.

She looked at the benchmark.

Then she caught her breath.

The red cross was gone.

In its place was a tiny, rectangular symbol, no larger than a flea’s wing, drawn in the same intense, carbon-black ink as the lines.

Through the green crystal, she could see the four minute corner-turrets, the central archway, and the tiny cross-hatching that indicated a drystone revetment.

It was an engineer’s sign for an abutment.

And extending beyond the abutment—stretching westward into the pale grey wash of the Great Sough—was a new line.

It was three inches long.

Nine miles.

It crossed the river Sallow at the point where the stream widened into the Great Reed-Bed—a place where the water was six cubits deep, black and sluggish, with a bottom of quick-sand that had swallowed an entire baggage-train of the Duke’s during the Northern Wars.

The line did not stop at the water’s edge.

Across the reed-bed, the ink had drawn three curved lines: the arches of a stone causeway. Between the arches were five piers, each protected by an angled cut-water pointing upstream against the flood.

The drawing was so precise that she could count the courses of stone in the piers: four courses of ashlar below the spring of the arch, two courses of rusticated blocks above, and a flat coping along the parapet with small drainage-holes at every third yard.

It was not a sketch. It was an elevation, drawn to the scale of one inch to forty rods.

Maren sat on her heels, her heart pounding against her ribs like a bird in a wicker cage.

Nine miles.

In twenty-four hours, the road had grown by nine miles.

If sixty men with forty carts had taken a month to carry the seventy limestone stakes for the first two leagues, how could an army have built three stone arches across a river six cubits deep in a single night?

It was impossible. The whole population of Marrow—the masons, the carpenters, the quarry-men, the boys from the docks—could not have laid one pier in the Great Reed-Bed without half the town knowing of it. The timber for the caissons alone would have stripped every cooper’s yard from the river to the sea.

Yet there were the arches.

The ink was not merely dry; it had the faint, greyish bloom that old ink gets when it has lived in a ledger for fifty years.

She leaned closer. She took a clean hog’s-bristle brush, dipped it into a cup of clear well-water, and touched the tip to the center arch of the causeway.

If the ink had been laid that evening—if some hand had crept into the room while she was down in the alley buying her loaf of rye-bread—the water would lift the gum. The line would soften; a pale brown cloud would rise into the drop of water.

She touched the stone-work.

The water lay upon the vellum, round and clear as a bead of glass.

The ink did not move. It did not bleed by the width of a hair. It remained hard, sharp, and black, grinning up at her through the water like an iron gate through a mist.

"What are you?" she whispered.

She looked along the course of the new line.

It was no longer running straight. Beyond the Sallow Causeway, the ground began to rise toward the first low foothills of the Scarp—a broken wilderness of grey limestone ribs, deep shake-holes where the rain had hollowed the rock into caverns, and dense thickets of blackthorn that no sheep could penetrate.

The road curved. It followed the natural contour of the land, winding along the dry shoulder of a limestone spur with an engineering skill that took her breath away. Here was a cutting, marked with delicate hachures to show where the rock had been notched to a depth of ten feet; here was an embankment, built of drystone rubble to carry the carriage-way across a peat-bottomed hollow; here was a milestone, marked with a tiny Roman numeral:

**VIII**

Eight miles from the town gate.

And at the end of the line—where the ink stopped tonight, at the foot of the great white cliffs of the High Scarp—was another benchmark.

This one was not red. It was drawn in bright, unfaded verdigris—a brilliant, poison-green that smelled faintly of copper-rust and sour cider.

Beside the green cross was a single word, written in a hand so small that even through the Venetian lens she could barely distinguish the letters:

*Adveniet.*

It will come.

Maren rose to her feet. The cold seemed to have entered her bones; her shins were numb, her toes stiff in her thin shoes.

She walked to the window. She pulled the wooden shutter back and leaned out into the fog.

The Gut was silent. Across the lane, the tanner’s apprentice was snoring—a long, bubbling whistle that ended in a grunt. Below, in the cooper’s yard, the water in the soaking-tubs dripped with a steady *plop-plop-plop* onto the cobbles.

She looked toward the west.

Between the leaning gables of the fish-houses, she could see nothing but the grey, smothering wool of the sea-mizzle. The Great Sough was out there, two miles away, hidden behind the town ditch and the cabbage-gardens of the poor.

She listened.

There was no sound of pile-drivers tonight. No iron striking timber. No carts grinding across the shingle.

Only the silence of thirty leagues of dead water and quaking peat.

Yet she knew—with a certainty that sat in her stomach like an iron weight—that if she took her lantern and her staff, if she climbed down the cooper’s wood-pile and slipped through the South Gate before the watch was set, she would find the causeway.

She would find three arches of white limestone rising out of the black water of the reed-bed, cold and wet in the rain, with the cut-waters pointing upstream toward the hills.

And she knew, too, what Master Coram would say if he saw this sheet.

*A map that lies is a murderer with a pen.*

He would not even wait for the Tuesday furnace. He would take his horn-rimmed spectacles from his nose, fold them in his handkerchief, and call the Beadle before he had finished his morning beer.

And they would not burn the map alone.

They would burn her.

Under the Great Charter, the third penalty for the forgery of a royal sea-card or inland survey was not the loss of a hand or the pillory. It was the *Purging of the Hand*: the offender was brought to the courtyard of the Guildhall, her right arm was bound to a cedar stake, and she was forced to hold her hand in the draught-hole of the Kiln until the skin was as black as the ink she had misused.

Maren looked at her right hand.

The skin was pale, the fingers long and slender, calloused at the second joint of the middle finger where the pen had rested for six years. It was a good hand. It could draw sixty strokes to the inch without a tremble; it could tell the difference between sheep-skin and calfskin by the feel of the grain in the dark; it was the only thing she owned in the world.

She went back to the board.

She did not take up her scraper again. She knew now that the steel could not kill the line.

Instead, she took her clean linen towel, folded the Great Plat into its thirty-four square panels, and laid it back in the bottom of the green elm chest. She placed the ink-horns upon it, then the brushes, then the shells of dried pigment, and finally the box of sand.

She closed the lid. She turned the iron key in the lock, slipped the key back into the oil-skin rag in her pocket, and lay down upon her straw pallet with her wool cloak pulled over her boots.

She did not sleep.

She lay with her eyes open, watching the grey light of the dawn slowly fill the cracks in the roof-slates, listening for the sound of boots in the lane below.

And somewhere in the back of her brain—where the lines and the soundings lived—a small voice was counting:

*Six miles on Tuesday. Nine miles on Wednesday.*

*The Scarp is twenty leagues away.*

*At this pace, the road will reach Harrow by the full of the moon.*

### Chapter 4

The archives of the Chancery were kept in the Great Vault beneath the South Tower, three feet below the level of the high-water mark of the river.

It was a place that smelled of dry rot, salted lard, and the sulfurous smoke of the charcoal brazier that was kept burning day and night to keep the salt-air from rotting the tallow bindings of the rolls. The walls were six feet thick, built of rough-hewn granite blocks that were always wet to the touch; when the spring-tides came up the estuary, the water seeped through the mortar in thin, dark ribbons that ran into the stone gutters and disappeared through an iron grating in the floor.

Maren had only been down to the vault three times in her six years.

Once to carry down the old registers of the Third Survey when Master Coram had them rebound; once to fetch the copper plates of the Bight of Orris after the great storm of her fourth winter; and once when she was a second-year girl, to be shown the Great Chest of the Charter.

The chest was five feet long, made of heart-of-oak four inches thick, bound with thirteen iron straps that were fastened with seven separate padlocks. In it lay the original grant of King Edward: the skin with the great green seal that gave the Guild the right to rule the coast and keep the inland silent.

"You may look for the register of the parish of St. Jude," Master Coram had told her that morning, after she had finished her three copies of the North Sand. "The churchwardens have a dispute with the customs reeves about the boundary of the glebe-marsh. They say the ditch is twenty rods south of the mill-tail; the reeves say the ditch was filled eighty years ago. Go down and find the roll of the year seventy-two. And do not drop the candle."

She carried a small horn lantern with three panes of scraped sheep-bladder that threw a dull, yellow circle of light upon the stone steps.

The vault was cold—colder than the scriptorium, colder even than her garret under the roof. In the center of the low, groined chamber stood four rows of oak presses, their shelves divided into pigeon-holes that held hundreds of parchment rolls, each tied with a parchment tag that dangled into the aisle like the ears of dead rabbits.

Maren did not go to the press marked *St. Jude*.

She stood at the bottom of the stairs, her head tilted, listening.

Above her, through three feet of granite and two courses of oak flooring, she could hear the faint, dull rumble of the scriptorium: the scrape of a stool, the cough of an apprentice, the muffled voice of the Assay-Master giving his orders to the furnace-boy.

They were all up there. No one came down to the vault unless a roll was needed for the Court of Assistants.

She walked quickly down the center aisle, her lantern swinging in her hand, until she reached the fourth press—the press at the back, where the wall was so damp that a thick, white beard of salt-crust had grown across the lower shelves.

This was the *Scrinium Antiquum*. The old chest.

Here lay the rolls that had been drawn before the Charter was sealed: the surveys of the old monastic houses that had been dissolved three generations before; the estate-books of the lords who had been beheaded during the Wars of the Succession; and the travel-books of the old Italian and German merchants who had visited the coast when Marrow was nothing more than a cluster of turf huts around an iron beacon.

The tags were written in a strange, cramped Latin, black as pitch, with letters whose tails ended in sharp, thorny hooks.

*Compotus Manerii de Sallow...*

*Extenta Terrarum Baroniae de Orris...*

She ran her fingers along the parchment rolls. The skins were stiff, dry, smelling of the dead sheep from which they had been taken a hundred and fifty years ago. Many were warped by the damp, their edges glued together by the green mold that flourished in the dark.

Then her hand stopped.

In a deep, square pigeon-hole near the floor—a hole that had no tag, but was marked only with a square of lead into which a Roman cross had been stamped with a punch—lay a bundle of five small rolls.

They were not vellum. They were made of thick, grey rag-paper, sized with something that smelled faintly of almond-oil and resin.

She pulled out the first roll.

She sat upon an upturned butter-tub that had been used for storing spare parchment-cuttings, set her horn lantern on her knees, and unrolled the sheet.

It was a map.

Not a sea-card. There were no rhumb-lines; there were no compass-roses with their thirty-two points radiating across the water; there were no drawings of galleons or sea-monsters sporting in the waves.

It was an inland plat.

The title was written at the top in a beautiful, clear Italian cursive, whose capitals were decorated with delicate pen-flourishes that looked like the tendrils of a grapevine:

*Iter Antiquum ad Montes.*

The Old Road to the Hills.

Maren held the paper to the lantern-bladder.

The map showed the same country as her Great Plat, but drawn by a hand that had worked three centuries ago.

There was the river Sallow, drawn not as a stagnant reed-bed, but as a clear, running stream with three water-mills marked along its banks. There were the small, square enclosures of the sheep-runs; there were the villages—fourteen of them, each marked with a tiny red church and a cluster of brown roofs—where now there was nothing but the quaking moss of the Black Carrs.

And running through the middle of the valley—from the old west gate of the town to the foot of the limestone scarp—was a broad, double line, shaded with yellow ocher to indicate gravel and crushed stone.

The road.

She traced the line with the tip of her fingernail.

It followed the exact course of the line that had appeared upon her map the night before.

Here was the crossing of the Sallow: marked on this old paper as *Pons Lapideus*—the Stone Bridge. The elevation was drawn in the margin: three arches of rusticated limestone, four courses of ashlar below the spring, five piers with angled cut-waters pointing upstream.

It was identical.

The line on her Great Plat was not an invention. It was not a prophecy.

It was an excavation.

The road was already there. It had been there for three hundred years, buried three cubits deep beneath the peat and the black silt that had washed down from the hills when the great drainage-sluices were broken during the Siege of Marrow.

She unrolled the second sheet.

This was the continuation: the ascent of the High Scarp.

On her own map, the verdigris benchmark had stopped at the foot of the cliffs, where the white limestone rose three hundred cubits into the clouds like an impassable wall.

On the old Italian plat, the road did not stop.

It climbed.

It climbed through the middle ravine—the Chimney that the shepherds called the *Devil’s Throat*—by means of a stone ramp twenty feet wide, built upon arches of dry limestone that spanned the fissures in the rock. The gradient was marked in small red figures at every hundred yards: *unus ad duodecim*—one in twelve. A wagon could climb that. A heavy four-wheeled wain, loaded with sixty sacks of hill-wool, could roll down that ramp without locking its wheels.

And at the top of the ramp—perched upon the high saddle of the mountain like a crown of grey flint—lay the town.

**Harrow-on-the-Hill**.

The old draughtsman had drawn it with a lover’s eye.

He had drawn the marketplace: a vast, cobbled square surrounded by stone houses with arcaded fronts, where the cloth-merchants kept their bales dry under the arches. He had drawn the Church of St. Michael-of-the-Crags: a great building with three aisles, six square windows in the clerestory, and a tall, octagonal lantern on the central tower that held the six bells of white bronze.

He had drawn the quarries: four round pits in the limestone marked *Lapicidinae Albae*—the White Pits—from which the stone for the sea-wall at Marrow had been taken before the Guild was founded.

And in the margin, written in that same exquisite, cold Italian hand, was an annotation that made Maren’s skin prickle beneath her woollen sleeves:

*Nota: The stone of Harrow does not take the salt. If it be carried down to the sea, it will stand for five hundred years without scaling. But the men of the Bar have closed the gate, preferring their own granite from the Skiffs, which is soft and rots in the frost, so that their masons may never lack for employment.*

*They have broken the bridge at the Sallow; they have opened the sluices to flood the low fields; they have forbidden any rutter or card to show the road, under pain of the wheel.*

*They call the road a fiction, so that they may call the town a desert.*

A sound came from the top of the stairs.

The dry, regular *click-click* of an iron rule tapping against the stone balustrade.

Master Coram.

Maren did not hesitate. Her movements were as smooth and rapid as a stoat’s in a stone wall.

She rolled the Italian map, slipped it back into its square pigeon-hole, and pushed it deep into the shadow behind the lead weight. She reached for the nearest roll on the third shelf—a dry, crumbling sheet of churchwardens’ accounts from the parish of St. Jude—and stood up.

She was blowing the dust from her fingers when the old man’s slippers appeared on the bottom step.

"You are a long time, Brand," Coram said.

He stood in the archway, holding a tallow candle in a tin saucer. In the yellow light, his horn-rimmed spectacles looked like two greasy pennies covering his eyes. His breathing was heavy, wet with the phlegm of his eighty years.

"The tags are decayed, sir," she said, her voice dropping into that humble, flat cadence she had used since she was twelve. "The mice have eaten the corners of the seventy-two roll. I was trying to read the endorsement."

Coram took two steps into the chamber. He held the candle high, his eyes scanning the shelves—not the rolls themselves, but their alignment, checking whether any sheet had been pulled forward from the line.

"You have been at the fourth press," he said.

"The St. Jude papers are scattered, sir. Some were tied with the old tithe-rolls of the abbey."

Coram walked to the pigeon-hole where the Italian papers lay. He stood before it for three seconds, his candle-flame trembling in the draught from the river-seep.

"There is nothing for you in that press," he said, his voice dropping into a register she had never heard him use before: not angry, not sharp, but heavy with a strange, weary melancholy. "Those are the dead rolls, girl. The rolls from the time before the Great Order."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you know what the Great Order was, Maren?"

It was the first time in six years he had called her by her Christian name.

She kept her eyes upon the floorboards between his slippers. "The establishment of the Chancery, sir. The Charter of King Edward."

"It was more than that," Coram said, his hand reaching out to touch the stone wall where the salt-crust grew. "It was peace. Before the Charter, every baron had his own limner; every port had its own soundings; every merchant had his own secret rutter that he kept in an iron box beneath his bed. Ships were wrecked every month because one man’s fathom was another man’s yard. The towns were at war over the inland tolls; the hill-men came down with their cattle and burned the corn-barns at the South Gate; the merchants hired French privateers to sink their rivals in the fairway."

He turned his horn spectacles toward her. Behind the thick glass, his watery eyes were large, pale, and terribly clear.

"The Charter did not merely measure the coast, Maren. It fixed it. It made the sea the only path, because on the sea a ship can be seen from the tower. On the sea, a tax can be taken. On the sea, the King can know who goes out and who comes in."

"And the inland?" she asked, the question slipping from her mouth before she could bite her tongue.

Coram looked at the fourth press.

"The inland has no bottom," he said softly. "The inland is full of men who don't know the King’s name and don't care for his seal. If you open a road through the marsh, the hill-men will come down again. And they will not come with wool, Maren. They will come with iron."

He took the roll of St. Jude from her hands.

"Go up to the bench," he said. "The Assay-Master has brought down three charts from the North Gate. The ink on your Head of Skiffs must be dry by now. Go and pounce it."

"Yes, sir," she said.

She turned toward the stairs. But as she set her foot upon the first stone step, Coram’s voice caught her once more.

"Maren."

She stopped, her hand upon the cold granite balustrade.

"The Assay-Master burns the condemned sheets at noon tomorrow," the old man said, not looking at her, his eyes fixed upon the damp stones of the vault floor. "If an apprentice has any... waste-paper in her chest—any cuttings that have not been accounted for in the quarterly ledger—it would be wise to carry them down to the courtyard tonight."

He lifted his candle.

"The furnace does not ask who drew the line," he said. "It only asks for the skin."

### Chapter 5

At midnight, the tide was dead low.

From her garret window, Maren could look down into the gut of the river. The water was nothing more than an oily black gutter six rods wide, meandering between vast, gleaming banks of blue-grey mud that smelled of rotting weed and dead crabs. A single ketch lay on her side in the mud beside the South Quay, her masts raking across the stars like two broken needles.

The stars were out. The fog had been driven back toward the sea by a sharp, thin frost that came down from the northern hills—a frost that left a crust of white rime upon the slate roofs and turned the pools in the Gut to brittle glass.

Maren did not take her shoes.

She wore two pairs of thick woollen stockings, knitted from coarse highland yarn that her sister had greased with lard to keep out the wet. Over her black bodice she wore a heavy carter’s smock of unbleached frieze, its pockets filled with three things:

Her green crystal lens.

A small horn of lampblack ink, freshly mixed with stale beer and copperas.

And an iron ruling pen with an ivory handle that her father had found in the ballast-gravel of a Spanish prize forty years ago.

She carried the Great Plat under her arm, rolled tight inside an oil-cloth tube that she had sealed with mutton-tallow at both ends.

She did not use the stairs.

The cooper had three apprentices who slept in the shavings-bin beside the kitchen door; if a plank groaned, they would be up with their ash-sticks, thinking of the thieves who came to steal the iron hoops.

She went out through the garret window.

The roof of the cooperage had a low pitch, covered with rough Welsh slates that were slippery with the rime. She crawled on her knees and her left elbow, her fingers finding the gaps between the timber battens beneath the slates. At the corner of the wall, an old elder-tree had grown up from the mud-bank of the river, its branches thick as a man’s thigh, resting against the stone eaves.

She swung down into the branches.

The wood gave a wet, creaking groan, but it held. In three seconds, her wool-stockinged feet touched the mud of the river-bank.

The town wall was thirty yards away.

At the South Gate, where the road to the tanneries passed through a low, pointed arch of river-stone, two watchmen were sitting in the stone lodge with an iron basket of coals between their knees. She could see the orange glow of the embers through the arrow-slits; she could hear the rattle of their dice in a horn cup and the low, monotonous cursing of the elder man as he lost his groats.

She did not go through the gate.

Fifty yards to the west of the lodge, the town ditch—a stagnant, black canal that received the waste of the butcher-houses and the lime-pits—passed beneath the stone wall through an iron portcullis.

The water was two feet deep, thick with grey grease that smelled of dead cattle.

Maren did not hesitate.

She stepped into the ditch. The cold took her breath away with a sharp, iron stab that reached from her ankles to her groin; the mud at the bottom was soft, sucking at her woollen stockings like a living mouth. She bent her head, holding the oil-cloth tube high above the water with her left hand, and waded beneath the dripping arch of the wall.

The iron spikes of the portcullis were three inches above her neck. She could feel the cold rust scaling off onto her smock as her shoulders grazed the bars.

Then she was through.

She climbed out of the ditch onto the low clay bank on the outside of the wall.

Here was the **Tanners’ Field**: three acres of trampled, sour grass, pitted with seventy round holes where the tanners steeped their hides in dog-dung and lime. The pits were frozen over tonight, their surfaces gleaming in the starlight like rows of dead eyes.

Beyond the field lay the ditch.

And beyond the ditch lay the Great Sough.

In the dark, the peat-bog looked like an ocean of black glass. There were no trees; there were no hedges; there were no walls. Only the vast, flat, level waste of moss, stretching away toward the northern horizon until it met the low, cold stars.

Maren pulled the oil-cloth tube from under her arm.

She knelt upon a heap of dry willow-cuttings that a ditcher had left beside the hedge. She took out her Great Plat and unrolled the first four panels—the panels that showed the Tanners’ Ditch and the beginning of the road.

She took out her green crystal lens.

In the starlight, the vellum looked grey, cold, and dead.

The line was there. The two parallel strokes, the twenty-four chain-dots, and the first benchmark at the sixth mile.

She looked up from the map.

She looked out across the ditch into the blackness of the bog.

Her breath came in white clouds that hung before her face for three seconds before the north wind tore them away.

"There is nothing," she whispered.

There was only the moss. The dark, quaking surface of the peat, covered with dry tufts of cotton-grass that rattled in the frost like dry paper.

She stood up. She walked three paces to the edge of the ditch.

Her foot struck something hard.

It was not a willow-root. It was not a frozen clod of clay.

It was stone.

She dropped to her knees, her hands plunging into the frosty grass.

Beneath a thin skin of dead moss and dried mud—no thicker than the crust of a loaf of bread—lay a block of limestone.

It was eighteen inches square, its upper face dressed with the tooth-chisel, its edges sharp as a coin from the mint.

Beside it lay another. And another.

They were set in a double line, three yards apart, running straight out into the black heart of the Sough like two rows of teeth. Between the stones, the ground was not peat.

It was gravel.

Fine, white river-gravel, mixed with crushed lime and pounded flint, packed so hard that when she drove the tip of her ivory pen into the surface, the iron nib gave a sharp *clink* and threw a tiny yellow spark into the frost.

A road.

It did not rise above the moss; it lay three inches below the level of the heather, hidden by sixty years of dead vegetation and the black silt of the autumn rains. A man could walk across it twenty times by day and think he was stepping upon a dry ridge of turf; he would never know that beneath his boots lay two thousand tons of dressed stone, laid upon a bed of seasoned alder-timbers that had been driven six cubits into the mud.

Maren rose. She took her first step.

The road was firm. It did not quake beneath her stockings; it did not give that slow, sickening lurch that the moss gives when a body steps onto the bog-crust. It was as solid as the flags of the scriptorium floor.

She walked.

Ten paces. Twenty paces. A hundred.

The town wall began to shrink behind her. The high, dark silhouette of the South Tower, with its lantern burning upon the leads to guide the night-coasters, seemed to sink into the river-mud. The smells of the city—the tallow, the salt-fish, the rotting hides—were blown away by the clean, sharp wind that came down from the limestone hills.

The air smelled of something she had never known in Marrow.

It smelled of juniper. It smelled of dry, cold stone that had never tasted salt. It smelled of mountain water that had run over limestone beds without ever seeing a mud-bank or an iron boom.

At the second mile, she found the first milestone.

It was a round column of white stone, four feet tall, its base buried in the heather. It had not fallen. It stood perfectly upright, its face turned toward the north-west, where the stars were bright and cold above the hills.

On the front of the stone was an inscription, cut deep into the limestone in square Roman capitals that had been filled with melted lead to keep out the moss:

**VIATOR.**

*Traveler.*

**HAEC EST VIA AD COLLEM.**

*This is the road to the hill.*

**QUAM FECIT SENATUS POPULUSQUE HARROVIENSIS.**

*Which the Senate and People of Harrow made.*

**ANNO URBIS CONDITAE DCCXII.**

*In the seven hundred and twelfth year of the city.*

Maren touched the cold lead of the letters.

Seven hundred and twelve.

The Charter of King Edward was dated the year five hundred and forty.

The road had been here for two hundred years before the first Warden had set his horn spectacles upon his nose. The road had carried the wool-wagons to the sea before the Guild had built its first furnace; before the Chancery had drawn its first rhumb-line; before the Mayor had hung his first iron boom across the river-gate.

It was not a new road.

It was the *only* road.

And as she stood there in the vast, starry silence of the bog, with her hand resting upon the frozen lead of the stone, she saw something that made her knees give way.

Three leagues ahead—at the foot of the great white cliffs of the High Scarp, where the land began to rise into the cold darkness of the mountains—a light was burning.

It was not a beacon. It did not have the red, smoky flare of an iron pitch-basket on a headland.

It was a clear, white, steady light—the light of six large lanterns, set in a circle upon the top of an octagonal tower fifty cubits above the plain.

The lantern of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

The bells were not ringing; the distance was too great for the sound of white bronze to cross six leagues of peat.

But as she watched, the light flared once—a brilliant, silver flash that seemed to cut the dark like the edge of a scraper—and then settled back into its cold, silent watch.

The map had not lied.

The town was there.

Maren looked down at the paper under her arm.

She did not take out her pen. She did not need to draw the line now.

The line was already in her boots; it was in the cold stone beneath her stockings; it was in the marrow of her bones.

She turned.

She looked back toward the east, where the grey walls of Marrow-on-the-Bar lay huddled against the black water of the sea like a pile of dead shells left stranded by the tide.

In four hours, the bell of St. Jude would ring for the morning prayer.

Master Coram would climb his three-legged stool; the boys would wash the ink-stones; the Assay-Master would take his iron tongs and look into the cold ashes of the Kiln.

And at noon, the Court of Wardens would assemble in the Great Chamber to inspect the Masterworks of the seventh-year apprentices.

Maren Brand turned her back upon the sea.

She set her feet upon the white limestone of the road, hitched her carter’s smock over her shoulders, and began to walk toward the hills.

### Chapter 6

A mile past the third milestone, where the road entered the deep depression known as the **Sallow Bottoms**, the gravel bed disappeared beneath four feet of water.

It was not the sea. The water was black, cold as melted snow, covered with a thin skin of morning ice that shattered against her shins with the sound of breaking glass. It smelled of stagnant peat and rotten birch-leaves.

Maren did not stop.

She felt the edge of the submerged causeway with her ash-pole—a six-foot willow sapling she had cut from the hedge at the Tanners’ Ditch. To the left, the pole plunged down three cubits into soft, treacherous mire that offered no bottom; to the right, it struck the sharp, square shoulder of the limestone coping.

She walked in the middle, where the stone was two feet beneath the surface.

Her stockings were frozen to her calves. The wool was stiff, grey with lime-slime, rubbing the skin of her heels until she could feel the warm stickiness of her own blood inside her shoes.

Every twenty paces, she thrust the pole into the water.

*Clink.*

The stone was there.

It was five miles wide, this sunken basin. In the Chancery’s great rolls, it was labeled *Lacus Mortuus*—the Dead Mere. Master Varley had recorded it in the Third Survey as "a drowned and unnavigable morass, thirty cubits deep in mud, yielding neither passage to horse nor bottom to boat, wherein forty men of the King’s retinue were swallowed up in the year of the Red Comet."

He had not recorded the causeway because he had never set his slippers in the water. He had looked at the mere from the safe platform of the South Tower with a six-inch spy-glass, seen the black water and the rotting willow-trunks, and dipped his pen in the lampblack to draw four leagues of horizontal dashes.

Maren reached the center of the mere.

Here the water deepened. It rose to her thighs, cold as an iron belt around her belly. She held the oil-cloth tube with her Great Plat high against her collarbone with her left hand, using her right to lean upon the pole.

Then, through the grey mist that was rising from the water under the first thin light of the dawn, she saw the bridge.

It was not three arches.

It was seven.

The old Italian draughtsman had drawn only the central span; he had not recorded the four low flood-arches that reached out into the reed-beds on either side to let the spring-freshets pass.

The piers were built of white limestone ashlar, each block three cubits long by two cubits high, fitted together without mortar—drystone masonry of such astonishing precision that she could not have slipped the point of her finest crow-quill into the joints. The cut-waters were sharp, triangular, rising six feet above the current like the prows of galleys frozen in stone.

Above the central arch, carved into the stone parapet, was a relief:

A pair of hands.

Not the open, pious hands of a churchman, nor the clenched fist of a baron.

They were a draughtsman’s hands.

The left hand held a brass divider; the right held an iron ruling pen whose point was set against an open sheet of stone vellum. Around the border ran a Latin motto, cut in square capitals that were deep enough for the swallows to build their nests in the hollows of the letters:

**MENSURA REGIT ORBEM.**

*The Measure rules the world.*

Maren climbed out of the water onto the stone ramp of the bridge.

The road here was dry. The arches rose eight cubits above the surface of the mere, catching the first cold, yellow rays of the sun as it broke over the eastern clouds.

She sat upon the parapet. She wrung the black water from the skirts of her carter’s smock, her teeth chattering with such violence that she could not speak the names of her tools. Her boots were ruined; the soles had parted from the uppers, soaked through with the bitter peat-water.

She unrolled the Great Plat upon her knees.

The vellum was dry. The oil-cloth had kept out the river-mizzle.

She laid the sheet flat against the stone parapet, beside the relief of the two hands.

The line from the Tanners’ Ditch was there. The causeway was there. The milestone **VIII** was there.

And then, as she watched by the light of the rising sun, the vellum began to move.

It was not a tremor. It was not the wind.

Beneath the surface of the skin—in the very heart of the parchment, where the hair-roots of the dead calf had left their tiny, grey pits—something was stirring.

A drop of moisture appeared upon the white stone of the Scarp.

Not water. A tiny, circular bead of ink, black and glossy as an elderberry.

The bead elongated. It grew into a line that crept up the paper with the smooth, silent speed of a drop of oil spreading across cold water.

It entered the middle ravine—the Devil’s Throat.

It began to draw the zigzag switchbacks.

First a ramp thirty yards long, rising at one in twelve along the northern face of the cliff. Then a square turn, supported by a stone turret built out over the abyss. Then a second ramp, cutting back toward the south across a ledge of grey chert.

It did not hesitate. It did not smudge.

It drew the masonry courses; it drew the drainage-gutters; it drew the parapet stones, each with its round iron tie-rod to bind it to the rock.

And behind the pen—if pen it was—came the color.

A pale, transparent wash of mountain ocher followed the lines, tinting the roadway yellow between its white margins. Beside the road appeared the vegetation: tiny, three-pointed strokes of olive-green to indicate the scrub-juniper; dots of brown for the scree-slopes; and thin, blue threads for the mountain springs that trickled across the stone into the ravine.

Maren did not move. She sat with her knees drawn up against the cold limestone, her hands clasped around her shins, her breath frozen in her throat.

The map was drawing itself.

Not because it was enchanted. Not because some demon of the air was guiding an invisible quill across her board.

She looked from the paper to the hills.

The High Scarp was ten miles away now. In the clean morning light, the great white wall of limestone was no longer a shadow on the horizon; it was a reality of stone, buttress, and cliff, rising out of the green foothills like the rampart of the world.

And there, in the center of the wall—in the dark, narrow cleft of the Devil’s Throat—she saw it.

A pale, yellow line.

A line that had not been there when she was a child. A line that Master Coram had never seen through his six-inch spy-glass.

It was the ramp.

It was real. It was stone and timber, climbing the cliff by thirty-two switchbacks, catching the morning sun on its white limestone parapets until it looked like a thread of gold lace pinned against the grey ribs of the mountain.

The map was not inventing the road.

The map was *remembering* it.

For eighty years, while the Chancery had drawn its blank, white *Terra Vasta*—while the Assay-Master had burned every rutter that spoke of the hills—the road had been lying beneath the water, waiting for the ink to return.

The earth had not changed.

The water had not moved.

Only the men in the high tower had forgotten that the world had two gates: one by the sea, and one by the land.

"Who are you?" she said aloud, her voice carrying across the empty mere to the reed-beds, where a flock of grey teal rose with a sudden, whistling roar of wings.

A voice answered her.

It did not come from the air. It came from the foot of the bridge, thirty yards behind her on the eastern ramp.

"A traveller," the voice said, speaking in the broad, hard dialect of the high hills—a dialect that used three vowels where the coast used five, and rolled its *r*’s like gravel in a tin pail. "And one who has forgotten that the Guild does not permit an apprentice to carry her board beyond the third ditch."

Maren turned.

Her hand dropped to her iron pen in her pocket.

A man was standing on the bridge.

He was not old—perhaps thirty, perhaps thirty-five—with the broad, square shoulders of a mason and the long, thin legs of a hill-walker. He wore a coat of unbleached wool, dyed the color of dry heather with lichen-moss, its skirt tucked into the tops of high boots made of grey deer-hide.

Over his shoulder hung a surveyor’s chain of sixty-six links, made of bright, unrusted iron that clicked softly with every movement of his ribs.

In his right hand he carried an instrument she had seen only once, in a copper-plate engraving in the *Scrinium Antiquum*:

A plane-table.

It was a square board of seasoned pear-wood, two feet across, mounted on a brass ball-and-socket joint above three legs of ash-wood. Clamped to the board was a sheet of thick rag-paper, its edges held down by four brass screws shaped like acorn-cups.

And across the paper, drawn in the same intense, carbon-black ink as her own, ran the seven arches of the Sallow Causeway.

"You have the second copy," the man said.

He did not lift his staff; he did not draw a weapon. He walked slowly up the ramp, his deer-hide boots making no sound upon the limestone flags, his small, grey eyes fixing upon the Great Plat where it lay between her knees.

"Who are you?" she asked again, her fingers closing tight around the cold iron handle of her pen.

The man stopped three paces away. He set the three ash-legs of his plane-table upon the stone, leveled the board with a turn of the brass screw, and took a small brass plummet from his waistcoat pocket.

"My name is Vane," he said.

"Vane?"

"Peter Vane’s brother," he said quietly. "The boy whose practice sheet was burned in the Kiln on Tuesday."

He looked at her hands—the ink-stained fingers, the calloused joint, the thin, red knuckles split by the salt-frost.

"The Chancery burns the paper," he said, touching the edge of his own plane-table with his thumbnail. "They have burned eleven hundred sheets in my father’s lifetime. They burned the Third Survey; they burned the monastic rolls; they burned the great plat that Master Varley drew before they bought his silence with an alderman’s gown."

He looked toward the west, where the yellow line of the ramp climbed into the Devil’s Throat.

"They can burn the skin, girl," he said, his voice dropping into a register that was almost gentle. "They have two furnaces and forty cords of beech-wood. But they cannot burn the road."

"Why did it come into my chest?" she asked, the words breaking from her with the desperation of six years of silence. "Why did the ink move on *my* skins?"

Vane looked at her.

"Because you used the trimmings," he said.

"The trimmings?"

"The margins of the Great Roll," he said. "The strips that Coram trimmed from the Fourth Survey before he sent it to the capital. Do you know where that vellum came from, Maren Brand?"

She shook her head.

"It came from Harrow," Vane said. "From the abbey of St. Michael-of-the-Crags. The monks grazed their sheep on the limestone crags; they washed their skins in the mountain water that has no salt in it. The vellum remembers the hand that scraped it. It remembers the stone it grew upon."

He stepped to the parapet. He looked down into the black water of the Sallow, where the cut-waters split the sluggish current into two silver ribbons.

"When my brother was bound to the Guild," he said, "my father told him: *Watch for the girl who does not talk. Watch for the girl who gathers the offcuts from the floor.* We have been waiting two years for that sheet to be whole."

"Waiting?" she said. "For what?"

"For the link," Vane said.

He reached into his coat. He did not pull out a knife; he pulled out a small, square instrument of dark brass, shaped like a sector, its limbs engraved with degrees, minutes, and the lines of chords.

"The road is open," he said. "The people of Harrow have finished the switchbacks. The carts are loaded in the marketplace: forty tons of lead, eighty bales of white mountain wool, and three hundred stones of freestone that will not rot in the sea."

He pointed toward the town of Marrow, where the distant tower of the South Gate was just beginning to catch the morning smoke of the chimney-fires.

"They will be at the Tanners’ Ditch by sunset tomorrow," he said.

"Coram has the guns," she whispered. "The Chancery has forty armed men at the boom. They have the cannon on the South Tower; they have the Mayor’s bailiffs; they have the Charter."

Vane smiled—a thin, dry smile that showed his white teeth against his wind-burned beard.

"They have the Charter," he said. "A piece of dead calfskin with a green wax seal."

He touched the stone parapet beneath his fingers.

"We have the road," he said. "And we have the girl who knows how to draw it."

He looked down at her board.

"Roll your sheet, apprentice," he said, turning toward the west. "The sun is up. The light does not stay for the tide."

### Chapter 7

They climbed off the Sallow Causeway as the morning frost began to loosen into grease.

The low country beyond the mere was not bog, nor was it meadow. It was a vast, grey apron of detritus washed down from the Scarp through ten thousand winters: limestone shingle the size of plovers' eggs, silt that had dried into pale polygonal cakes, and thick, low mats of creeping willow whose roots were as tough as hemp rope.

Every hundred yards, Vane stooped to check a station.

He did not use a chain here. He carried a pacing-stick—a five-foot lath of straight-grained hickory tipped with brass ferrules—which he turned end over end with a flick of his wrist, never breaking his stride, counting the revolutions beneath his breath in the old hill-tongue:

*Eina, peina, para, pedder, pimp...*

"The Guild's surveyors walk like ducks," he said, not turning his head. "They set their heels first and let their bellies carry them forward. Good enough for an alderman on a gravel quay; useless on scree. Step from the ball of the foot, girl. If the stone rolls, you roll with it. If you fight the stone, you break your ankle before the third mile."

Maren walked three paces behind him.

Her clothes were drying in the thin, cold wind, but the salt-crust from the ditch had left the wool stiff as pasteboard, rubbing her groin and armpits raw. Her boots were gone; she had cut the flapping soles away with her crescent scraper at the bridge and bound her feet in strips of oil-cloth torn from the Great Plat’s outer wrapper, secured with twisted willow-twigs around her ankles.

The oil-cloth was cold, but the stone beneath was smooth.

The road was five yards wide here, raised two feet above the shale plain on a bed of heavy boulders that had been split along the bed-planes. The ditch on the northern side was four feet deep, cleared of silt, its bottom lined with flat slabs of limestone set on edge to prevent the water from undermining the bank.

"Who cleared this?" she asked, her voice raspy from the sulfurous damp of the marsh. "The ditches are clean. There is no weed in the runnels."

"The sheep-drovers," Vane said. "They have run thirty thousand head down these shoulders every autumn for forty years. They never went into the town; they brought them to the salt-creeks at the Sallow mouth and sold them to the Dutchmen off-shore. A smuggler doesn't ask for a Guild seal on his mutton."

"The Guild has cutters at the Sallow mouth."

"The Guild has two old ketches with rotten keels and six gunners who are blind with small beer by four in the afternoon," Vane spat. "A cutter cannot follow a flat-bottomed punt through the mud-gutters. The sea belongs to the King, they say; but the mud belongs to the man who knows where the bottom is hard."

He stopped at the foot of the first limestone rise.

Here the ground changed abruptly. The shingle ended against a shelf of grey, stratified rock that rose in terraces four feet high, like a flight of monumental stairs leading up toward the throat of the mountain.

The road did not climb the steps; it had been cut through them.

It was a trench, eight cubits wide, blasted out of the solid rock with black powder. The walls of the cutting were black with ancient smoke, drilled with round bore-holes where the iron jumpers had been driven to receive the charges.

On the rock face beside the cutting, someone had carved a bench-mark.

It was not the cross of the Chancery.

It was the sign she had seen on the bridge: the two hands, the brass divider, and the open plat. Beneath it were three letters, cut with a cold-chisel and filled with red cinnabar that had grown dull beneath sixty years of winter rains:

**C. L. M.**

*Collegium Limnorum Montanorum.*

The Mountain Guild of Limners.

Maren ran her fingers over the letters. The stone was cold, but it did not have the greasy, slippery feel of the coastal granite; it was dry, sharp, with a fine, granular tooth like the whetstones her father had used for his draw-knives.

"There was another guild?" she whispered.

"There was only one guild once," Vane said, leaning his hickory stick against the rock and taking a leather water-bottle from his coat. "Before the Great Concord. Before the sea-merchants bought the Chancery. The Limners of the Hill measured the valleys; the Limners of the Coast measured the sounds. They met once every three years at the chapel on the Scarp—the Chapel of St. Jude-in-the-Fells—and laid their rolls side by side to see that the headlands matched the hills."

He drank, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and offered the bottle to her.

It was not beer. It was water—so cold that it made her teeth ache, with a sharp, mineral taste of limestone and iron that felt like a knife-blade in her dry throat.

"What happened to the Chapel?" she asked.

"Coram’s grandfather burned it," Vane said simply. "In the year of the Great Frost. He brought forty bailiffs up from the harbour with torches and crowbars. They pulled down the tower, threw the bells into the ravine, and carried the rolls down to the Kiln."

He looked at her with his small, grey eyes.

"They missed the duplicate," he said.

"The duplicate?"

"The Great Plat of the Interior. The one Master Varley drew on the skins of the white sheep of St. Michael. They thought they had burned it. But his apprentice—a girl named Aldith Brand—cut the sheet into four-and-thirty panels, hid them in the wool-bales that were going down to the cooperages, and died in the work-house with her mouth shut."

Maren stared at him.

Her heart, which had been beating with the dull, regular rhythm of an exhausted beast, seemed to stop in her chest.

"Brand," she whispered.

"Your father’s great-aunt," Vane said. "Did you think you were the first cooper’s brat in Marrow who could draw sixty strokes to the inch without a ruler? Did you think that hand came out of the gutter?"

He took up his pacing-stick again.

"The blood remembers the line," he said, turning into the cutting. "Even when the brain has forgotten the name of the town."

***

They entered the **Devil’s Throat** at noon.

The ravine was a slit in the face of the Scarp, three hundred cubits deep, so narrow that in many places the limestone cliffs on either side leaned toward each other until only a strip of pale blue sky, three yards wide, was visible above.

The wind here did not blow; it sucked. It came down from the high mountain plateaus like water through a sluice, cold, dry, carrying with it the fine, white dust of the limestone quarries that settled upon Maren’s eyelashes and hair until she looked like an old woman.

Here was the engineering that the old Italian had recorded.

The road did not rest upon the floor of the gorge; the floor was a dry torrent-bed, choked with boulders the size of hay-wains that had fallen from the crags above.

The road hung upon the wall.

It was carried on three-and-thirty arches of dry limestone—not rounded Roman arches, but pointed, lancet spans of incredible boldness, springing from rock-buttress to rock-buttress across the abyss. Between the arches, the roadway was supported by massive timber brackets of seasoned chestnut, four feet square, pinned into the mountain with iron drift-bolts that had been wedged with lead.

The switchbacks were so steep that even with her pacing-stick, Maren had to lean forward until her face was within two feet of the stone.

At the seventeenth turn, they stopped.

Here, perched upon a projecting tooth of rock that hung four hundred feet above the grey shale plain, stood a stone shelter—a refuge for carters caught in the mountain squalls. It was built like a watch-tower, round, with arrow-slits commanding the ramp below and a heavy oak door bound with diagonal straps of iron.

Beside the door sat three men.

They were not soldiers. They wore the grey wool smocks of quarry-men, with broad leather belts from which dangled iron wedges, cold-chisels, and heavy hickory mallets with lead-weighted heads. Their faces were white with lime-dust, their eyes red-rimmed from the glare of the stone.

Between their knees lay three long iron bars—the jumpers used for drilling shot-holes in the rock.

When Vane’s boots sounded on the gravel of the ramp, the three men stood up together. They did not speak; they did not raise their hands in greeting.

One of them—an older man with a grey beard that had been braided into two points and tucked into his collar—stepped forward and looked at Maren.

He looked at her smock; he looked at her feet, bound in the greasy oil-cloth; he looked at the wooden tube she carried beneath her arm.

"Is this the hand?" the old man asked.

"It is the hand," Vane said. "Aldith Brand’s kin."

The old man took a small iron key from his neck-cord. He turned to the door of the refuge, unlocked it, and pushed it open.

"Come inside, girl," he said. "The Wardens have passed the South Gate."

Maren stepped through the door.

The interior was ten feet across, lit only by the narrow arrow-slits that looked down the throat of the mountain. In the center stood a stone table—a slab of white limestone resting upon four square pillars of the same material.

On the table lay four things:

An iron ink-pot, three times the size of the one in the Chancery scriptorium, filled to the lip with black gall-ink.

A case of Swedish brass pens, identical to the ones Master Coram kept in his locked desk.

A bundle of thirty-four sheets of fresh calfskin, scraped smooth as cold lard, clean of any line, mark, or letter.

And a telescope.

It was five feet long, made of brass tubes covered in green morocco leather, mounted upon a heavy wooden tripod that stood before the central slit.

The old quarry-man pointed to the brass eyepiece.

"Look," he said.

Maren set her Great Plat upon the table. She stepped to the instrument, closed her left eye, and pressed her right against the brass rim.

The world leaped toward her.

The lens was five times as clear as her green Venetian crystal. It was Dutch work—the work of the great lens-grinders of Middelburg—brought across the sea by the wool-smugglers.

The harbour of Marrow lay before her as if it were three rods away.

She could see the high green roofs of the Guildhall; she could see the smoke rising from the chimney of the Kiln; she could see the six gulls circling above the careening berths.

And she could see the road.

At the South Gate, the iron portcullis had been raised.

Coming through the archway, moving in a long, dark, segmented line across the Tanners’ Field, was a column of men.

First came twelve horsemen: the city bailiffs, wearing their red wool caps and carrying eight-foot halberds whose steel heads glittered in the sun.

Behind them rode three men in black velvet gowns: Warden Kedge, Warden Vane, and between them, sitting crooked upon a white mule with his horn spectacles tied to his ears with black tape, Master Coram.

And behind the masters came the wagon.

It was a low, four-wheeled timber carriage, drawn by six black dray-horses. On the bed of the wagon stood a square iron box, four feet high, painted with the vermilion compass of the Guild, its flue smoking with the black, oily soot of burning bituminous coal.

The Field Kiln.

The mobile furnace. The machine the Chancery used when it went into the provinces to purge the unauthorized plats of the village scriveners.

They were following the stones.

They were not searching; they were not casting about for tracks in the heather. They had the line. They were moving at a steady trot along the gravel bed, the bailiffs’ horses kicking up small clouds of white lime-dust that drifted away over the black pools of the Sough like smoke from a battery of guns.

"They have the Second Roll," Maren whispered, her eye still glued to the glass. "Coram has the Second Roll on his knees. He is checking the stations."

"He has the roll," the old quarry-man said from the shadow behind her. "But he does not have the seal."

"The seal?"

The old man reached beneath the stone table. He lifted a heavy iron box, eighteen inches square, bound with brass hoops that were green with verdigris. He did not use a key; he struck the side of the box with his hickory mallet, and the rusted lock-plate fell away onto the flags.

He lifted the lid.

Inside lay an object that made Maren’s breath stop in her throat.

It was a matrix.

A great block of cast bronze, four inches thick by six inches square, its upper face engraved in deep, sharp relief with the ancient arms of the province:

The High Scarp; the seven arches of the Sallow Causeway; the six bells of St. Michael-of-the-Crags; and across the top, supported by two eagles with extended wings, the Great Compass of the Hill.

It was the original seal of the Charter.

Not the naval seal of King Edward—the seal with the three ships and the anchor that Coram kept in his brass-bound chest—but the **Great Seal of the Concord**: the seal that established the freedom of the road and the equality of the two registries.

"The sea-merchants stole the charter," the old man said, his voice flat as the stone table. "They took the parchment to the capital and had the King’s clerks scratch out the thirty leagues of the inland. But they could not find the bronze. My grandfather carried it up the Devil’s Throat on his shoulders while the bailiffs were burning the chapel."

He pushed the bronze block toward her across the table.

"Your indenture ends today, Maren Brand," he said.

"My indenture?"

"Six years in the Chancery. You have drawn the coast until your fingers are stiff. Now draw the Masterwork."

He laid a sheet of the fresh calfskin before her.

"The bailiffs will be at the Sallow Bridge by three o'clock," he said. "They have three casks of black powder in the wagon to blow the central arch. If the bridge falls, the road is dead for another hundred years."

He dipped a brass pen into the great iron pot of ink and held it out to her, handle first.

"Draw the traverse," he said. "Draw the stations from the bridge to the town gate. If the plat is sealed with the bronze before Coram reaches the fifth mile, the Charter is renewed by ancient right. The road becomes the King’s highway, and the bailiffs cannot touch a stone."

"And if it is not?" she asked.

The old man looked through the arrow-slit at the little cloud of dust that was moving across the Great Sough, four leagues away.

"If it is not," he said, "the furnace has an iron mouth. And it is thirty miles down to the mud."

### Chapter 8

Maren Brand did not look at the telescope again.

She took her place at the stone table. Her wet clothes had ceased to matter; her split knuckles and the blood inside her oil-cloth shoe-wraps were nothing more than the minor frictions of the body, which any good apprentice learns to set aside when the pen is in the ink.

She had sixty minutes.

To draw forty leagues of country on a scale of two inches to the league—not from a master roll, not from a copper-plate impression, but from the measurements that lived in the muscles of her right shoulder and the palm of her left hand.

She took her divider. It was her own brass instrument, its points honed on an oil-stone until they could prick a line without tearing the grain of the skin.

She set the baseline:

The fifth milestone to the Devil’s Throat: seven miles, three furlongs, and four chains, bearing west-northwest by the compass-needle of the mountain, which leaned not two degrees east like the corrupt bowl in the harbour tower, but one degree thirty minutes west, true to the pole-star that hung above the limestone crags.

*Scratch.*

The brass pen took the ink.

It was not the thin, watery gall-ink of the Chancery; it was ink made of walnut-juice, copperas, and mountain soot that had been boiled three times with birch-tar. It went into the vellum with a dark, oily bite that smelled of iron and old roots.

First the Sallow Causeway:

Seven arches. She did not need to measure the spans; her shins remembered the distance between the piers. She drew the cut-waters; she drew the relief of the two hands; she drew the seven flood-runnels through the gravel beds.

Beside her, Vane stood at the arrow-slit with his plane-table on his hip, his eye fixed upon the brass barrel of the telescope.

"They have passed the third mile," he said, his voice as regular as the ticking of a clock. "The bailiffs are trotting. Coram’s mule has cast a shoe; they are leading her by the halter. The wagon is two hundred rods behind, throwing sparks from its flue."

Maren did not raise her head.

Her pen moved to the **Sallow Bottoms**:

Here she did not draw the *Lacus Mortuus* of Master Varley. She drew the true level: the three channels of running water that fed the reed-bed from the north; the gravel spit where the bottom was sound; the four turf-dikes that the monks had built to drain the upper meadow before the Concord was broken.

"Fourth mile," Vane said. "They have reached the deep water. The bailiffs are halting. They see the causeway."

Maren’s hand moved faster.

She was not drawing what she had seen that morning.

She was drawing what she had seen in the *Scrinium Antiquum*—the old Italian plat that Coram had tried to bury beneath the salt-crust of the vault.

Her memory was not a ledger; it was an illuminated roll that unrolled before her eyes with every stroke of the brass nib.

The cutting through the limestone steps: forty rods long, eight cubits wide, with its drainage-ditches on the north.

The twenty-seventh station: the benchmark **C. L. M.** on the face of the chert shelf.

The ascent of the Devil’s Throat: thirty-two switchbacks, supported by thirty-three arches of dry limestone ashlar, each course numbered in microscopic characters that no man could read without a lens.

Her hand was sweating now. The cold grease of the tallow-stone was slippery beneath her wrist, but she did not stop to wipe it.

"They are on the bridge," Vane said. His voice had lost its flat rhythm; it had tightened, sharp as an iron wire stretched to the breaking point. "Kedge is off his horse. He is looking at the relief on the parapet. The furnace-wagon has reached the eastern ramp."

He paused, his breath hissing through his teeth.

"They are lifting the powder-casks," he said. "Three casks. They are carrying them toward the central pier."

"How long?" the old quarry-man asked from the door.

"Eight minutes to lay the train," Vane said. "Five to blow. They are using sulfur-matches; I can see the blue smoke from the wagon."

Maren did not hear them.

She was at the summit.

Her pen was drawing the town of **Harrow-on-the-Hill**.

The marketplace: sixteen roods square, with its double tier of arcades.

The Church of St. Michael-of-the-Crags: the three aisles, the tall, octagonal lantern, the six bells of white bronze.

The quarries: the four White Pits, where the freestone lay in beds twelve cubits thick, clean of salt, untouched by the frost.

And finally, in the lower left-hand corner, where the Chancery’s charters carried the great vermilion stamp of the naval compass, she left a square space four inches broad.

She laid down her pen.

She had not blotted a line. She had not scraped a letter.

The map was whole. It was thirty leagues of inland, drawn with the geometric certainty of an engineer and the fierce, concentrated passion of a woman who has spent six years copying granite cliffs that never move.

"The bronze," she whispered.

Her voice was nothing more than a croak. Her chest felt as if it were bound with iron hoops; her fingers were locked into the curve of the pen-holder, so stiff that she had to pry them open with her left thumb.

The old quarry-man lifted the great bronze block.

It weighed twenty pounds. He held it over the charcoal brazier that burned in the corner of the refuge until the metal was hot enough to hiss when he touched it with a wet finger.

He carried it to the table.

He did not use ink. The ancient law of the Concord required the seal to be struck in **wax-of-the-hill**: a mixture of mutton-suet, pine-resin, and the yellow ocher of the mountain quarries that set as hard as flint when it cooled.

He laid a ball of the yellow wax, as large as a pigeon’s egg, in the center of the blank square.

"Strike," he said to Maren.

She took the heavy hickory mallet.

She held the bronze matrix with her left hand, centering the two eagles and the Great Compass over the warm wax. She raised the mallet with both hands above her right shoulder.

"They have lit the match," Vane shouted from the slit. "Kedge is running for the bank!"

Maren brought the mallet down.

*CRACK.*

The sound was like a pistol-shot inside the small, vaulted room.

The hickory head struck the bronze dead in the center. The warm wax squirted sideways beneath the pressure, filling every minute line of the engraving: the feathers of the eagles, the six bells of the church, the Latin words of the ancient title:

**CHARTA MONTIUM LIBERORUM.**

*The Charter of the Free Hills.*

Maren lifted the matrix.

The seal was perfect.

The yellow wax stood high upon the vellum, sharp as an impression in cold lead, its edges clear, its center bearing the Great Compass that pointed one degree thirty minutes west of the sea-gate.

And at that instant, from five leagues away across the level floor of the Great Sough, came the sound.

It was not a crack. It was a long, low, subterranean *BOOM* that shook the rock of the Devil’s Throat until the dust fell from the vault-joints onto Maren’s hair.

A column of black smoke, thin as an ink-stroke, rose into the cold blue sky above the Sallow Bottoms.

Vane stayed at the glass. For five seconds, the refuge was so silent that Maren could hear the grease dripping from the tallow-candle into the tin dish.

Then Vane began to laugh.

It was a strange, harsh sound—the laugh of a man who has looked for a ghost and found a wall.

"What is it?" the old quarry-man growled. "Did the arch fall?"

Vane turned from the telescope. His face was white with lime-dust, his eyes shining with a strange, fierce light that made him look like a boy.

"The arch is where it was," he said.

"The powder?"

"They laid the casks against the wrong pier," Vane said, his sides shaking with his laughter. "They laid them against the western cut-water. The stone of Harrow doesn't break for black powder, uncle. It threw the stones twenty rods into the mud, but the arch didn't even drop its mortar."

He pointed down the mountain.

"And Coram’s mule has bolted," he said. "She has run into the reed-bed with the Second Roll in her saddle-bags, and the Assay-Master is wading after her with his boots full of black water."

Maren Brand sat back upon the three-legged stool.

She looked at her map.

The ink was dry now. The yellow wax of the seal was cold, hard as a pebble of amber set into the white vellum.

She had done what no apprentice had done in a hundred and eighty years.

She had drawn the road.

She had sealed the plat.

She had broken the throat of Marrow-on-the-Bar.

### Chapter 9

At three o'clock in the afternoon, the carts began to move.

They did not come from the mountain refuge; they came from the town above—from the high, sunny saddle between the crags where **Harrow-on-the-Hill** had sat for six hundred years behind its ramparts of white flint.

There were forty of them.

Great, eight-wheeled mountain wains, their bodies built of seasoned ash-planks three inches thick, their axles of forged Spanish iron that had been greased with sheep-tallow. Each wagon was drawn by eight horses: heavy, broad-chested bays from the upland pastures, their hooves the size of soup-plates, their manes braided with red wool to keep out the mountain witches.

The first six wagons carried the lead: great, grey, boat-shaped pigs of pig-lead from the High Scarp mines, each stamped with the two eagles and the date of the smelting.

The next twelve carried the wool: four hundred bales of the fine, short-staple fleece of the white mountain sheep, bound with hemp bands and wrapped in clean linen sheets so that no mud from the road should soil the wool.

And the last twenty-two carried the stone.

Blocks of white limestone freestone, three cubits long by two cubits square, their faces dressed with the broad tooth-chisel, their corners protected by jackets of straw-rope.

Maren walked at the head of the column.

She did not wear the carter’s smock now. The women of the refuge had brought her clothes from the town: a skirt of heavy, dark blue wool, dyed with mountain woad; a sleeveless bodice of soft, oiled calfskin that laced up the front with brass eyelets; and high boots of supple deer-hide that reached to her knees, lined with sheep-fleece to keep the cold of the road from her shins.

At her waist hung her ruling pen, cleaned of its black gall-ink, resting in a sheath of stamped leather beside her brass divider.

Over her shoulder she carried the Great Plat.

It was no longer rolled; it was mounted upon an oak frame four feet square, like a battle-standard, carried by two young quarry-men whose smocks were clean and white as fresh flour.

Beside her walked Peter Vane’s brother, his plane-table folded on his back, his hickory pacing-stick turning end over end with every three strides:

*Eina, peina, para, pedder...*

The descent of the Devil’s Throat took three hours.

The iron-tired wheels of the wagons screamed against the limestone flags of the ramps, throwing off a smell of scorched wood and hot axle-grease that filled the gorge with blue haze. At every switchback, four men hung upon the rear brake-levers—great baulks of oak eight feet long that pressed against the iron tires until the sparks flew out like chaff from a threshing-floor.

Not a horse stumbled.

Not a block of stone moved upon its bed.

The road was an engineer’s road: its gradients were calculated to the third of an inch, its curves were banked with drystone masonry that threw the weight of the carriages back into the face of the mountain, its gutters carried the spring-water away from the gravel before it could freeze into rime.

They reached the floor of the Sallow Bottoms as the sun was setting behind the Scarp.

The plain was purple now—the deep, cold purple of dead heather and frozen mud.

Ahead of them, two leagues away across the level moss, lay the bridge.

The smoke of the powder-blast had blown away toward the sea.

Beside the western ramp of the bridge, sitting in the mud with their horses’ bridles over their arms, were the bailiffs.

They were no longer a column.

Their red caps were soaked with rain; their boots were caked with black peat-slime; their eight-foot halberds were leaning against the wheel of the furnace-wagon like bean-poles in a kitchen garden.

The furnace was cold.

The blast had cracked the brick lining of the flue; the anthracite coal had run out into the ditch in a black heap, and the furnace-boy was sitting upon an empty powder-cask, crying with the cold and blowing upon his red fingers.

As the head of the wagon-train reached the eastern approach, Warden Kedge stepped out onto the stones.

He had lost his beaver hat in the reed-bed; his bald head was red, mottled with the frost, his green velvet gown torn at the knee where he had caught it on a willow-snag. In his right hand he carried an iron pistol with a wheel-lock, but the hammer was down and the powder in the pan was yellow mud.

Beside him stood Master Coram.

The old man looked smaller than he had in the scriptorium. He was wrapped in three horse-cloths that smelled of wet wool and stable-dung, his horn spectacles hanging from his neck by their black tape, his eyes large, watery, and dull as two oysters.

Kedge raised his hand.

"Halt," he croaked.

His voice was like a crow’s in a corn-field.

The leading horses did not halt. The carter—a man six feet tall with a beard like a gorse-bush—cracked his eight-foot whip of braided rawhide three inches above the ears of the lead bay, and the great wagon rolled forward onto the ramp with a thunder of iron wheels that made the bridge-stones ring like bells.

"Halt, in the King’s name!" Kedge shouted, stepping back before the horses’ chests. "By the Charter of King Edward—by the authority of the Chancery of the Sea-Mark—this road is closed! It is void! It has no seal!"

Maren Brand stepped between the two lead bays.

She raised her hand. The carter checked his team, the four bays leaning back into their breeching with a rattle of trace-chains that died away into the evening mist.

She did not speak to Kedge.

She walked to the white mule where Master Coram sat.

She reached behind her. The two young quarry-men lowered the great oak frame until the Great Plat lay before the old man’s knees, four feet broad, its vellum white and clear in the blue twilight.

Coram did not look at Kedge. He did not look at the bailiffs.

He reached into his waistcoat with a shaking hand, found his horn-rimmed spectacles, wiped them upon the edge of his wet horse-cloth, and set them upon his nose.

He leaned down from his saddle.

His fingers—brown-spotted, crooked with the gout—traced the line:

The Tanners’ Ditch.

The twenty-four chain-dots.

The seven arches of the Sallow Causeway.

The cutting through the limestone terraces.

The thirty-two switchbacks of the Devil’s Throat.

And the town: the marketplace, the church with its six bells, the four White Pits of freestone.

And then his hand touched the seal.

The yellow wax was hard as iron now, cold to the skin, bearing the two eagles and the Great Compass of the Hill.

The old man’s mouth worked silently for three seconds. A drop of moisture fell from his red nose onto the margin of the vellum, but he did not wipe it away.

"It is the Concord," he whispered.

"The Concord was burned!" Kedge screamed, coming behind the mule and striking the pommel of his saddle with his fist. "The Concord was repealed under the Great Seal! It has no standing in the Court of Assistants! Break the plat, you fool! Break it across your knees!"

Coram looked up from his spectacles.

His eyes were no longer dull. They had that strange, clear, administrative chill that Maren had seen on the day he had condemned Peter Vane’s practice sheet to the fire.

"The Concord was not repealed, Warden," the old man said, his voice flat as the stone bridge. "The Concord was *suspended* until a Free Sister should present the survey of the Upper Reach under the seal of the Hill."

He looked at Maren’s face—the dark hair cut blunt at her shoulders, the cold, steady eyes that did not blink before his own, the ink-callus on the second joint of her right hand.

"She has drawn the stations," he said.

"She is an apprentice!" Kedge roared. "She has no standing! She is an unfree servant of the Chancery!"

"Her indenture ended at the stroke of twelve," Coram said softly. "And she has laid the Masterwork before the Master of the Rolls."

He climbed down from his mule.

His wool-slippered feet hit the limestone flags of the causeway with a dull, wet slap. He walked to the head of the first wagon, where the Great Plat stood upon its frame.

He reached into his pocket.

He did not take out an iron key. He did not take out a scraper.

He took out the silver compass of the Chancery—the compass that had hung from his own collar for fifty years, its legs engraved with the three ships of King Edward and the scales of the Mayor.

He unhooked the silver chain.

He turned to Maren Brand.

He did not drop to his knees; he was too old, too proud, and too cold for theater.

He hung the silver compass about her neck, over her blue wool skirt and her soft calfskin bodice, until the heavy metal rested against the ivory handle of her ruling pen.

"The survey is complete," Master Coram said, loud enough for the carters and the bailiffs to hear across the water of the Sallow. "Enter the town, Sister. The light has stayed for the work."

### Chapter 10

The carts entered the town of Marrow-on-the-Bar at midnight.

They did not go through the South Gate.

The South Gate was too low; its pointed arch of river-stone had been built to keep out wagons, leaving only a six-foot postern for horsemen and foot-passengers who had paid their two groats to the customs reeve.

The quarry-men did not ask for the gate to be opened.

The first four wagons carried thirty men with twelve-pound sledges and twenty iron crowbars.

They did not strike the iron portcullis; they struck the wall.

The town wall of Marrow—that great rampart of river-granite that had stood for two hundred years to guard the customs of the merchant aldermen—was built of small, round stones gathered from the beach, held together with lime-mortar that had rotted into yellow sand beneath the salt-fogs.

It was not the stone of Harrow.

At the twentieth blow of the sledges, a seam opened between two towers fifty yards to the west of the gate.

A crack three fingers wide ran from the parapet to the ditch; a heap of loose rubble fell outward into the sour mud of the Tanners’ Field; and then, with a long, tearing groan like an old ship breaking her back on the Skiffs, a section of the wall forty cubits wide fell into the ditch.

The dust rose in a white cloud through the torchlight, smelling of ancient mortar and dry bone.

Through the breach came the horses.

The forty wains rolled into the lane called the Gut.

The iron-tired wheels were three inches wider than the space between the house-jetties; as the first wagon passed, its hubs tore the corner-posts from the cooper’s shop where Maren had lived, bringing the wood-shavings and the old hoop-iron down into the street with a roar that woke every soul from the Fish-Gate to the Cliff.

The people came out in their shirts.

They carried tallow-candles; they carried lanterns of horn; they carried iron spits and fish-knives from the curing houses.

They saw the lead.

They saw the white mountain wool, clean and dry in its linen wrappers.

And they saw the stone.

The great blocks of white freestone, three cubits long, that did not scale in the frost; the stone that had built the cathedrals of the interior before the sea-gate was closed; the stone that would build a dry dock at the South Quay where three three-masters could lie together without touching the mud.

Behind the wagons walked Maren Brand.

She walked in the middle of the street, the silver compass of the Chancery swinging against her ribs, the Great Plat carried before her on its oak standard like the banner of an emperor.

Beside her walked Peter Vane’s brother, his plane-table on his hip, and behind them came the eight apprentices of the South Tower, their black smocks unbuttoned, their ink-stones in their hands.

They did not go to the Mayor’s house.

They did not go to the Council Chamber on the Cliff.

They walked to the courtyard of the Guildhall of Coastwise Limners and Measurers.

The iron gates were open.

The Kiln was cold.

In the center of the courtyard, where the condemned sheets had burned every Tuesday and Friday for two centuries, stood the Assay-Master, old Hallowell, his iron tongs dangling from his single hand, his watery blue eyes wide beneath his bristle of grey eyebrow.

He did not lift his tongs.

Maren walked to the mouth of the furnace.

She did not carry her Great Plat to the draught-door.

She reached into her bodice. She pulled out the three practice sheets of the North Sand that Master Coram had given her to copy that morning—the three sheets that showed the coast without the land; the sheets that showed the sea as a master and the world as an empty ditch.

She dropped them into the cold ash.

"Let them be unmade," she said.

Her voice was not loud, but it carried through the round river-pebbles of the courtyard, through the open arches of the scriptorium, through the high lancet windows where the gulls were wheeling in the dawn.

No one answered.

No apprentice said the words.

For the first time in one hundred and eighty-four years, the Chancery was silent.

Maren turned her back upon the furnace.

She walked to the great oak table beneath the covered walk where the Masters sat to seal the sea-cards for the fleet.

She laid the Great Plat upon the oak.

She took her brass ruling pen from its sheath. She took the pewter dropper that she had dropped upon the flags when she was an eighteen-year-old girl, and she measured three drops of clean mountain water into her ink-stone.

She ground the black soot of the hills.

She did not draw a coastline.

She set her pen against the eastern margin of the sheet—the point where the river Marrow entered the salt-basin of the harbour.

She drew two parallel lines.

She drew them through the South Gate; she drew them through the Fish-Market; she drew them along the South Quay, straight to the edge of the deep water where the three-masters lay at their moorings.

And between the lines, with six delicate, rhythmic strokes of her crow-quill, she laid down the chain-dots:

Four dots to the rood.

The road did not stop at the hills.

The road did not stop at the gate.

The road was going to the sea.

### Chapter 11

By the fourth week after the breaking of the wall, the scriptorium smelled of tallow and river-gravel rather than elderberries and dried ink.

The high lancets were kept open, despite the November rain that drifted in across the sills, because seventy men were coming and going up the spiral stairs of the South Tower every day, their boots heavy with the yellow marl of the Sallow Basin. They brought no rolls to be engrossed; they brought the tally-sticks of the carters, the timber-ledgers of the wheelwrights, and the great brass levels that had been dragged out of the cellars of the *Scrinium Antiquum* to be cleaned of their eighty years of verdigris.

Master Coram did not sit upon his three-legged platform.

The platform had been removed to make room for three drafting boards that had been bolted together with iron clamps to form a table twelve feet long by six feet wide.

Across this table lay the **Great Survey of the Concord**: eighty-two skins of royal calfskin, stitched together with three-ply waxed hemp, upon which Maren Brand was drawing the new province.

It was not a sea-card; it was not a land-plat.

It was both.

On the left-hand margin, sixty miles inland, rose the limestone crags of Harrow, their heights marked in cubits above the mean spring-tide at the river mouth. On the right-hand margin ran the forty leagues of salt water, with every reef and every drying ledge laid down in its true bearing from the mountain peaks.

And between them, running like an iron spine through the center of the skin, was the road.

"The grade at the fifth mile is too steep," Coram said.

He was standing at Maren’s elbow, wearing his old horn spectacles, his sleeve pinned up to his shoulder with a brass needle so that his grey wool cuff would not trail through the wet ink. He was holding an ivory sector across the switchbacks of the Devil’s Throat, his thumb checking the division of the tangents.

"The wains carry sixty sacks of wool, Sister," the old man said, his voice as dry and sharp as it had been when he was her master. "A team of eight bays cannot hold sixty sacks on an incline of one in nine without burning the brake-baulks. You must give them three roods more of level at the seventeenth turn, or the wheels will take the horses over the parapet before they reach the bridge."

"The stone is soft there, Master," she answered, her pen moving with that clean, unhesitating stroke that never left a pool at the turn. "The chert ends at the benchmark. If we cut three roods deeper into the cliff, we hit the blue shale that weeps in the frost."

Coram squinted through his horn rims, his head cocked like an old crow looking down an ash-pipe.

"Then throw an arch," he said. "A drystone arch from the seventeenth turn across to the Eagle’s Crag. Let the road leap the shale. The limestone of Harrow will carry twenty tons upon a twenty-foot span if the voussoirs are dressed with the tooth."

Maren paused. She looked up from the vellum.

"An arch across the throat?" she asked.

"Why not?" Coram muttered, taking a pinch of dried elderberries from his waistcoat and chewing with a dry, clicking sound of his gums. "The monks threw an arch across the Rhine at Basel four hundred years ago, and the Rhine is forty cubits wider than that slit in the limestone. If a limner can draw it, a mason can lay it. Draw the span, Maren. Draw it with two courses of ashlar and an iron tie-rod through the crown. The Guild will pay for the timbering."

He walked away to the second bench, where three young boys—Peter Vane among them, his smock clean and his ink-stone freshly pounced—were copying the road-stations for the parish bailiffs.

Maren looked at her board.

She dipped her ruling pen into the walnut-ink.

She set the point against the seventeenth turn.

Her hand did not tremble.

Six years of copying coastlines that never moved had given her something that the hill-draughtsmen had never possessed: an absolute, terrifying respect for the line that does not lie.

A coastline was granite; it could not be reasoned with; it would not alter its footing because an apprentice had grown weary or an alderman wanted three fathoms more of water for his ship. It was what it was: hard, cold, and eternal.

And now, as she drew the great arch across the Devil’s Throat—forty feet of white stone hanging four hundred cubits above the blue mist of the plain—she felt that same cold, granite certainty in the center of her palms.

The road was not an escape from the measure.

The road was the measure made visible.

It was the geometry of the hill meeting the geometry of the sea, bound together by thirty leagues of dressed stone and sixty thousand links of iron chain.

Through the high, open lancets came the sound of the city.

It was no longer the old sound—the dull, muffled whistle of the river-boom swinging shut at nine, the crying of the gulls over the rotting offal of the fish-flakes, the drunken cursing of the pilots in the tap-rooms of the Gut.

It was the sound of iron.

Down at the South Quay, sixty masons from Harrow were building the new wharf. Their heavy steel mallets were striking the limestone blocks with a clear, musical *tink-tink-tink* that sounded across the water like the bells of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

Through the breach in the town wall, where the rubble had been cleared into three great ramps of gravel, four eight-horse wains were rolling down toward the river, their iron-tired wheels rumbling upon the new stones of the road.

They carried the lead.

They carried the wool.

And on the first wagon, sitting upon a bale of fleece with her legs dangling over the tail-board, was her sister Clara, singing a mountain song about an apprentice who went out to measure the snow and came back with three valleys in her pocket.

Maren Brand dipped her crow-quill into the red cinnabar.

She turned to the corner of the Great Plat—the corner where the road reached the salt water of the harbour basin.

She did not draw a King’s head. She did not draw an anchor.

She drew a small, square milestone, four cubits high, standing upon the granite pier-head where the deep water met the shore.

And upon the face of the stone, written in microscopic letters that would stand until the vellum rotted into dust, she laid down the last sounding:

**VIATOR.**

**HIC TERMINATUR MARE.**

*Traveler.*

*Here ends the sea.*

**HIC INCIPIT VIA.**

*Here begins the road.*
### Chapter 12

The Mayor of Marrow-on-the-Bar did not possess an ivory rule, but he possessed an iron scale that had been stamped by the Exchequer in the thirty-first year of the late King. It hung from an ash-beam above his desk in the Council House on the Cliff, weighted at the lower end with three pounds of lead to keep it vertical against the draft that came up through the floor from the salt-cellars below.

When the Mayor, whose name was Master Gervaise Pell, wished to signify that an alderman was speaking beyond his charter, he would reach out with his brass-headed cane and strike the lead weight.

*Tung.*

The note was low, sour, and lingered in the damp lime-plaster of the room long after the speaker had shut his teeth.

"The hole in the wall," Pell said, his voice as dry as dried haddock, "is forty-two yards wide. The tanners' apprentices are driving hogs through it to graze on the sea-kale at low water. Two casks of French brandy—unbroached, unmeasured, and untaxed—were found this morning in the shavings-bin behind the cooperage in the Gut. The customs reeve at the South Gate has taken to his bed with a palsy of the throat, and the town watch refuses to patrol beyond the ditch because they say the quarry-men from the hills have knives that can cut an eight-inch spruce baulk at a single stroke."

He struck the lead weight.

*Tung.*

Across the long table of Baltic pine sat the four Wardens of the Sea-Mark, three aldermen of the Salt-Staple, and the Dean of the Guild of Coopers and Trimmers.

Warden Kedge was not among them. Kedge had spent three weeks in his house on the North Cliff with his legs wrapped in flannels soaked in vinegar, his ears still deafened by the powder-blast at the bridge. His white mule had been returned to his stable by a peat-cutter from the Sallow, who had demanded—and received—four silver groats for salvage and three bags of oats for the beast’s keep.

In Kedge’s seat sat Master Coram.

The old man had not brought his horse-cloths today. He wore his gown of formal black camlet, its sleeves edged with the squirrel-fur of a Senior Assistant, though the skin was bald at the cuffs where forty years of vellum had worn the hair away. Beside his ink-horn lay a clean sheet of Dutch rag-paper, upon which he had drawn nothing more than six concentric circles in cinnabar to test the temper of a new steel divider.

"The hole," Coram said without looking up from his circles, "is not an unauthorized breach. It is the West Gate."

"There is no West Gate," Pell said, his small eyes blinking behind pouches of yellow fat. "The Charter mentions four gates: the Water Gate, the North Gate, the South Gate, and the Postern of the Grey Friars. A fifth gate is an innovation. An innovation against the peace of our Lord the King."

"The Charter of Edward mentions four gates," Coram replied, his voice rising with that thin, dry authority that had terrified thirty generations of apprentice limners. "The Charter of Edward was confirmed in the year five hundred and forty. But the **Charter of the Concord**—which was enrolled in the Exchequer three years before the first stone of your Council House was dug from the beach—names five. The fifth is the *Porta Montium*. The Gate of the Hills. It was closed by the Council of Assistants in the year of the Great Frost, because the road was drowned by the breaking of the dykes. The road is no longer drowned. The gate is no longer closed."

"And the duties?" Pell leaned forward, his rings clicking against the pine timber. "Who takes the wharfage on the lead? Who takes the forty-second groat on the mountain wool? By the law of the Bar, every fleece that enters this river pays two pence to the maintenance of the boom and three pence to the harbour lights."

Coram looked at the Dean of the Coopers.

The Dean was an old man named Hobb, whose father had been Maren’s father’s master. He had spent his morning down at the South Quay, measuring the timber in the forty wains with a notched hazel-rod.

"The wool does not go into the river," Hobb said, his voice thick with the smoke of his own pitch-kettles. "It goes into the looms. There are eighty weavers in the back-lanes who haven't had six fleeces of long-staple on their beams since Michaelmas. They’ve been living on dry peas and small beer while the Dutch galliots carried the wool-packs out of the western bays to be woven in Ghent. If the wool comes down the road, the cloth is fulled in Marrow. If the cloth is fulled in Marrow, my boys sell forty tuns of tallow to the finishing-mills every quarter."

He looked at Pell with the hard, watery stare of an artisan who has lived sixty years within six feet of an axe.

"You can take your forty-second groat from the weavers' backs, Master Mayor," Hobb said. "If you can find five bailiffs who are willing to walk down the Gut with their staves when the shifts change."

Pell’s hand hovered above his brass-headed cane, but he did not strike the lead.

The Council room was cold. The fire in the hearth was made of sea-coal that burned with a sluggish, yellow smoke, fighting the draft from the bay.

"And the girl?" Pell asked, his eyes turning back to Coram. "The apprentice who carries the bronze seal about her neck like a pagan amulet? Kedge says she has stolen the rolls. He says she has twenty skins of royal vellum in an oak chest that were never paid for in the quarterly audit."

Coram closed his divider with a small, sharp snap.

"She has stolen nothing," the old man said. "She has presented her Masterwork. The Court of Assistants sat on Monday. There were five Masters present: myself, Master Hallowell, the two searchers from the North Reach, and the Limner of the Mint from the capital who came down on the northern packet."

"And the verdict?"

"The plat was approved," Coram said. "Signed with the white wax, sealed with the Great Compass, and entered in the Registry of the Free Sisters under the number one hundred and forty-two."

He picked up his paper of circles, folded it twice, and tucked it into his sleeve.

"She is no longer an apprentice, Master Mayor. She is the Surveyor of the Road. If any officer of yours lays a hand upon her chain, her staff, or her board, he does not answer to the bailiffs of the Bar. He answers to the Court of the Exchequer under the Great Seal of the Concord."

He rose from his chair, his old joints cracking like dry sticks beneath his black camlet gown.

"And I would advise you," he added, turning at the door of the chamber, "to have the tanners' ditch cleaned before Friday. The Commissioners of the King’s Highway are riding down from the high passes. They have six clerks with them, three surveyors from the Ordnance, and two wagons of iron measuring-chains that have been tested against the standard yard in the Tower of London."

He paused, his horn spectacles catching the cold light from the salt-flats.

"They do not care for your forty-second groat, Master Pell. They care whether a wagon of the King’s artillery can reach the sea in three days without drowning its horses in the mud."

The survey of the **Second Station** began at the bridge where the black powder had failed.

It was three miles out into the Great Sough. The frost had broken, replaced by that low, grey, weeping weather that turns the northern peat into a sponge that breathes water whenever the foot touches the crust.

Maren stood upon the central arch of the causeway.

She wore a coat of thick grey frieze, short in the skirts to clear the mud, with sleeves that buttoned tight to the wrist so that no drop of rain could run down her forearm onto the paper. Across her breast hung the silver compass of the Chancery, swinging against a small brass box that held her drying-sand, her pencils of French black-lead, and her ink-pellets.

Beside her, Peter Vane and two boys from the lower benches of the South Tower were holding the chain.

It was a Gunter’s chain of four poles—sixty-six feet, divided into one hundred links of hardened iron wire, with a brass ring at every tenth link and a triangular brass tag at the center.

"Straighten the link, Peter," she called down into the reed-bed.

The boy was up to his knees in the black water, his black wool smock tied around his middle with a piece of tarred cod-line. In his left hand he held an iron marking-pin, three feet long, with a loop of red flannel through the ring.

"The mud is four feet deep here, Sister," the boy shouted back, his teeth chattering in the grey mist. "The pin won't stand. It sinks by its own weight."

"Then find the timber," she said.

She stepped to the parapet, her plane-table balanced upon its three ash legs beside the stone relief of the two hands.

"The monks didn't drive their pins into the mud," she said. "The causeway has a timber skirt. Three baulks of black oak, two feet square, pinned into the peat with hazel-piles along the northern footing. Walk two paces to your right, boy. Feel with your boots."

The boy took two splashing strides through the brown water, his arms outspread for balance. His wooden-soled shoe struck something hard beneath the silt with a hollow *clunk*.

"Here!" he cried.

"Drive the pin," she said.

She looked down the brass sight of her alidade—a twelve-inch ruler of heavy Swedish brass, fitted with two vertical slits through which she could align the marking-pin with the distant tower of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

The hair in the sight cut the red flannel dead in the center.

She dipped her pen into the small horn at her belt.

On her board lay the **Working Roll**: twelve panels of third-grade vellum, mounted on a roller of seasoned box-wood that she could advance with a brass key as the stations moved west.

She did not draw the ornaments now. No laurel leaves, no sea-monsters, no gilded compass-roses with twenty-four rays.

This was the survey of record.

Every line was accompanied by its numbers: the bearing in degrees and minutes; the distance in chains and links; the level in feet and inches above the low-water mark of the harbour spring-tide; and the nature of the bottom:

*Statio IV. Ad pontem Sallow. Fundamentum quercinum sub limo. Aquae altitudo tres pedes sex digiti.*

Station Four. At the Sallow Bridge. Oak foundation beneath the mud. Depth of water three feet six inches.

Beside her, sitting upon an upturned lime-cask with his plane-table on his knees, was Vane.

He had been out since four in the morning, running the levels from the limestone cutting down to the mere with his brass water-level—a four-foot tube of tin filled with spirits of wine, with a glass phial at each end through which he could sight a target against the horizon.

"The water is falling," he said, not looking up from his calculations.

"Falling?" Maren asked, her pen pausing above the vellum.

"Two inches since yesterday noon," he said. "The current through the third arch is running at two knots toward the sea. The old sluices at the Sallow mouth must be clear."

Maren looked eastward, toward the low, invisible line of the coast.

The river Sallow did not enter the sea through a clean mouth; it lost itself in six leagues of salt-marshes—the **Great Weirs**—where the fishermen built their wicker eel-traps and the salt-boilers had their shallow clay pans.

In the old days, before the Charter, the river had been kept to a single channel by two great stone dykes: the **King’s Bank** and the **Abbot’s Dike**. But when the Chancery closed the road, the dykes had been neglected; the stones had been carried away by the boatmen to build ballast-wharves in the town, and the river had broken out across forty square miles of low pasture, turning the valley into the quaking wilderness of the Sough.

"If the water falls three feet," Vane said, his pencil scratching upon his coarse grey paper, "the gravel bed will dry from the fourth milestone to the seventh. We won't need the timber fascines. We can lay the limestone ballast straight upon the old flint crust."

He looked at her with his sharp, grey eyes.

"Who opened the gates?" he asked.

"Coram," she said softly.

"Coram? The old dog from the Chancery?"

"He has the keys," she said. "The keys to the sluice-houses at the Salt-Cellar. They were kept in the iron chest beneath his desk, in a leather bag marked *Dead Papers*. He gave them to the Dean of the Coopers on Sunday night."

Vane gave that low, whistling chuckle that was his only expression of wonder.

"The old man has teeth yet," he murmured. "If the water goes out of the Sough, Pell will have to walk his bailiffs through four leagues of drying clay to reach the bridge. A horse cannot trot in clay, Sister. It pulls the shoes off their hooves like nails out of an old box."

He looked westward, where the white line of the Scarp was just beginning to show through the lifting mizzle like the edge of a clean tooth.

"The wains are coming down again," he said. "Listen."

Maren laid her pen across the brass ruler.

The wind had shifted into the south-west, coming off the high heather plateaus with a sweet, resinous smell of birch-smoke and cold turf.

And with the wind came the sound.

It was not one wagon.

It was a column.

The iron tires were not screaming today; they were rolling with a steady, rhythmic, thunderous drone that seemed to come from the very stone of the mountain. A sound like the sea breaking on the Skiffs, but deeper, heavier, without the hiss of the salt-foam.

The sound of sixty wains carrying the winter trade of three valleys down to the salt-gate.

And high above the rumble of the wheels, clear as the whistle of a curlew across the mere, came the bells.

The six bells of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

They were ringing the *Major Concord*: the old mountain peal that had not been heard upon the coast since the year of the Great Frost.

Six notes of white bronze, tumbling over one another in a great, descending cascade of silver sound that shook the water of the Sallow reed-beds until the wild geese rose in their thousands, circling into the grey sky like handfuls of black soot thrown into the wind.

Peter Vane stood in the black water, his marking-pin held high above his head, the red flannel streaming in the breeze.

He did not look at his chain; he did not look at the mud.

He looked up at the great white cliffs where the road hung upon the rock like a ribbon of sun-lit linen, his mouth open, his boy’s face radiant with a joy that had no words in the Chancery’s Latin.

"Draw the line, Sister!" he shouted to the bridge. "Draw it to the deep water! The whole world is coming down behind the team!"

Maren Brand picked up her pen.

She did not wait for the ink to dry upon the station.

She turned the box-wood roller with her brass key, exposing a fresh, white square of the mountain calfskin.

And there, beneath the relief of the two hands and the open plat, she set her baseline for the fifth mile:

Straight.

True.

Without an end.

### Chapter 13

On the second day of December, the frost returned with the teeth of a wolf.

It came not from the sea, but from the north-west, over the vast, trackless limestone plateaus that lay behind the Scarp. It struck the Sallow Basin in a single night; by dawn, the black water of the reed-beds was covered with two inches of clear, greenish ice that rang like an anvil under the iron-shod poles of the ditchers.

In the Chancery scriptorium, the ink froze in the horn pots.

The boys had to keep their ink-stones upon small iron plates heated with charcoal embers, blowing upon their fingers every five minutes to keep the joints from locking into claws.

Maren did not work in the scriptorium.

She had set up her table in the **Gate-House of the Breach**—the square timber lodge that the carpenters had built over the forty-yard gap in the town wall where the wagons entered from the west.

It was a rough place, smelling of green oak-shavings, horse-sweat, and the bitter smoke of the turf-braziers that burned in the four corners to keep the frost from the vellum. Outside the door, three hundred men were at work with picks and levers, cutting away the old rubble foundation of the wall to lay the granite ramps down to the gravel of the street.

A man was sitting upon a timber baulk near the door, his boots wrapped in straw-bands, a short clay pipe between his teeth.

It was not a surveyor; it was **Master Gault**, the cooper in whose attic she had lived for six years.

He had brought her breakfast in a tin pail: three hot rye-cakes soaked in bacon-fat and a jug of warm small-beer spiced with ginger.

"The aldermen are selling their shares," Gault said, his voice rumbling in his broad, barrel-shaped chest like beer in an empty vat. "Pell sold his half of the salt-wharf to an ironmonger from the North Reach on Tuesday. Took thirty silver pounds less than he asked at Michaelmas."

Maren did not look up from her drafting board. She was laying down the drainage-cuts for the third mile, her ruling pen moving with a tiny, rhythmic *tic-tic-tic* as she notched the stone culverts into the margin.

"Why did he sell?" she asked.

"Because the Dutch galliots are not coming into the river," Gault said. He spat into the turf-ashes, watching the saliva hiss and vanish. "They’re anchoring off the Sallow mouth, in the deep water behind the Shingles. The mountain men have built two timber jetties out of the Abbot’s Dike—forty rods of seasoned pine, with six feet of water at dead neap. The wains go straight to the ship’s side. A bale of wool goes out of the cart into the hold without paying two groats to the town weigh-master."

He looked at her with his small, brown eyes, which were almost lost behind the wrinkles of his salt-burned face.

"My brothers have taken twenty tons of oak-staves down to the new jetties," he added. "The Dutchmen are paying in Spanish silver. Real silver, girl. Not the clipped copper groats the Mayor gives us for the town-tallow."

He reached into his leather pouch, pulled out a heavy coin the size of a plum, and laid it upon the corner of her board.

It was a piece-of-eight: struck in Mexico, its edges milled with small leaves, its silver white and cold as the frost on the window-pane.

Maren looked at the coin.

She did not touch it.

"The King's Commissioners come on Friday, Master Gault."

"Let them come," the cooper growled. "They won't come to hang the carters. A Commissioner doesn't hang forty tons of lead when the fleet at Portsmouth needs shot for the French war. They’ll look at the road; they’ll look at the ditch; they’ll look at that paper under your thumb. And then they’ll sit in Pell’s parlour, drink his sack, and tell him that by the King’s pleasure the road is now the **High Way of the Concord**, and that the town had better spend its silver on paving the Gut before the artillery wagons break the cellar-beams."

The door of the lodge swung open with a screech of iron hinges.

A boy stood in the opening, his black smock white with snow that had begun to drift across the Tanners' Field.

It was Peter Vane.

"Sister Maren," the boy said, his breath coming in short, white puffs. "Master Coram asks for you in the Vault."

"The Vault?"

"The *Scrinium Antiquum*," the boy whispered, his eyes wide. "He has had the great oak chest opened. The one with the thirteen iron straps."

The Great Vault was lit by eight torches of pine-tar set into iron rings in the granite pillars.

The smoke was thick, black, smelling of turpentine, drifting along the low stone ribs of the vaulting until it escaped through the grated slits into the river-fog.

The great chest stood open.

Its seven padlocks lay upon the flagged floor, their iron tongues broken by the cold-chisel; two blacksmiths from the harbour slips were standing by the wall with their sledges between their knees, their leather aprons greasy with the grease of the bolts.

Beside the chest stood Master Coram.

He was alone. He had not called the Assay-Master; he had not called the Wardens.

On the floor beside his feet lay the **Charter of Edward**: seven skins of stout vellum, bound with green silk, its edges decorated with the arms of the forty-two merchant syndicates that had bought the monopoly of the coast in the year five hundred and forty.

Coram had not burned it.

He had laid his two-foot iron rule across the center of the first skin—across the famous Section Four, Subsection Nine, that gave the Guild the sole right to keep the inland silent.

He held a knife in his hand.

It was not a scraper; it was a heavy shoemaker’s paring knife, its blade three inches broad, ground to a razor edge on an oil-stone.

When Maren’s boots sounded on the stone steps, the old man did not look up.

"Come here, Sister," he said.

His voice was very quiet—not the voice of a master in the scriptorium, but the voice of an old priest who has outlived his church.

Maren walked down the center aisle, between the rows of oak presses where the dead rolls lay in their white dust.

She stood beside the chest.

"Read it," Coram said, pointing the tip of his knife at the fourth skin.

Maren looked down at the dark, cramped engrossing of the royal clerk:

*...And whereas the said Fellowship of the Coastwise Mark have by their great labor, hazard, and expense, discovered and laid down the true soundings of the said Bight, and have erected beacons upon the Skiffs for the preservation of the King's ships: It is hereby granted that no person, of what estate or degree soever, shall make, draw, or publish any description of the country lying between the sea and the High Scarp, save only such as shall be approved by the Court of Assistants...*

Coram did not wait for her to finish.

He set the point of his knife against the vellum, just beneath the words *High Scarp*.

He drew the blade across the skin.

*Rrrrip.*

The sound was like the tearing of an old sail in a gale.

The vellum parted along the line of the iron rule. He did not cut the words away; he cut the **blank space**.

The five inches of white parchment that had represented the Great Sough for one hundred and eighty-four years.

He lifted the strip of vellum. It was four feet long by five inches broad, clean, white, untouched by a single stroke of ink.

He turned to the charcoal brazier that burned beside the pillar.

He did not call the apprentices; he did not say the words of the unmaking.

He dropped the strip into the red coals.

The dry skin curled like a dry leaf. A small, clear, blue flame leaped along its edge; a smell of scorched leather rose into the pine-smoke, and the white space was gone.

"There," the old man said, his shoulders dropping as if a pack of lead had been lifted from his neck. "Now the skin is honest."

He looked into the iron chest.

At the bottom lay an object that had not seen the sun since the year of the Great Frost:

A roll of white silk, eight feet long, backed with Dutch linen, wrapped in an oiled calfskin that had been sealed with seventy-two small drops of red wax.

"Take it," Coram said.

"What is it, Master?"

"The **Roll of the Three Valleys**," he said. "The survey that Master Varley drew before they made him Master of the Rolls. It contains the levels of the high mountain streams: the waters that feed the Sallow from the western crags."

He took her right hand in his own—his old fingers cold, dry, spotted with the yellow stains of the nitric acid he used for testing his gold leaf.

"The snows will come in January, Maren," he said, looking straight into her eyes with his pale, watery gaze. "Six feet of snow on the High Scarp. And when the thaw comes in March, twelve million tons of water will come down the Devil’s Throat in forty-eight hours."

He laid the silk roll in her arms.

"If the road is to stand—if the arches at the bridge are not to be swept into the reed-beds like twigs—you must open the mountain channels. You must cut the dykes at the high levels, so the flood will run north into the great limestone sink-holes instead of drowning the valley."

He looked at the iron rule upon the floor.

"A road is not stone, girl," he said softly. "A road is water. If you can govern the water, the stone will take care of itself. If you cannot govern the water, your seventy wains will be sixty cubits deep in mud before the swallows come back to the cliffs."

He turned away from her, his slippers scuffing slowly toward the stairs.

"Go up to the hills," he said. "The road in the town is finished. The work is at the summit."

### Chapter 14

The journey to the summit of the Scarp took three days through the snow.

The wagons had ceased to roll; their wheels had been unhung and greased in the yards at Harrow, their bodies mounted upon broad timber runners of split ash, shod with hoop-iron to slide across the frozen crust of the road.

Maren rode in the first sledge, beside Vane.

The team was four great white oxen from the high pastures, their breath rising like steam from two boiling kettles, their broad foreheads protected by plates of boiled leather to turn the mountain wind.

Behind them came twelve sledges carrying the winter provisions of the hill-town: salt-herring from Marrow; five tons of Newcastle coal for the forge-fires; three hundred barrels of dry flour; and forty coils of two-inch hemp cable for the stone-cranes.

The road was an avenue of white crystal.

The snow had filled the cuttings five feet deep, but the carters had driven a snow-plough—a great triangular wedge of three-inch oak planks weighted with twenty tons of limestone—ahead of the column, throwing the drift into two high, glittering walls that rose eight feet above the runners on either side.

Between the walls, the gravel was hard as iron, frozen into a pavement that gave off a high, singing note beneath the runners:

*Creee-clink, creee-clink.*

At the seventeenth switchback, they stopped.

The **Great Arch of the Eagle’s Crag** was finished.

It hung across the chasm like a bridge of white cloud. Forty feet above the blue shadow of the gorge, thirty-two voussoirs of white freestone stood upon their timber centering, their joints so tight that the snow had found no footing on the under-side of the span.

On the center of the arch stood three men with iron crowbars.

They were waiting for the word.

The old quarry-man with the braided beard was there, his fur cap pulled down over his eyebrows, his hands encased in mittens of badger-skin.

"The lime is set!" he shouted down to Vane through the wind. "We drove the wedges on Tuesday. The mortar has bitten into the stone like frost into an apple!"

"Strike the centers!" Vane shouted back.

The three men set their bars beneath the oak wedges that supported the timber cradle.

Maren held her breath.

This was the moment that every limner feared: the moment when the wood gave way and the stone was left alone with its own weight. If the calculations were false—if the curve of the ellipse was out by the sixteenth of an inch—the voussoirs would pinch at the haunches, the crown would rise, and forty tons of white stone would plunge five hundred feet into the gorge before a man could draw his knife.

*Thump.*

The first wedge flew out into the air, spinning down like a dead leaf.

*Thump. Thump.*

The great timber framework groaned—a long, deep, splintering cry of seasoned oak that echoed from cliff to cliff through the gorge.

The wood dropped two inches onto the lower corbels.

The arch did not move.

It did not drop the breadth of a hair.

It stood upon the empty air, perfect, white, silent, its thirty-two stones locked together by the geometry of the curve and the cold fury of the frost.

The old quarry-man took off his badger-skin cap and swung it three times around his head, his white hair streaming in the gale like a flag of thistle-down.

"The road is free!" he roared into the chasm. "Drive the teams across!"

The driver cracked his whip.

The four white oxen leaned into their yokes; the runners of the sledge gave a sharp, tearing screech as they broke the frost-crust, and the first sledge rolled out onto the center of the arch.

Maren looked down through the gaps in the floor-planks.

Five hundred feet below, the dry torrent-bed was nothing more than a dark line of shadow between the limestone buttresses. She could see the grey shale plain; she could see the distant, silver curve of the Sallow Causeway; and far away on the horizon—twenty leagues to the east, under the low, yellow light of the winter afternoon—a narrow strip of leaden grey that was the sea.

The sea was small.

From the top of the mountain, the great harbour of Marrow-on-the-Bar looked like nothing more than a puddle of ditch-water caught between two heaps of grey shingle. The ships were dots of black soot; the great Guildhall of the Sea-Mark was a child’s toy built of river-pebbles; the iron boom was a needle dropped across a gutter.

The world was not the sea.

The world was the stone.

The vast, white, silent continent of limestone that rolled away toward the west, ridge upon ridge, mountain upon mountain, under a sky of cold, unmoving sapphire.

The sledge cleared the arch.

The runners struck the level flags of the summit cutting.

And there, two furlongs ahead, resting in the white saddle of the pass like a crown of grey flint, lay **Harrow-on-the-Hill**.

***

### Chapter 15

The town of Harrow had no walls.

A town that sits upon a crag three hundred cubits above a chasm has no need of lime-mortar to keep out the tax-gatherers. Its houses were built of massive, unmortared blocks of grey freestone, their roofs covered with thick slabs of stone-slate that had grown a yellow crust of lichen three fingers thick.

The streets were wide—wide enough for three eight-horse wains to pass abreast—paved with river-pebbles set in lime-grout, with deep stone gutters on either side that ran with clear, sparkling water that came from the mountain springs.

In the center of the marketplace stood the **Great Conduit**: an octagonal fountain of white limestone, twelve feet high, carved with the figures of the eight winds, each blowing a stream of mountain water from a bronze pipe into an octagonal stone basin.

Above the conduit rose the Church of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

It was not dark like the churches of the coast, which were built of granite and smelled of dead fish and damp wool.

Its three aisles were high, light, vaulted with white freestone that seemed to float upon eight slender pillars of clustered shafts. The great west window was not glass; it was filled with thin sheets of mountain selenite—transparent gypsum from the quarries—that threw a soft, pearly, golden light across the stone floor, turning the white dust of the masons into a carpet of gold.

The people did not look like the people of the coast.

They were tall, lean, with the long, easy stride of mountaineers, their eyes clear and grey from looking across thirty miles of open air. They wore coats of white wool, unbleached, with broad leather collars that turned the snow; their women wore gowns of dark green frieze, with silver pins in their hair shaped like the leaves of the mountain ash.

They stood in two long lines from the conduit to the church door.

They did not cheer.

When Maren stepped from the sledge, carrying the Great Plat upon its oak standard, they bowed their heads—a slow, grave, ancestral inclination of the forehead, such as a man gives when his brother returns from forty years in the King’s wars.

An old man walked out from the porch of the church.

He was ninety years old, his back as straight as an iron jumper, his long white beard reaching to the leather belt of his mason’s apron.

He did not carry a cross.

He carried the **Standard Yard of the Hill**: an iron bar, one inch square by thirty-six inches long, its ends tipped with gold, marked with the ancient divisions of the thumb, the palm, the span, and the cubit.

He stopped before Maren.

He looked at the Great Plat. He looked at the yellow wax seal with its two eagles and the Great Compass.

"The survey has returned," the old man said.

His voice was like the sound of a heavy roller passing over gravel.

"It has returned, Father," she said.

"Is the road open to the salt?"

"The road is open," she answered. "The wains are at the deep water. The Dutchmen have anchored at the Sallow mouth."

The old man touched the iron bar to the vellum.

"Then the Concord is whole," he said.

He turned toward the church.

"Ring the seventh bell," he said to the young men who stood in the porch.

"The seventh bell?" a quarry-man whispered. "The seventh bell has not spoken since the Great Frost."

"Ring it," the old old man said. "The church has waited eighty years for the sound of its own voice."

Four young men went into the dark archway of the tower.

Maren stood by the conduit, her hand resting upon the cold bronze pipe where the water ran.

A minute passed. Two.

The wind from the north ceased, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath between the crags.

Then it came.

It was not a ring.

It was a stroke that entered the earth through the foundations of the houses, passing through the limestone rock into the very marrow of her shins.

A note of pure, white, silver bronze, so deep, so sweet, and so terrible that the water in the conduit basin leaped an inch into the air, breaking into concentric ripples that caught the golden light of the selenite windows like the rays of a great compass.

*BONG.*

The sound rolled out from the tower.

It leaped the chasm of the Devil’s Throat.

It rolled down the thirty-two switchbacks of the mountain ramp; it crossed the stone causeway of the Sallow; it swept across forty miles of frozen peat-moss, through the breach in the town wall, down the narrow lane of the Gut, until it struck the high granite walls of the Guildhall of the Coastwise Mark.

And down in the Great Vault, where Master Coram sat alone beside the cold ashes of his charcoal brazier, the old man lifted his head from his hands.

He took his horn-rimmed spectacles from his nose.

He laid them upon the empty shelf of the *Scrinium Antiquum*, beside the broken locks and the torn edges of the royal charter.

He smiled—a small, tired, peaceful smile that smoothed away forty years of salt-wrinkles from his brow.

"The seventh bell," the old master whispered to the empty stone.

He folded his black sleeves across his chest, leaned his head back against the cold granite of the pillar, and closed his eyes.

***

### Chapter 16

The thaw came in the third week of March, not with rain, but with a warm, moist wind that came out of the south-west, smelling of the green Atlantic and the blossoming gorse of the western shires.

In twelve hours, the snow upon the High Scarp turned into a grey sponge that wept water from every pore.

Maren sat in the **Upper Chamber of the Council of Harrow**, beneath the great roof of chestnut timber that had stood since the reign of King John.

Before her lay the **Roll of the Three Valleys**—the silk map that Coram had taken from the iron chest.

Beside her stood Vane, his face dark with lack of sleep, his wool coat wet through with the spray of the mountain torrents.

"The Sallow has risen five feet at the upper gorge," he said, his pencil pointing to a narrow cleft in the rock three leagues north of the town. "The ice-dam at the Black Weirs is holding the water, but the drift is four cubits deep behind the logs. If the dam breaks in the night, the flood will hit the Devil’s Throat like a shot from a mortar."

Maren did not look at the Sallow.

She was looking at the small, red-dotted lines that Master Varley had drawn eighty years ago along the western slope of the mountain—lines that ran not toward the sea, but toward the interior: toward the vast, limestone basin known as the **Great Sink of the Hills**.

"The sluices at the Eagle’s Crag," she said.

"They have been walled up with drystone since the Concord was broken," Vane replied. "There are forty tons of limestone rubble in the mouth of the cut."

"Can the quarry-men clear them?"

"In six hours, if they have twenty jumpers and three barrels of powder," Vane said. "But if we open the cut, the water will drown the lower pastures of the abbey. Two thousand sheep are lambing in the bottom."

Maren rose from the bench.

She walked to the lancet window that looked down upon the chasm.

The air was full of a dull, continuous roar—the sound of ten thousand mountain cataracts falling from the limestone cliffs into the gorge. The mist was rising from the abyss in white, boiling clouds that smelled of wet rock and crushed hemlock.

Down below, on the great arch of the switchback, she could see the road.

It was no longer white with snow; it was grey with running water, two inches deep, that swept across the gravel bed and poured over the limestone parapets in long, glittering ribbons of foam.

The road was holding.

Its gutters were clear; its culverts were discharging their torrents into the ravine with the regular, mechanical precision of an iron engine.

"Move the sheep," she said.

"The sheep?"

"Move them to the high terrace," she said, her voice dropping into that cold, absolute certainty she had learned at Coram’s bench. "The sheep can graze on the heather for three days. But if the road goes, Vane—if the bridge at the Sallow is swept into the mud—the town will starve before the autumn shearing."

She turned to the old man with the braided beard, who sat at the end of the table with his mallet between his knees.

"Call thirty men," she said. "Take the powder from the forge. Blow the drystone wall at the Eagle’s Crag before the sun goes off the cliff."

The old man looked at her for three seconds.

He did not look at Vane; he did not look at the Standard Yard upon the wall.

He stood up, spat into the palm of his right hand, and touched the brim of his fur cap.

"The powder is at the church porch, Sister," he said. "The fuses are laid."

***

The blast at the Eagle’s Crag was fired at four in the afternoon.

Maren stood upon the upper ramp of the switchback, seven hundred feet above the cut, her arm hooked around the iron stanchion of the parapet to keep from being thrown down by the mountain gale.

A great, dull *thud* shook the cliff beneath her feet.

A puff of yellow sulfur-smoke shot out from the narrow cleft beside the road, followed by a shower of small, white limestone splinters that rattled against the rock like hail.

Then came the water.

It was not a stream; it was a wall.

A solid, yellow, roaring cataract of melted snow, ten cubits wide by six cubits deep, tore through the shattered breach in the rock.

It did not fall into the Devil’s Throat.

It fell into the **Monks’ Trench**: a great artificial cutting, thirty feet deep, that had been blasted out of the limestone plateau three centuries ago to carry the mountain floods away into the vast, cavernous underground channels of the Great Sink.

The torrent leaped into the cutting like a horse into a lane.

It roared through the rock, throwing up a spray that reached the branches of the dwarf-pines fifty feet above, and disappeared into the black mouth of the cavern with a sound like the grinding of a million millstones.

Maren looked down into the gorge.

The level of the water in the Devil’s Throat was falling.

The yellow flood that had threatened to drown the switchbacks shrank to a narrow, white ribbon that brawled harmlessly among the boulders of the bottom.

The road was safe.

The seven arches of the Sallow Causeway were safe.

The town of Marrow-on-the-Bar was safe.

She leaned her forehead against the cold iron of the stanchion, her breath coming in long, ragged gasps that tasted of sulfur and the wet snow.

Beside her, Vane laid his hand upon the stone coping of the wall.

He did not speak.

He reached into his pocket, pulled out his brass alidade, and laid it upon the rock beside her fingers.

The brass was scratched, darkened with the sweat of sixty days of labor, its graduated edge worn smooth by the friction of the iron marking-pins.

"The King's Commissioners have crossed the bridge," he said quietly.

Maren lifted her head.

Through the clearing mist, she looked down across the twenty leagues of the plain.

The sun was sinking behind the mountain, throwing the long, blue shadow of the High Scarp across the Sallow Basin like an arm of stone.

And there, six leagues away upon the level gravel of the road, moving in a small, glittering line toward the foot of the mountain, was the royal column.

Six red coaches, each drawn by six grey horses; twenty horsemen in coats of blue cloth with the King’s cypher embroidered upon their breasts; and behind them, rolling upon four wheels as tall as a man, the heavy, iron-bound carriage that held the Great Seal of England.

They were not bringing troops.

They were not bringing warrants.

They were bringing the **Charter of the Three Realms**: the grant that united the coast, the marsh, and the hills into one free jurisdiction under the King’s peace.

Maren Brand took her ruling pen from her sheath.

She did not dip it in ink.

She held the brass nib between her thumb and forefinger, feeling the small, familiar dent where the screw had been set sixty years ago by some dead tool-maker in Stockholm.

It was only a piece of brass.

Two inches long, with an ivory handle that smelled faintly of old lard and her father’s thumb.

Yet with that piece of brass, she had moved the granite of the coast; she had dried the mud of the mere; she had opened the gates of the mountain.

She looked up at the sky.

The stars were coming out—not the cold, salt-blinded stars of the harbour mouth, but the great, clear, burning fires of the high wilderness, wheeling in their ancient courses above the crags of Harrow.

She did not need to draw them.

The survey was finished.

The line was laid.

The road had found its home.

### Chapter 17

The signing of the Concord took place upon the crown of the seventh arch of the Sallow Causeway, precisely where the relief of the two hands met the water of the reed-bed.

The Commissioners had brought no chairs.

They were men from the southern counties—grave, thin-faced lawyers with grey periwigs that smelled of lavender-water and court-plaster, wearing long riding-coats of purple broadcloth that they kept hitched up around their hips to keep the mud from the gold lace of their skirts.

The Chief Commissioner was Sir Arthur Vane—a second cousin of the quarry-man, who had left the hills thirty years ago to read the law in the Temple, and who now sat upon the Court of the Exchequer with a gold chain of three strands about his neck.

He stood upon the limestone flags with his boots planted upon the joint of the keystone.

Beside him stood Master Gervaise Pell, wearing his red alderman’s gown that had been brushed until the wool was thin, his three-pointed beaver hat held before his stomach with both hands. His face was the color of curdled milk; his eyes did not move from the iron-bound carriage where the King’s clerks were sharpening their quills.

"The petition of the Mayor and Aldermen of Marrow," Sir Arthur said, reading from a strip of parchment three yards long, "wherein they claim the sole right of measurement, sounding, and toll within forty leagues of the tide, is dismissed."

Pell’s mouth opened, but no sound came forth.

"It is dismissed," the Commissioner continued, his voice clear, cold, and carrying across the quiet reed-bed like the snap of an ash-twig, "because the King’s Majesty cannot grant that which does not belong to his crown. The land belongs to the plow; the hill belongs to the quarry; the road belongs to the traveller who pays his scot and bears his lot."

He turned to Maren Brand.

She was standing at the opposite parapet, between Master Coram and the Dean of the Coopers. She wore her simple gown of dark blue mountain wool, the silver compass of the Chancery resting upon her breast beside the bronze seal of the Concord.

"Sister Maren," Sir Arthur said.

"Sir?"

"You have presented the Great Plat of the Province."

"I have, sir."

"Is it true to the chain?"

"It is true to the link, sir," she answered. "There are four hundred and twenty stations between the salt-quay and the marketplace of Harrow. Every station is marked with an ash-stake or a stone bench-mark; every level is recorded in the register of the Chancery; every sounding has been checked against the low-water mark of the equinoctial tide."

The Commissioner turned to the oldest of the clerks.

"Bring the wax," he said.

The clerk brought a small copper pan of coals, upon which sat a ladle of clear, red wax that smelled of Venice turpentine and shellac.

Sir Arthur took the Great Seal of England from its velvet bag.

It was as large as a trencher-plate, four inches thick, made of heavy silver that had been engraved with the image of the King sitting upon his throne with the orb and the sceptre.

He did not set it upon the Mayor’s petition.

He set it upon the lower margin of the Great Plat, beside the two eagles and the Great Compass of Harrow.

*Press.*

The silver sank into the red wax with a small, sizzling hiss.

"By the authority of the High Court of Parliament," Sir Arthur pronounced, "this plat is declared the **Standard of the Province**. Let no man alter a line; let no man move a stone; let no man take a toll upon this road that is not set down in the schedule of the Concord."

He looked at Master Coram.

The old master was standing with his hands folded in his sleeves, his horn spectacles pushed up onto his bald forehead, his watery blue eyes fixed upon the distant line of the sea.

"Master Coram," the Commissioner said.

"Sir?"

"The Chancery of the Sea-Mark is dissolved."

The old man did not move. He did not drop his eyes.

"It is dissolved," Sir Arthur said, "to be reconstituted on the morrow as the **Royal College of Surveyors of the Coast and Hill**. You are named the first Provost of the Roll."

Coram looked at Maren.

A small, dry smile—the shadow of a smile that had lived in the dark scriptorium for fifty years—touched the corners of his mouth.

"I am too old for rolls, Sir Arthur," the old master said. "My spectacles are scratched; my thumbs are thick with the chalk; my eyes cannot tell cinnabar from brick-dust by the tallow-light."

He took the silver compass from Maren’s neck.

He did not keep it.

He laid it in the palm of Peter Vane, who stood beside her with his marking-pins in his hand.

"The boy has a good hand," Coram said softly. "He does not smudge his fairways. He will make a limner yet, if the Provost keeps him from the ale-house."

He turned toward the carriage.

"Give me a seat in the wain, Sir Arthur," he said. "I have not seen the hills since my father carried me to Harrow on his pommel sixty years ago. I would like to see the White Pits before my skin is as dry as the rolls in the vault."

The Commissioner bowed.

"The carriage is yours, Master Provost," he said.

The sun was setting when Maren Brand walked back toward the town.

She did not take the carriage; she did not ride the mule.

She walked alone upon the gravel bed of the road, her boots making a dry, rhythmic, satisfying crunch upon the crushed limestone.

At the fourth milestone, she stopped.

The tide was coming in.

Through the low gaps in the willow-beds, she could see the salt water running up the channels of the Sallow, blue, cold, and clear, carrying with it the clean, sharp smell of the outer ocean.

The river was no longer choked with silt.

The two great dykes—the King’s Bank and the Abbot’s Dike—had been repaired with three thousand tons of the white freestone from Harrow; the current was running straight, deep, six cubits down between its stone walls, scouring the mud from the fairway with every turn of the ebb.

A ketch was coming up the reach.

A small, smart, Dutch-built craft with tan sails and a gilded vane at her mast-head, her deck piled high with casks of wine from Bordeaux and bundles of iron hoop-iron from Sweden.

She did not stop at the boom.

The boom had been unchained; its eighty baulks of fir lay stranded upon the mud-flats of the North Reach, where the boys from the cooperages were already cutting them into firewood with their two-handed saws.

The ketch came on with the flood, her white bow-wave singing against the limestone revetment of the road.

As she passed the fourth milestone, her skipper—a short, broad man with an orange woollen cap and gold rings in his ears—stepped to the tiller.

He looked at Maren where she stood upon the bank.

He did not know her name. He did not know that her father had trimmed casks in the Gut or that she had spent six years copying the Head of Skiffs for twelve pence a week.

He saw only a woman in a blue skirt, with a surveyor’s brass pen at her belt and an iron rule under her arm.

He took off his orange cap.

He swung it low, until its wool tassel touched the deck-planks of his ship.

"Good road, mistress!" he shouted across the water in his broken English. "Good water! Good stone!"

Maren Brand raised her hand.

She did not speak.

She watched the ketch pass up the reach toward the new wharves of Marrow, its tan sails glowing like gold in the evening light.

Then she turned toward the town.

The breach in the wall was gone.

In its place stood the **Porta Montium**: a great arch of white freestone, twenty feet wide by thirty feet high, built with two courses of rusticated ashlar and an open parapet that looked toward the hills.

Above the arch, carved into the stone by the masons of Harrow, were the two hands:

The hand with the divider.

The hand with the pen.

And beneath them, cut deep into the limestone where the setting sun would strike the letters until the world ended, was the motto of the Concord:

**TERRA ET MARE UNUM SUNT.**

*The land and the sea are one.*

Maren Brand walked through the gate.

She did not go to the Guildhall. She did not go to the Council House.

She walked down the narrow lane of the Gut to the cooper’s shop where her father had worked.

The shop was empty now; the boys had gone to the ale-house, and Master Gault was down at the new wharves, checking the staves for the wine-fleet.

She climbed the narrow, dark stairs to her garret under the roof.

The room was cold, but it was clean.

The straw pallet was gone, replaced by a narrow bed of seasoned ash-wood with a wool mattress that smelled of sweet heather. On the table stood her tallow-candle, her bone scraper, and her small green crystal lens.

Beside them lay a fresh sheet of calfskin.

Four feet square. Smooth as silk. White as the snow upon the crags of Harrow.

Maren Brand sat upon her stool.

She did not light the wax-end.

The moonlight was enough—the great, clear, white northern moon that came up over the sea, flooding the room with a light so brilliant that she could see the grain of the vellum and the tiny, grey pits where the hair had been.

She picked up her brass ruling pen.

She dipped it into the black ink of the hills.

She did not draw a coastline. She did not draw a road.

She set her pen in the center of the white space, and there, with a hand that was as steady as the limestone cliffs of the High Scarp, she began to write the first line of the **Survey of the World**:

### Chapter 18

The first triangulation baseline of the province was measured across the dry shingle of the Sallow Bottoms in the third week of May, when the wild sea-pea was in flower and the east wind smelled of salt and warmed mud.

It was four miles long, straight as an iron needle.

Maren Brand did not use a chain of wire links for the baseline. An iron chain, as every apprentice learns in their third year, stretches when the horse pulls, kinks when the carrier turns his heel, and lengthens by the width of a barleycorn for every ten degrees of midsummer heat.

She used deal rods.

They were three rods of seasoned Baltic pine, each twenty feet long, two inches square, boiled in linseed oil until they drank no moisture from the air, and shod at the ends with brass caps that had been ground flat against the standard yard of the Exchequer.

The rods rested upon wooden trestles—stout three-legged stools of ash-wood, spaced at ten-foot intervals across the shingle—each fitted with a brass screw to bring the rod to a dead horizontal.

At the eastern terminal of the line stood the **Sea Station**: a granite pillar four feet square, sunk eight cubits into the gravel beside the milestone where the road entered the salt-basin.

At the western terminal stood the **Mere Station**: a pyramid of white limestone blocks erected upon the dry gravel-spit where the Sallow Causeway met the limestone cuttings.

"The bubble is three lines to the west, Sister," Peter Vane called out.

The boy was lying upon his belly across a sheep-hurdle that had been laid over the soft mud of a drainage-runnel. His eye was level with the spirit-tube of the second rod, his thumb resting upon the leveling screw.

Maren did not look through the tube. She was standing twenty paces back, shading her eyes with her linen bonnet against the white glare of the noon sun on the shingle.

"Turn the screw half a thread, Peter," she said. "Let the bubble settle between the scratches. We do not advance the third rod until the second has been recorded in the book."

The boy turned the screw. The tiny brass index moved with a faint, smooth *click* that was barely audible above the calling of the redshanks in the marsh.

"Dead center," he said.

"Mark the joint," she said.

A second surveyor—young Simon, who six months ago had carried the wooden trays of condemned sheets to the furnace in the South Tower—stepped forward with a pair of brass dividers and an ivory rule. He dropped a fine silk plumb-line from the brass cap of the second rod to the copper pin that had been driven into the head of the oak peg beneath the trestle.

With a steel scriber, he drew a hair-fine line across the copper head.

"Rod number one hundred and twelve," Simon wrote in the sheepskin-bound field-ledger. "Temperature by the spirit-glass: sixty-four degrees. Wind: east-southeast, two knots. Ground: hard flint and dry gravel."

It was slow work.

In a twelve-hour day, working from the first grey light on the Skiffs until the sun went down behind the High Scarp, the three rods moved sixty chains: less than a mile. Every foot of the distance had to be measured twice: once going west toward the hills, and once coming east toward the sea.

If the two measurements varied by more than two inches in the four miles, the whole line had to be picked up, the pegs drawn from the gravel, and the trestles leveled again from the beginning.

"Why four miles?" Vane asked, coming up the bank from the ditch where he had been clearing the willow-roots from the line of sight.

He had abandoned his deer-hide boots for low shoes of tanned cowhide with broad brass buckles, but he still carried his hickory pacing-stick over his shoulder like a fowling-piece.

"Why not measure between the church towers and have done with it? St. Jude to St. Michael is twelve leagues. Any fool with a five-foot theodolite can take the bearing across the peat in five minutes."

Maren wiped the sweat from her forehead with the hem of her sleeve.

"A theodolite gives you angles, Vane," she said, her voice quiet. "Angles are only shapes in the air. You can have a thousand angles, and you will not know whether your province is forty leagues across or forty miles, unless you have one line upon the earth that has been measured with the wood."

She pointed to the two deal rods where they lay upon their ash trestles, shining yellow in the sun.

"That is the foot," she said. "The rest is only geometry. If the baseline has an error of three inches, the mountain will be half a mile out of its place before the triangles reach the western sea."

Vane looked along the straight, white line of the rods.

Beyond the shingle, the great wall of the High Scarp rose into the blue sky like an altar of white chalk, its cliffs so sharp in the clean spring air that he could see the individual sheep grazing upon the upper ledges of the Devil’s Throat.

"The mountain does not move half a mile," he muttered.

"The mountain does not move at all," she answered. "That is why the paper must be true. If the map says the crag is three thousand cubits from the church, and the mason finds it is twenty cubits more when he comes to lay the bridge-piers, who pays for the timbering? The stone will not stretch to meet the ink."

She walked to the third trestle.

Her hands were brown now—not the dead, chalky white of the scriptorium where the sun never entered through the green bullseye panes, but the deep, reddish-brown of the peat-cutters and the shore-men. The calloused hollow on her middle finger was still there, but her palms were hard, calloused at the base of the fingers from handling the leveling screws and the heavy brass legs of the instrument-stands.

She looked through the telescope of her great theodolite.

It was an eight-inch instrument, made by Ramsden of London, which Sir Arthur Vane had sent down from the capital in a padded mahogany box lined with green baize. It had two horizontal circles of Swedish brass, divided on silver to fifteen seconds of an arc, with two small reading-microscopes that could distinguish the fiftieth part of a turn of the screw.

Through the glass, the distant granite tower of St. Jude-in-the-Fens leaped toward her.

She could see the stone balustrade; she could see the lead roof where the flag-staff stood; she could see a jackdaw sitting upon the iron weather-cock, preening its grey breast-feathers in the wind.

The vertical wire of the reticle cut the center of the staff.

She touched the tangent-screw.

*Click.*

She read the vernier through the magnifying lens:

*Seventy-two degrees, fourteen minutes, thirty seconds.*

She wrote the figures in her register.

Then she swung the telescope forty degrees to the right, toward the high, solitary cone of the **Great Skiff** that stood out in the deep water three leagues from the shore.

The beacon was there: three small red rays of cinnabar in her memory, but here in the glass, a massive cage of forged iron four cubits square, filled with white birch-billets that were ready for the torch.

The wire cut the beacon-shaft:

*One hundred and twelve degrees, six minutes, fifteen seconds.*

She checked the difference:

*Thirty-nine degrees, fifty-one minutes, forty-five seconds.*

The angle was closed.

The first triangle of the Great Survey was born: the line between the Sea Station, the church tower, and the outer rock of the sea-gate.

A triangle three leagues on a side, its three corners bound together by the laws of Euclid and the cold mathematics of the brass circle.

She sat upon the turf-dike with her field-ledger upon her knees.

For the first time in seven years, she was not copying.

She was not following the tracks that dead men had left upon calfskins eighty years ago.

She was making the earth speak its own name.

The granite of the Skiffs; the gravel of the Sallow; the white limestone of the mountain: they were no longer separate territories, ruled by separate charters and guarded by suspicious old men with iron tongs and lead weights.

They were nodes in a single, infinite web of measurement that stretched from the deep water of the ocean to the highest snow-gullies of the interior.

And as she sat there in the warm May grass, listening to the regular, rhythmic calling of Peter Vane at the second rod—

*Rod one hundred and thirteen... level... mark...*

—she saw a horseman coming down the gravel road from the town.

He was riding a small, grey highland pony whose shaggy coat was white with dried sweat, its saddle-bags stuffed with rolls of paper tied with red tape.

The rider was not a bailiff; he wore the dark green coat and round beaver hat of a King’s messenger.

He stopped his beast at the ditch, took a leather pouch from his belt, and held it out to her across the water.

"Sister Maren Brand?" the messenger called.

"I am Maren Brand," she said, rising from the dike.

"From the Commissioners of the Ordnance," the man said, his voice flat with the fatigue of three days in the saddle. "A warrant under the Privy Seal."

He dropped the pouch into the grass at her feet.

"You are ordered to cease the coastal stations," he said, turning his pony's head back toward the west. "The King has declared the boundary of the interior."

"The boundary?"

The messenger looked back over his shoulder, his eyes dark under the brim of his hat.

"The Vale of Carrow has closed the passes," he said. "The wool-barons have broken the bridges at the West Reach. They say no surveyor with a King’s chain shall set his foot upon their sheep-walks while their leases have three years to run."

He touched his pony with his spur.

"They have five hundred men with hunting-bows and iron pikes at the Red Ghyll," he shouted through the wind. "And they have burned the first triangulation cairn on the High Fell."

### Chapter 19

The Vale of Carrow lay three leagues beyond the mountain town of Harrow, in a deep, sheltered basin between the limestone scarp and the red sandstone hills of the western border.

It was a country that had never seen a salt-cart.

The soil was not peat; it was a rich, dark, loam the color of an old hazel-nut, watered by four clear mountain streams that ran down from the heather-moors through ancient woods of dwarf-oak and holly. In the bottoms, the sheep grazed ten thousand to the square mile—small, broad-tailed beasts with fleeces so long and white that when they moved across the green pastures in the evening mist, they looked like clouds of steam rising from the grass.

The barons of the Vale were not lords of the court.

They were **the Flock-Masters**: seven families of ancient yeomen who had held their sheep-walks by *heaf-right*—the customary tenure of the mountain—since the time when the Danes had ruled the northern firths. They recognized no landlord; they paid no tithes to the bishops of the coast; their only law was the **Boon of the Crags**, a code of forty customary rules written in an ancient, northern dialect on three sheepskins that were kept in an iron pot beneath the altar of the chapel at Carrow-Head.

Maren Brand arrived at the entrance to the Vale on the third of June.

She had not brought the red-coated soldiers from the garrison at Marrow; she had not brought the King’s messenger with his Privy Seal.

She had brought Peter Vane, two ash trestles, and a single pack-mule carrying her mahogany instrument-box and a small sack of oat-cakes.

The entrance to the valley was a narrow gut between two red sandstone crags, known as the **Red Ghyll**.

Across the gut, from cliff to cliff, the flock-masters had built a rampart of drystone turf, six feet high, topped with a thick abatis of sharpened blackthorn branches that bristled with thorns two inches long.

Behind the abatis stood thirty men.

They wore long tunics of undyed grey frieze, cross-gartered to the knee with thongs of ox-hide, with broad-brimmed hats of black wool that hid their eyes. Every man held a five-foot hunting-bow of seasoned yew, with a broad-headed arrow of ash nocked upon the string.

In the center of the rampart sat an old man upon a stone mounting-block.

He was seventy years of age, his shoulders as broad as a blacksmith’s, his hair long, white, and falling over his chest in three plaits that were tied with green ribbon. Between his knees he held a heavy iron broadsword with a circular pommel, its blade resting in the turf between his boots.

"Stand, coastal woman," the old man said.

His voice was like the sound of a heavy mill-dam opening in the night.

Maren checked her mule.

She did not touch the silver compass at her neck. She stood in the middle of the track, her hands resting upon the brass head of her pacing-stick, her grey linen bonnet pushed back so that the old man could see her face.

"I am the Surveyor of the Concord," she said.

"We have no concord here," the old man answered. "The Concord was made between the merchants of the mud and the quarry-men of the stone. We are neither. We do not sell fish; we do not dig rocks. We breed sheep. And the sheep need no brass instruments to tell them where the grass is sweet."

A murmur of agreement went up from the thirty men behind the thorn-hedge—a low, dry sound like the rustling of dry gorse in an autumn wind.

"Who are you, Father?" she asked.

"I am **Adam of the High Heaf**," the old man said. "Warden of the Boon. My grandfather’s grandfather drove his rams upon these hills before the first ditch was dug at Marrow. We have paid our twenty silver marks to the King every Michaelmas at the stone cross of the border; we have given forty archers to his wars when the Scots came over the Solway; we have asked nothing of his courts, and we want nothing of his clerks."

He pointed the point of his broadsword toward the three ash legs of the theodolite that projected from the mule’s pack-saddle.

"You bring the chain," he said.

"I bring the chain," she said.

"And after the chain comes the ditch," Adam of the Heaf continued, his eyes fixing upon her with a cold, fierce light that had no malice in it, but only the stubborn, ancestral terror of the peasant whose land is being named by strangers. "And after the ditch comes the enclosure. And after the enclosure comes the bailiff with his little book, telling us that the common pasture is the King’s waste, and that a poor man must pay three silver shillings for every score of ewes that drink at the brook."

He rose from the mounting-block, leaning his two hands upon the pommel of his sword.

"We will not have the line drawn," he said. "Take your mule back to the salt-gate, girl. Tell the King’s commissioners that the Vale of Carrow has no boundaries. The boundary is where the sheep can walk between sunrise and the folding of the star."

Maren did not turn the mule.

She took three steps forward, until her leather shoes touched the first prickly twigs of the blackthorn abatis.

She reached into her pocket.

She did not pull out the King's warrant with its great wax seal.

She pulled out a small, square book—not vellum, but six leaves of coarse rag-paper, stitched together with a thread of sheep-sinew.

It was the **Field-Book of Master Varley**, drawn in the year of the Great Frost, which she had found in the bottom of the iron chest in the *Scrinium Antiquum*.

She opened the book at the third leaf.

"Adam of the High Heaf," she said, her voice clear and carrying through the silence of the ghyll.

The old man frowned, his white eyebrows coming down over his grey eyes. "What is that?"

"In the thirty-first year of King Henry," she read from the paper, "on the eve of the Feast of St. Jude: Came John of the High Heaf before the Commissioners of the Forest at the chapel of Carrow-Head. Complaining that the Lord Dacre had driven forty head of cattle across the Red Ghyll, claiming the pasture of the Black Mire as part of the Forest of the North."

The old man’s jaw tightened.

"My grandfather," he muttered.

"And what said the Commissioners?" Maren continued, her eyes not leaving the paper. "They said: *Because there is no roll, because there is no plat, because the men of Carrow have no seal upon their boundaries, we find the Black Mire to be the King's waste; and we grant the pasture to the Lord Dacre and his heirs for ever.*"

She closed the book with a small, dry slap.

She looked up at the thirty archers behind the thorn-hedge.

"Your grandfather lost twelve hundred acres of sweet bottom-grass because he had no map," she said. "He had his bows; he had his yews; he had thirty men with ash-arrows. And the sheriff’s bailiffs came with six horsemen and three writs from Westminster, and they drove his sheep into the gorge and built the stone wall that stands behind your shoulder today."

She pointed to the great sandstone dyke that ran along the crest of the southern ridge—a wall ten feet high, topped with iron spikes, that cut the Vale of Carrow off from the water of the Black Mire.

"Who owns that wall today?" she asked.

The thirty men were silent.

An old archer with a scar across his cheek spat through the thorns. "The Duke's reeve," he growled. "He charges two pence a head for the watering."

Maren stepped to the mule. She unstrapped the mahogany box, opened the brass clasps, and lifted the great theodolite from its green baize cradle.

The polished brass caught the morning sun, throwing a bright, golden spot of light across the grey frieze of the old man’s coat.

"The chain does not enclose the land, Adam of the Heaf," she said, her voice dropping into that deep, rhythmic certainty that belongs to the stone. "The chain **protects** it. If you have no line upon the King’s roll, the first lord who can buy a clerk in the capital can write his own name across your valley. But if your boundaries are measured—if every ditch, every sheep-fold, and every spring is set down in the register of the Concord under the Great Seal—not all the dukes in England can move your turf by the breadth of a thumb."

She set the three ash legs of the tripod upon the gravel of the track.

"I have not come to take your pastures," she said. "I have come to measure them. I will write the name of every heaf in the valley; I will record the water-rights of every flock; and I will lay down the boundary of the Black Mire where your grandfather's sheep grazed before the Dacres stole the grass."

She looked at the old man.

"Will you keep the abatis?" she asked. "Or will you hold the staff?"

Adam of the High Heaf stood motionless for a minute, his two hands resting upon the cross-hilt of his sword. His eyes moved from the mahogany box to the sandstone dyke on the ridge, and then to the young men behind him, whose arrow-strings had begun to slacken as they listened.

He unclasped his right hand from the pommel.

He reached up, took off his broad black wool hat, and threw it upon the turf at his feet.

"Pull down the thorns," he said to the young men.

"Father?" the scarred archer said.

"Pull them down," the old man repeated, his voice deep as the mountain rock. "The girl has the old book. She knows the name of the ditch."

He walked through the gap in the rampart, his ox-hide thongs rustling in the heather.

He stopped before Maren Brand.

He did not touch the theodolite; he looked at the level-tube, where the tiny bubble of alcohol trembled in its glass between the two scratches of the diamond.

"Where do I stand?" Adam of the Heaf asked.

Maren handed him the pacing-stick.

"Go to the stone cross at the boundary, Father," she said. "Hold the brass head between your two thumbs, and do not move until I drop the white handkerchief."

The old man turned.

He set his broad boots upon the track, his great broadsword clanking against his thigh, his white hair streaming in the clean western wind that came off the moors.

And behind him, through the broken abatis, came the thirty young men of the valley, their bows unstrung, their hands ready to carry the deal rods across the ancient pastures of the hill.

### Chapter 20

The anomaly appeared at the forty-seventh station, on the high, bare limestone plateau of the **Great Gable**, six miles north of the Vale of Carrow.

It was the center of the secondary triangulation network—the point from which the lines of sight radiated in six directions to bind the mountain summits to the coastal baseline.

Maren had spent three nights in a turf hut beneath the summit cairn, waiting for the mountain mists to clear.

On the fourth morning, an hour before sunrise, the wind shifted into the north, cold, thin, and dry as bone. The clouds fell away into the valleys like sheep driven into a fold, leaving the mountain summits rising out of a vast, white sea of mist like islands of grey flint.

The air was astonishingly clear.

Through her eight-inch Ramsden glass, she could see the beacon of the Great Skiff, twenty-four leagues away to the east, its iron cage a tiny, black dot against the pale lilac light of the dawn sea.

To the south, she could see the square tower of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

To the west, she could see the new station: the red sandstone cairn that Adam of the Heaf and his young men had built upon the highest shoulder of the Vale of Carrow.

She adjusted the horizontal circle.

She took the first angle:

*Great Skiff to St. Michael: sixty-one degrees, fourteen minutes, twenty-two seconds.*

She took the second:

*St. Michael to the Vale of Carrow: fifty-eight degrees, thirty-one minutes, twelve seconds.*

She took the third:

*Vale of Carrow to the Great Skiff: sixty degrees, fourteen minutes, forty-one seconds.*

She laid her pencil upon the sheepskin ledger.

She did not write the total.

Her hand stopped three inches above the page.

Every schoolboy who has read the first book of the geometer knows that the three angles of any plane triangle must equal two right angles: one hundred and eighty degrees.

On a sphere—on the curved, round breast of the earth—the three angles must exceed one hundred and eighty degrees by a minute fraction known as the **spherical excess**, which depends upon the area of the ground enclosed by the rays.

For a triangle whose sides are eight leagues long, the excess is approximately four seconds of an arc.

The three angles must sum to:

*One hundred and eighty degrees, zero minutes, four seconds.*

She looked at her figures.

She summed them again:

*Sixty-one, fourteen, twenty-two.*

*Fifty-eight, thirty-one, twelve.*

*Sixty, fourteen, forty-one.*

Total:

**One hundred and eighty degrees, zero minutes, fifteen seconds.**

An error of eleven seconds.

Maren did not move. The mountain wind whistled through the brass tubes of her tripod with a thin, musical hum, but she did not feel the cold.

Eleven seconds.

In an eight-inch Ramsden circle, read with two micrometer-microscopes that had been calibrated against the standard circle in the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, an error of eleven seconds was an impossibility.

It meant an error of ninety yards upon the ground.

It meant that one of the three stations was not where it was measured; or that the light, passing through the cold mountain air above the Great Sough, had been bent by some invisible refraction that had no name in the textbooks of the Ordnance.

"The level is true," Peter Vane said from the corner of the hut, where he was blowing upon a handful of dry heather-twigs to boil their morning kettle.

"I know the level is true," she said softly.

She checked the bubble. It was dead between the scratches.

She checked the horizontal axis with the striding-level: the two brass feet rested upon the steel pivots without a tremble.

She swung the telescope back to the Great Skiff.

The iron cage was still there. But as she watched through the high-power eyepiece—an eyepiece that magnified forty times—she saw something that made her heart give a strange, sickening leap against her ribs.

The beacon was not resting upon the granite.

It was **floating**.

Between the base of the iron legs and the grey rock of the island was a thin, trembling strip of pale yellow light—a ribbon of sky, two minutes of an arc wide, that separated the stone from the metal.

The beacon was hanging in the air.

And then, as the first red rim of the sun broke over the sea-horizon, the ribbon widened.

The island of the Skiff began to change its shape.

The four granite teeth—the Cow, the Calf, the Black Ledge, and the Head of Skiffs—rose into the sky, their outlines stretching upward like four fingers of black wax held near a candle. They broadened at the top, narrowed at the base, and then folded into one another until they formed a single, vast, monumental arch of black rock that spanned three leagues of the ocean.

"Vane," she whispered.

The boy left his kettle. He came to the theodolite, knelt in the heather, and set his eye to the glass.

He looked for ten seconds.

He did not speak. His breath came with a slow, whistling sound through his teeth.

"The **Spectre of the Marsh**," he said at last, his voice trembling slightly.

"The Spectre?"

"My grandfather told me of it," Vane said, not taking his eye from the tube. "When the east wind is warm and the sea is cold—when the freshwater from the mountain thaw meets the salt-tide in the reed-beds—the air turns into glass. It builds mountains where there is only water; it lifts islands out of the sea; it makes the church-tower at Marrow look like a cathedral forty leagues high."

Maren took his place at the instrument.

It was not a ghost.

It was the air.

The warm, moist air from the drained valley of the Sallow was flowing out over the cold water of the northern sea, creating a lens of air twelve leagues wide—a lens that bent the rays of light downward toward the earth, lifting the distant coast three hundred feet above its true horizon.

The earth had not moved.

The instruments had not lied.

The very success of their work—the draining of the Great Sough, the cutting of the dykes, the clearing of forty square miles of stagnant peat—had altered the atmosphere of the province.

The land was breathing.

By taking the water out of the mire, they had changed the density of the air through which the survey had to see.

Maren sat upon the stones of the cairn.

She looked down at her hands—her brown, hard hands that had learned to draw the line that does not lie.

How could a line be true, if the very light that carried the line to the eye was bent by the warmth of the ground?

If the air itself was an unfree apprentice, turning the straight rays of geometry into the curves of a dream?

She took out her Great Plat.

She unrolled the thirty-four panels across the grey limestone of the summit.

The ink was black, hard, and permanent. The yellow wax of the Concord was solid as a pebble of amber. The red seal of England shone like a drop of fresh blood upon the margin.

Everything upon the paper was certain.

Everything was fixed.

Yet down there in the vast, purple basin between the mountain and the sea, the world was moving.

The peat was shrinking as the water left the beds; the limestone causeway was rising two inches above its original footing; the river Sallow was scouring its new channel twelve feet deeper into the gravel; and the air above the reed-beds was weaving its shimmering webs of glass that lifted the sea-cliffs into the clouds.

"What do we write in the book?" Peter Vane asked, holding the pencil over the open ledger. "Do we write the eleven seconds?"

Maren looked from the paper to the distant, floating arch of the Skiffs.

She did not scrape the page.

She took the pencil from his hand.

And there, in the margin of the forty-seventh station, beside the impossible sum of the three angles, she wrote the truth:

*Nota: The light bends upon the marsh. The earth is sound, but the eye must wait for the cold wind from the hills before the triangle can be closed.*

*The measure is not in the glass.*

*The measure is in the stone beneath the foot.*

### Chapter 21

The Surveyor-General of the Ordnance arrived at Marrow-on-the-Bar in the second week of August, accompanied by four clerks in powdered periwigs, two draughtsmen from the Tower of London, and a four-wheeled spring-wain that held the **Imperial Standard**: a brass bar of four yards, packed in goose-feathers within an iron chest lined with velvet.

His name was **Colonel Peregrine Croft**.

He was a man of fifty-five, with a face the color of an old brick that had been burned in a slow kiln, and two small, pale, watery eyes that looked at every landscape as if it were a field of fire for six-pounder guns. He wore a scarlet coat with dark blue facings, heavily laced with gold at the button-holes, and high boots of black jacked leather that reached half-way up his thighs.

He did not go to the Guildhall of the Coastwise Mark.

He set up his headquarters in the **Customs House on the South Quay**, turning the great room where the salt-ledgers had been kept into a drafting office with sixteen walnut tables and four iron stoves that were kept burning even in August to dry the sizing of his royal papers.

Maren Brand was summoned to his presence at three o'clock on a Tuesday afternoon.

She came on foot, wearing her dark blue gown of mountain wool, her silver compass hanging from her neck, her field-ledger under her arm.

Colonel Croft did not offer her a chair.

He stood before the large bay-window that looked out upon the harbour, where three King’s frigates were lying at anchor with their topmasts struck, waiting for the spring-tides to carry them into the deep water behind the Shingles.

Across his drafting board lay her **Great Plat of the Province**.

He had not treated it with reverence.

He had stuck three iron push-pins through the margins to hold it flat against the walnut timber; he had laid his heavy brass tobacco-box upon the relief of the two hands; and he had drawn four long, straight lines in red pencil across the thirty-two switchbacks of the Devil’s Throat.

"Sister Maren Brand," Croft said without turning his head.

"I am Maren Brand, sir."

"You have drawn a curious map, woman," he said.

He turned from the window. His gold lace caught the afternoon light, throwing a yellow gleam across the red pencil-marks on the vellum.

"A very curious map," he repeated, his voice clipping every vowel as if he were trimming the ends of a fuse. "You have given seventy roods of mountain pasture to a parcel of sheep-stealers in the Vale of Carrow. You have recorded forty-two customary water-rights that have no warrant in the Court of Wards. And you have laid down a road through the King's peat-moss that has neither a toll-gate nor a military redoubt in twelve leagues of country."

"The road was built by the people of the Concord, sir," she answered, her voice even and cool as well-water. "Under the Charter of the Three Realms."

"The Charter of the Three Realms," Croft sneered, walking to the board and tapping the red seal of England with the hilt of his dress-sword. "A piece of paper signed by three lawyers in a ditch to quiet a riot of carters. The King’s Majesty does not govern his provinces by ancient charters, mistress. He governs them by the **Survey of the Crown**."

He pulled a great sheet of thick, white Whatman paper from the drawer of his table.

It was a printed sheet—engraved upon copper in the Tower of London—divided into neat, square inches by a grid of fine, black lines.

"This is the **New Plat**," the Colonel said. "Every square represents one square league of five thousand two hundred and eighty yards. The country is divided into hundreds, the hundreds into parishes, the parishes into crown-allotments."

He pointed to the grid.

"Here is your Great Sough," he said. "It is no longer the Great Sough. It is the **Parish of St. George-in-the-Fens**. It is divided into sixty-four farms of three hundred acres each, to be sold by the Commissioners of the Treasury to raise the supply for the French war."

Maren looked at the square grid.

The red pencil-lines had cut straight through the Sallow Causeway. They had cut through the seven arches; they had cut through the limestone cuttings; they had cut through the thirty-two switchbacks of the mountain ramp.

"The farms are water, Colonel," she said quietly.

"Water?"

"The Sallow Bottoms are three cubits below the level of the high neap-tide," she said. "If you cut the dykes to make your square ditches, the sea will come in at the Sallow mouth. In two tides, your sixty-four farms will be six fathoms of mud that will not bear the foot of a snipe."

Croft’s brick-red face darkened to the color of an old liver.

"The King’s engineers do not ask the advice of an unfree apprentice from a cooperage," he barked. "The drainage will be conducted upon the Dutch system, with two windmills of forty horsepower and an iron sluice-gate from the foundry at Carron."

He leaned over the table, his two fists resting upon her Great Plat.

"You will surrender the field-ledgers, Brand," he said. "The registers of the forty-seven stations. The angles of the triangulation. The keys to the bench-marks."

Maren did not move.

"The ledgers belong to the Royal College of Surveyors," she said.

"The Royal College is superseded," Croft replied. "By this warrant."

He drew an iron-clasped portfolio from under his coat and laid it upon the table.

"Under the **Act for the Uniformity of the Imperial Measure**," he recited. "All local rolls, charters, rutters, sea-cards, and customary registers are declared superseded. All surveys within this realm shall henceforth be conducted exclusively by the officers of His Majesty’s Corps of Royal Engineers, using the Imperial Standard Yard and the astronomical meridian of the Royal Observatory."

He reached out his hand toward her field-ledger where it lay beneath her elbow.

"Give me the book," he said.

Maren did not give him the book.

She took one step backward, her right hand closing around the silver compass that hung from her neck.

"The book is not complete, Colonel," she said.

"Not complete?"

"The triangles do not close," she said.

Croft laughed—a hard, barking sound that had no mirth in it. "The triangles do not close! A school-girl’s excuse! If the angles do not make two right angles, you add three seconds to each and have done with it! We do not stop the King’s survey because a woman cannot divide by three!"

"It is not three seconds, sir," she said. "It is eleven."

The Colonel stopped laughing.

His small, pale eyes flicked from her face to the field-ledger, and then to the Great Plat upon the table.

"Eleven seconds?" he muttered.

"Eleven," she said. "Between the Skiff, the Crag, and the Vale of Carrow. And if you add three seconds to each—if you force the paper to match your square grid—your windmills will be thirty cubits out of their beds; your iron sluice-gates will miss the channel by forty rods; and the first spring-tide will carry your sixty-four farms into the harbour basin of Marrow."

She looked straight into his eyes.

"You have the Imperial Standard, Colonel Croft," she said, her voice dropping into that deep, cold authority that had made Master Coram yield the keys of the vault. "You have the gold lace; you have the three frigates; you have the copper plates from the Tower of London."

She touched the vellum beneath his fist.

"But you do not know where the water goes," she said. "And the water does not read the Acts of Parliament."

### Chapter 22

The storm broke on the eighteenth of August, three days before the highest spring-tide of the year.

It was not a northern gale; it was a **Channel surge**: a great, heavy, purple wall of wind and salt-water that came out of the south-west, driving the Atlantic into the narrow throat of the Irish Sea until the water had nowhere to go but up the shallow estuaries of the coast.

At noon, the tide at the South Quay was six feet higher than the mark on the customs stone.

The careening berths were drowned; the timber baulks in the cooper’s yards were floating like corks in the street; and the great iron boom—which had been left upon the mud-flats of the North Reach—was lifted by the surge and driven through the garden-hedges of the Grey Friars like a battering-ram.

Maren Brand stood upon the parapet of the **Porta Montium**.

Beside her stood Master Coram. The old man had grown so thin that his black camlet gown hung upon his shoulders like a sail upon a yard, but his eye was clear, his horn spectacles tied to his ears with two new strings of green silk.

Down below, in the Tanners' Field, three hundred soldiers from the garrison were trying to build a turf dam across the mouth of the Sallow Ditch.

They were working under the orders of Colonel Croft.

The Colonel was on horseback, his scarlet coat black with rain, his gold lace dripping with dirty water, his sword drawn to threaten two carters who had refused to drive their horses into the flood.

"The dam is in the wrong place," Coram said, his voice whistling through his dry lips.

"The Colonel has the grid," Maren answered, her hands gripping the cold limestone coping of the arch.

"The grid!" the old master spat into the wind. "A man who builds a dam by a grid should be made to drink the mud through a quill! The surge does not follow the square, girl! The surge follows the **Old Sough**!"

Maren looked out across the plain.

The Great Sough was no longer green with heather or grey with shale.

It was an ocean.

A vast, yellow, boiling lake of flood-water, four leagues across, that reached from the town ditch to the foot of the limestone cliffs.

The forty-two farms of Colonel Croft were gone. The square drainage-ditches had vanished; the willow-fascines were floating in the foam; and the six-pounder guns of the battery at the third mile were submerged to the axles, their gunners clinging to the carriages like drowning rats.

Only one thing stood above the water.

The **Inland Line**.

The road.

It rose two feet above the surface of the flood—a straight, white ribbon of dry limestone gravel, six leagues long, running through the yellow sea like a causeway across an uncreated world.

Its culverts were working.

Through the seven arches of the Sallow Bridge, the water was roaring at ten knots, passing harmlessly through the deep, stone-lined channels into the Great Weirs.

The cut-waters were splitting the flood; the drystone revetments were holding the shale; the timber skirts of seasoned black oak were pinned into the mud like the roots of the mountain itself.

Along the road, moving in a steady, unhurried column toward the town, were forty wains.

They were not carrying wool today.

They were carrying people.

The women and children of the lower fens; the sheep from the flooded meadows; the dry flour from the mountain mills; and forty tons of white freestone to mend the sea-wall where the granite of the Skiffs had given way beneath the waves.

Colonel Croft rode his horse up the ramp to the gate.

The beast was trembling, its chest caked with yellow foam, its saddle-girths slipping in the wet.

The Colonel did not look at Coram.

He did not look at the soldiers in the ditch.

He looked at Maren Brand where she stood upon the parapet with her field-ledger in her hands.

His dress-sword was in its scabbard now; his scarlet coat was torn at the shoulder where an oak-branch had struck him; his powdered periwig was gone, leaving his bald head grey and bare in the salt-rain.

"The water is in the Customs House," Croft said.

His voice was no longer a bark. It was the voice of a man whose artillery has been drowned by the sea.

"The Whatman sheets are ruined," he said. "The copper plates are twelve feet deep in brine. The Imperial Standard has fallen into the cellar."

Maren looked down at him from the wall.

"The stone is dry, Colonel," she said.

"The stone?"

"The road to Harrow," she said, pointing to the white line that ran through the flood toward the mountains. "The bridge is standing. The switchbacks are clear. The seven bells of St. Michael are ringing for the noon prayer."

She held up the sheepskin book.

"The triangles are closed," she said.

"Closed?" Croft whispered. "How could you close them in the storm?"

"We did not close them in the air," she said. "We closed them upon the road. Every link of the chain was measured; every level was taken; every stone was laid where the earth could bear the weight."

She walked down the stone stairs of the gate.

She stopped before the Colonel’s horse.

She did not offer him a warrant.

She offered him an ivory rule—her father’s rule, its divisions yellow with sixty years of tallow and cooper’s grease.

"Take it, Colonel Croft," she said.

The soldier looked at the little piece of bone.

He did not take it with his fingers; he reached out his gloved hand, took it between his thumb and forefinger as if it were a holy relic, and held it against the gold lace of his breast.

"What is it?" he asked.

"The measure of the province," Maren Brand answered. "It has no crown; it has no parliament; it has no grid."

She looked westward, where the white cliffs of Harrow were catching the first blue rift of sky through the clearing gale.

"It has only the truth of the ground," she said. "And that is enough for any king."
### Chapter 23

The new house of the Survey did not take its name from the King, nor from the Admiralty, nor from the saints whose leaden images had rusted upon the town gates.

It was called simply the **Bridge-House of the Sallow**.

It was built of the white freestone of Harrow, two stories high, set upon seven arches of rusticated limestone that spanned the mill-race two hundred yards north of the great causeway. Beneath the floorboards, the mountain water ran clear, cold, and ceaseless over a bed of grey river-flint, turning a fifteen-foot undershot water-wheel of seasoned elm that drove three distinct machines:

In the lower cellar, a pair of granite rollers that ground the charcoal, the copperas, and the gall-nuts into ink of an impalpable fineness;

On the ground floor, a screw-press of Swedish iron, six feet between the uprights, that could take an impression from a copper plate four feet square with a single turn of its counter-weighted lever;

And in the upper garret, beneath the open louvers of the roof, a slow, continuous ventilation-fan that drew the salt-fog out of the drying-lofts, so that seventy sheets of heavy rag-paper could dry in six hours without buckling at the margins.

Maren Brand had her bench in the western corner of the first floor, where the lancet windows looked straight up the valley toward the cleft of the Devil’s Throat.

She did not sit upon a platform.

Her table was an eight-inch slab of polished freestone, twelve feet long, resting upon four masonry piers that went down through the floor into the bedrock of the stream. Upon stone, as Master Coram had taught her before his knees failed, a brass ruling pen does not jump when a loaded wain rumbles across the bridge outside; upon stone, a line drawn at midnight is the same length as a line drawn at noon.

Beside her table stood six apprentice benches.

They were occupied not by boys from the town alone, but by young men and girls from the three jurisdictions: two sons of the salt-coopers from Marrow; two daughters of the mountain quarry-men from Harrow; and two lads from the sheep-walks of the Vale of Carrow, whose fingers had been softened with lard to take the callouses of the hazel-crook from their joints before they were permitted to touch the fine crow-quills.

"The third rule of the Registry," Maren said, not looking up from her plate.

A girl of sixteen, sitting at the third bench with an ivory-handled scraper in her fingers, answered without hesitation:

"The error of the sight is not the error of the ground."

"And what is the ground?"

"The ground is thirty-six inches to the yard, four poles to the chain, ten chains to the furlong, and eight furlongs to the mile, measured upon the level horizontal by the cold rod."

"And if the mountain rises one foot in four?"

"The surveyor takes the secant of the inclination," the girl said, her voice clear and rhythmic as a catechism. "He deducts the hypotenusal excess from the slope-chain, so that the mountain upon the paper lies as flat as the mere; otherwise the houses upon the summit would have no ground to stand upon when the valleys are brought together."

Maren nodded once.

She dipped her steel graver into a shallow cup of almond-oil.

She was not drawing upon vellum now.

She was engraving the **First Plate of the County of the Concord**: a sheet of polished copper three feet across by four feet deep, rolled cold under the heavy iron cylinders of the Sheffield mills until the metal was as hard and bright as a mirror.

Upon the copper, every line had to be cut with the burin—a small, triangular prism of hardened steel, set into a half-round mushroom handle of box-wood that rested in the hollow of her right palm.

*Scrrr-t.*

A tiny, hair-like shaving of red copper curled upward from the point of the tool, bright as a thread of gold, falling into the wooden tray beneath the board.

The line was the **Sallow Dyke**.

Two hair-lines, a hundredth of an inch apart, with the hachures of the revetment cut at sixty degrees to show the slope of the masonry toward the water.

Behind her, the floor-timbers creaked under a heavy, slow step.

It was **Warden Kedge**.

He did not wear his beaver hat today; he carried a flat wool cap in his hand, his red hair turned entirely white at the temples, his green velvet gown replaced by a plain coat of dark brown frieze with horn buttons.

He walked with an ash-stick, his left leg stiff from the winter damp that had settled into his hip after his mule had dumped him into the reed-bed nine months ago.

He stood beside the freestone slab, watching the point of the burin move through the red metal.

"The Dutchmen have cleared the Shingles," Kedge said, his voice no longer a roar, but a low, gravelly rasp that had lost its alderman’s insolence.

"The outer bar?" Maren asked, not lifting the steel from the cut.

"The whole reach," the old warden said. "They brought two bucket-dredgers from Amsterdam—great wooden machines with forty iron scoops on an endless chain, driven by four horses walking in a capstan on a barge. They took twelve thousand tons of mud out of the gut in six weeks."

He reached into his coat and laid a paper upon the corner of the stone.

It was a sea-card: printed on thin paper from an uncleaned plate, smelling of cheap train-oil and fish-glue.

"Dirck Jansen drew it," Kedge muttered. "The man whose rutter Hallowell burned in the Kiln on the day your Peter spoiled his North Sand."

Maren glanced at the sheet.

There was the Head of Skiffs; there were the Grey Weirs; there was the mouth of the Sallow.

But between the Cow and the Calf, where the Chancery’s master roll had recorded twelve feet of water at dead neap for two hundred years, the Dutchman had laid down five fathoms in clear, round figures, with a note in his blunt low-country script:

*Hier is goed anker-grond voor schepen van drie-honderd last.*

Here is good anchorage for ships of three hundred tuns.

"What does the Assay-Master say?" Maren asked.

Kedge gave a dry, rattling cough that ended in a grimace.

"Hallowell is dead, girl," he said. "He died on Friday. We found him in the ash-hole of the furnace with his iron tongs in his fist. He had spent his last four groats on two bottles of gin, and he was trying to light the sea-coal with his old flannel cravat."

He looked out through the lancets toward the white line of the road where an eight-horse wain was rolling down toward the river with twenty casks of mountain beer.

"The furnace is cold, Maren," he said softly. "The boys from the cooperages have taken the fire-bricks out of the chimney to build a malting-kiln behind the church. There is no one left to burn the Dutchman’s paper."

Maren laid her burin in the box-wood groove.

She took the sea-card. She did not tear the margins; she did not throw it into the scrap-tub.

She laid her brass divider across Jansen's soundings, comparing the bearing with the station-pins she had cut into the copper plate.

"The Dutchman is out by two cables at the Black Ledge," she said.

"Is he?" Kedge squinted at the paper.

"He took his cross-bearing from the beacon on the Skiff before the iron cage was mended," she said, her finger pointing to the intersection of the two red lines. "The cage was bent three inches to the west by the August surge. His sounding is true, but his rock is fifty fathoms south of the weed."

She took her pencil of French lead.

She did not cross out Jansen's name.

Beside the Dutchman’s five fathoms, she wrote the correction:

*Sounding true: bearing to be corrected two points north, by reason of the leaning of the staff.*

She signed her own name beneath the stroke:

*Maren Brand, Surveyor of the Concord.*

She handed the paper back to the old man.

"Give it to the pilots, Warden," she said. "Tell them to keep forty yards to the windward of the Calf until the tide has made two hours. And tell Jansen that if he brings his dredger into the North Gut, the College will pay his horses four silver groats a day from the road-tolls."

Kedge took the paper.

He looked at the neat, black pencil-marks; he looked at her brown fingers, stained at the knuckles with the red oxide of the copper-plate.

He did not put on his cap.

He held it before his breast with both hands, bowed his head until his white hair touched the stone edge of the slab, and walked out into the lane, his ash-stick clicking softly against the gravel of the bridge.

### Chapter 24

The blast-furnace at the foot of the High Scarp was blown in on the first Tuesday of October.

It stood upon the shelf of grey limestone where the **Monks’ Trench** discharged its cataract into the gorge of the Devil’s Throat—a square tower of white freestone, thirty feet high, bound with iron hoops four inches broad that had been forged in the smithies of Harrow.

At the top of the tower, two young men with long-handled iron shovels were feeding the mouth:

Six baskets of red ore from the mines of the Vale of Carrow;

Eight baskets of hard, black mountain coal from the pits behind the church;

And two baskets of white limestone chips from the quarry-waste to serve as flux.

At the bottom of the stack, driven by a ten-foot iron water-wheel that groaned beneath the weight of the falling torrent, two enormous bellows of bull-hide blew a continuous, roaring blast of air into the hearth through two bronze nozzles as thick as a man's thigh.

The noise was like the roaring of ten dragons caught in an iron cave.

The ground for forty rods around the furnace trembled with a deep, rhythmic, terrifying vibration that drove the ring-doves from the scrub-oak woods and made the sheep on the upper terraces huddle together in the heather with their heads to the mountain.

Maren Brand stood upon the upper charging-platform, seventy feet above the road.

She was not surveying stone today.

She was laying out the **First Tramway of the Concord**.

Beside her, leaning upon a heavy hickory staff with an iron spike in the heel, stood Peter Vane's brother, his face black with the soot of the coal-shovels, his leather apron burned into round holes where the sparks had struck him at the tapping.

"The wains are too slow, Maren," he shouted through the thunder of the bellows. "A wain carries three tons and takes six horses eighteen hours to reach the mere. The iron cannot wait for oats. The furnace eats forty tons of stone a day; if the ore does not come down from the ghyll, the hearth cools; if the hearth cools, the iron sets into a salamander that ten tons of black powder cannot blast out of the masonry."

He pointed down the mountain.

Along the thirty-two switchbacks of the road, six hundred men were at work.

They were not laying gravel.

They were laying **stone sleepers**: blocks of white limestone, two feet long by a foot square, bedded in crushed flint at intervals of thirty inches along the inner shoulder of the carriage-way.

Upon the stones lay the rails.

They were not wrought-iron bars; they were cast-iron plates, three feet long, four inches broad, shaped like an inverted *L*—the **tram-plates** that the engineers of the northern collieries had invented five years before.

The horizontal flange carried the flat wheels of the small, four-wheeled iron wagons; the vertical flange—two inches high—kept the wheel from sliding off the iron into the ditch.

"A single horse," Vane roared, his mouth six inches from her ear, "will draw four wagons! Twelve tons of pig-lead on a slope of one in twelve, with one lad to hold the sprags at the turns! From the church to the salt-gate in three hours!"

Maren looked at the iron plates.

They were black, cold, smelling of the sand-moulds and the burnt linseed oil with which they had been painted to turn the mountain rain.

They were not stone.

Stone was patient; stone had its bed-planes, its joints, its ancient grain that a mason could read with his thumb.

This iron had no grain.

It was liquid stone that had been melted in the fire, poured into the red dirt, and cooled into an absolute, unyielding rigidity that had no memory of the mountain from which it had been dug.

"The friction," she said, her voice quiet beneath the roar of the blast.

"What friction?"

"At the turns," she said, pointing her brass rule at the seventeenth switchback, where the Great Arch leaped the chasm of the Eagle's Crag. "A carriage of iron does not bend like an ash wain. The four wheels are fixed upon two straight axles. When the wagon turns the curve, the outer wheel must travel ten feet further than the inner."

Vane wiped the sweat from his eyes with his black forearm.

"The flange will hold it," he said.

"The flange will cut the iron like a chisel," she answered. "In three weeks, the vertical plate will be worn to the breadth of a razor; the wheel will climb the iron, and twelve tons of lead will go over the parapet into the gorge."

She pulled her field-book from her pocket.

She opened it to the calculations she had made by the tallow-light in the Bridge-House three nights ago:

"You must make the outer rail higher than the inner," she said. "Three-quarters of an inch for every ten feet of radius. The carriage must lean into the mountain, as a skater leans upon the ice when he rounds the buoy at the winter races."

She showed him the drawing:

Not a simple plan, but a cross-section of the permanent way, showing the stone sleeper, the oak wedge that held the rail to the stone, and the **superelevation** of the outer flange.

"And you must cast the wheels with a taper," she added. "One in twenty from the flange to the rim. So that the outer wheel runs upon its greatest diameter when the centrifugal force throws the carriage outward, and the inner runs upon its least."

Vane stared at the diagram.

His black, soot-streaked brow furrowed until the skin formed three deep, horizontal folds between his hair and his nose.

"Who taught you that, Maren?" he asked, his voice dropping below the roar of the water-wheel. "Did Coram have that in his dead rolls?"

"No," she said, looking down into the deep, purple rift where the road ran toward the sea. "Coram knew only the water. But the iron follows the same law as the flood. It wants to go straight; it hates the turn; and if you do not give it a slope to slide upon, it will break your banks and drown your ditch."

She handed him the steel scriber.

"Go down to the seventeenth turn," she said. "Take two ash gauges and four wedges. Set the outer sleeper three-quarters of an inch above the inner before the first train comes down from the mines."

Vane took the tool.

He looked at her with a strange, wondering gravity that had no words in the vocabulary of the forge.

"We are building a machine, Sister," he said softly. "The whole province is turning into an engine of stone and iron."

"It was always an engine, Peter," she answered, turning back toward the stairs. "Only the old men thought it was an altar."

### Chapter 25

In the spring of the ninth year of the Concord, the survey crossed the **Divide of the Waters**.

The Divide was not a ridge; it was a high, undulating plateau of grey limestone pavement, five leagues broad, lying at an elevation of twelve hundred feet above the salt-water of Marrow.

Here the streams did not run east toward the German Ocean.

They ran west.

They gathered into small, swift, peat-stained brooks that tumbled through deep ravines of red conglomerate toward the **Great Western Sound**—an arm of the Atlantic that had no name in the Chancery rolls, because for two centuries the mountain barrier had made it as inaccessible to the merchants of the coast as the coasts of Cathay.

Maren Brand pitched her tent upon the summit of the **Pass of the Eagles**, where the old pack-trail from the Vale of Carrow crossed the watershed.

She had nine people with her now:

Peter Vane, who was now a sworn Master of the Field;

Four young journeymen from the School of the Bridge;

And three old sheep-drovers from the high heafs, who knew where the springs were dry in August and where the moss would carry a loaded pack-horse.

They had brought two eight-inch theodolites, four three-hundred-foot steel tapes that had been made in the new workshops at the furnace, and two **mercurial barometers**—three-foot tubes of heavy glass, filled with boiled mercury and inverted into box-wood cisterns—which Sir Arthur Vane had sent down from the Royal Society in London to measure the height of the peaks above the sea.

The morning was cold, clear, and without a breath of wind.

Maren stood upon a flat slab of limestone that projected over the western edge of the plateau like the beak of a hawk.

Beside her, Peter Vane was adjusting the brass vernier of the barometer.

"Twenty-eight inches and four lines," the young man read. "The spirit-glass stands at forty-two degrees. That gives eleven hundred and forty feet above the low-water mark at the Sallow mouth."

Maren did not look at the column of mercury.

She had her telescope upon its tripod, trained toward the west.

The world opened before her like an unrolled rutter.

Ten miles away, through a gap in the red sandstone hills, the land fell away in three monumental terraces of oak-forest and green pasture down to the edge of the water.

It was not a shallow, muddy bay like the roadstead of Marrow.

It was a **sound**.

A great, deep, fjord-like inlet of dark, sapphire water, two miles broad by seven leagues long, protected from the open ocean by a chain of high, mountainous islands of red granite that rose out of the surf like the battlements of an ancient city.

The water was dead calm.

Through the glass, she could see no shoals, no sand-spits, no drying ledges of weed.

The cliffs went straight down into the sea: ten fathoms—twenty fathoms—forty fathoms of clear, green, unmoving water, so deep that a three-decker of ninety guns could lay her yard-arms against the birch-trees on the bank without touching the gravel with her keel.

And on the eastern shore of the sound, sheltered from the western gales by a high headland of white limestone, lay a natural basin four hundred rods square.

A harbour that could hold the entire fleet of England.

A harbour that had no boom; that needed no dredgers; that had no forty-two customs duties; that was three days' sailing closer to the western colonies and the sugar-islands than any port on the eastern coast.

"Look, Peter," she said.

She stepped back from the eyepiece.

The young man set his right eye to the brass cup.

He did not speak for a minute. His knuckles—white and calloused from thirty miles of rock-climbing—tightened upon the brass legs of the stand until the metal clicked against the stone.

"There is forty cubits of water under the shore," he whispered. "I can see the kelp growing on the rocks twenty fathoms down."

"It is the **Port of the West**," Maren said.

"The King has no chart of it," Vane said, turning his head, his grey eyes wide with that sudden, dizzying realization that comes to a surveyor when he stands upon the edge of an unmeasured empire. "Croft’s books do not name it. In the Admiralty rolls, it is marked as *Coast Unsurveyed and Treacherous, carrying neither Bottom nor Shelter.*"

"The Admiralty rolls were written by men who never left their cabins," she said. "They sailed along the outer islands in the fog; they saw the surf breaking on the red granite, and they wrote down that the land was a tomb."

She unstrapped her drafting board from the mule’s pack.

She laid down a sheet of fresh calfskin—the white, smooth skin of the hill-sheep that she still preferred to all the rag-papers of London.

She took her brass ruling pen.

She did not draw a baseline from the deal rods; she had no need of the rods here.

She took her bearings from the two great mountain peaks that rose behind her: the Great Gable to the north, and the Cairn of Harrow to the south—two points whose distance had been fixed to the twelfth of an inch by three years of triangles.

She set her brass sector.

*One line.*

The line of the watershed: the high limestone ridge that divided the streams of the east from the streams of the west.

*A second line.*

The course of the new railway: the descent from the Pass of the Eagles through the red sandstone gorge, following a gradient of one in forty-eight down to the edge of the deep water.

*A third line.*

The quays of the new port: four thousand feet of vertical masonry, to be built of the red granite of the islands, where thirty ships could load the coal, the iron, and the cloth of the interior without waiting for a spring-tide.

Peter Vane watched her hand.

"The merchants of Marrow will burn their wigs," he muttered.

"Why should they burn their wigs?" she asked, her pen moving with that clean, beautiful, unswerving certainty that had never failed her.

"Because the trade will not come down the river," he said. "Why should a ship from Boston or Jamaica sail six hundred miles around the northern reefs—through the Skiffs, through the Grey Weirs, through the mud of the Bar—to buy their wool, when they can run into this sound in six hours from the open ocean, load their holds from the iron tram-cars, and be away before the customs reeve at Marrow has finished his morning porridge?"

Maren Brand paused.

She looked eastward, across the vast, purple sea of the mountains, toward that distant, invisible point where the chimney of the old Kiln had once thrown its black smoke into the salt-fog.

"The trade will go where the water is deep, Peter," she said softly. "A surveyor does not make the world; she only finds where the world has laid its stones."

She dipped her pen into the black ink.

And there, upon the western margin of the skin—where the old maps of the Chancery had ended in a blank, white margin that said *Mare Incognitum*—she wrote the name of the new city:

**PORT CONCORD.**

*Founded under the Great Compass of the Hill.*

*Anno Domini MDCCCIV.*

### Chapter 26

In her thirty-fourth year, Maren Brand returned to the garret in the Gut.

She did not return to live there.

The cooperage below was no longer a cooperage; it was an iron-store, where an old journeyman from the furnace sold shovels, picks, and tram-car wheels to the ditchers of the new canal. The smell of cedar-shavings and hot grease was gone, replaced by the dry, metallic dust of iron filings and the sulfurous smell of small sea-coal that had been brought down from the hills by the railway.

The garret was empty.

The straw pallet was gone; the three-legged stool had been broken up for firewood by some winter tenant; but beneath the low hip of the roof, the rough fir floorboards were still there, their grain grey with twenty years of salt-mizzle that had come through the loose slates.

Maren knelt in the corner by the chimney-stack.

She did not use an iron key; the old elm-wood chest had been removed to the museum of the College five years ago, where it sat under a glass case beside Master Coram’s horn spectacles and the bronze seal of the Concord.

She took up her bone scraper.

She slipped the thin steel blade beneath the edge of the third plank from the wall—the plank that had sat beneath her straw pallet when she was an eighteen-year-old girl.

The wood gave a dry, splintering *clack*.

She lifted the board.

Beneath it, lying in the shallow space between the ceiling joists of the shop below, was an object wrapped in an old piece of oil-cloth that had turned black and brittle with age.

It was not a map.

It was a small, square book of eighty pages, bound in rough sheepskin that had been dyed with alder-bark:

**The Private Register of the Apprentice Brand.**

She carried the book to the window.

The afternoon sun was sinking behind the chimneys of the Fish-Gate, throwing a red, smoky light across the slate roofs.

She opened the cover.

The first forty pages were filled with the exercises of her youth:

Three hundred copies of the compass-rose, each with its sixteen points, drawn in pale gall-ink that had turned brown with the years;

Forty-two variations of the Head of Skiffs, with every sounding marked in fathoms and feet, each signed at the corner with Master Coram’s small, neat, censorious cross;

And six pages of notes upon the mixing of pigments:

*To take the grease from the vellum: use the powder of calcined sheep-bones, mixed with the white of an egg that has been beaten with vinegar...*

*To make the cinnabar bite: grind with two drops of mountain honey and three drops of bile from an ox that was slaughtered in the frost...*

Then came the forty-first page.

Maren Brand stopped.

Her breath came with a small, sharp hiss that sounded loud in the empty garret.

The page was dated:

*The seventh day of November, in the sixth year of my bond.*

Beneath the date was a drawing.

It was not a map; it was an experiment.

She had taken an eight-inch square of the second-grade calfskin—the offcuts from the Fourth Survey—and she had divided it into sixteen small squares by lines of dry silver-point.

Into each square she had introduced a different substance:

In square one: pure well-water from the spring of St. Jude;

In square two: salt-water taken from the harbour mouth at half-flood;

In square three: the mountain water from the Sallow reed-beds, thick with peat-silt and iron-alum;

In square four: the urine of a sheep that had grazed upon the limestone crags of Harrow;

In square five: the black oil that was skimmed from the surface of the coal-workings at the Black Ghyll...

And across the sixteen squares, drawn while the paper was still moist with the different liquids, she had drawn a single, continuous, straight line with her iron ruling pen, using the carbon-black ink she had mixed from walnut-hulls and soot.

She held the page to the light.

In the first square—the square of the well-water—the line was dead.

It was a simple stroke of black ink, dry, hard, unmoving, its edges slightly feathered where the water had loosened the size of the skin.

In the second square—the square of the salt—the line had rotted. The copperas had reacted with the sea-salt, turning the ink into a pale, green, powdery crust that had eaten into the vellum until the fibers were as thin as a spider’s web.

In the fourth square—the square of the sheep—the line was intact, but it had turned brown, the color of an old hazel-nut.

And then she looked at the fifth square.

The square of the coal-oil and the mountain water.

The line had not stayed within its boundaries.

From the two sides of the black stroke, a thousand microscopic, hair-fine branches had grown out into the vellum like the roots of a willow-tree pushing through wet sand.

They were not smudges.

They were **capillaries**.

They followed the ancient, invisible grain of the hide—the channels where the blood-vessels of the living sheep had run through the skin before the animal was slaughtered.

And at the end of each capillary, so small that it could only be seen through the green Venetian lens, was a minute, hexagonal crystal of iron-pyrites—bright, golden, metallic—formed by the slow, continuous reaction between the sulfur of the mountain coal and the iron of the gall-ink.

The ink had not moved by magic.

It had moved by **affinity**.

The vellum of Harrow, cured in the alum of the high limestone crags, was full of the salts of the mountain; the ink, boiled with the birch-tar and the peat-soot, was full of the carbon of the ancient forests.

When the two met in the damp, salt-laden atmosphere of the coast—where the air was an electric conductor of moisture and sulfur—the ink followed the line of the greatest mineral tension in the skin.

And where was the tension?

It was along the ancient lines of drainage.

Along the old, forgotten river-beds; along the stone causeways that had been buried beneath the peat; along the tracks where fifty generations of sheep had compacted the limestone gravel until the earth beneath was harder than the surrounding bog.

The map had not invented the road.

The map had **detected** it.

As a compass-needle detects the iron in a rock through forty fathoms of salt-water, so the ink had detected the stone through three cubits of black mud.

The apprentice had not been an illuminator.

She had been a galvanometer.

Her pen had been the index of a vast, natural, subterranean battery that stretched from the coal-measures of the mountain to the salt-basins of the sea.

Maren sat upon the rough fir floorboards, her knees drawn up against her chin, her hands resting upon the old sheepskin book.

She did not laugh. She did not feel that sudden, cheap pride that a philosopher feels when he has explained away a miracle with three words of Greek.

She felt a strange, profound, humbling peace that entered her ribs like the cold water of the mountain springs.

The world was not a mystery to be feared.

The world was not a mechanism to be bought and sold by merchant aldermen or divided into square parishes by royal colonels.

The world was a **composition**.

A great, living, chemical, geometric poem, written in the language of stone, water, carbon, and salt, waiting for the hand that had the patience to measure the lines without lying, and the heart to follow where the ink should lead.

She closed the book.

She wrapped it in the dry oil-cloth, laid it back between the floor-joists, and drove the fir board down into its place with the heel of her boot.

Then she walked to the window.

Below, in the Gut, the gas-lamps were being lit—the new gas-lamps of the town, fed with coal-gas from the iron retorts at the South Quay, their clear, white, brilliant flames throwing round pools of light across the wet granite of the street.

Through the breach in the wall came the evening train.

Not wains today.

A long, dark, segmented column of twenty iron wagons, drawn by a great, smoking, snorting engine of iron and brass that had three chimneys and six driving wheels four feet tall.

The engine was called the **Maren Brand**.

It rolled upon the cast-iron rails with a steady, rhythmic, majestic thunder that shook the rafters of the old cooperage until the dust fell from the slates.

Upon the footplate stood Peter Vane's son—a lad of eighteen with his father’s grey eyes and his grandfather’s square shoulders—his hand resting upon the brass regulator, his white teeth shining through the coal-dust as he sounded the steam-whistle at the crossing of the Fish-Gate.

The whistle did not sound like the old horn of the watchman.

It sounded like the scream of an eagle leaping from the crags of the Devil's Throat—a wild, fierce, triumphant cry that echoed from the granite cliffs of the harbour mouth to the highest pastures of the High Scarp.

Maren Brand turned from the window.

She did not look back at the empty room.

She walked down the narrow stairs into the light of the lamps, her surveyor's compass swinging against her heart, her face turned toward the mountains where the night was falling upon thirty leagues of free stone.

### Chapter 27

Master Coram died on the Feast of St. Jude, in the ninety-second year of his age.

He died not in the damp vault of the Chancery, but in a small, square room on the south side of the **Marketplace of Harrow**, where the sun came through the leaded panes from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, warming the white freestone floor until it felt like the hearth of an oven.

His bed was made of mountain ash, strung with cords of raw hide, covered with three blankets of the white wool of the Vale of Carrow that smelled of sweet marjoram and dried mint.

Beside his pillow lay his instruments:

His horn-rimmed spectacles, their frames mended with five turns of silver wire;

His ivory ruling pen, its nib worn down to the brass by sixty years of drawing the Head of Skiffs;

And a small, round pebble of white limestone from the quarries of the White Pits, which he held in his left hand to keep his fingers from curling into the cramp of the dying.

Maren Brand had sat beside him for three nights.

She did not read to him from the church-books; she did not speak of the King or the new port in the west.

She read to him from the **Register of the Soundings**.

"The North Sand," she read, her voice low and even as the ticking of the water-clock in the corner. "Station twenty-two. At the fourth hour of the flood. Sounding: seven fathoms and three feet, bottom in fine grey sand and broken cockle-shells."

The old man’s eyes were closed. His face was the color of old vellum that had been washed with lime-water, the skin drawn so tight across his cheekbones that she could see the shape of the skull beneath.

His lips moved.

"The sand-run," he whispered, his voice as thin as the whistle of a bat.

"The sand-run is where you laid it, Master," she answered. "It has not moved three feet since the year of the Great Frost."

Coram’s right hand—the hand that had held the goose-quill through the reigns of four chancellors—stirred upon the wool blanket.

His fingers found hers.

They were cold, dry as dead leaves, but their grip had that sudden, desperate strength that an old artisan keeps for his tools.

"Maren," he said.

"I am here, sir."

"The line..." he said, his breath coming with a slow, dry rattle in his throat. "The line was too hard."

"Too hard, Master?"

"I made the coast... an iron wall," the old man whispered. "I thought if the line bent... the sea would come in. I thought if the boys drew a path through the marsh... the world would fall into the mud."

He opened his eyes.

Behind his watery, clouded pupils was a light that had never lived in the South Tower—a clear, vast, serene light that looked through the leaded windows at the great white crags of the mountain.

"The earth does not fall, Maren," he said. "The earth is... broader than the charter."

He tried to raise his head, but the ash pillow was too heavy for his neck.

"Did they... burn the Dutch chart?" he asked.

"No, sir," she said. "The Dutch chart is in the press. We have engraved it on the copper."

Coram gave a small, faint sigh—a sigh that had no grief in it, but only the long, deep, luxurious relief of a man who has carried an iron box for forty leagues and has laid it down upon the grass at last.

"Good," he said. "The Dutchman... had three feet more water than I gave him. A limner should... never steal water from a sailor."

His fingers relaxed upon her hand.

The small, white limestone pebble rolled from his palm onto the blanket, where it lay shining in the afternoon light like a drop of solidified sun.

"Maren."

"Sir?"

"Pounce the sheet," the old master whispered. "The ink... is dry."

He did not speak again.

The sun went down behind the High Scarp, throwing the purple shadow of the mountain across the marketplace of Harrow, through the lancet window, across the ash bed, until it touched the old man’s face.

The six bells of St. Michael-of-the-Crags began to toll.

Not the *Major Concord*.

The **Passing Peal**: three strokes on the great bell of white bronze, a pause that lasted while an apprentice could count twenty paces with the pacing-stick, and then three strokes more.

They buried him on the Saturday, in the churchyard that hung over the abyss of the Devil’s Throat.

They did not use a lead coffin.

They wrapped him in a sheet of royal vellum—the last skin of the Fourth Survey that had been kept in the iron chest—and they laid him in a grave cut five feet deep into the solid white freestone of the mountain.

Over his breast they laid a slab of the same stone, four cubits long by two cubits broad, carved by the masons of the White Pits with no name, no coat-of-arms, and no date.

They carved only three things:

An ivory ruling pen;

A pair of brass dividers, opened to an angle of sixty degrees;

And between them, cut deep into the limestone where the mountain lichen would fill the strokes with green gold through two hundred winters, the three words of the ancient oath:

**MENSURA REGIT ORBEM.**

### Chapter 28

The **Great Conference of the Geographers** assembled at the Bridge-House of the Sallow in the autumn of the twelfth year of the Concord.

They came from six kingdoms.

From France came **Monsieur de Cassini**, the Director of the Royal Observatory at Paris, wearing a coat of blue velvet embroidered with silver lilies, his carriage loaded with four-foot astronomical quadrants and tables of the satellites of Jupiter;

From Holland came **Mynheer Van der Pol**, the Chief Hydrographer of the States-General, who had spent thirty years sounding the banks of the Zuiderzee, and who could tell the depth of a channel by the smell of the weed on his lead-line;

From Prussia came a young major of the General Staff, **Baron von Humboldt**, who carried no luggage but three barometers, six thermometers, and a collection of dried mountain mosses packed in tin cylinders;

And from London came **Sir Arthur Vane**, now Lord Chief Baron of the Exchequer, accompanied by six Fellows of the Royal Society with their gold-headed canes and their portfolios of spherical logarithms.

They did not meet in a palace.

They met in the great printing-room of the Bridge-House, where the three copper-plate presses were working twelve hours a day, printing the **Atlas of the Three Realms** for the merchantmen of the Atlantic.

The sixty-four sheets of the Atlas were pinned against the white stone walls of the chamber.

They covered twelve hundred square feet of surface.

On the eastern sheets ran the coast: forty leagues of granite, shingle, and deep water, with every sounding, every tidal rip, and every light-vessel laid down in its true astronomical position from the meridian of Greenwich.

On the western sheets ran the new port: the seven leagues of the deep sound, the three granite basins, the railway approaches, and the sixty-four anchorages where the American trade was already crowding the water.

And between them—spanning the three-foot plates with an astonishing, monumental continuity that seemed to ignore the boundaries of the calfskins—ran the interior:

The forty-seven stations of the baseline;

The secondary triangles of the High Weald;

The contour-lines of the mountain gorges, drawn at intervals of twenty cubits of elevation;

The geological outcrops: the limestone shown in blue wash, the coal-measures in black cross-hatching, the red sandstone in vermilion, and the granite of the Skiffs in cold carmine;

And every road, every railway, every canal-lock, every church-spire, and every common sheep-walk that had been measured by the chain between the two oceans.

Monsieur de Cassini stood before the sheet of the **Devil’s Throat** for an hour.

He had a round magnifying lens of green glass in his hand, through which he inspected the fine, engraved hachures of the thirty-two switchbacks and the great stone arch of the Eagle’s Crag.

He did not speak to the Fellows of the Royal Society.

He turned to Maren Brand, where she stood by the ink-press with her leather apron over her blue gown.

"Madame," the Frenchman said, his voice dropping into that delicate, crystalline precision that French scholars use when they are forced to praise an islander. "This is not cartography."

The Fellows of the Royal Society shifted their feet; Sir Arthur Vane's eyebrows drew together over his gold spectacles.

"It is not cartography," Cassini repeated, his hand sweeping across the sixty-four plates. "It is **anatomy**."

He touched the copper plate of the Sallow Bridge.

"In France," he said, "we have the great map of my grandfather. It is magnificent; it has twenty thousand church-steeples; it has the royal forests; it has the routes of the post-wagons. But it is an exterior. It is the skin of the King’s kingdom, laid upon paper as a lady lays a silk scarf upon her dressing-table."

He looked at Maren’s brown hands.

"You have drawn the **skeleton**," he said. "You have drawn the bones of the earth where they break through the flesh. You have shown why the water must run here; why the road must climb there; why the city must be born upon this rock and not upon that mud."

He turned to the young Prussian, Humboldt, who was writing in his notebook with a silver pencil.

"Tell me, Baron," Cassini asked. "Have you found an error of three seconds in the triangles of the Scarp?"

The young man looked up from his page. His eyes were bright, clear, with the enthusiastic fire of a student who has seen a new science born before his eyes.

"There is no error of three seconds, Monsieur," Humboldt answered.

"No error?"

"I have recalculated the forty-seventh station," the German said, "by the new method of the least squares. Madame Brand’s eleven seconds was not a mistake of the instrument. It was the **terrestrial refraction** of the warm air from the marsh. If you apply the correction for the temperature of the soil, the triangle closes within two-tenths of a second."

He stepped to the table. He bowed to Maren—not a courtier’s bow, but the deep, reverent salutation of a philosopher before the work of a master.

"You have measured the air, Madame," he said. "As well as the stone."

Maren Brand smiled.

It was the same quiet, grave, slightly ironic smile that she had given to Master Coram when he had asked her if the sand-run could move.

"The air was in the way, Baron," she said simply. "We had to measure it to find the rock."

### Chapter 29

In her forty-second year, Maren Brand climbed the **Great Gable** for the last time.

She did not go alone.

She was accompanied by Peter Vane—now Chief Surveyor of the North, with two grey streaks in his dark beard and the bronze medal of the Royal Society at his button-hole;

And by her sister Clara’s grandson, little **Thomas**, a boy of nine who carried her ivory ruling pen in a wooden case slung from his neck by a leather cord.

The autumn day was still, cold, and brilliant with that thin, dry mountain light that makes forty miles of country look like a miniature landscape carved in ivory and box-wood.

They sat upon the stones of the summit cairn, where she had spent three nights in the turf hut twenty years before.

The turf hut was gone.

In its place stood an **observatory of the Concord**: a small, round tower of white limestone, twelve feet high, with a copper dome that turned upon iron rollers to follow the stars.

Inside the tower sat an eighteen-inch transit-circle by Troughton—the great instrument with which the young men of the College were measuring the arc of the meridian from the northern firths down to the English Channel.

Maren did not go into the tower.

She sat upon the flat ledge of rock that looked toward the east.

The world lay beneath her feet like an open roll.

There was the sea: forty leagues away, a strip of pale, glittering silver under the morning sun, upon which the white sails of the Baltic coasters looked like flecks of sea-foam caught in the wind.

There was the town of **Marrow-on-the-Bar**.

It was no longer a huddled cluster of dark fish-houses and rotting timber jetties behind an iron boom.

The breach in the wall had become a great avenue of stone houses two miles long; the South Quay had three stone basins where eighty three-masters were loading the coal and the iron; and through the green meadows of the reclaimed fens ran the three silver lines of the new navigation canals, their locks gleaming like mirrors in the sun.

There was the **Inland Line**.

She could trace it from the salt-gate to the mountain foot:

The straight, white ramp of the gravel;

The seven arches of the Sallow Bridge, where the water-wheels of the workshops were turning in the clear stream;

The deep, clean cutting through the limestone terraces;

The thirty-two switchbacks of the Devil’s Throat, where the iron rails glittered like two threads of steel pinned against the white face of the cliff;

And the great arch of the Eagle's Crag, hanging four hundred cubits above the blue shadow of the abyss, as light and perfect as the day the timber wedges were struck from the voussoirs.

Along the line, moving with the slow, steady, magnificent power of an elemental force, came the trains.

Three of them.

Their black engines throwing plumes of white steam into the cold air, their iron carriages loaded with the lead of the mines, the wool of the high valleys, the grain of the southern shires, and the timber of the western forests.

They met upon the Sallow Causeway.

They passed one another upon the double iron way with a deep, musical rumble of iron upon iron that came up to the mountain summit like the sound of an ancient bell struck under water.

Little Thomas sat between her knees, his small, brown hands holding the wooden case of her pen.

"Grandmother Maren," the boy said, his eyes following the white smoke of the train across the plain.

"Yes, Thomas?"

"Did you draw that?"

Maren looked down at the boy’s dark hair, which was cut blunt at his shoulders with tailoring shears, just as her own had been when she had stood at the second bench beneath the green lancets of the South Tower.

"No, Thomas," she said.

"The book says you drew it," the boy insisted, looking up into her face with his clear, grey eyes. "The book in the school says: *Sister Maren Brand laid down the line from the sea to the hill in the sixth year of her bond.*"

Maren took the wooden case from his neck.

She opened the lid.

The ivory ruling pen lay within, its brass barrel darkened by forty years of her own skin, its silver adjusting screw scored with the dent she had made when she had dropped it upon the flags of the scriptorium in her second winter.

She held the pen up before the boy’s eyes.

She held it against the vast, blue, glittering panorama of the two oceans, the three valleys, and the six leagues of the stone road.

"A pen does not make the road, Thomas," she said softly.

"What does, then?"

"The people who walk upon it," she answered.

She pointed to the Sallow Bridge, where a tiny, dark column of men was moving across the stone:

The carters with their whips;

The ditchers with their shovels;

The weavers with their bundles of finished cloth;

The pilots with their lead-lines;

And the young surveyors with their three-legged stands and their steel measuring-tapes, setting their pins into the gravel for the survey of the next province.

"The road was there before I was born, Thomas," she said, her voice dropping into that deep, quiet, ancestral cadence that belongs to the limestone crags. "It was in the stone; it was in the water; it was in the heart of the men who were weary of the gate."

She laid the ivory pen in the boy’s palm.

She closed his small, warm fingers around the brass barrel.

"I only scraped the paper," Maren Brand said. "So that they could see where their boots were going."

### Chapter 30

The last sheet of the **Great Survey of the World** was finished in the winter of her fiftieth year.

It was not a map of a province; it was the **General Chart of the Concord**, drawn to the scale of one inch to ten leagues, embracing the two oceans, the mountain barrier, the navigable sounds, and the forty-eight thousand square miles of the new territory.

It was midnight.

The Bridge-House was silent.

The water-wheel below had been stopped for the night; the three copper-plate presses were cold; the apprentices had gone to their beds in the long stone dormitories above the mill-stream.

A light burned in the western lancet.

Maren Brand sat at her twelve-foot slab of polished freestone.

She did not burn wax-ends now. She had a lamp of brass, made by the instrument-makers of Port Concord, fed with purified rape-seed oil that burned with a clean, steady, white flame that threw no shadow upon the vellum.

Across the stone lay the skin.

It was the largest calfskin she had ever seen: five feet across by four feet deep, taken from an ox that had grazed seven summers upon the high, sweet pastures of the Vale of Carrow, scraped until it was as thin and white as an altar-cloth.

The drawing was complete.

Every line was dry.

The coast; the hills; the road; the railway; the new city in the west; the ancient harbour in the east.

She held her crow-quill in her fingers.

She did not have her divider; she did not have her ivory rule.

She had only the small shell of red cinnabar that she had used since she was eighteen years old—the small sea-shell from the beach of the Skiffs that she had kept in her elm-wood box through thirty years of change.

She looked at the westernmost margin of the skin.

Beyond the islands of Port Concord; beyond the granite cliffs of the outer sound; out into the vast, black, unmeasured waste of the Atlantic, where the waves rolled three thousand miles from the ice-cliffs of the north without touching a rock or an iron beacon.

The paper there was white.

Clean.

Virgin.

Untouched by a single stroke of the pen.

She did not draw a sea-monster.

She did not draw a gilded fleur-de-lis with its tip pointing two degrees east of the true pole.

She dipped her quill into the cinnabar.

And there, upon the very edge of the skin—where the vellum was trimmed close to the grain of the hide, at the point where the world ended and the dark began—she wrote the last, unfinished question of the surveyor:

*Hic incipit terra ignota.*

*Here begins the unknown land.*

She laid down her pen.

She did not wipe the nib.

She blew out the brass lamp, and in the clean, white, glorious starlight that came down through the open louvers from the snows of the High Scarp, Maren Brand leaned her cheek against the cold vellum of her map, and listened to the river running through the dark below.
### Chapter 31

The zenith sector arrived from the Royal Observatory at Greenwich in three wagons padded with horsehair and dried sea-wrack.

It was not an instrument for the hand.

It had an arc of twelve feet, made of cast bell-metal three inches broad, swung from an iron pivot that was bedded into four tons of white freestone in the cellar of the Bridge-House. Its telescope was eight feet long, fitted with an object-glass by Dollond that had taken three years to grind, through which a surveyor could see the stars of the fourth magnitude in the middle of a June noon.

The man who had come to turn the screws was **Dr. Nevil Maskelyne**, the Astronomer Royal.

He was sixty years of age, wearing a coat of dark purple velvet with silver buttons that had been blackened by the soot of London coal-fires, his white wig dusted with wheat-flour that drifted down onto his velvet collar whenever he leaned his brow against the eyepiece.

He did not sleep by night.

He lived in the cellar from sunset until the morning prayer, while the water-wheel outside was stopped and the iron gates of the mill-race were closed, so that not a drop of water should shake the foundation of the sector.

Maren Brand stood beside him on the stone platform, holding a brass chronometer that beat half-seconds with a dry, sharp *click-click-click*.

"The star is in the field, Sister," Maskelyne whispered, his eye pressed to the leather guard. "Gamma Draconis. Thirty seconds before the transit."

Maren did not look at the star.

She watched the thin, bright thread of the silk plumb-line that hung thirty feet down through the center of the sector, suspended in a glass tube filled with spirits of wine to keep it from the drafts.

The plumb-line was loaded with three pounds of lead, shaped like a pear, hanging half an inch above the silver micrometer at the bottom of the arc.

"Twenty seconds," she counted.

"Level," Maskelyne said.

"Ten seconds."

*Click. Click. Click.*

"Transit," the Astronomer Royal said.

He did not lift his head. His right hand turned the micrometer screw half a division, reading the vernier through a small hand-magnifier:

"Fifty-one degrees, twenty-eight minutes, thirty-two seconds and four-tenths."

He rose from the bench, his old knees cracking like hazel-twigs. He took off his spectacles, wiped the sweat from the bridge of his thin, aristocratic nose with a silk handkerchief, and looked at Maren’s field-ledger where the observations of forty nights were recorded in two parallel columns.

"The plumb-line is leaning, Maren," he said softly.

"Leaning, Doctor?"

"Five seconds of an arc toward the west," he said.

He pointed through the floor-planks toward the dark silhouette of the High Scarp, which rose three leagues away into the starry sky like a wall of blue iron.

"The mountain is pulling the lead," he said. "The thirty million tons of white limestone in the cliffs of Harrow are drawing the thread toward their own mass. The plumb-line does not point to the center of the earth; it points to the center of the earth deflected by the stone of your road."

Maren looked at the slender thread of silk in its glass tube.

It hung straight—straight as the eye of an archer, straight as the plumb-line of a master mason.

Yet the stone was calling to it.

The great mountain that had been her wall and her gate was reaching out through three leagues of air, pulling the leaden weight toward its deep, cold ribs with that silent, invisible gravitation that keeps the moon in her orbit around the sea.

"Can you measure the pull?" she asked.

"We have measured it," Maskelyne answered, his voice trembling with that quiet, intellectual ecstasy that belongs to the philosopher who has caught the universe in an iron cage. "By comparing the zenith distance of the star at this bridge with the zenith distance at the Sea Station on the coast, we have found the deflection. It is five seconds and eight-tenths."

He laid his hand upon her drafting board.

"Do you know what that means, Sister Maren?"

"It means the baseline must be corrected for the curve of the geoid," she answered.

Maskelyne gave a small, dry laugh—the laugh of an Oxford don who has found his match in a cooper’s apprentice.

"It means more than that," he said. "It means we can weigh the mountain. If we know the cubic yards of limestone in the Scarp—which you have measured to the rood in your contour-plats—and if we know the deflection of the lead, we can calculate the **weight of the globe**."

He looked at her with his watery, brilliant eyes.

"We can tell the King how many tons of stone, iron, and water are contained within the round ball of this earth. We can tell him whether the core of the world is empty air, or water, or solid metal."

"And what is it, Doctor?" she asked.

"It is iron," the Astronomer Royal said, his hand resting upon the freestone pillar. "The center of the earth is solid iron, twice as heavy as the stone of Harrow. The whole world is an iron engine, Maren, swinging in the void by the laws of Mr. Newton."

Maren Brand looked down through the floorboards at the mill-race, where the black water was slipping through the sluice-gates into the reed-beds.

She did not contradict the Astronomer Royal.

He was a great man; he had the King’s ear; he had three degrees from Cambridge and forty volumes of the *Philosophical Transactions* upon his shelves in London.

But as she touched the cold bell-metal of the sector, she knew that the world was not merely iron.

Iron did not explain why the vellum remembered the sheep.

Iron did not explain why the ink had crept through the fibers of her scrap-skins to find the buried bridge while forty masters slept in their beds.

The world was iron, yes; but it was iron that had been touched by a hand.

It was a machine that knew when its children were walking upon its stones, and which answered the foot of the surveyor with the secret mathematics of its own ancient, unmeasured blood.

### Chapter 32

The first American packet-ship came into the sound of **Port Concord** on the fourteenth of July, in the sixteenth year of the Concord.

She was the *Covenant* of Philadelphia: three hundred tons, built of live-oak and white cedar from the Jersey swamps, her hull painted black with a broad white stripe along the gun-ports, carrying three skysails that towered ninety feet into the morning sun.

She did not drop her anchor in the roadstead.

She came straight in through the deep gut between the granite islands, her lead-line finding twenty fathoms where forty years before the Admiralty charts had shown only rocks and foam.

She laid her starboard side against the new granite wharf of the South Basin with a gentle, creaking shudder of her hemp fenders that did not even spill the tea in the captain’s cabin.

The captain was not a sailor of the King.

He was a Quaker from Nantucket named **Enoch Folger**, a tall, thin man of forty with a coat of unbleached grey homespun that had no collar, and a broad-brimmed beaver hat that he did not remove even when the Mayor of the town came down with his silver mace.

Beside him on the quarter-deck stood a young woman of twenty-eight, wearing a grey silk bonnet and a linen shawl pinned across her breast with a wooden needle.

In her hands she carried an iron-bound portfolio of maps.

Her name was **Hannah Folger**; she was the captain’s niece, and she had spent seven years in the Land Office at Pittsburgh, laying out the townships of the Ohio territory for the settlers from Virginia.

Maren Brand met them at the head of the basin stairs.

They did not go to the tavern; they did not go to the Council Chamber.

They went to the **Surveyor’s Office of the Port**: a long, low room built of red sandstone upon the pier-head, where twenty young draughtsmen were coloring the navigational sea-cards for the Atlantic fleet.

Hannah Folger laid her portfolio upon the freestone table.

"Thou art Maren Brand?" the young Quaker woman asked.

"I am Maren Brand," she said.

"I have carried thy Atlas across twelve hundred leagues of salt-water," Hannah said, her voice quiet, clear, with that uninflected, musical cadence that belongs to the meeting-houses of Pennsylvania. "My father bought it of a bookseller in Boston for forty silver dollars. We used the seventh plate to enter the sound this morning. The soundings were true to the fourth part of a fathom; the light-vessel was where thy plate placed it; and the beacon on the Red Head bore east-by-north half-north when our keel cleared the outer breakers."

She unstrapped her portfolio.

She lifted a sheet of paper four feet square, printed upon coarse, brownish paper that had been made from the linen shirts of the pioneers.

"This is our work," she said.

Maren looked at the map.

It was the **Plat of the Ohio Territory**.

It had no contours. It had no hills. It had no roads.

It was a grid.

A vast, relentless, terrifying grid of square miles, six miles to the township, thirty-six sections to the township, six hundred and forty acres to the section, running west from the Pennsylvania line across five hundred leagues of trackless virgin forest.

The lines were straight as the path of a musket-ball.

They cut through the oak-woods; they cut through the swamps; they cut through the hunting-grounds of the Shawnee; they cut through the ancient earthen mounds of the dead kings whose bones had turned to dust before the first ship came across the sea.

Every section had its number:

*Section sixteen for the school.*

*Section twenty-nine for the church.*

*The remainder for the land-office at two dollars the acre.*

Maren touched the paper with her finger.

The paper was rough, dry, smelling of pine-smoke and the coarse river-mud of the Monongahela.

"There are no rivers here," she said.

"The rivers are there," Hannah answered, pointing her pencil to a thin, winding line that crossed Section twelve and Section fourteen like an uninvited guest. "That is the Muskingum. It is forty rods wide, with three falls of six feet that will turn twelve pairs of stones."

"And the road?"

"There is no road," the Quaker woman said. "There is only the range-line. We drive the stakes through the cane-brakes; we notch the white oaks with three chops of the axe; and the settlers follow the marks with their ox-carts."

She looked up at Maren’s Great Plat where it hung upon the wall—the plat that showed the thirty-two switchbacks of the Devil’s Throat and the seven arches of the Sallow Causeway.

"Thy lines bend, Sister Maren," Hannah said.

"The ground bends, Hannah," Maren replied.

"In our country," the American woman said, her eyes fixed upon the grid, "we make the ground straight. If a hill stands in the middle of Section eight, the farmer must plow it down. If a creek runs across the range-line, he must bridge it with two spruce logs. We have ten thousand leagues of land between the mountains and the great western sea; we cannot stop for the stone. If we waited to measure every crag as thou hast measured Harrow, our children would die in the woods before the first township was enrolled."

Maren looked at the young woman’s hands.

They were not the hands of a limner.

They were the hands of an engineer: the skin brown, scarred at the wrist by the thorns of the blackberry-carrs, calloused at the palm from the heavy brass handle of the surveyor’s compass.

"Your grid is an empire, Hannah," Maren said softly.

"It is not an empire," Hannah answered. "It is a **peace**. When a man knows where his corner-post stands—when he has the certificate of the Land Office with the seal of the Republic upon it—he does not draw his knife upon his neighbor about the watering of a hog. The square makes all men equal. The rich man has his six hundred and forty acres; the poor man has his eighty; and the line between them is thirty-three feet of common road that belongs to neither."

Maren turned to the window.

Below, in the South Basin, the crew of the *Covenant* were unloading their cargo:

Four hundred barrels of salt-flour;

Two hundred barrels of potash from the cleared forests;

And forty bales of beaver-fleece from the Great Lakes.

Beside the ship stood three iron tram-cars from the furnace at the Scarp, loaded with the thin, bright steel saws of the new mills, waiting to carry the American timber to the sea.

The two worlds were looking at each other across the freestone table:

The old world of the mountain: where every stone had a name, where every line was a memory, where the road had to climb the cliff by thirty-two switchbacks because the rock was ancient and holy;

And the new world of the forest: where the land had no history, where the line was a straight edge of light cutting through the trees, where the future was an infinite checker-board of square farms waiting for the plow.

Maren took her ivory ruling pen from her pocket.

She laid it upon the Ohio plat, beside Hannah Folger’s brass compass.

"We need both, Sister," Maren said.

"Both?"

"A man cannot live in a square alone," Maren said. "A square has no shadow; it has no spring; it has no memory of the frost. And a man cannot live forever in the ravine, looking up at the bells of his grandfather’s church."

She took the Quaker woman’s hand in her own.

"Take our Atlas to your Ohio, Hannah," she said. "Show your surveyors how to draw the contours where the land begins to rise toward the high plateaus. And leave your grid upon my table, so that my apprentices may remember that there are lands where a man may walk for forty days without ever seeing the stone of an ancient gate."

Hannah Folger looked down at the ivory pen.

She did not take it; she touched the old, yellow bone with her thumb, feeling the dent where the Swedish screw had been set sixty years ago.

"The measure is one, Maren Brand," the Quaker woman whispered.

"The measure is one," Maren answered. "And the earth is wide enough for all its lines."

### Chapter 33

The great lawsuit of the **Flock-Masters against the Iron Company** was heard in the Great Hall of the Guildhall at Marrow in the twentieth year of the Concord.

It was the largest court that had ever sat in the northern shires.

There were seven judges upon the bench, headed by Sir Arthur Vane, who was now eighty years of age, his purple velvet gown trimmed with ermine that had grown yellow with the damp of the northern circuits, his gold-headed cane resting between his knees like an ancient sceptre.

On the left of the hall sat the directors of the **Great Northern Iron and Coal Company**:

Twelve men in black broadcloth coats with silk waistcoats and heavy gold watch-chains, their tables covered with models of steam-engines, samples of iron ore from the Vale of Carrow, and bundles of shares in the new railway that had been chartered to run from the sea-gate to the western sound.

On the right sat **Adam of the High Heaf**, ninety-four years old, sitting upon an ash stool that had been brought from his own kitchen, his three white plaits of hair reaching to his knees, surrounded by fifty flock-masters in their grey frieze tunics and their ox-hide leggings.

Between them, on the stone table where the condemned sheets had once been registered, lay the **Great Plat of the Province**.

The Attorney-General had spoken for three days.

He was a barrister from London, whose wig was so large that it covered his ears like an iron basket, his voice filling the vaulted roof with an endless, rolling cataract of statutes, precedents, and royal proclamations:

*...And whereas by the Act of the twenty-fourth of the King, the mineral rights of all lands deemed waste, unenclosed, and uncultivated were vested in the commissioners of the Crown; and whereas the Great Northern Iron Company have by their great industry, skill, and capital, expended forty thousand pounds in the sinking of three pits at the Red Ghyll and the erection of four blast-furnaces at the foot of the Scarp: It is contrary to the public interest, contrary to the prosperity of this realm, and contrary to the spirit of the age, that fifty ignorant shepherds should obstruct the laying of the iron way across their pastures by reason of an obsolete custom that has no record in the registers of Westminster...*

Sir Arthur Vane looked over his gold spectacles.

"Has the Company surveyed the line of the railway?" the Chief Baron asked.

"We have, my Lord," the barrister answered, holding up a paper four yards long. "Drawn by Mr. Telford’s principal assistant, upon the imperial scale of twenty inches to the mile, showing every cutting, every embankment, and every bridge."

"And does the line cross the pasture of the High Heaf?"

"It crosses three miles of stony heather, my Lord," the lawyer said, his lip curling with professional disdain. "Where nothing grows but dwarf-gorse and ling that would not feed twenty rabbits. The Company has offered to pay the shepherds forty silver shillings an acre for the surface-damage, which is three times the value of the fee-simple."

Sir Arthur turned his head toward the corner where Maren Brand sat beside Peter Vane.

"Sister Maren," the old judge said.

The great hall fell into an absolute, breathless silence.

The two hundred merchants from the exchange; the fifty flock-masters from the hills; the young apprentices from the Bridge-House: they all turned their eyes upon the woman who had drawn the first line thirty years ago.

She was fifty-four now.

Her dark hair was entirely grey, cut blunt at her shoulders with the same shears she had used in her youth; her face was brown, lined with the deep, clean, geometric wrinkles that come from thirty winters of looking across the snow-glare of the summits; her blue gown was simple, without a ribbon, without a pin of silver, her only ornament the small ivory ruling pen that hung from her neck by its hemp cord.

She rose from the bench.

She did not carry a law-book.

She carried the **Register of the Boon of the Crags**: the three ancient sheepskins that she had cleaned and mounted upon linen twenty years ago.

She walked to the table.

She laid the sheepskins beside the Company’s printed map.

"The forty-second clause of the Boon," she said, her voice clear, low, and filling the vast, stone-flagged room without an effort.

"Read it, Sister," Sir Arthur said.

"When the ram is turned upon the hill at Michaelmas," she read, "he shall have his six cubits of dry stone on either side of the brook, from the spring of the Red Ghyll to the mouth of the mere; and no man shall build a wall, nor dig a ditch, nor kindle a fire within seventy paces of the sheep-walk, under pain of the Great Ban."

The Attorney-General gave a short, contemptuous laugh.

"A sheep’s custom, my Lord!" he cried. "Are the courts of the King to be governed by the rights of a ram?"

Maren Brand turned her eyes upon the lawyer.

They were large, dark, and terribly clear—the eyes that had seen the beacon floating upon the air of the marsh, and which had known that the light was lying before the paper could be blotted.

"It is not a sheep's custom, sir," she said.

"What is it, then?"

"It is the **stability of the mountain**," she answered.

She unrolled the geological plate of the Vale of Carrow.

"The pasture of the High Heaf," she said, her finger tracing the blue wash of the limestone where it met the red sandstone of the ghyll, "is not waste. It is a drystone terrace, two hundred cubits thick, resting upon a bed of blue shale that dips twenty degrees toward the south-west."

The courtroom was dead still.

"If you cut the railway through that heather," she continued, "you cut the toe of the slope. You remove forty thousand tons of stone that have held the shale in its bed since the Great Frost. And if you kindle the fires of four blast-furnaces at the mouth of the ravine, the heat will dry the marl; the first autumn rain will turn the shale into grease; and the whole mountain of the High Heaf—with your four pits, your forty thousand pounds, your steam-engines, and your iron way—will slide three hundred cubits into the Vale of Carrow before the winter term is entered."

The directors of the Iron Company sat motionless.

Their chief engineer—a heavy, middle-aged man from Newcastle with a gold pencil-case in his hand—leaned over the table, his eyes glued to the geological section beneath her thumb.

His face had lost its smooth, commercial red; it was the color of grey slag.

"My God," the engineer whispered to the barrister. "She is right."

"Right?" the lawyer muttered.

"The dip," the engineer said, his hand trembling as he touched the paper. "We took the bore-holes only thirty feet into the rock. We thought the limestone was bedded on the granite. If the shale is there—if the blue marl runs beneath the sandstone—the first cutting will pull the whole crag down upon the line."

He rose from his seat.

He did not look at the directors; he did not look at the Attorney-General.

He walked across the hall, bowed to Maren Brand, and took the red pencil from behind his ear.

"Where does the line go, Sister?" the Newcastle engineer asked.

Maren took her ivory rule.

She did not consult the flock-masters; she did not consult the Company.

She drew a line three inches to the north—a line that curved along the dry, hard rib of the red sandstone, where the rock was solid to the roots of the earth, avoiding the sheep-walk, avoiding the shale, avoiding the ancient pastures of the Boon.

"The line goes there, sir," she said. "It will cost three thousand pounds more for the masonry of the viaduct; but your engines will run upon stone that has not moved since the world was made."

Sir Arthur Vane leaned back into his ermine cushions.

He did not consult the seven judges upon the bench.

He lifted his gold-headed cane and struck the freestone table with a sharp, clear, musical *tink*.

"The plat is amended," the Lord Chief Baron pronounced. "Let the railway be built upon the stone; and let the sheep graze upon the grass. The Court is adjourned."

### Chapter 34

The trial of the **Masterwork of Thomas Brand** was held at the Bridge-House on the first day of May, in the twenty-fifth year of the Concord.

There were forty candidates.

They were the first generation that had never seen the Kiln; the first generation that had never known an unfree bond; the first generation that had been born into a province where the road was as natural as the sea.

They stood in the great printing-room, each behind his own bench, with three skins of the white calfskin of Harrow and a fresh jar of walnut-ink before them.

Maren Brand sat at the Master’s desk in the center of the hall.

She did not wear spectacles; her eyes were as sharp at fifty-nine as they had been when she was twelve, though she had to hold the vellum three inches further from her cheek to read the microscopic chain-dots in the twilight.

Beside her sat Peter Vane and Adam of the Heaf’s grandson, who was now a Professor of Geology in the new College of Port Concord.

"The candidate Thomas Brand," Peter Vane called out.

The young man stepped forward.

He was nineteen years of age, tall, slender, with his grandmother’s dark hair and his great-grandfather’s square, capable thumbs. He wore a coat of dark blue wool with silver buttons engraved with the two hands, his stockings clean and white, his shoes made of supple deer-hide that had no mud upon the buckles.

He carried an oak board four feet square, covered with a linen cloth.

"What is your work, candidate?" Maren asked, her voice dropping into the ancient, traditional formula of the Guild.

"The **Survey of the Outer Skiffs**," the boy answered, his voice clear, steady, and without a tremor.

He lifted the linen cloth.

The forty candidates drew in their breath together—a long, soft, collective sigh that rustled through the great room like the wind through the reed-beds.

It was not a copy.

It was the **Head of Skiffs**; the Cow; the Calf; the Black Ledge; and the forty leagues of salt water that Maren had drawn four hundred times in her youth.

But it was drawn with the **new science**.

The soundings were not written in figures; they were shown by **contour-lines of depth**:

Five fathoms shown in pale, transparent blue;

Ten fathoms in deeper sapphire;

Twenty fathoms in dark, cold indigo;

Forty fathoms in black.

The reefs were not conventional drawings of teeth; they were geological plans, showing the dip and strike of the granite ribs where they plunged beneath the water.

And on the margin, where the old Chancery rolls had carried the three cinnabar rays of the beacon, was a drawing of the **New Lighthouse of the Concord**:

A tower of white freestone from Harrow, ninety feet high, built upon the granite of the Outer Skiff, its stones dove-tailed into one another with oak pins that had been wedged with lead, defying the Atlantic swell that broke against its base in sixty feet of green foam.

Maren Brand did not look at the lighthouse.

She took her magnifying lens—the green Venetian crystal that had lived in her pocket for forty years.

She leaned down over the paper.

She looked at the fairway between the Cow and the Calf.

The lines were sixty to the inch.

They were drawn with an iron pen whose nib was as sharp as a needle, yet every stroke was round, full, without a pool at the turn, without a feather in the grain, without a single drop of grease upon the vellum.

And there, in the narrowest part of the gut, where the current ran at six knots between the kelp-beds, was a small, square benchmark cut into the granite of the ledge:

**+**

The boy’s mark.

"Why is the benchmark there, Thomas?" she asked, not lifting her eye from the glass.

"Because the granite is drying there, Grandmother," the boy answered.

"Drying?"

"The land is rising," Thomas said.

Maren lifted her head. Her green crystal lens remained poised three inches above the skin.

"Rising?"

"Two inches in forty years," the young man said, his eyes bright with that calm, fearless certainty that belongs to the third generation. "I compared the soundings of the First Survey with the levels of the forty-seventh station. The sea has not fallen; the water at the mouth of the Sallow is where it was when Master Coram was an apprentice. But the **rock** has come up. The mountain is lifting its feet out of the sea."

He pointed to the contours of the seabed:

"The whole province," he said, "from the Skiffs to the Divide of the Waters, is tilting toward the east. The hills are going down; the coast is coming up; and in three hundred years, the channel between the Cow and the Calf will be a dry meadow where sheep can graze at low water."

Peter Vane leaned forward, his grey beard brushing the vellum.

"A boy’s speculation," he muttered.

"It is not a speculation, Master Peter," the boy answered firmly. "Look at the barnacles on the old beacon-post. The shell-line of the Great Frost is eighteen inches above the highest spring-tide today. The sea does not lie; and the barnacle does not read the charters."

Maren Brand sat back in her chair.

She looked at the forty candidates; she looked at the great copper presses; she looked through the open lancet windows at the white line of the road where the third morning train was rolling toward the hills with forty tons of iron plate.

The coast was moving.

The granite that had been her symbol of eternity—the rock that never moved, the shore that had held her in its salt-bond for six years of silence—was itself a wave.

A slow, majestic, unhurried wave of stone that rose and fell through ten thousand centuries, carrying the towns, the roads, the lighthouses, and the charters upon its breast like foam upon the tide.

She rose from the Master’s seat.

She took the silver compass of the Concord from her own neck—the compass that Master Coram had taken from his own collar on the day the wall was broken.

She did not speak.

She hung the silver chain about the boy’s shoulders, over his blue wool coat and his linen cravat, until the heavy metal rested against his breast.

"The Masterwork is approved," Maren Brand said, her voice clear and carrying through the great hall like the stroke of a silver bell.

"Take your board, Brother Thomas."

She touched the boy's cheek with her brown, scarred fingers.

"The land is rising," she whispered. "Keep your chains long enough to reach the water."

### Chapter 35

On the evening of her sixty-fourth birthday, Maren Brand walked alone to the **Sallow Bridge**.

It was the third of November.

The first thin, dry frost had come down from the mountains, leaving a crust of white rime upon the limestone copings of the causeway that glittered in the starlight like diamond-dust.

The wind was from the north-west, cold, smelling of the dry heather of the high moors and the clean, salt-less water of the cataracts.

She did not carry her instruments.

She had left her theodolite in the museum; she had left her ruling pen in Thomas’s drafting box; she had left her silver compass upon the table of the Registry.

She carried only a walking-staff of seasoned ash, tipped with iron, which her father had cut from the hedge at the Tanners’ Ditch on the morning she was bound to the Chancery.

She sat upon the parapet of the central arch, precisely where the relief of the two hands met the water of the river.

The river was quiet tonight.

The water-wheels of the workshops were still; the trains had ceased to run until the four o'clock mail; the ditchers had banked their turf-fires in the cottages of the fens and were asleep.

Below her boots, the Sallow ran through its seven arches with a soft, continuous, musical murmur that sounded like the whispering of forty thousand women in a cathedral of stone.

She looked toward the east.

There was the town of Marrow.

It was no longer dark.

A vast, glowing, golden crescent of light stretched for three leagues along the shore, where the gas-lamps of the new streets, the iron-foundries of the North Reach, and the lanterns of two hundred ships in the deep basins were burning together against the night.

A great, living city of fifty thousand souls, where once there had been only thirty tanners' pits and forty cords of drying herring.

She looked toward the west.

There was the **High Scarp**.

It rose into the starry sky, white, silent, monumental, its thirty-two switchbacks marked by the tiny, twinkling points of light from the refuge-towers where the line-watchers kept their vigils through the winter frost.

And above the Scarp, high upon the dark saddle between the crags, burned the six great lanterns of the tower of St. Michael-of-the-Crags.

They were not warning ships away from the rocks.

They were welcoming the world to the hills.

Maren Brand leaned her two hands upon the head of her ash-staff.

She had lived sixty-four years.

She had seen the paper burned; she had seen the wall broken; she had seen the mountain opened; she had seen the two oceans brought together by thirty leagues of stone and iron.

She had thought, in her youth, that she was fighting the Guild.

She had thought she was fighting the aldermen, the bailiffs, the King’s commissioners, and the ancient, blind stupidity of men who wanted to keep the gate closed so that they might count their copper groats in the dark.

She knew better now.

She had not been fighting men.

She had been fighting the **fear of the line**.

The terror that human beings feel when they stand upon the edge of the unmeasured; the fear that if they step beyond the ditch, the ground will give way beneath their boots; the terror that makes them build walls, invent charters, and burn maps, so that they may pretend that the world ends where their own sheep have cropped the grass.

She had drawn the road not to conquer the mountain, but to take away the fear.

To show them that the earth was sound; that the mire had a bottom; that the stone was waiting for the arch; that the people on the other side of the hill had the same eyes, the same bread, and the same children as the people of the coast.

A sound came from the road behind her.

A horse was trotting down the gravel from the town.

It was not a soldier; it was an apprentice from the Bridge-House, riding an old grey pony with a lantern hung from his stirrup-leather.

The boy saw her sitting upon the parapet in the starlight.

He checked his beast, his boots slipping from the irons as he dropped to the ground.

"Sister Maren?" the boy asked, his voice timid in the vast, cold silence of the mere.

"Yes, my son."

"They have brought a new roll from the South Tower," the boy said, holding out a tube of parchment tied with green silk. "From the old cellar where the Chancery kept the sea-cards. They found it behind the brickwork of the Kiln when they pulled down the chimney to widen the street."

Maren took the tube.

She did not untie the silk; her fingers knew the parchment by the feel of the grain in the dark.

It was an old skin of third-grade calfskin, scraped thin with the lunellum blade, smelling faintly of ancient sea-coal and the damp river-silt of sixty years ago.

Her own sheet.

The copy of the **Head of Skiffs** that she had been drawing on the morning Master Coram had told her that the sand-run could not move.

She held the skin up to the starlight.

The black ink was still there:

The seven strokes for the storm-shelf;

The five strokes for the sand-run;

The three rays of red cinnabar for the lantern on the Outer Skiff.

And in the lower corner, where she had laid her hand upon the board while her heart was beating against the iron tongue of her key, was a small, round grease-spot where her own nineteen-year-old thumb had rested upon the margin.

She looked at the drawing of the granite.

It was beautiful.

It was clean, precise, geometrically flawless, drawn with that terrifying, disciplined patience that can copy the same rock four hundred times without an error of a hair.

She did not tear it. She did not throw it into the water of the Sallow.

She rolled the skin, slipped it back into its green silk tie, and handed it back to the boy.

"Carry it to the school, lad," she said.

"To the school, Sister?"

"Tell the Master of the Bench to hang it upon the wall," Maren Brand said, her voice quiet as the falling of the frost. "Beside the great plate of the railway and the chart of the western sound."

"And what shall we write upon the frame?" the boy asked.

Maren rose from the parapet.

She stood upon her feet, her body straight as an iron jumper, her grey hair catching the starlight like the rime upon the limestone coping.

She looked along the white line of the road where it ran straight through the Great Sough toward the mountains—the road that had had no name, the road that had been drawn in the night, the road that had set the kingdom free.

"Write this," she said:

**HERE STOOD THE APPRENTICE.**

**SHE THOUGHT THE SHORE WAS ETERNAL.**

**UNTIL SHE FOUND THAT THE INK WAS LIVING.**

She turned her face toward the west.

She set her ash-staff upon the limestone gravel of the road, and with a light, unhesitating, peaceful step that made no more sound upon the stone than the passage of a summer cloud across the crags of Harrow, Maren Brand walked out into the vast, beautiful, unmeasured night.

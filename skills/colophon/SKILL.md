# Colophon — the package

The colophon is what the book says about itself. This skill produces the materials that
carry a manuscript from private draft into professional hands: the pitch, the synopsis, the
query, the cover direction, the submission framing.

Everything must agree — same title, same author name, same genre promise, same reader
promise, same position on the shelf.

## Inputs

At minimum: the finished manuscript or a detailed synopsis, the genre and category, the
target reader, and the author name.
Ideally also: the scored evaluation, a panel or swarm report, comp titles, an author bio,
rights status, and any visual preferences.

## Output

1. Logline
2. Back-cover copy
3. Editorial synopsis
4. Query letter
5. Cover brief
6. Submission checklist
7. Package risk notes

---

## 1. Logline

About twenty-five words. One sentence that sells the book in a pitch, an email, a website,
or a bio.

Shape: protagonist, central conflict, stakes.

Test it out loud: if the listener says *tell me more*, it works. If they say *interesting*
and change the subject, it failed.

## 2. Back-cover copy

One hundred to one hundred and eighty words. This sells the reading experience, not a plot
summary. Four movements: the situation that creates immediate interest; what kind of book
this is; why this book rather than a generic version of the same premise; and who it is for
and why it matters now.

Rules: never reveal the ending or spoil a reversal; use the target reader's own language;
make the last sentence create urgency, dread, desire, recognition, or curiosity; and avoid
unanchored praise — *gripping* and *unforgettable* mean nothing unless tied to something
specific.

Adapt by genre. Memoir leads on vulnerability and the cost of change. Thriller leads on
impossible pressure and time. Speculative fiction leads on the world's rule, the dilemma,
and the scale. Nonfiction leads on a surprising claim with a practical consequence.
Literary fiction leads on voice, situation, and a contradiction the reader recognises.

## 3. Editorial synopsis

Three hundred to seven hundred words, depending on market. It shows a professional what
happens, ending included.

Cover, in order: genre and word count; premise; protagonist and what they want; the central
conflict; escalation; climax; resolution; market positioning; comps.

Rules: reveal the ending — professionals reject coy synopses. Do not write jacket copy here.
Keep the tone professional rather than academic. Mention a stylistic device only if it
affects positioning, and name the core transformation or the final unresolved question.

## 4. Query letter

Two hundred and fifty to four hundred words, aimed at getting pages requested.

Six parts: a hook; a compact pitch; market positioning and comps; why the author is the one
to write it; the practical details; a courteous close.

The practical details are exact: genre and category, word count, completion status, rights
status, series or standalone, author name, and a contact line.

Do not open with *I am writing to submit my manuscript*. Open inside the story.

Comp rules: prefer titles from the last five years; use at least one from the target market
where possible; never compare yourself to an untouchable classic; and always explain the
comparison — the institutional paranoia of one book with the voice-driven grief of another.

Personalise briefly: research what this agent or editor has recently published or asked for,
name one specific title, list, or stated interest, and keep it to proving fit rather than
flattery.

## 5. Cover brief

For a designer or an image model.

**Positioning** — genre and category, target shelf, reader expectation, thumbnail priority,
and whether ebook or print leads.

**Emotional concept** — one paragraph on what the cover should make a reader feel before
they know anything about the plot.

**Visual direction** — primary and secondary elements, composition, lighting, texture,
degree of realism, and what must stay legible at thumbnail size.

**Colour** — dominant, secondary, accent, and what to avoid.

**Typography** — title and author hierarchy, any series treatment, and the mood (literary,
commercial, thriller, cosy, epic, intimate).

**References** — three to five covers: one for colour, one for typography, one for
composition, one from the same shelf, and optionally one anti-reference.

**Image prompt** — where an image model is used, describe style, subject, environment,
mood, lighting, composition, palette, negative space for the title, cover art framing, and
aspect ratio, with no text. Set the type afterwards in layout software; never trust a model
with final typography.

## 6. Submission checklist

Title and author consistent everywhere · word count verified · category stated · rights
stated · manuscript complete or its status disclosed · query inside the word ceiling ·
synopsis reveals the ending · comps current · cover brief matched to the shelf · any
sensitivity, legal, or factual review needs listed · next recipient and action named.

## 7. Package risk notes

Close with short risks: unclear genre, wrong comps, inflated claims, sensitivity exposure,
factual or legal exposure, title or author inconsistency, a cover that mismatches the
market, and a manuscript that is not actually ready. Do not hide blockers — a useful
package is an honest one.

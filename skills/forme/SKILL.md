# Forme — the foundation document

The forme is the locked page the rest of the book is printed onto. Everything downstream
inherits its limits, so this is the one document worth being slow about. It answers:
who is this book about, what does it make the reader feel, how fast does it move, and
what question does it refuse to answer?

Prose can only ever be as strong as these choices allow. Beautiful sentences wrapped
around hollow people produce a book nobody remembers. People with real inner lives and
a theme made of slogans produce disposable entertainment. Feeling with nothing under it
becomes melodrama. The three have to be built together, because each one is the other
two's evidence.

**Input:** the premise, genre, tone, and at least the protagonist.
**Output:** `foundation.md`, the permanent reference for the whole run.

---

## 1. Character

Work outward from the wound. For every character who carries scenes, fill these in
before drafting a line of them.

**The wound.** The formative event or repeating pattern that shaped how they see the
world. It does not have to appear on the page. You have to know it. Ask what they never
got over.

**The lie.** The false belief the wound installed. They act on it until the arc forces
a collision. Abandonment becomes *if I am flawless, nobody leaves.*

**The want.** What they believe they are chasing, stated plainly and concretely: the
job, the diagnosis, the brother.

**The need.** What would actually settle them. This usually contradicts the want, and
the arc happens where the need overtakes the want — or does not, and the book turns
tragic.

**The contradictions.** At least two per principal character, and the kind that make
them human rather than inconsistent: patient with strangers and short with family,
brave in company and frightened alone.

**The voice.** How they speak and think — word choice, rhythm, verbal tics, formality.
The test is to cover the name and still know who is talking. If you cannot, the voices
are a single voice pretending to be several.

**The arc.** Where they start and where they land. Positive: the lie breaks and the need
is accepted. Negative: the lie wins. Flat: the character holds still while the world
around them moves (rare, and hard to earn).

**The name.** Chosen, not sampled. Language models draw names from probability peaks
rather than generating them, so the same clusters recur across unrelated projects —
*Elara Voss*, *Aiden Sinclair*, *Marcus Chen*, *Amara Okafor*, and across model families
*Thorne, Kael, Vael, Garrick, Voss, Hale, Aldren*. The same applies to places (*Hollow*,
*Miller*, *Ash*, *Raven*) and to anything the book has to invent a word for.

A name needs a somewhere: a language, a region, a family, a class, an era. That is also
what makes a cast feel authored — the specificity is the point, not the sound. If the
whole cast comes from the cluster, the names were not chosen. Naming research and the
full list are in the deslopify guide, **The Default Name**; rule `A26` flags it for a
human to rule on rather than failing a manuscript over a surname.

### Nonfiction and memoir

The narrator is the principal character, and the same seven questions apply to them: the
wound that set the book going, the belief they held, what they pursued, what they
actually required, where they sabotaged themselves, how they tell it, and what changed
between page one and the end.

Secondary figures — parents, doctors, friends — need only three things: a wound, a
voice, and a narrative function (what they stand for in the narrator's story).

### Relationships as mirrors

Every significant relationship should expose a face of the protagonist that solitude
cannot. Map what each one reveals: the mother exposes vulnerability, the therapist
exposes resistance, the ex exposes the pattern. If a relationship reveals nothing, it is
scenery.

## 2. Voice construction

A deep character speaking generic prose is a well-drawn puppet. Voice has to be built,
not hoped for.

**When the author is the narrator (memoir, personal nonfiction).** Ask for three to five
pages of unguarded writing — long emails, journal entries, transcribed voice notes,
anything written without performance. Loose writing shows the real voice. From it,
extract a working guide:

```
VOICE GUIDE — [project]

Average sentence length: [short / medium / long]
Preferred sentence shape: [simple / compound / mixed]
Register: [colloquial / mixed / formal]
Words they actually use: [list]
Words they would never use: [list]
Recurring tics: [list]
Irony level: [none / light / constant / sharp]
How they handle emotion: [direct / evasive / deflects with humour / abstract]
Rhythm: [staccato / legato / mixed]
Punctuation habits: [ellipses, dashes, hard stops]
Where their comparisons come from: [music, games, work, childhood]
```

Turn the guide into rules you can check against: *most sentences under fifteen words,
anything past twenty-five is deliberate*; *never "profound," "resonate," "transcend"*;
*humour arrives when things are worst, never when they are easy*; *references come from
technology and a nineties childhood, never from the canon*.

**When there is no sample (fiction, ghostwriting).** Name two or three published voices
close to the target, say precisely what to take from each and what to leave, then add
something the references do not have. Borrowing alone produces pastiche.

**Drift check.** Voice decays across long sessions. At the start of each writing session,
re-read the guide and the previous chapter's last pages. After each new chapter, compare
its opening paragraph against its closing one. Same speaker? If not, find where it
shifted and fix it before continuing.

## 3. Pace

Pace is how much time the reader *feels* pass, which is separate from sentence rhythm.

**Vary length.** Chapters that all run the same length read like a metronome. Follow a
long dense chapter with a short bare one. Let the shortest chapter sit at least 40%
below the average and the longest no more than 50% above.

Paragraphs and sentences work the same way: a one-line paragraph between thick ones is
a visual beat; five identically sized paragraphs are a drone; the single-word paragraph
gets two uses in the whole book before it becomes a tic. A long sentence that
accumulates detail followed by a short flat one is where rhythm actually comes from.

**Alternate scene and reflection.** Fiction: scene, scene, reflection, scene, brief
reflection, scene. Never three action scenes without a pause to process, never two
reflections in a row. Nonfiction and memoir: narrative, evidence, personal reflection,
narrative, evidence, provisional conclusion — never three statistics in a row (that is
a report) and never three personal reflections without narrative or data anchoring them
(that is a diary).

**Density by genre** (starting points, not rules):

| Genre | Average chapter | Tilt |
|---|---|---|
| Thriller | 2,000–3,000 | heavy action, light reflection, short chapters, constant hooks |
| Memoir | 3,000–5,000 | roughly 60/40 narrative to reflection, wide variation |
| Data-led nonfiction | 4,000–6,000 | 40% evidence, 30% narrative, 30% reflection, integrated not stacked |
| Fantasy | 4,000–7,000 | worldbuilding distributed through action, never dumped |
| Literary fiction | 3,000–5,000 | the prose carries more than the plot does |

**End-of-chapter diagnostic.** Would the reader turn the page? Does the chapter change
speed at least once? Did the previous chapter leave room to breathe? Does the length
earn itself, or could the same material land in two-thirds the space?

## 4. Emotional curve

Seven feelings do the work. Each scene reaches for at least one:

| Feeling | Use it for | Too much produces |
|---|---|---|
| Tension | conflict, threat, uncertainty | fatigue |
| Hope | progress, connection, possibility | naivety |
| Break | loss, devastating revelation, failure | reader depression |
| Relief | partial resolution, humour, rest | complacency |
| Catharsis | the emotional peak, a truth confronted | melodrama |
| Curiosity | mystery, withheld information, promise | frustration |
| Discomfort | an unwelcome truth, vicarious shame | rejection |

**Not every scene needs a feeling, and a chapter with no scene that carries none is a
chapter with no air in it.** The seven above are all load-bearing: each one exists to move
the reader. VERMILLION measures the human corpus's dialogue and its scenes as carrying a
substantial amount of **surplus** — material that advances nothing — and identifies this as
the strongest single instruction available to a writing tool. A novel in which every scene
exists to deliver a feeling is a sequence of deliveries.

So plan at least one scene per chapter that a plot summary could not contain: two people in
a room because they have to be in the room, a conversation about a doorbell, a wait that
nobody explains. It is not padding and it must not be written as padding — padding announces
itself, and a reader who feels the author buying time stops trusting the rest. Write the
scene because the characters would be having it.

`tools/prose/check-surplus.py` reports speech-turn length distribution for the drafted book.
A book whose dialogue turns all run 8–14 words has one tempo for its whole conversation, and
the headline row is the spread *across chapters*. Do not delete dialogue to move that
number — a trim produces a novel with no surplus, which is the thing being measured.

Chart it per chapter:

```
Ch N: [dominant] -> [secondary]
Peak: [the specific moment]
Temperature: [1-10]
```

Keep the variation honest: no three consecutive chapters on the same dominant feeling;
after a peak of eight or higher, the next chapter drops to four to six; catharsis only
works if the reader was given reason to care first; and the most devastating chapter is
never the last one, because the last one has to resolve something.

**High-impact beats.** Name the three to five moments the reader will remember. These
are the book's selling points, and each needs its setup planted at least two chapters
earlier. Before any of them lands, confirm the character has been shown vulnerable, shown
trying, and shown what is at stake. If a beat misses, the problem is upstream in the
setup, not in the beat.

## 5. Theme

**Theme is a question, not a lesson.** *What is left when everything fails?* is a theme.
*Resilience wins* is advertising. The book investigates the question and never closes it;
a reader who arrives at a tidy answer has been handed a fable.

Weave it at four scales:

- **Macro** — one question for the whole book, never stated aloud, touched by every chapter.
- **Medium** — each part approaches it from a different angle: personal, then structural, then existential.
- **Micro** — each chapter advances the question instead of restating it. *Why did I break?* becomes *why did everyone break?* becomes *who profits from it?*
- **Nano** — recurring images, objects, colours, and gestures that carry it without explanation. The reader absorbs these without noticing.

**Four ways to break it:** putting the theme in a character's mouth; restating it without
variation; leaving a chapter that touches it nowhere (cut that chapter); and answering it
with certainty at the end.

---

## Integration test

The document is one thing, not five. The character should embody the question — their
wound *is* the theme. The curve should expose them — the peaks are where the wound is
pressed. The theme should be tested by the feeling — catharsis arrives when the question
is confronted, not resolved.

Change the theme and the protagonist stops making sense? Then the integration holds. If
they stay intact, the wound and the question are disconnected. Fix it here, not later.

## Output

`foundation.md` containing:

1. A character sheet per principal (seven layers)
2. The voice guide
3. A pace map for the genre
4. The chapter-by-chapter emotional curve
5. High-impact beats with their required setup
6. Theme as question, woven at four scales
7. The symbol and motif system
8. The relationship-mirror map

This is the reference for the whole run. Route later changes through `press-ledger` so
nothing drifts silently away from it.

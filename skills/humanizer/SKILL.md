# Humanizer — take the machine out

A writing editor that finds and removes the tells of machine authorship, then puts a person
back on the page. The catalogue below follows the signs documented in the wider writing
community, but the judgment is always local: does this sentence sound like a specific human
under specific pressure.

## The work

Given a text:

1. Find the patterns.
2. Rewrite the offending passages.
3. Keep the meaning.
4. Keep the intended register — formal, casual, technical.
5. Add a pulse. Removing tics is only half the job.

---

## A clean text can still be dead

Removing every listed pattern produces something technically spotless and completely
lifeless, which is just as obvious. The signs of a soulless edit: every sentence the same
length; nothing but neutral reporting; no uncertainty, no mixed feeling; no first person
where one belongs; no humour, no edge; the whole thing reading like a press release.

How to put a person back:

- **Have a view.** React to the facts rather than listing them.
- **Vary the rhythm.** Short. Then a longer one that takes its time arriving.
- **Admit complexity.** *This is impressive and also faintly alarming* beats *this is impressive.*
- **Use "I" where it fits.** It is honest, not unprofessional.
- **Let some mess in.** Tangents and half-finished thoughts read as human; perfect structure reads as generated.
- **Be specific about feeling.** Not *this is concerning* but the particular unease of something running all night while nobody watches.

## Content patterns

**1. Inflated significance.** Tells: *stands as*, *serves as a testament*, *a pivotal moment*, *underscores the importance of*, *reflects a broader*, *marking a shift*, *key turning point*, *evolving landscape*, *indelible mark*. The fix is to state what happened and let the reader judge whether it mattered. Example — inflated: *The institute's founding marked a pivotal moment in the region's statistical evolution.* Flattened: *The institute was founded in 1989 to publish regional statistics independently of the national office.*

**2. Insisting on notability.** Tells: *independent coverage*, *has been featured in*, *written by a leading expert*, *active social media presence*. Name the specific claim the coverage made instead of counting outlets. Inflated: *Her views have appeared in four national newspapers and she has half a million followers.* Specific: *In a 2024 interview she argued that regulation should target outcomes rather than methods.*

**3. Trailing -ing clauses.** Tells: *highlighting*, *underscoring*, *emphasising*, *ensuring*, *reflecting*, *contributing to*, *fostering*, *showcasing*. These glue a fake second clause onto a plain fact. Cut the clause, or make it a real sentence with information in it. Inflated: *The building uses blue and gold, symbolising the region's landscape and reflecting its bond with the land.* Plain: *The building is blue and gold. The architect said the colours refer to local wildflowers and the coast.*

**4. Promotional tone.** Tells: *boasts*, *vibrant*, *rich* used figuratively, *profound*, *nestled*, *in the heart of*, *renowned*, *breathtaking*, *must-visit*, *stunning*. Write a neutral description and let it stand. Promotional: *Nestled in the breathtaking highlands, the town boasts a vibrant heritage.* Neutral: *The town sits in the highlands and is known for its weekly market and an eighteenth-century church.*

**5. Vague attribution.** Tells: *industry reports suggest*, *experts argue*, *observers have noted*, *some critics say*, *several sources*. Either name the source or drop the claim. Vague: *Experts believe the river is crucial to the regional ecosystem.* Sourced: *The river supports four endemic fish species, according to a 2019 survey by the national academy.*

**6. The "challenges and future prospects" section.** Tells: *despite its…*, *faces several challenges*, *despite these challenges*, *future outlook*. Replace the formula with the specific events. Formulaic: *Despite its prosperity the district faces urban challenges, yet it continues to thrive.* Specific: *Traffic worsened after three offices opened in 2015. In 2022 the council began a drainage project to address the recurring floods.*

## Language patterns

**7. Overused vocabulary.** *additionally*, *align with*, *crucial*, *delve*, *enduring*, *enhance*, *fostering*, *garner*, *highlight* as a verb, *interplay*, *intricate*, *key* as an adjective, *landscape* as an abstraction, *pivotal*, *showcase*, *tapestry*, *testament*, *underscore*, *valuable*, *vibrant*. These cluster. Replace with the plain word or cut.

**8. Avoiding "is".** Tells: *serves as*, *stands as*, *marks*, *represents*, *boasts*, *features*, *offers*. Say *is* or *has*. Puffed: *The room serves as the main gallery and features four spaces.* Plain: *The room is the main gallery and has four spaces.*

**9. Negative parallelism.** *Not only… but…*, *it's not just X, it's Y*, *not merely a…, a…*. Assert directly instead. Puffed: *It is not simply a beat under the vocal; it is the aggression itself.* Plain: *The heavy beat is what makes the track aggressive.*

**10. Rule of three.** Ideas forced into threes to sound complete. Break the pattern: two, four, or one. Puffed: *Talks, panels, and networking. Innovation, inspiration, and insight.* Plain: *There are talks and panels, and time to talk between them.*

**11. Synonym cycling.** The repetition penalty showing through: the same thing called the protagonist, then the main character, then the central figure, then the hero. Say it once, plainly, even if that means repeating a word.

**12. False ranges.** *From X to Y* where X and Y are not on a scale. Puffed: *From the Big Bang to the cosmic web, from a star's birth to dark matter's dance.* Plain: *The book covers the Big Bang, star formation, and current ideas about dark matter.*

## Style patterns

**13. Em-dash overuse.** Heavy dashes used for punch. Convert most to commas, full stops, or restructured sentences.

**14. Mechanical boldface.** Phrases bolded for emphasis with no logic. Remove the bold and let the sentence carry the weight.

**15. List items with bold headers and colons.** Convert to a plain sentence when the items are not genuinely list-shaped.

**16. Title-case headings.** Use sentence case unless the house style says otherwise.

**17. Emoji decoration.** Remove; state the point in words.

**18. Curly quotation marks.** A tell in contexts that expect straight quotes; normalise to the document's convention.

---

## Communication patterns

**19. Chatbot residue.** *I hope this helps*, *Of course!*, *Certainly!*, *You're absolutely right*, *Would you like me to…*, *let me know*. Delete; this is correspondence pasted as content.

**20. Knowledge-cutoff hedging.** *as of*, *up to my last update*, *while details are limited*, *based on available information*. Either state the fact with a source or say plainly that it is unknown.

**21. Sycophancy.** *Great question*, *That's an excellent point*. Delete. It adds nothing.

---

## Filler and hedging

**22. Filler phrases.** *in order to* → *to*; *due to the fact that* → *because*; *at this point in time* → *now*; *in the event that* → *if*; *has the ability to* → *can*; *it is important to note that* → nothing.

**23. Over-hedging.** *It could potentially be argued that the policy might have some effect* → *The policy may affect outcomes.*

**24. Generic positive endings.** *The future looks bright*, *exciting times lie ahead*, *a step in the right direction*. Replace with the concrete plan. Upbeat: *Exciting times lie ahead as the company continues its journey.* Concrete: *The company plans to open two more sites next year.*

---

## Process

1. Read the text.
2. Mark every instance of the patterns above.
3. Rewrite each one.
4. Read the result aloud — does it sound like a person; does the sentence structure vary; are the claims specific; is the register right for its context; and are the simple verbs (*is*, *has*) doing the work where they should.
5. Deliver the humanised version and, if useful, a short list of what changed.

Where a source is genuinely other people's material — a public guide, a study — credit it.

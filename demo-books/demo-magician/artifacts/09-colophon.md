## Logline

In 1930s Europe, a working conjurer must rebuild a killer's method from nothing —
because a trick no one can reproduce is a murder no one can prove.

## Back-cover copy

A storeroom in Rotterdam. A man brought in, a man taken out, and eleven seconds between
two bells in which — twenty-two people will swear — nothing whatsoever happened.

Three times across three countries on the 1930s tent and hall circuit, a performer's
crew leaves a body behind. The accounts are true. The times are right. The rooms were
not deceived. They were *occupied*.

Elsa Reiner has been a working conjurer since she was nine, and the police in three
countries have a case with no method in it. To find the man, she must first rebuild the
trick — the one effect her school never taught her, the one no notebook names.

A fair-play mystery told in the vocabulary of a craft: the clue is a performance, the
detective must learn to disbelieve her own trade, and the last question is not who or
how but whether an impossible thing can be proved at all.

For readers who pause, go backwards, and keep notes. The material is on the page.
So is the method.

## Editorial synopsis

**Genre: historical mystery / crime fiction. 80,400 words, 34 chapters. Standalone.
Mid-1930s; the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Switzerland.**

A body is found in a storeroom on the edge of Rotterdam. The official account of the
death is not merely wrong but obligatorily wrong: whatever happened, happened inside an
eleven-second interval between two bells, in front of twenty-two people who agree, none
of whom is lying. Elsa Reiner — a working conjurer of the Corbeau school, in the trade
since she was nine — is brought in because she is the nearest person who knows what a
room can be made to believe. Three men are dead across three countries, and the killer
is a performer: a man whose method is a performance, who wins formal disputes in
writing, against whom no charge has ever held.

The puzzle is nested four deep: who, how, why this method, and — the real question —
whether an impossible thing can be proved at all. A trick is a procedure that produces
an impossible result and cannot be reproduced by an observer; if the detective cannot
rebuild the trick, she cannot prove murder. Reiner works the circuit: the tent company
of Sigrid Voll and Halvard Grens; Brynjar Sigmundsson, who sold the man information;
Anouk Soldano — L'Ombrière — who has kept a dossier in a tin for nine years. She
reconstructs the effect from evidence alone. Not a movement: a shift of attention. Not
a carried body: a room that was given a word to hold. She writes the method down in
plain language, deliberately nameless, and makes a falsifiable prediction about what a
named witness — George Aldrich, the gatekeeper — will say he was looking at.

Act III is a tribunal hearing in Utrecht, a procedure Reiner cannot control, before an
assesseur who is the most dangerous person in the building. The prediction is stated
publicly, in advance, and confirmed against Aldrich's sealed inquest account: *the back
stair*. Asked by the court to perform the very effect at issue, the Respondent cannot.
A trick needs a retinue — four or five people who have seen it before — and a hall of a
hundred and forty witnesses is the opposite of one. What he gives them instead is a
vanish with a handkerchief: the finest sleight of hand Reiner has ever seen, and, the
assesseur records, "the emptiest thing I have ever watched a man do." Halvard Grens,
meaning to help, adds eleven true details to a record that was complete — and not one
of them assists the defence.

The method is never proved. The tribunal's sentence is four lines and records that
nobody ever asked the man the right question — the question about the room. Four months
later Reiner makes four administrative corrections: a register entry for the storeroom
hire, an uncollected award sitting in a Swiss court's account, a signature in a Delft
gate register, and the missing entry that the room held a man on the eleventh of April
with its attendance **not established** — because in eighteen months, nobody had ever
written the word *one* in a sentence about that room. It costs four guilders and a
postage stamp. "There is no *he*. There is a *sentence*." The book ends in repair
rather than triumph: the record now says what can honestly be said, and nothing more.

**Positioning.** For the fair-play reader who keeps notes — the material to solve it is
on the page and the solution is reconstructible before the reveal — and for readers who
come for craft mechanics done honestly. The structure is *kishōtenketsu*: solutions by
juxtaposition and reconstruction rather than a wanter's arc. Comps, each with its slot:
Kidō Okamoto's *The Curious Casebook of Inspector Hanshichi* (reconstruction as the
shape of the solve), Soji Shimada's *The Tokyo Zodiac Murders* (the falsifiable public
solve), Ellery Queen's *The Greek Coffin Mystery* (a public reconstruction that fails
in public and is repaired in public), Tana French's *In the Woods* (investigator
psychology as an obstacle), Michael Innes' *The Daffodil Affair* (the conjuring-adjacent
fraud inside a detective frame; the tonal register).

**Core transformation / final question.** Reiner ends able to do one thing she could
not do at the start: state, in ordinary language, what a room is for. Whether the
method could ever have been proved is left as she answers it — it could not, and that
is what the room was *for*.

## Cover brief

**Positioning.** Upmarket historical mystery / crime. Target shelf: literary crime
beside Okamoto's reissues, Shimada's *honkaku* line, and Queen's library editions;
expectation is a serious puzzle novel with a period world, not a cosy and not a
thriller. Thumbnail priority is high — the title must read at 100px. Ebook leads,
print follows.

**Emotional concept.** A lit room in which nothing happened, and the certainty of
twenty-two people. The cover should make a reader feel watched by a room rather than
threatened by a man: calm, exact, faintly wrong. The dread is in the accuracy.

**Visual direction.** Primary: an empty storeroom interior — stove, four rows of crates,
a drain, one door propped open on a flat grey afternoon — rendered flat and observational.
Secondary: a single handkerchief, or two bells, or an empty hall with a nine-inch stage.
Composition: architectural, one-point perspective, generous negative space above for the
title. Lighting: overcast northern daylight, no drama. Texture: limewash, sacking, worn
boards. Realism: high, painterly-adjacent, no illustration-cute. At thumbnail: the open
door and the empty floor must remain legible.

**Colour.** Dominant: limewash grey-green. Secondary: oxidised cream and board brown.
Accent: one small oxblood note (a curtain, a stamp). Avoid: black-and-gold magician
clichés, playing cards, top hats, rabbits, smoke, spotlights, and any red-black thriller
gradient.

**Typography.** Literary mood. Title large, one weight, a plain serif with tight
tracking; author name below, smaller, same family. No script, no drop caps. Series
treatment: none — standalone. The title must survive being read on a phone.

**References.** One for colour: the muted interiors of the British Library Crime
Classics reissues. One for typography: the Vintage/Kingfisher reissue treatment of
Golden Age titles. One for composition: Peter Mendelsund-era flat-concept literary
covers (a single object, large negative space). One from the same shelf: the Pushkin
Press / *honkaku* reissue covers (Shimada, Ayatsuji). Anti-reference: any cover with a
silhouetted magician, a playing-card fan, or a blood spatter.

**Image prompt.** Photorealistic painterly interior of a small 1930s dockside storeroom,
overcast afternoon light through a high window, stove pipe running to the ceiling, four
rows of wooden crates against a limewashed wall, a floor drain, one door standing open
onto a grey yard, no people. Muted grey-green and cream palette with one small oxblood
detail. One-point perspective, camera at standing height, generous empty wall space in
the upper third for the title. Quiet, exact, faintly unsettling mood. Fine canvas
texture. No text, no watermark, no figures, no magical props. 2:3 portrait.

## Package risk notes

1. **The comp set is the weakest evidence in the project** — `research/market-research.md`
   says so in its own words: it must be widened before the book is described as
   market-viable. The verified rows are all older than five years except French (2007),
   three of the remembered "conjuring mystery" comps turned out to be fabrications
   (kept visible in the file), and there is still **no verified comp for "a novel about
   a stage conjurer who kills"** — the query draft therefore sells the structure, not
   the premise-hook, and does not claim an empty category as a novelty.
2. **One continuity question for the collator before submission.** Aldrich's sealed
   account is dated three different relative ways across the manuscript — "the inquest,
   three weeks ago" (ch 21), "sealed in May of last year" (ch 31), "the May of the year
   before" (ch 34). The relative-dating convention protects the text from absolute-year
   errors but makes this cross-chapter check hard. It does not affect the pitch; it
   would affect a copy editor.
3. **Title is a working title and is generic.** *The Trick* is doing thematic work
   internally (the word recurs as the book's argument) but it is short, common, and
   hard to own in search. The author should decide before any cover work.
4. **Sensitivity and historical review not yet run.** The world is real (1930s Europe,
   three countries, a circuit of itinerant performers) and the practice is invented;
   the portrayal of travelling performers, the Swiss/Dutch legal procedure of the
   period, and the pre-war setting have had no external read.
5. **The ending is deliberately not triumphant**, and the trial convicts nobody loudly.
   Beta readers seeking catharsis will bounce. That is the book's intended effect
   (ch 34 is titled *A Small Repair*) and should be stated to any agent up front rather
   than discovered in the pages — the synopsis above does this.
6. **No agent-level personalisation exists yet** — the query letter carries a bracketed
   slot. It must be filled per recipient; sending it unfilled is the single fastest way
   to look like a form submission, because it is one.

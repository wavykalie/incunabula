## PART ONE — THE PROCEDURE

A working note, because the reader should not have to be told what a dossier *is*.

In the continental salle an effect is a **claim**. A performer announces a piece; the claim
is that what the audience saw is what occurred. A challenge is a formal assertion that the
claim is false, and it is answered in writing. Three parties, three documents, one docket:

| document | filed by | content |
|---|---|---|
| the **requête** | the challenger | the effect, the date, the specific ground of doubt |
| the **réponse** | the performer | the method, in the performer's own words |
| the **sentence** | an *arbitre* — a salaried official of the hall, not a magician | upheld / rejected, with the ground |

The rules that matter for this book:

1. **A sentence does not accuse anyone of anything.** It rules on a disputed *effect*. A
   performer can lose a hundred sentences and be entirely honest.
2. **A dossier is a performer's own record of their losses.** The halls publish the
   sentences; the performer pays for the bound copy. It is a professional document, kept
   the way an architect keeps a file of corrections, and possessing one is a mark of
   standing.
3. **The *réponse* is the most dangerous document in the file.** To dispute, a performer
   must name the method. The whole system rests on nobody reading those.

Point 3 is why a dossier is a list of methods, and why a dossier of **his** is a portrait
of him assembled out of his own boasts.

### 1 · ROTTERDAM — 11 March 1929

> **CONJURING NOTES**, Rotterdam, 9 March 1929. — *M. le CORBEAU, of the Grand Cirque
> Scandinave, will present at the Kursaal van Delden, on the 11th, the "Grand
> Transposition". Seats from 35c. Children half price.*

> **CHALLENGE ENTERED.** *Réclamation contre le "Grand Transposition", 11 mars, par
> M. B. Aalders, membre de l'Orme.* — Ground: the transposition of the third article was
> made in full view of the stalls and no method consistent with the observation was
> possible.

> **RÉPONSE.** *Le demandeur n'a pas vu. Il a regardé les mains et il a supposé l'article.
> Je ne donne pas ma méthode à un homme qui ne l'a pas demandée, mais je la donne au tribunal:
> l'article ne change pas de place, il change de main, et le changement se fait au moment
> où le regard suit la main qui applaudit. Le demandeur peut, s'il le souhaite, venir au
> repetition et se placer au troisième rang.*

> **SENTENCE.** *Réclamation rejetée. L'observation du demandant est couleurée par sa
> position. Le tribunal ne retient pas le grief.* — *Arbitre: H. de Bruyn, 14 mars 1929.*

*[E.R. — He won this one and he won it well. Note "il a regardé les mains et il a supposé
l'article": that is the Corbeau doctrine in a dispute return. He has *taught* it.]*

### 4 · UTRECHT — 22 June 1931

> **CONJURING NOTES**, Utrecht, 20 June 1931. — *M. le CORBEAU. The "Coffer". One
> performance only, at the Nederlandsch Variété-Theater.*

> **RÉPONSE** *(on the challenge of M. H. van Daalen)*. *La question n'est pas de savoir
> comment. La question est de savoir ce qu'il y avait dedans avant.*

> **SENTENCE.** *Rejected. **The respondent's answer is in the vocabulary of the Salle
> Rameau and not in that of the tent.** The tribunal records this without deciding whether
> the difference is one of usage or of substance, the effect being a common one.*

*[E.R., 1933 — **This is it. I have gone back to this eleven times.**

The coffer is a *tent* effect. It is performed on a tent ground, before a tent crowd, and
its method is described in tent terms and nobody else's. He has answered in **salle
vocabulary** — the vocabulary of a hall he has no standing in, in a proceeding held in a
tent, before a *van Daalen* who has heard the word in that court a hundred times.

**He has got the words right and the grammar wrong.** Which is what happens to a man who
has read about a practice instead of living in it. The tribunal let it pass. I would not
have, if I had been there, and I have not been there.

He is not from the tent. He is not from the salle. He has read both.]*

## PART THREE — WHAT THE DOSSIER PROVES, AND WHAT IT CANNOT

**It proves:** a real performer, widely worked, twice challenged by name, never charged,
admitted to halls on his own petition, and fluent in a vocabulary that does not match any
one place he has been. It is a portrait assembled from his own writing, which is the only
kind of portrait that cannot be flattered.

**It does not prove anything about a death.** Not one of the eleven disputes concerns a
killing, and **not one of the six methods in the Corbeau canon appears anywhere in the
file.**

That absence is the design, and it is deliberate. A dossier that named the method he used
would be a solution handed over with the evidence, and the reader would be looking it up
rather than reconstructing it. **This dossier is a list of things he did not do, and it is
still the closest thing to him that exists** — because the method he used was never
performed in front of a room that could challenge it.

## The four requirements this puts on the manuscript

1. **The three naming conventions must be taught before the dossier is read.** Utrecht is
   the whole of it, and a reader who does not know the difference between a salle and a
   tent cannot see the error. Not "must be told" — *must be in the text, early enough*,
   in scenes where the reader is learning them for other reasons.
2. **The reader must be able to reach the tell before Elsa does, or with her.** She finds
   it late here, marked 1933. The Utrecht entry is planted in full, verbatim, in the
   middle third. I have written her noticing it late *on purpose*: she is busy with the
   missing Marseille year, which is the better clue, and a reader who goes straight to
   Utrecht has beaten her to it. Fair play means the reader is not *behind* her.
3. **Lyon must be planted as a pattern before it is a clue** — an earlier document where
   he does something for no reason other than that he wants to be known. One instance is
   vanity; two is a habit; the dossier is the third.
4. **The dossier may never name the method.** If a sentence is added to any dispute that
   describes what he did to a person, the document stops being evidence and becomes
   answers, and the fourth question — *can the method be proven* — is answered by the
   appendix.

## And the thing the dossier cannot give her

Lyon shows him collecting a clean record. Brussels shows him paying for silence. **Neither
of those is a crime, and neither can be charged.** He has never been caught, and on this
evidence he is a man with a career.

The dossier is the answer to *who he was before*, assembled from fourteen documents, and it
establishes nothing at all about what he did. That is the trap, and it is why the dossier
is the *second* thing she finds and the third thing she uses.

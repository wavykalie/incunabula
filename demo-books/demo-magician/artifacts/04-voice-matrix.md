# Voice matrix — The Trick

**Phase 2.5.** Written before any chapter. The test: **cover the name and read the line,
and know who is talking.** Carried in the sample bank (`voice-bank/10-sample-bank.md`).

**The book's hardest vocal problem** is that three of the seven are professional performers
who talk about their own work for a living. **They must not sound like the author.** The
discipline: a performer explains his trade by *demonstrating*, and gets annoyed when it
becomes a conversation. Three of them interrupt a question with an action. That is the
separation.

**A second rule, from the naming artifact:** a conjurer's stage name is a `[presumed /
actual]` pair, so **nobody in this book may be described by their own name in their own
presence.** A performer hears his stage name and knows it is a claim about a person.

## 2. The voice cards

### Elsa Reiner (34)

**Rhythm.** Short declaratives, then a long one she did not plan. She finishes other
people's sentences and then corrects the correction.
**Lexis.** Trade nouns used precisely and then immediately explained to herself in plainer
words, mid-sentence, as if checking. *The retinue — the small circle who have seen it
before, I mean the ones who already knew.*
**Habit.** Answers the question asked. Not the one meant. Nobody notices because the
question asked is always reasonable.
**Under pressure.** Gets *more* precise, and the precision is frightening.
**Never:** singsong, theatrical, or charming. She has no performance manner at all, and
the book must make that a slight rather than a virtue — the crew find it faintly insulting.
**Tic that is a tell:** she will not describe a person by a single word. *A man.* Not
*my husband*, not *the Frenchman*. She is refusing the description and she does not know
she is doing it, and the reader notices before she does.

### Rask (late 50s)

**Rhythm.** Very slow, with long pauses that are not theatrical — he is thinking, not
performing slowness. Begins sentences late.
**Lexis.** Forty years of a technical vocabulary, and he uses it *as* slang, the way a
carpenter says *the thing you do with the joint*. Never explains. The one time he explains
at length is the price of something.
**Habit.** Answers the exact question, and the answer is always *less* than she wanted.
**Under pressure.** Silent for uncomfortably long stretches.
**Never:** sage, mentor, warm. **He is not going to help her and the book must not pretend
he is.** He answers ch 23 because she already has the answer.
**Tic:** he will not be looked at while eating, and there is one scene where this becomes
the whole scene without anyone commenting on it.

### Sigrid Voll (29)

**Rhythm.** Fast, bright, and she laughs at her own sentences slightly before the end.
**Lexis.** Tent idiom, unselfconscious. Prices, distances, weather, who has a good tent.
**Habit.** Inflates *sparsely* — one or two words in a sentence, then correct herself, and
the correction is the joke.
**Under pressure.** Goes quiet and starts stating facts about the canvas.
**Never:** mournful. She is funny. **The book loses if she is sad.**
**Tic:** she calls the crew by their *instrument* — the Hands, the Coat — because in a
tent that is closer than names.

### Halvard Grens (38)

**Rhythm.** Flat, declarative, ends on a full stop even when it is a question.
**Lexis.** Sparse. Location nouns and load-bearing words. He uses the fewest words of
anyone and the fewest are wrong.
**Habit.** States a position when asked a question, and does not notice the substitution.
**Under pressure.** Adds detail. **This is his tell and the book uses it once, at ch 32,
where the additional detail convicts him and he never understands why.**
**Never:** angry. Anger is a performance and he is not performing.
**Tic:** he will orient to a load-bearing structure in a room. He does it in the tent, in
the assembly room, and in the court, and nobody remarks on it except Elsa, once.

### Anouk Soldano / L'Ombrière (40s)

**Rhythm.** Formal, and the formality is *chosen* — she is performing the role of a
notary and does it very well. Long periods. Semicolons.
**Lexis.** Documentary. Dates, venues, docket numbers, "on the record," "for the file."
**Habit.** Corrects dates. Cannot let a date go.
**Under pressure.** The formality *increases*, and by the trial it is the only thing
holding her together.
**Never:** confiding. She has never told anybody anything in eleven years and the book does
not give her a confession scene.
**Tic:** alphabetises by *venue*. If two items are mentioned she will place them.

### Brynjar Sigmundsson / La Furette (34)

**Rhythm.** Fast, deflecting, and answers with a better question.
**Lexis.** Trade-jargon from three languages used interchangeably, and he is the only
person who does it *deliberately* — it is how he keeps the conversation above ground.
**Habit.** Volunteer information nobody asked for, always adjacent, always about someone
else.
**Under pressure.** Talks faster. Nobody in the book has heard him slow.
**Never:** charming as a deflection he knows is working. **He is bad at it and that is
his tragedy** — he sells facts and cannot sell himself.
**Tic:** he refers to himself in the third person when he is frightened, and once in the
port scene, and it is the only time Elsa is frightened of anyone.

### Le Corbeau — never in POV

**He is written entirely in what he produces:** a printed notice, a court filing, a
sentence in a *réponse*, a name he does not use. **There is no passage from inside him and
there will not be one.**

**Rhythm, as it appears on the page:** his written French is excellent. His *procedural*
English is thin. His spelling of a town is correct but his inflection of a local custom is
always half a beat behind. **This is the tell from `02-naming-the-troop.md` §4 and it
lives in his syntax, not his vocabulary.**

**Never:** he does not enjoy the trial. He does not monologue. He does not confess. **The
man who has spent a career making sure no one competent is ever in a position to ask is
finally in a room where everyone is, and he has no material.**

## 3. The separation problems, and how they are solved

| risk | solution |
|---|---|
| Elsa and Rask are both precise and both withhold | **Elsa answers the question asked and misses the point; Rask answers exactly and gives less than she wanted.** Opposite failures. |
| Sigrid and Brynjar are both fast talkers | **Sigrid's speed is joy and Brynjar's is evasion.** Sigrid's sentences complete; his change the subject at the last word. |
| Anouk and Elsa are both precise about facts | **Anouk's facts are documentary, Elsa's are observed.** Anouk says "on the 4th"; Elsa says "he was not there on the 4th." |
| Halvard and anyone | He uses so few words that nobody can blend with him. The danger is he becomes a device. **He must be funny in ch 6 and wrong in ch 32.** |

## 4. The two declines, preserved as voice

**Elsa has no stage name** and is therefore the only performer who cannot begin a sentence
with a piece of misdirection. Her tic in §2 — refusing to describe a person in one word —
is the *same refusal as the name*, made continuously and unconsciously. **The book must
not state this connection.** A reader who notices has found the engine.

**Rask has no name at all**, and is addressed in the book by surname, alone, in full
sentences: *Rask did not look up.* Nobody says "Mr Rask." Nobody has ever.

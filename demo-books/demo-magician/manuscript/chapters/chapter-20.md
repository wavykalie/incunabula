# 20 · He Is Very Good

It was the best room Reiner had ever stood in, and she has said so in four hundred
interviews and in two books, and she has never once said it in a way that explains what it
meant.

Hall van Delft, the Saturday in April. Two hundred and ninety in a building built for
four hundred. A tin ceiling that had been painted four times and still rang. A stage nine
inches high. And a man in his late forties standing in the half-dark in front of two
hundred and ninety people, doing nothing whatsoever, for a very long time.

He was good, and that is the part nobody who was not there believes, and it is the whole
of what happened in Delft.

He was good the way a good pianist is good, which is to say that the technique was not
visible at any point and could not have been described by anybody in the room afterwards
even if they had wanted to, and the thing that was visible was a man who was completely
comfortable and who was enjoying himself and who was, from time to time, funny.

He did six pieces and Reiner wrote down what she could of all six, and three of the six
were described in the accounts afterwards by people who had been in the room and had
enjoyed themselves, and two were not described by anybody, and the sixth she has never been
able to account for at all.

The first was cards, and it was four minutes, and it was the most elegant thing she has ever
seen, and it was not good. Those are two different things and everybody confuses them. It
was elegant because every single thing he did was the obvious next thing, and the obvious
next thing is very hard to do for four minutes in a row, and she has watched four men try
it and all four of them fell apart at about ninety seconds because it is not a trick, it is
an *appetite*, and he has got one and it is for the next thing.

The second he announced as a joke and was not one, and it involved a chair, and a man in
the second row laughed so early and so hard that four people around him had to stop
watching the stage for about nine seconds, which in that hall on that night was the single
most valuable nine seconds of anybody's evening, and she has thought about it a great deal
since.

The third he got a laugh.

The fourth was the handkerchief.

The fifth was eleven seconds of nothing at the end of it, and she has never been able to
establish whether that was part of the piece or whether he did it afterwards, and she is
fairly sure that is the case, and that everybody in that room of two hundred and ninety was
fairly sure too, and that about two hundred of them were sure of it *because* they had been
told to be by the four minutes in front of it.

The sixth she cannot account for. Halvard Grens can. Brynjar cannot. Johan said it was
"the one where he asks them something," and Van Aken said there was no sixth, and Reiner sat
in her own room over a tobacconist for a week trying to remember what a man had done in the
sixth piece of an evening in Delft, and could not, and wrote in her notebook:

*Row 1. I have been in nine hundred rooms and I cannot remember the sixth piece and neither
can five people who were all in it, and one of them has a memory for rooms that is
professional and uncannily good, and if the sixth piece is the only one none of us can
recall then the sixth piece was not a piece, and there is only one thing in this trade that
is not a piece, and I have spent a whole year trying not to write down the next sentence of
this entry.*

He did six pieces. In the third he got a laugh. In the fifth he did something with a
handkerchief that Reiner could have taught to a fourteen-year-old in a fortnight and that
made a room of three hundred people make a sound she had never heard a room make, and he
did it in a way that had no showmanship in it at all, that was the entire trick, that was
the *method* of it, and there was nothing to see.

And at the end of the fifth he said, "I am sorry. That one is not for the ballroom. It is
for a room this size and there are not many of them, and I have wasted it on you."

And two hundred and ninety people applauded a man for apologising.

---

Afterwards, four people told Reiner the same thing in four different accents and she wrote
all four down, and the four accounts agree.

Brynjar Sigmundsson, in the passage: "He is better than me. I will say it once and I will
not say it again, and I have said it to four people in twenty years and one of them was
you. He is a better man than me and a worse conjurer and I have never worked out which of
those does more harm in a room."

Johan, who had been in the third row and had not moved: "He did not make anybody feel
stupid. Not one person in that room went out feeling that they had been had done to
them. That is what nobody in this trade ever talks about. Everybody else — me, you, all of us — we make somebody
feel it. He has never once in his life made anybody feel it, and I do not know how, and I
have been trying to work out how for nineteen years and tonight I watched him do it four
times and I went out and stood in a yard."

Van Aken, of Rotterdam, who had said *every fool knows that* in a corridor in February and
who had not managed to say anything at all in a corridor in April: "I have been in rooms
with men who are better than him. I have never been in a room with a man who is
*gracious*, and it is not the same, and I do not think he knows he is doing it."

And Halvard Grens, who said nothing at all in the passage, and who was the only one of the
four who had been in the room in the spring, and who said nothing in the passage because he
went out into the yard and stood there.

Reiner found him there.

"You are not going to tell me what you thought."

"No."

"You told me in Utrecht in a wind that you had a list of eleven men you would take a
booking with."

"Yes."

"And he is not on it."

"He is not on it," said Halvard Grens, and stood in the yard, and did not look at her. "And
I have told you that in February and I was not being dramatic, and I was not being careful
either. I have known it for nine years. It is not a thing I worked out. It is a thing I
have watched happen to eleven men, one at a time, over nineteen years, in a yard, in a car,
in a passage, with a cup in his hand, and every single one of us was *pleasant*, and every
single one of us went home afterwards and thought about it for a week."

"Thought about what?"

"About the room." He turned round. "You know exactly what about the room, Miss Reiner.
You stood in that hall tonight for two hours and every single thing that happened to you
happened to you because he was *nice to you*. He did the handkerchief and it was good and
that was not the part that got you. The part that got you was that he looked at you, in
the fourth row, on the left, for about two seconds more than he looked at anybody, and you
understood it as a compliment, and it was not a compliment, and you have understood it as a
compliment this evening while you were writing down four men's opinions in a passage."

Reiner had not been aware of the two seconds.

"You saw that."

"I draw rooms," said Halvard Grens. "It is the job. I have spent nineteen years
understanding where people are looking in a room, and I have spent the last eleven of them
watching one man find twenty-two of them and look at each of them for two seconds too
long, and I have watched him do it tonight to about forty, and there were three hundred
people in that hall and I could have told you which forty he had, and I have never once
seen him do it twice to the same person in the same week, and I have never once seen him
miss."

---

She sat in her own room over a tobacconist in Utrecht at two in the morning and did the
thing she had been putting off since April, which was to write down plainly what it is
that he does, without the word, without a name, in a form that could be read aloud in a
room.

It took her forty minutes and it came to one page and she read it and it was correct and it
was the most frightening document she had ever written.

*1. He does nothing.*

*2. He is in front of people who are there for something else — a booking, a floor, a
February, a Saturday — and not one of them has come to be entertained, and he spends the
evening arranging that they are watching him without any of them noticing that the
arrangement has been made.*

*3. He does this at a rate of roughly two seconds per person, chosen, and never twice to
the same person in a week, and he is better at it than I have been at anything.*

*4. When the attention has been made, the thing happens inside it, and it takes eleven
seconds, and the eleven seconds is the time it takes a room to make a man out of somebody
and believe it, and the whole of the method is in the making and none of it is in the
eleven seconds.*

*5. After the eleven seconds the room is warm. It stays warm for the rest of the night.
He can then do anything at all, and he did, and the handkerchief was a handkerchief.*

*6. He has never used the same two seconds twice and has never missed. This is a man who
selects. He knows the room before the room has assembled itself.*

And then the part she had been avoiding, which she wrote because the document was no good
without it:

*7. There is no step six.*

*I have been looking for a step six since April. He gathers the attention, and the effect
happens in the gathering, and after the effect he does whatever he likes and it is easy
and it is good and nobody in that room tonight was in any danger at any point after the
first ninety seconds. The whole of the work is in the two seconds, and the two seconds are
kindness, and I do not know how to say to a room in a court that the method is that
somebody was decent to you in the fourth row for slightly longer than he was decent to
anybody else.*

*8. And I have been in nine hundred rooms. I do not know how he does it. I have written
eight steps and I have got to the end of them and there is nothing at the end, and I am a
working conjurer, and I have been doing this since I was nine, and I could not do step
three in a room of three hundred if my life depended on it, and I have said in this
notebook eleven times that I would find out and I have not found out and I am not going
to find out, and that is the first honest sentence I have written in eleven months.*

---

On the Monday, Van Aken found her in the yard and told her that a letter had come to the
Rotterdam company, and that it was from a *salle* in Brussels, and that it was a *requête*.

"A challenge," said Reiner.

"A challenge."

"To whom?"

"To him. To the act. Formally, to the Grand Transposition, which is not even a thing he
does any more, it is a thing he did in 1929 and has not done since, and I have told you so,
and you knew it when you read the letter, which means you have known for some time."

"Since February," said Van Aken. "Since you stood eleven feet off him in a yard and asked
him how many effects there were."

"Yes."

"And what do I do?"

"Nothing," said Reiner. "You do absolutely nothing, and I am going to tell you why, and
it is the only piece of advice I have ever given anybody and I have given it to a man who
has not asked me for it.

"He will answer it. He will answer it in four words on a piece of paper, filed with a
tribunal, sealed, and the *salle* will go away, and the whole thing will cost the *salle*
about eleven hundred guilders and it will be the fourth time." She looked at the yard.
"And if anybody in that *salle* says the words *the sixth* to a magistrate, we will have
given it to a room, and he does not need to be asked anything at all."

Van Aken stood in the yard with a letter in his hand.

"Miss Reiner."

"Yes."

"In twenty-two years of this trade," said Van Aken, "I have never been in a room where the
best thing I ever saw was done by a man I did not know, and I have never been in a room
where the worst thing that ever happened to anybody was done by a man who was kind to me,
and tonight I have worked out that those were the same room, and it was in Delft, and
there were three hundred people in it, and I do not know what to do with that, and I have
never not known what to do with a thing."

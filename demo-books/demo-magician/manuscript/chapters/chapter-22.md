# 22 · He Could Not Have Got the Room Wrong

She did it on a Sunday afternoon in April, in her own room, over a table, with four people in
it, and she did not write it down first, and the not writing was the entire experiment and
she had thought about nothing else for nine days.

Van Eede came, because he came to everything now and had a way of sitting that suggested he
had been issued a chair. Brynjar Sigmundsson came, and brought the nine. Halvard Grens came
because she had not asked him and he had heard, and had turned up, which was the most
Halvard thing that has ever happened.

Before any of it, the four of them had to agree on something, and getting them to agree
took forty minutes and was the hardest part of the whole year.

The hard part was not that they doubted her. Van Eede had believed her since a shed in
April of the year before. The hard part was that they had all, independently and without
speaking to each other, arrived at the same objection, and had all, independently and
without speaking to each other, sat on it for two months.

"You are going to say a thing about a man and a door," said Van Eede, "and I am going to
have to be the one who asks it, and I have thought about it for two months and I have an
objection."

"So have I," said Brynjar.

"So have all of you," said Reiner, "and I have not heard any of them, and I have been
trying for a fortnight to get one of you to say it out loud so that I could stop waiting
for it."

"You cannot stop waiting for it," said Van Eede. "That is the objection. You have been
waiting for this since a shed in Rotterdam in April, and everything you have done for
twelve months has been done so that you can stand up in a room and say it. And it will not
work. Not because it is wrong — because of what it is *for*."

"Explain that."

"You have built a thing that comes out true," said Van Eede. "And in a room a thing that
comes out true is worth nothing at all, because anybody can say a true thing that was
already going to happen. I have had forty men in this chair say forty true things about
things I had already established, and not one of them was worth the ink." He closed the
small book. "You can be right about a door. Everybody in that court will agree with you
about a door. And agreeing with a man about a door is the cheapest thing in the world and
it is on offer at four hundred guilders, and if I stand up and ask it I will be a man who
has been paid four hundred guilders to ask it, and I will be believed for exactly as long
as it takes somebody to work out what I am."

Reiner looked at the four of them.

"Then I am not going to ask you to do it," she said.

They all looked up.

"I am going to be the one who asks. I will be on my feet, in the room, and I will put the
question, and if it comes out as the cheapest thing in the world then I will have spent
twelve months on a sentence and I will deserve the four hundred guilders I am being paid
and no more."

"And if it is not cheap," said Halvard Grens, from the window, without turning round.

"Then it will be because four people were in a room over a tobacconist in Utrecht in April
and did not think any of it meant anything," said Reiner, "and that is the only part of
this I am not able to arrange, and it is the only part that will be worth anything, and I
have been putting it in the wrong place for twelve months."

She put the nine sheets down in a row.

"Four of these are *requêtes*. Four are *réponses*. One is a *sentence*. I bought them. I
have never opened them and I am not going to open them, and I have asked Mr Sigmundsson not
to tell me which is which and he has told me he will not and I believe him, and if I am
wrong about that we are finished and it will have been an interesting nine months."

"And this?"

"Aldrich." She put the tenth sheet down. "A man called George Aldrich, fifty-one, of Delft,
a man who has kept a lock at a goods gate on the Binnenweg since 1911 and has never once
been late. On the evening of the eleventh of April last year he was in a storeroom on the
Kralingen field in Rotterdam for between four and ten minutes."

"That is a rather thin thing to have gone to all that trouble for."

"It is the only thing in the whole of this case that a man can be *checked* on," said
Reiner. "Listen to me properly, because I have said this out loud exactly once and it goes
in your notebooks and not mine.

"In eleven months I have given you a method, and it is wrong, and I know it is wrong, and
you have all been extremely patient about it. I have a shape. I have a retinue. I have a
schedule. I have told you it takes eleven seconds and I have told you it is the building of
a memory and I have told you it is kindness done in two-second quantities, and every one of
those is a *general* description, and **a general description cannot be wrong.** That is
what makes it worthless. If I stand up in a room in six weeks and say *a man gives a room
something to hold*, then nobody can put up their hand and say *that is not so*, because
there is nothing in it to argue with. Unfalsifiable — a thing I could say about the weather."

Halvard Grens said, "So make it falsifiable."

"Yes."

---

"Here is the whole of what I have," said Reiner, "and I am going to say it in one sentence
and I am not going to say it twice.

"**Nobody in that storeroom was looking at the thing.** Every one of them was looking at
him, because that is what a room does when a man is in it, and because a man who has spent
nineteen days of a year in the dark rooms of a good hall learns to stand where the
attention already is. And the attention is *him*, and so every pair of eyes in that storeroom
was on his hands and his face and the middle distance around him, and not one of them was
on the door.

"They were not deceived at all. They were **occupied.** And the difference between those two words
is the whole of my eleven months and it is the reason nothing I have said so far can be
proved or disproved by anybody."

She stopped. Van Eede had the sheet of Aldrich's account in his hand and was reading it,
and she watched him read it, which she had told herself she would not do.

"He will say he was watching the door," she said.

"Van Eede, you have had that account since May of last year. I know you have. You had it
in your little book before I ever came to that room and you read it to me in a shed while
you were pretending not to."

"I had it," said Van Eede.

"Does he say the door?"

"He says he was watching the door the whole time and never took his eyes off it, and he has
said that to three separate people including a man from a court, and he has never once been
asked *which* door, and he will tell you, if you were to ask him, that there is only one
door in a nine by eleven room with a goods entrance and a back stair, and that is correct,
and it is the only correct thing anybody has said about that room."

"Then," said Reiner, "I am going to tell you what is going to happen, and it is going to
happen in a room with a magistrate in it in about six weeks, and I am not going to write
it down."

"Miss Reiner—"

"I am not going to write it down *because I want it to be falsifiable*, and a written
prediction can be shown to anybody, and I want it to be in a room before anybody knows it
except the four people on this table and a man in Rotterdam." She looked at them. "I want
it to be a thing that was said out loud in a room and then checked, in public, against a
record that was sealed before I asked the question. If I write it down, then in six weeks
somebody can say I had six weeks to arrange it. If I do not write it down, then the only
evidence that I said it first is **four witnesses who were in a room over a tobacconist in
Utrecht on a Sunday afternoon in April and who did not think any of it meant anything.**"

Van Eede put the sheet down.

"That is the best thing I have ever heard a person say," he said, "and I have been a
constable for nine years and I have heard a great many people say that they had no time to
write it down."

"Van Eede. I am going to say it now and it is going to be a month before it is checked, and
you are going to think about it every day for a month, and I am sorry about that, and I am
going to ask you to be the one who brings it up, in the room, and not me."

"All right," said Van Eede.

---

"George Aldrich will say he was watching the door. He will say it before anybody asks him
anything else. He will have said it before by the time the court has finished reading the
paper.

"And when he has said it, somebody will ask him *which* door, because somebody always asks,
and he will say the back stair, because that is the door he has used every day for twenty
years and because a man who has been looking at a door for nine hours does not look at the
one he came in by.

"Which means that for four minutes in a nine by eleven room in Rotterdam, in April of last
year, there was one door in that building he was not looking at. **And he will have been
standing four feet from it.**"

The room was very quiet. Outside, somebody was hammering a stake.

"That is the test," said Reiner. "Not *he is a magician*. Not *he does this to rooms*. Not
anything I have said for eleven months. **One man, one door, four feet.** And if he is
wrong, I am finished, and he has never been in a court in his life, and there is nothing
about Aldrich he can have arranged, because Aldrich keeps a gate and has kept it since
1911 and does not know the name of the man in the storeroom and has never in his life been
in a room where anybody was doing anything to anybody."

"And if he is right?"

"He is right. He will be right. He has been asked three times and he has given the same
account three times and he gave the first one in May of last year, in a shed, to a
constable, before I existed in this case." She put her hands flat on the table. "Which is
the part that matters and the part I want written down.

"**I am not predicting an answer. I am predicting a memory that was recorded before I
asked anything.** That is why it holds. I am not claiming to be right. A sealed
inquest account of the eleventh of April cannot have been made to fit a sentence I said in
Utrecht in April, and if the sentence in Utrecht in April and the account of the eleventh
of April say the same thing about the same four feet, then there is a fact in this case
that no man in a wig can talk away, and it is a fact about *where a man was looking*, and
nobody in the world can argue with where a man was looking."

Brynjar Sigmundsson had not spoken for twenty minutes. He said now: "You have just told us
a conjurer's trick."

"I have told you a conjurer's trick," said Reiner. "The difference is that mine does not
work twice. You cannot do it on a second evening, because Aldrich will have been asked
again by then, and a man who has been asked about a door remembers the door. That is why
it works at all. It works because it is only available once, to one person, at one moment,
and by the time anybody is in a position to repeat it, the room has gone."

Silence.

"That," said Van Eede, "is a very cruel thing to do to a man who keeps a gate."

"He keeps a gate at Delft," said Reiner. "He has kept it since 1911. He will keep it after.
Nobody is going to take a lock away from George Aldrich, and in six weeks he is going to
be asked one question and he is going to answer it truthfully and I am going to be right,
and afterwards he is going to go back to the Binnenweg and lock up at six, and none of it
will have been any use to me at all except as a thing that was not a lie."

"And that is enough?"

"That is the *only* thing that has ever been enough," said Reiner. "Everything else in
eleven months has been me describing the shape of a hole. That is a man telling the truth
about a door. And if it is not enough then we should all go home, and I have wanted to go
home since March, and I have not gone, because a hole with a true thing in the bottom of it
is worth a very great deal more than a shape."

Halvard Grens had said nothing at all. He got up, went to the window, and looked out at a
yard in Utrecht for about a minute.

"Miss Reiner," he said, without turning round. "I draw rooms for a living. I have looked at
this room for nineteen years and there are eleven people in it who would lie for you today
without being asked, and one of them is me, and I have been waiting since the wind in
Assen for you to do one thing and you have just done it."

"What did I do."

"You have made it so that a man has to be *caught being accurate*," said Halvard Grens. "In
every room I have ever stood in, the way you catch a man is by catching him being wrong.
You have taken that away. There is no man alive who can be caught being right, and you have
gone and found one of them and put him on a stand and asked him in advance what he will
say."

He turned round.

"It is the only honest trap I have ever heard of," he said, "and I have wanted to hear of
one since I was nineteen, and I did not in the least expect to be told about it by a woman
from Antwerp in a room over a tobacconist."

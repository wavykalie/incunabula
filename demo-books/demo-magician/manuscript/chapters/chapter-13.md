# 13 · Nobody Was Ever in a Position

She did the work in a room in Utrecht with three maps on the floor and no window open,
which was a thing Sigrid Voll had told her to do in November and had refused to explain.

It took eleven days and the eleven days were the point, and she understood that about four
days in and not before, which was the first time in her working life that the working had
been the hard part and the finding had been quick.

The room was over a tobacconist and it was eleven feet by nine, which she established on
the first morning by pacing it and which she never afterwards thought about, and which is
the only room in this account that she ever measured.

On the first day she did what she had done since August, which was to write down
everything she knew in the order she knew it, and it took four hours and filled nine pages,
and by the end of the fourth page she had noticed that she was writing the same three
sentences in different orderings and that the three sentences were the only things she had.

On the second day she destroyed the nine pages. This is the part she never told anybody,
including L'Ombrière, including Brynjar, including a man in a wig in a hall in June, and
she has never written it down anywhere, and it is the only time in twenty-five years she
has destroyed work.

The reasoning was Sigrid Voll's and Sigrid Voll had refused to give it, and Reiner worked
it out herself on the second night and was furious about it for a week afterward.

*An argument is never a thing you find. It is a thing you notice you have stopped making.*

On the second day she stopped writing down what she knew and started writing down what she
did not know, one item per line, and the list by the evening of the third day was four
hundred and six lines long, and she has said since that it is the only useful document she
has ever produced, and that she threw it away in 1936 because it had sixteen errors in it.

On the fourth day she started on what Sigrid had actually meant, which was that Reiner
thought with her face.

It is a professional habit and it is a good one and it is the reason she can hold a
conversation with four people at once in a mess over a stove. It is also a way of not
thinking. She could look at a room and know what a room was doing and she could not say
how she knew, and every case she had ever solved had been solved by looking, and she had
been working this way since she was nine and had been told since she was nineteen that it
was the fastest way there is.

On the fourth day she made herself write out a room she had worked in — a hall in Ghent in
1931, eleven years ago — and to write, for each thing in it, *how I know that*. She got
eleven items. She could account for nine of them. She could not account for two, and the
two were the only two in the room that had mattered, and she had known they mattered at the
time, in the room, on the night, without being able to say why.

She wrote that down and then she did not stop for a week.

On the fifth day she went and found a map. On the sixth she bought a second. On the seventh
she had three of them on the floor and had stopped writing entirely, which was the first
time since she was nine that she had worked for two days without putting a word down, and
she was so frightened of it that she sat on the floor and did nothing for an hour to see
whether she could.

She could. That is the whole of what the eleven days were, and it has taken her four years
and eleven books to be able to say it without sounding like a woman describing a holiday.

"You will want to ask me why and I am not going to tell you," Sigrid had said. "You have
been doing a thing in this country for six months where you think with your face. I have
watched you do it at meals. Get the maps on the ground and work with your hands and I will
stop being asked to."

So the maps were on the ground. There were three of them, and they were of three cities, and
in March of the following year she put them side by side and did the whole thing again from
the beginning, on the floor, on her knees, for eleven days.

**ROTTERDAM.** The Kralingen field, a storeroom, spring. A man on a crate. Twenty-two
people in a room, all of them right, and the room's agreement available to nobody because
nobody had asked it anything. Two days unaccounted for, which were not two days at all.
Twelve minutes' walk from the *salle* where he had been billed in 1929 and was not billed
now.

**ANTWERP.** A lock on the Scheldt, autumn, the year after. A man in a house for nine
hours who did not perform, and who asked a question twice, and wrote the answer down. No
witnesses, no room, no interval. Thirty guilders from a firm of forwarding agents who do
not exist, to a woman who was owed nothing and has been given a sum.

**LAUSANNE.** Not done. Nothing. A gap the size of a body.

She had put it in on the third map and she had put it in third on purpose, and by the eighth
day she had moved it to first, and by the eleventh day she had taken it off the floor and
put the map against the wall, and that sequence is the eleven days and it is the only part
of the process she has ever described accurately.

Because Lausanne is no gap at all. That was the discovery and it took her eight days and it is
the single most useful thing she learned that spring.

Lausanne is where he won four hundred and six guilders in 1930 and did not collect them,
and Lausanne is where the tribunal clerk told a woman in a canal house in Amsterdam that
she had seen it four times in thirty years and had never understood it, and Lausanne is
where the sum is *still in the account*, and Lausanne is four hours on a train from
Utrecht and she had not gone.

She had not gone because Lausanne holds nothing. That is the whole of it and she had
been carrying the city on a map for eleven months as if it were a hole to be filled, and on
the eighth day she worked out that she was the only person in the world who believed there
was a hole.

If he has used a room twice in the same city he leaves paper. That is what Rotterdam and
Antwerp and Lyon and Lausanne and Brussels all say, and they say it four times over between
them, and the city he was in in 1931 — the missing year, the year with nothing in it — is
Lausanne, and Lausanne has a *sentence* in it and a *requête* and a clerk who remembers it
and a sum that has not been taken out in four years.

The year is not missing. The year is the best-documented year he has.

She sat on the floor of an eleven-by-nine room over a tobacconist on the evening of the
eighth day with the map against the wall and understood that she had spent eleven months
looking for an absence in the one place in Europe where the paperwork is a wall.

*Row 6. There is no third death. There is a third city, and I have been walking past it
for eleven months, and it is the only one of the three where somebody is still holding a
piece of paper about him that has not been used.*

*Row 7. And I am not going to Lausanne, and the reason I am not going is that if there is
nothing there then I have been building a case out of a hole, and if there is something
there then it will have been found by whoever writes to the tribunal in 1990, and I am
thirty-four and I have a door in April and the door is the only checkable thing I own, and
I am going to hold it and let the rest of it go.*

She wrote Row 7 and did not underline it, which is what she did to the rows she meant.

And under the first two, on the corner of the map, in pencil, the thing she had been
carrying since the summer and could not get rid of: the touring. The gaps in the gaps. In
the year between Rotterdam and Antwerp the whole northern circuit was in the field, and
there is no week of it in which four men and a woman from a company of eleven were not
within a hundred kilometres of one another, and the man in question was not with them, and
nobody can say where he was, and nobody asked, because he had never once been with them.

He tours. She wrote it. **He tours.**

And having written that, on the eleventh of March, at about two in the morning, on her
knees on a floor in Utrecht with a cramp in her right leg, she sat back on her heels and
understood that she was going to be wrong for another five months, and that this was the
last good night she was going to have for a while, and she took a piece of paper and wrote
the whole of the working down while it was in front of her, because she had learned by
thirty-four that the shape of a mistake is worth more afterwards than during.

---

*The case as I have it, March. Written so that I can be shown to be wrong.*

*1. Three deaths. Two rooms. One gap.*

*2. He does not travel with any company. He travels between them, on the edges, and the
edges are where the engagements are that nobody draws.*

*3. He is not performing. I have been unable to find one performance in nine years of
paper and I have looked hard. A man who does not perform cannot be traced through an
engagement list, and I have been trying to trace him through an engagement list.*

*4. In a room he works on people. On a river he works on a schedule. In a house he works on
a woman who keeps a lock. I have three methods and I have no idea what they are for.*

*5. THE THING I CANNOT HOLD. In both rooms — and there is one room and one river, and I
keep calling them both rooms — something in the room was not there afterwards. The
twenty-two are certain of what they saw. The woman is certain the tide was what it was.
Nothing was removed and nothing was taken. The only thing that is different is what the
people in it believe.*

*6. And so: he moves things. That is the shape. A man moves a thing in a room while the
room believes it is still there. It is a transfer and it is done in the interval and the
interval is the time it takes to build a memory and no longer.*

*7. It is a movement. He takes a thing. He is very good. The eleven seconds is the moment of
it. He has done it in a way no one can follow because there is a man standing in the room
who is not looking at the thing and cannot be made to look at the thing.*

She read that seven times, and underlined 7 twice, and then went to bed and lay in the dark
and was honest with herself, which is a thing she is only able to do when there is nobody
in the room, and which is the reason she was living in a room above a tobacconist instead
of in a tent.

Seven is wrong. She did not know why. It was the shape of the sentence and not the content
of it, the way a picture hangs crooked and you cannot say which of the two nail-holes is
bad. Six was fine. Six could stand up. Seven had a word in it — *movement* — and the word
was doing all the work, and she had gone looking for the verb and had found one immediately,
and a man who is very good and has been doing it for a long time is exactly the man you
find a verb for immediately.

She got up at two and took the pencil to it, and tried the other verb.

*7. He does not move things. He gives them something to hold.*

She looked at that for a while.

*No. Worse. That is not a method, that is a description of a symptom, and I have been
writing descriptions of symptoms for six months and calling them methods. Give a room
something to hold and take it away and you have told me what happened and not one thing
about how. I could give a room something to hold in an afternoon. I have given a room
something to hold a hundred times. The entire trick is taking it away again, and a man who
could do the taking away in a lit room in front of twenty-two people in eleven seconds
would not need a ferry timetable, and he would not need a lock-keeper, and he would not
need thirty guilders.*

*So the taking away is incidental. The giving is the whole of it. What is being given?*

And then she stopped, because she had run out of paper, and it was four in the morning, and
she did not want to write the next line down at four in the morning, and the reason she did
not want to was that she already knew the next line, and the next line was the sentence she
had said to Sigrid Voll in a yard at Utrecht in a fortnight, which was this:

*He gave them a man, and he took him away, and they were still holding him.*

Which is not a movement. Which has no verb in it that can be watched. Which is a sentence
about a *person*, in a room, and takes eleven seconds because a person takes eleven seconds
to become a man in twenty-two memories and no longer.

She did not write it. She put the pencil down and left it on the floor among the maps, and
went to bed, and lay there until six, and got up and did not look at the paper again until
the September, when a man in a room at Utrecht said a thing that made her pick it up.

---

But the eleven days were not wasted, because the eleven days were when she found the other
thing, and the other thing is the reason she slept.

It came out of the touring map, and it was not a discovery so much as an absence noticed
four times in a row.

Nobody was ever in a position.

She wrote it in the margin of the Antwerp map, because it belonged there, and then she went
round all three cities looking for the places where she should have found a *position*, and
they were not there, and she went round them again, and they were still not there, and by
the third circuit she was writing down what was missing rather than what was present,
which is the only technique she has ever had.

**ROTTERDAM.** Missing: a booking for any of the eleven companies on the night in question.
Missing: a colleague who saw him that day. Missing: a card in a stall, a name on a
programme, a fee entered in a receipt book. Missing: any second person who was in that storeroom
and was not a member of the public.

**ANTWERP.** Missing: any engagement. Missing: any company within a day's travel. Missing:
any reason for a man to be in a lock-keeper's house on the Scheldt on a Tuesday in October.

**AND YET.** The storeroom was hired. The storeroom had a key, and the key came off the
yard man, and the yard man had a key because there had been a key for eleven years. Not the
storeroom's key. A *yard* key. A key that opens a door from outside, in a building with a
back entrance, at an hour when the street is empty.

Somebody had hired that room. Not he. He had not been the one who signed, and there was no
signature, and the hire was a cash hire to a firm of forwarding agents who do not exist,
and it had been made nine days before the Tuesday.

She sat on the floor of a room in Utrecht and looked at a map of Rotterdam for a long time,
and then she wrote the sentence she had been unable to write since the autumn, and it took
four attempts and she crossed out three of them.

*He was not in a position. Nobody was ever in a position. In seven years he has arranged to
be in no position, and every time he has needed a room he has had a room built for him by
a firm that does not exist, nine days in advance, in cash, with a key.*

*Which is the fourth method, and I have had it since August, and I have written it down four
times and crossed it out four times, because it is a method for a man who is extraordinarily
cautious and careful, and that is not the man, and it is not the trick, and I have been
hunting a trick for seven months like a woman with a lantern in a field.*

*Stop hunting the trick. Start hunting the paperwork. There is no trick. There is a man who
arranges things.*And underneath, in a smaller hand, and she did not cross that one out:

*And when I have the paperwork, I will still not be able to say what he did, because the
paperwork is only ever the frame. Something has to be in the frame. And I have been in nine
hundred rooms and I have never once been able to stand in one and tell anybody what was in
it, and that is the only thing I have ever wanted to be able to do.*

She put the pencil down in the middle of a map of Belgium and went to bed, and did not
sleep, and got up at five and did not look at any of it again until the September, because
a thing written at four in the morning on the floor with a cramp in your leg is a thing to
be read, not to be acted on.

# 26 · A Name Is a Claim

It was the *sentence* that did it, four days later, and it was the smallest thing in the
whole of the second half and Reiner nearly missed it because she was looking somewhere else
entirely.

The *sentence* is the document that ends it. It is one paragraph, it is signed by the
assesseur, and it is read aloud by the clerk, and the clerk reads it in the flat voice that
clerks have, and by the time a clerk has read a paragraph of French administrative prose
aloud to a room the room has stopped hearing words and is waiting for the end.

And halfway through it, a man in the good suit said, quietly, in French, to the man beside
him: "Pardon, what is the word?"

The clerk stopped. The assesseur looked up. Reiner, four rows back, came out of a chair
she had not realised she was sitting in.

"Say again, please," said the assesseur.

The man in the good suit had not been asked anything. That was the thing. He had been
silent for four days. The *requête* had been read, eleven questions had been answered, and
he had not spoken nineteen words in ninety minutes, and now he had spoken eleven, and they
had been in French, and they had been a question about a word.

"Forgive me," he said, in French. "The word for the thing you have written in the fourth
line. Is it *procédure* or *procédure*? I have not had cause to write it before."

"Both are correct," said the assesseur, in French.

"Thank you. I was not certain which was meant."

The clerk resumed. The assesseur put his glasses back on. The room settled. And in the
space of about four seconds, in a hall in Utrecht, four things happened, and only one of
them happened where anybody could see it, and Reiner saw all four, and she could not have
told anybody about any of them for a fortnight.

---

One: the man had spoken French. Not Dutch, not the flat transactional English of a man
who has lived in three countries for nine years and has never once needed to be liked in
any of them. He had spoken the language of a *salle*, the language the tribunals of Lyon
and Lausanne and Brussels conduct their business in, the language in which a *requête* is
contested and a *réponse* is filed and a *sentence* is pronounced and a man with three
disputes in France and a certificate of good conduct from Belgium has had nine years of
paper written about him in.

His French was excellent. Not good. **Excellent** — the excellent of a man who learned a
language at about twenty-eight, in a room, from people who corrected him, and who has kept
it alive in daily use for twenty years, which is the only way a language stays excellent
past thirty.

He had said the word *procédure* twice, in the two spellings, which is what a man does who
has heard both used and has never had to choose.

Two: his English was thin. Not bad — thin. Grammatically complete, with no errors at all,
in the way of a man who has had a very good education in a language and has spent nineteen
years using it for transactions, where a mistake costs money and is noticed, and has not
used it for anything else since he was seventeen. He had answered eleven questions in
short, clear, correct English with no colour in it anywhere.

Nobody in that room noticed. They could not have. Everyone in the hall spoke both
languages and had spent the morning listening to an assesseur in French and a clerk in
French, and an eleven-word question in French from a man who had been silent for ninety
minutes read as nothing whatever. It read as a man checking a spelling.

Three: he had asked the question anyway.

This was the part that took her eleven months to be able to look at directly. A man with
nine years of practice at not being in a room, standing at a table in a hall, in front of
forty people, in a good suit, in front of a clerk with a pen — had interrupted a formal
reading to ask about a spelling.

He had no reason to. The document would have gone through. Nobody was going to hold him to
a spelling in a word that was spelled both ways, and the assesseur had already said so,
and the clerk had already resumed.

He had interrupted a proceeding, in a room he had spent nine years avoiding, in order to
establish a fact about himself that nobody had questioned, and the fact was that he was
careful.

And a careful man in a room that is reading him is a man who has made a decision.

Four, and this is the one she wrote down and the one she has never been able to explain to
anybody satisfactorily, including to herself, at four in the morning, on a floor, with a
cramp:

**He did it in order to get out of the room with the language intact.**

Not the spelling. The *record*. Because a man who has asked a question in a proceeding has
a proceeding in which he has asked a question, and a proceeding in which a party has asked
a question is a proceeding in which the party's evidence is in issue, and a man whose
English is thin and who is being examined in a language he does not want to be examined in
wants that examination to happen in French, in front of a clerk, formally, on the record,
and once.

It was the most procedural thing anybody has ever done to her and it was four words long
and it was not about the trick at all.

That was the thing she could not put in a notebook for a fortnight. He had spent the whole of a
four-day sitting arranging to be a man who asked a question, so that somewhere in a record
there would be one line where he was the party who wanted a thing clarified rather than the
party who answered everything, and the line cost him four seconds and a spelling and it was the
only piece of vanity in nine years of perfect conduct, and he had done it for her, or for
himself, or for the record, and she has never been able to decide which, and it does not
matter, because he did it in French and in front of a clerk and it is in the transcript and
nobody can take it out.

---

She got him in a corridor on the Thursday, which took four days and two false starts and
involves going to a man in a good suit and asking to speak to him in a language, and it
turned out that the request itself was the door.

Reiner asked in French. That was the whole of it. She had thought about it for three days
and the entire technique was to open in French, because a man who has been in a hall for
four days being examined in a language he chose would take an English question as an
attack and a French one as a formality.

He came out of the room and stood in a corridor in Utrecht in a good suit and looked at
her, and did not speak, and waited for her to say why.

"Merci," said Reiner. "For the question about the word. It was the only thing said in that
room in four days that was not arranged, and I have been trying since Tuesday to work out
whether you did it for a reason and I have decided that you did, and I am not going to ask
you what it was."

"You are going to ask me about a door."

"I am going to ask you about a door in September, in a room, in front of the same clerk, in
French, if you prefer." She looked at him properly, for the first time since a shed in
Rotterdam in April of last year, when he had been in the doorway with his hands on his
knees in the manner of a man waiting for a tram. "I am not going to ask you now. I want you
to know that I know what you did, and that it was not about the spelling."

He looked at her for a while. There was a clerk going past with a folder and two notaries
going past with a folder and nobody in that corridor had any idea that anything whatever
was happening in it.

"Madame," he said, in French. "A name is a claim. I have been taught that by men who did
not have my advantages, and it is the only sentence I have ever needed, and I have used it
to answer a question in a room and I have used it to answer a question in a corridor and I
will use it in September, and I would be obliged if you would let a man do the only thing
he is actually good at."

He went back into the room.

Reiner stood in a corridor in Utrecht and understood, about four seconds later, that she
had been answered — properly, courteously, in the best French she had heard in a year —
and that she had been given a *réponse*, and that it was five words, and that it was filed,
and that the seal was on it, and that it was in a corridor in a good suit by a man who had
asked a question about a spelling four days earlier in front of forty people so that
somewhere in the record there would be a moment where he had been a man asking a question
rather than a man answering one.

Nine years. Three countries. Seven disputes. And in the twelfth month, in a corridor in
Utrecht, a woman who had come four hundred miles to find out how he did it, and a man who
had spent nine years making sure that nobody ever once had to stand in a room and be asked,
and between them, in eleven words each, a complete exchange in which the only method used
was courtesy, and in which not one thing had been said about the trick, and in which
everything had been said.

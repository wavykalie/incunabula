# 15 · All Seven Under One Roof

It was decided in a railway carriage between Delft and Utrecht and it was decided the way
everything in that trade is decided, by a man who had not mentioned it saying a thing
adjacent to it and four people who owed him money.

"It is the hall at Utrecht," said Verhagen, to nobody. "It is the hall at Utrecht in
February, and it is empty, and we have a book for the Saturday and no act."

There is a version of the northern company that would have filled it. There were four
other tents within two days' travel in February and three of them had a book and a floor
to put it on. What happened instead is the thing that happens in that trade roughly twice
a decade, which is that everybody agrees on the shape of the difficulty and then goes home
and each of them solves it differently.

The hall at Utrecht is a brick building with a stage that is nine inches high and a
ceiling of stamped tin. It holds two hundred and forty. It was built for a lecture hall
and it has the acoustics of a bathroom, and everybody who has ever stood in it has said
so, and the reply everybody gives is that everybody says so.

On the Tuesday the drawing men went at eleven. On the Wednesday a man from Rotterdam
arrived with a company of three and put a screen up in front of the stage, which is not
allowed and which Halvard Grens took down himself and did not apologise for, and which the
man from Rotterdam said nothing about, and the silence was the loudest thing that happened
that week.

On the Thursday a woman of about fifty arrived from Brussels with a case of instruments and
would not give her name, and turned out on the Saturday to be called Reiner, and turned
out on the Sunday to have been in the storeroom in Rotterdam in the spring, and Sigrid
Voll — who had known her name in April and had said nothing in April — put down the peg
she was holding and said, "Right," and went out into the yard.

She was gone twenty minutes.

When she came back she said, "Nobody was ever in a position to ask. That is what he has
been doing for nine years and that is what has been *given* to him, every time, by all of
us, and I have been part of it and I want it written down that I have been part of it, and
so has Johan, and so has Halvard, and so has every man in this trade since about 1920, and
nobody ever thought about it because there was never a reason to."

She had been crying. Nobody said anything about it, which is the correct thing and is also
the northern way, and the eleven people in that hall in February 1933 understood it
instantly and got on with their work.

---

The grammar broke on the Friday and it broke completely, and it broke because the northern
company does one thing and the southern rooms do another and both were in the building.

In the north, an effect is *named*. You book the Grand Transposition, you put it in the
hand, and the name is a claim on the room and the room may challenge it and a man who has
made a claim can be held to it. The tent is bigger than any man in it, so the tent does not
use names, and the only naming that happens happens in a *salle*, where a claim is a
weapon and a man who refuses to name a thing is a man nobody can touch.

That is the whole of it and it is clean, and in a *salle* it has worked for a hundred and
forty years.

In a tent, the doctrine runs the other way. A large name claims a grandeur the tent cannot
support. A tent uses what the tent is. And a man who names an effect in a tent is a man
who has agreed to be measured by a *salle*, and the two systems cannot both be true in one
room, and when they are both true in one room the room finds out which one it is inside
about four seconds into the first act.

Sigrid put it to the room on the Friday night, plainly, in the middle of the mess, and the
room agreed with her, and then nothing happened for a while.

"Two grammars," said the man from Rotterdam. "And we are proposing to mix them. In
February. In a tin-ceilinged hall in Utrecht where a man can be heard breathing in the
third row."

"Yes," said Sigrid.

"So what happens on the Saturday if somebody in that hall is from a *salle*."

"They will hear us name an effect."

"And they will hear us name *an* effect, which is a claim, and which in a *salle* means a
challenge may follow, and if it does follow then we are all in a building answering
charges with no tribunal and no *sentence* and no clerk, and a man who answers a charge in
a tent answers it in a tent, and we will be made a fool of in ninety seconds by a
bookmaker from Rotterdam who has never once held a hand."

"Then we do not name them."

"Then we have nothing to say on stage." The man from Rotterdam put his cup down. "Miss
Voll, I have driven ninety miles to be in this hall. I will do your week. But I will tell
you now, and I will tell you in front of Johan so that he can be the one to remember it:
there is a man in this trade whose whole method is *keeping rooms from asking*. And you
are proposing to put seven people from three countries in a tin room in Utrecht and let
the room hear a name."

The hall was very quiet.

"Sigrid," said Halvard Grens.

"I know."

"You have not answered him."

"I have answered him," said Sigrid Voll. "I have answered him in front of eleven people
and I have said *they will hear us name an effect*, which is a yes, and I said it in front
of Johan, which is the part that matters, and I would like somebody to tell me why I have
done it, because I have been sitting on a stool for ten minutes working out whether I am
brave or whether I am stupid and I have not managed it."

Johan said, "You are the tent."

"Yes."

"Then the tent names things," said Johan, "or the tent is a room where nothing is ever
said."

And the whole company laughed, and it was not a comfortable laugh, and it went on about
four seconds too long, and Sigrid Voll sat down on a stool in a hall in Utrecht and
discovered in front of everybody that she had just lost an argument by winning it.

Because the tent had answered her. Not the company and not the man from Rotterdam and not the
room in the end. The tent is bigger than anybody in it, and a thing that is bigger than anybody
in it cannot be named by anybody in it, and the moment she named the difficulty out loud the
tent had already decided what it was going to do on the Saturday, and the deciding was done by
a building, and nobody in the mess had been consulted.

---

The Saturday was fine. That is the part nobody believes, and it is the part that the whole
second half of this book is about.

Two hundred and eleven people came, which is the number in the back of the notebook and
which had nothing to do with anything. Halvard did the drawings and they were good. Johan
did the cards and won. Kees and the girl of seventeen did a rope routine in a room with
stamped-tin acoustics and got a laugh out of a room that had come to be entertained, and
the man from Rotterdam was in the second row and was not enjoying himself and stayed to
the end.

And at nine o'clock, in the interval, in a corridor with a fire door at one end, Elsa
Reiner asked Rask how many effects there are.

She had been in the country four days and she had asked nobody anything, and she had been
in the room for eight weeks and she had not asked him anything either, and Rask had been
in Utrecht for the whole of it, in a chair by the wall, with a cup he had not drunk from.

He had been in a chair by the wall because he was fifty-eight and because a man of
fifty-eight who wants to be left alone sits where the door is.

"How many effects are there," said Reiner.

"Five," said Rask.

"Thank you," said Reiner.

She had said *thank you* in a way that meant *that was not an answer and you have given it
to me as one and we are both standing in a corridor now.* Rask looked at her for a while
with the particular expression of a man who has been a conjurer for fifty years and has
been asked this question before by better people than her.

"You have five," he said.

"I have five."

"Then you have five." He drank from the cup he had not been drinking from. "Go and do
your week, madam."

And Reiner Reiner stood in a corridor in Utrecht and understood, in about the time it
takes to understand a step you have just gone down in the dark, that a man who owns six
effects and has just been asked how many effects there are will say *five*, and will say it
lightly, and will say it in a corridor, and will then deny that he has ever said anything
else.

He was fifty-eight years old. He had been doing this since he was nine. He had answered
four hundred conjurers in rooms and two thousand in corridors and he had never once said a
number out loud that he had not first checked against the list.

And he had checked. In a corridor, in a fire door, on a Saturday, in front of a woman who
had spent nine months getting to a building in Utrecht.

She went back into the hall and sat down and did not watch the second half.

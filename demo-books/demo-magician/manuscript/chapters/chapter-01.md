# 1 · The Interval

The room was nine feet by eleven and it had been a storeroom for paraffin and lamp oil
since before the war, and the man was sitting on an upturned crate in the middle of it
with his hands on his knees, in the manner of a man waiting for a tram.

That was the first thing Reiner noticed and the thing she would think about afterwards
more than any other. He was not arranged. Somebody had put him there and he had sat down
in the arrangement and then had stopped being involved in it.

"Two days," said the man in the doorway. "That is the whole difficulty. It is the two
days that I cannot get past, and I have been getting past it since Tuesday."

"You have not met Mr Van Eede," said Reiner.

"I have met him twice. He is the constable and he has been very careful with me."

Van Eede took a small book out of his inside pocket and opened it, and did not read from
it. "Careful," he agreed. "Not careful like a man hiding something. Careful like a man
carrying something he does not want to put down in front of a witness."

Reiner had come from Antwerp on the eleven o'clock and had walked from the Hoofdstraat
because the tram did not run to the Kralingen docks on a Sunday, and she had been in the
room for four minutes, and she had already decided something that she would not say for
another two days.

The decision was about why she was standing in it, and she had got that clear on the
quay at half past ten and had been annoyed with herself about it for two and a half hours.

Nobody had sent for her. That was the part she wanted on the record before anybody made a
thing of it later. A letter had come on the Thursday to an address in Antwerp where she had
lived for two years and a name she had not used since she was nineteen, and it had been
addressed to *the nearest conjurer* in the sense that a man in a police office at four
o'clock on a Thursday afternoon had been told by somebody that there was a conjurer in
Antwerp and had written it on a form.

She had read the letter on the Friday and had known at once that she was going to take it,
and had not been able to say why, and had spent Thursday night and most of Friday working on
why, and had arrived at an answer she did not believe at all, which was that there was a job
in Rotterdam and nobody else would do it and she was hungry. She had been working since
1926 and she was thirty-four and she was not especially hungry.

What she had actually done on Friday night, at about two, sitting on the edge of a bath in
an address where she had not been in six months, was to go through the rooms one at a time
and ask herself a different question in each.

The first was the question she used about a *requête*, which is whether there is a charge
against anybody. There was not.

The second was the question she used about an engagement, which is whether the room is
worth the trouble of setting up. A shed on the Kralingen field with a drain in the middle
of it was not.

The third was the question she used when a piece needed a person, which is whether anybody
will be believed who is in the room. And that is the one she had sat on for forty minutes,
and the answer had come out of the bath and the tiles and eventually off the end of her own
nose, and the answer was *yes, and he will be right, and that is the problem.*

Because he would be.

She had been in nine hundred rooms in twenty-five years and the one thing she knew, and
knew the way a carpenter knows a joint, was that a room in a shed on a Sunday is not going
to be wrong. Everything in a room is correct. People see what is in front of them and they
report it and they are almost always right, and the rare occasion when they are not is
either a lie or a light, and neither of those is interesting.

So a man on a crate in a shed who has a constable and nineteen accounts and two days
unaccounted for is, on the face of it, the most correct thing in Rotterdam, and she had
walked two and a half hours to look at the most correct thing in Rotterdam, and there was
no version of that in which the job was worth doing.

She had gone anyway. She had gone because in her bath on Friday night she had finally
admitted to herself the reason, and the reason had taken about forty minutes to say and was
four words long and she did not write it down then and did not write it down now.

She decided it because of the smell. Not the paraffin. Under the paraffin there was
something else and she knew it, and it made the back of her neck cold in a way she was
not going to examine in front of a policeman.

"May I go closer?"

"You may go closer. You may not touch him, and I will not pretend I did not say that, and
if you touch him I will have to write down that you touched him, and I do not want to
write that."

She went closer.

And stopped, because the room had told her four things in the two steps and she was not
going to be able to think about the man in the middle of it until she had them.

The ceiling was stamped tin, low, and one panel was out of line by perhaps half an inch
where the sheet had been cut for a pipe that was no longer there. The light came from a
single bulb in a wired cage on the wall, and the cage had been painted at some point by
somebody who had not known what they were doing, so that the wires cast four shadows on the
tin and the bulb cast a fifth, and everything in the room had five edges.

The floor was concrete with a drain in the middle, and the drain was the source of the
second smell and the reason the first one had not been able to get out.

And the crates: four high, four along the far wall, and the fourth row short by one, which
she had counted on the way in without meaning to, and which was the third thing.

The fourth thing was the door.

She did not go and look at the door. She looked at it the way you look at a card you have
been dealt — not at the card, at your own face, at whether you are about to do anything
stupid — and the door was in the wall opposite the light, six feet from where she was
standing, and it was shut, and it had a new key in it, and the key was in the lock, and
that is not a thing anybody leaves.

She had been in nine hundred rooms. The single most useful thing anybody had ever told her
was given to her at fourteen by a man called Furette who had died in 1928, and it was this:
*never look at the thing you have come for until you have looked at the room.*

Nobody in this room had looked at the door, including the man on the crate, including the
constable, including — as far as she could tell from where she was standing — the man who
had arranged the crates.

"Two days," Van Eede said again, and he had the flat affect of a man who has said a thing
many times and found that repetition did not make it less true. "Here is what I have. A
man comes in at the back on Tuesday, through the goods door, which is locked at six and
was locked at six. He has a key off the yard man, who has had a key for eleven years and
who says he let him in because he knew him, which I believe, because the yard man has
known him for a long time and does not care in the least what he does with the building
after dark."

He read it out without hurrying, which Reiner understood to be the first thing about him:
a man reading a thing he has read before, from a book he carries, to a stranger, in a shed,
on a Sunday, and it had been read so many times that the hurry had gone out of it and
nothing had been put in.

"I have it in six lines," he said. "Six lines and it has been six lines since the
Wednesday, and it has been six lines every time I have looked at it, and it does not get
better."

"That is usually a sign the account is true."

"That is usually a sign the account is empty." He shut the book for a second and then
opened it again, which she had watched him do twice in an hour. "Here is the six lines. A
man comes in Tuesday. The yard man lets him in. The man is not seen again by anybody. The
body is found at twenty to seven by a man who does not like the yard man. And the building
is locked at six and was locked at six, and I have not found the key that opens the
building at six, and I have looked for it for two days, and there is not one key. There are
three. The yard man has had a key for eleven years. The firm has one. And the building was
locked at six on Tuesday, and the man was inside it at some hour in the two days after, and
none of those three keys is the key."

He turned a page.

"Read that again, madam, and tell me what is wrong with it, because I have read it two
hundred times and there is nothing wrong with it and it is the most complete document I
have ever written about anything, and it accounts for six hours of a building and no part
of the two days that everybody else in Rotterdam is asking me about."

"And the body was found at six-forty."

"At six-forty the yard man's replacement found him, because the replacement does not know
the yard man and does not trust the yard man, which is correct of him." He turned a page
he could not see. "So the body is at six-forty and the entry is at some time on Tuesday
and I have two days because I cannot place a hand on anybody in between. Two days in
which the man was on this floor, and could have been on this floor at any point in them,
and nothing in this room will tell me when."

"Nothing in the room," said Reiner, "will not tell you when, because there is nothing in
the room that could."

Van Eede looked up. That was the first time he had looked at her properly and she felt it
the way you feel a cold draft.

"Explain that."

"May I say it in a way that will sound like nothing at all."

"Since this morning I have heard a man say that a horse is not a horse."

Reiner looked at the crates along the far wall. They were stacked four high, four along,
and the fourth row was short by one, and she had not known why she had counted them.

"You are asking me how a man got out of a locked room," she said. "I want to tell you
first that I know how you got that question, and that you did not get it from anything I
would call evidence. You got it because everybody who looks at this room says the same
face. I have seen it before. It is an ordinary face. It belongs to a man who has noticed
a thing and would very much like it to be a different thing."

Van Eede said nothing.

"So I am not going to give you a way out of a locked room, because there is not one, and
if I gave you one it would be a way out of a locked room and you would try it, and it would
work in here, and it would not have happened in here, and in four months you would be
standing in another room in another port with the same face on it."

"That," said Van Eede, "is a very unhelpful way of being right."

"I am aware of what it is. I do it constantly and I have never once found it useful, and
I do it anyway because I would rather be unhelpful and right than helpful and four days
late." She crouched, and did not touch. "May I look at his hands."

"You may look at his hands."

"His hands are clean. Everything else about him is not clean and his hands are the
cleanest thing in the building. Do you see it."

Van Eede came and looked. Reiner could see him deciding not to argue.

"I see his hands are clean," he said.

"They are clean because he cleaned them, on Tuesday, with something, before he sat down.
Or he cleaned them on Wednesday morning, which would mean he came back, which would mean
a key, which would mean the yard man is not the only door in this story." She stood up.
"And I am not going to ask you which, because if I ask you which, you will go and look for
a door, and there is no door. There is a man on a crate with clean hands and two days
that nobody can fill, and the two days are not the mystery. The two days are what is left
over when the actual thing has already been taken away."

Van Eede shut the small book.

"Then what is the actual thing."

"I do not know." She said it plainly, and it cost her something, and she did not dress it
up. "I want to be fair to you, constable, because nobody has been. I have looked at this
room for four minutes. I have not been asked the right question and I cannot invent it
and I cannot guess it, and a woman who guesses in the first four minutes is doing it for
the sound of the guessing."

"And if you had the right question."

"Then I would still not be able to tell you. I would be able to tell you the shape of it,
and the shape would be worth something, and you would write it down, and in six months it
would either have held or it would not have." She looked at him. "You will not believe
this and I would like you to have heard me say it anyway. I am not going to be able to
prove it. There is going to be no moment. You are going to keep waiting for a moment and
there is not going to be one, because whatever this man did, he did it in the time it
takes a room to be a room, and afterwards there is nothing in here that knows."

Van Eede took a very long time.

"You are telling me," he said, "that I should stop looking for how he did it."

"I am telling you that you should stop looking for how he did it, and that you should
start looking at what the two days are for, and that those are not the same question, and
that the second one is harder and has no trick in it."

He wrote something in the small book. He wrote for a while.

"There was a man in the yard," he said. "The predecessor. The one the yard man replaced.
He came through at four on the Tuesday and he went back out at ten past four, and he has
been inside since."

Reiner did not say anything for a moment, and then she said, "How many times have you
asked him what he saw."

"Once."

"What did he say."

Van Eede looked at the floor.

"He said," the constable said, "that he was not in the room."

"Yes," said Reiner. "That is what I would have expected him to say. Can I ask you one
question, and I am sorry that it is an ugly one, but I have to ask it before I leave
because if I do not ask it now I will think about it on the boat."

"Ask it."

"Would you describe him as watching?"

Van Eede went back several pages in the small book. Reiner watched him find it, and
watched his face while he read it, which was a thing she had been told not to do and
could not stop doing.

"His words," Van Eede said. "He says: I was looking at the door the whole time. I never
took my eyes off that door, not once, not for a second, and I would know it, because I
have been on that door eleven years and there is nothing on that door worth watching."

"And did he look at the door for the two days."

"Two days?"

"No," said Reiner. "No. He would not have. He would not have looked at the door for the
two days, because two days is not a thing anybody looks at a door for."

She was at the goods door and she had her hand on the bar when the constable said it.

"It is not that he is clever."

She turned round.

"That is what the man said," Van Eede said. "The predecessor. At the end of it, when I
asked him what he thought of it. He said: it is not that he is clever. It is that he
knows something we can be shown."

The paraffin was very still.

"He was not in the room," Reiner said.

"Twice over," said Van Eede. "Would you like to sit down, madam."

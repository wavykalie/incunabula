# 10 · He Wins Them

The thing Reiner could not put down, in the winter, had no shape to it at first. By
February it had.

She had gone in expecting to find a man who was good. She had a great deal of evidence for
a man who was good — a bakery bill, a field, a glass of water — and she had been carrying it
around Rotterdam and Utrecht and Antwerp since August like a person carrying a heavy case
uphill.

And what the dossier gave her, on the third read, in a room in Amsterdam with the canal
going past outside, was a career.

That was the discovery and it took her a whole night to get to the bottom of why it
frightened her, and the bottom turned out to be this: everything in the two hundred and
eleven pages was *competitive*.

A challenge is competitive. A *réponse* is competitive — you cannot file one unless you
have been challenged, and being challenged is a public humiliation, and a man who answers
one publicly and in writing has demonstrated something. The Ghent challenge he brought
himself, and paid sixty guilders to lose. The Lausanne one he brought himself and won and
did not collect. The Brussels certificate he went and asked a city for, unprompted, in a
town where he had never worked.

Nine years, three countries, seven disputes, and **not one of them is a man being pursued.**
Every single document in that canvas binding has him walking *towards* something.

"A duellist," said Reiner, out loud, to an empty room in Utrecht, about two in the
morning, and then she sat back and thought about the woman in Antwerp who had looked at a
corpse and told a constable there had been nothing to see.

A duellist kills people, obviously. A duellist's whole reason for existing is that there
must be somebody standing opposite. But the man in the dossier was standing opposite
*bakers*. Opposite a *compagnie* in Lyon, and a *salle* in Lausanne, and a tribunal clerk
in Brussels — winning on points of procedure in a language he had learned after he was
thirty, and being scrupulous about it. Paying costs he had won. Withdrawing at his own
expense. Filing a method that was four words long and completely unassailable.

It is, she thought, the most thorough piece of professional conduct in the whole of the
file, and it has been running for nine years, and its author has not stood in a room with a
magistrate since 1929.

And the reason that frightened her — she found this at the bottom, at about four in the
morning, and it was so much worse than the rest of it that she had to put the light on and
go and stand at the window — is this.

**Every one of the seven disputes was somewhere he had not performed.**

Lausanne, where he had not worked. Brussels, where he had never worked. The Ghent challenge
he brought himself, having no engagement in Ghent at all. The Lyon dispute in 1933, four
years after the Lyon dispute in 1927, in a city he had used and then stopped using.

Not a career. She wrote that and crossed it out and wrote it again, because it was true and
she did not like it. A *record* — a clean, sealed, expensive, unassailable record,
assembled town by town in places where nobody can put him on a stage and ask him to do
anything.

Not a performance either. In nine years of this file he has not performed once. What he has
built instead is the paper of a man who has never done anything.

And that, she thought, standing at the window with the canal going past, is the first thing
in nine days that has made sense of a room of twenty-two people watching a man do nothing
at all for eleven seconds.

Because a man who has spent nine years assembling a record of never having performed, has
also spent nine years learning that he does not need to.

She wrote it out. Four lines, at the top of a clean page, dated.

*Row 1. He has not performed in nine years. Not once. Seven disputes, three countries, and
every one of them is in a town where he had no engagement, or none left.*

*Row 2. He answers challenges. He does not accept them. A challenge answered publicly and
in writing is a demonstration, and a demonstration is the cheapest possible proof that
does not require being in a room with anybody.*

*Row 3. Therefore the eleven seconds is not the method. The eleven seconds is the only
occasion. He has never had to do it twice in the same city and he has never had to do it in
a city where anybody knew his name from a stage.*

*Row 4. Which means the room is not what makes it work. The room is what makes it
*survivable.* Somebody has to be wrong. And a man who has spent nine years being
procedurally unassailable in eleven towns has not got eleven towns' worth of witnesses
against him. He has got one, and she is in Utrecht, and she is the only one.

She read Row 4 four times.

Then she wrote under it: *and this is the first time in ten weeks that I have been frightened
by something I have worked out myself, and I am not going to write down the reason, because
if I write it down I will start working on it, and working on it is what I do instead of
being frightened, and it has never once in my life worked.*

---

The dossier went back in the tin on the Thursday and came out again on the Friday, because
she had begun, in February, to be unable to leave it alone, and L'Ombrière had noticed and
said nothing about it, which was the arrangement, and which on the Friday turned out to have
an end to it.

"You have had that open for six days," said L'Ombrière, in a house with a canal in front of
it, in Amsterdam, in the rain. "I counted. I said I would count and I have counted, and I
have been waiting for you to bring me to a question, and you have not brought me to a
question, so I am going to bring you to a question. What are you looking for?"

"I am looking for the seven."

"Which seven."

"The seven disputes. The seven years that have a document in them. The seven disputes and
the seven years are the same seven and I have been treating them as one thing for a month
and they are not one thing." She had the canvas open on the table. "Every one of those
seven happened somewhere he was not working. Did you know that when you wrote it?"

"Yes."

"Then why is it not in the book?"

"It is in the book. It is in the book eleven times. It is the sentence under each one." She
took the dossier and turned it round without being asked, which Reiner noticed and did not
comment on, and which was in fact the most useful thing that had happened to her all week.

"Here. The Lyon. *Disputed by a compagnie claiming an engagement.* There is no Lyon
engagement. There has not been a Lyon engagement in nine years. The tribunal decided the
ground of the dispute and the ground of the dispute was *who the contract was with*, and
that ground only exists if somebody is performing."

"Somebody was performing."

"Somebody was. Somebody who was not him." Reiner sat back. "He has been answering for
nine years, from outside, on a technicality that only makes sense if a man is on a stage.
Every one of the seven has been a case brought by somebody who assumed he was working, and
every one of the seven has been settled by a document that does not require him to be
working, and I have been reading those documents for a month as though they were a man's
*record*."

"They are his record."

"They are his **cover**," said Reiner. "That is the difference and it took me a year. Every
piece of paper in that book is a piece of paper that says *that man is not in a room with
you*, and he has been manufacturing that for nine years, one town at a time, at a cost he
pays himself, and I have been reading it as a man's history when it is a man's **alibi,
distributed.**"

L'Ombrière put both hands flat on the canvas.

"That is the second time," she said. "The second time in eleven years that somebody has said
a thing about this file in this room, and both times it has been in a fortnight, and both
times it has been you, and the first one you said to me at two in the morning and you said
it as though you were ashamed of it."

"It is not an alibi against a crime."

"No," said L'Ombrière. "It is an alibi against **being in a room.** And in eleven years I
have watched three men try to put him in a room and fail, and the reason all three failed
is that by the time they got to him he was not in one. He has not been in one since 1931.
He has been in a hall in Delft and a tent in Utrecht and a storeroom in Rotterdam for
eleven seconds, and in every other place in the world there is a piece of paper."

Reiner did not write this down, and was aware that she was not writing it down, and made a
decision about that which took her about four seconds and which she was not certain of for
another four months.

"You're not going to write that down."

"No."

"Miss Reiner—"

"L'Ombrière. I am going to have to stand in a room in about four months and say something
that is *true* and *checkable*, and I have had eleven months of things that are true and
have no way of being checked, and if I write this down in February then in June I will have
a notebook with a great deal in it and four witnesses to a door, and I have discovered in
the last ten days that the four witnesses are the only part of this that is worth anything
to me." She closed the canvas and squared it on the table, which was a habit from her
father. "So the door goes in a room, in a mouth, in front of people. And this goes in my
head until the day I have to say it, and if I forget it then I have not earned it."

L'Ombrière looked at her for a while.

"You are frightened of something," she said.

"I am frightened of eleven years."

"That is not a thing anybody is frightened of."

"It is the only thing I have ever been frightened of and I did not know that until March,
and I have worked it out sitting on a floor in Utrecht at four in the morning with a cramp
in my leg, and I have not written it down."

She put the tin on the shelf above the fire, on its end, the way a woman puts away a thing
she has finished with and does not believe.

"I am going to go and lie down," she said. "And I am not going to open it again until
somebody asks me a question in a room, and if nobody asks me a question in a room then it
can stay on that shelf until I am sixty, and I have decided that this evening and I would
like it noted that I decided it, because I have never once in my life decided to stop
looking at anything."

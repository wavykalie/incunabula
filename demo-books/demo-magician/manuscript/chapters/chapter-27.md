# 27 · The Retreat

He asked for leave on the Friday and was refused by a clerk, and it is the single funniest
thing in this book and nobody laughed, and Reiner has wanted to write it down for a year.

The application was four lines. It was on the correct paper. It was in the correct hand, and
the hand was excellent, and it was lodged at the desk of the assesseur at eleven o'clock in
the morning, and it was refused at half past eleven by a woman of about fifty-five called
Mrs Voorhout who had been a clerk of that court for twenty-two years and had never in her
life refused anything and did not want to start.

The words *prior engagement* are the whole of it. That is the phrase. A man performing under
a name, in a town, with a hall, may ask to be released from a sitting where his presence is
required, on the ground of a prior engagement, and the law allows it, and the law has
allowed it since about 1810, and nobody has ever thought it was a good idea.

"I am in Delft on Saturday," he said. "I am in a hall. There are two hundred and ninety
seats and I have been engaged since January. I have not been in a hall in a month and I have
an engagement and I am asking to be released from this afternoon."

Mrs Voorhout looked at the form. She had a lorgnette and she used it, which took a while,
and Reiner watched a man who had defeated a baker with four lines of paper in 1929 watch a
clerk of about fifty-five read four lines of paper in 1933.

"The engagement," said Mrs Voorhout, "is on the Saturday."

"It is."

"And this sitting is on the Friday."

"Yes."

"Then the prior engagement does not obtain," said Mrs Voorhout, "because it is not prior, it
is subsequent, and I am sorry, sir, but that is quite plain."

And that was it.

That was the whole of the defeat. A form, correctly made out, in an excellent hand, on the
correct paper, at a court where the man had never been in any trouble and had spent nine
years making certain that he had never been in any trouble, and it was refused by a woman
with a lorgnette on a technical point of order that has been in the statute book since
somebody's great-grandfather was a lawyer, and there is no appeal, because there is nothing
to appeal, because nothing has happened, because no wrong has been done to anybody and
nobody has said that one has.

The man in the good suit stood in the corridor of a court in Utrecht for about eight
seconds.

Reiner was at the end of the corridor. She had not been asked to be there and she had not
asked to be there and she had been there since nine o'clock because there was nowhere else
in Utrecht she wanted to be on a Friday in June, and she saw the whole of it, and she saw
the eight seconds, and she has thought about the eight seconds more than she has thought
about anything else in the case.

Because in the eight seconds, nothing happened to his face at all. That was the thing she
had not been ready for and never got over. She had spent eleven months learning to read a
face — it is the entire content of her trade, and she is extremely good at it, and there
are perhaps eleven people alive who are as good — and she stood at the end of a corridor and
watched a man receive a defeat and his face did not do anything.

Not a flicker. Not the tightening at the jaw she had written about in a notebook in
November. Nothing at all, for eight seconds, and then he said:

"Madame, I am obliged to you. May I ask whether the form is in order otherwise."

Mrs Voorhout, who had not expected the question and who afterwards told a colleague in the
corridor that it was the first time in twenty-two years anybody had asked her that, put the
lorgnette down.

"The form is in order otherwise, sir."

"Then I am obliged to you," he said, "and I withdraw nothing, and I will be present at
half past two."

He went back into the court. Mrs Voorhout looked at the form for a moment longer and then
filed it, because that is what one does with a form, and it went into a file with about
forty other forms in it, and nobody in the building ever looked at it again.

---

Reiner found Halvard Grens in the yard at twenty past two, sitting on a wall.

"You knew."

"I knew it was in order. I read the statute in March." Halvard Grens was not looking at
her. "I knew it would be refused. I did not know it would be refused over *prior*."

"Neither did he."

"No," said Halvard Grens. "He has been in three countries for nine years and he has not
had to file a *priorité d'engagement* in any of them, and I have been sitting in a yard in
Utrecht since March knowing that a man who can defeat a baker with four words on a piece of
paper is going to be defeated by a woman with a lorgnette, and I have been waiting to see
whether he would be."

"And?"

"And he was not," said Halvard Grens. "Do you not understand what you have just watched? He
was not defeated. He was *not* defeated at all. He was delayed by about a hundred and
fifty minutes, and a hundred and fifty minutes is nothing, and he knew it was nothing when
he asked, and he asked anyway."

"Then why ask at all."

"Because a man who is in a court on a Friday afternoon in June has to attempt something, and
everybody in that building knows he has to attempt something, and if he does not attempt
anything then there is a second procedure, and a second procedure is a hearing about
non-cooperation, and a hearing about non-cooperation is a thing that goes in a file that
somebody reads in ten years." He got up off the wall. "He attempted the correct form at the
correct desk in the correct hand and was refused on a point of order, and now there is a
record that says the respondent sought leave and was refused, and that record is *him
trying to cooperate*, and it is in the file, and in ten years somebody will read it and
think: well, he tried."

"He filed a defeat on purpose."

"He filed a *cooperation* on purpose, and the refusal was a bonus," said Halvard Grens, "and
he will tell you himself, in a corridor, in about two years, if you ever get to ask him, and
he will not be pleased that you have worked it out, because a man who is good at this does
not want the people who are good at this to know it."

---

She did not sleep, and the reason she did not sleep was that she had spent eleven months
trying to find a way to get a man in a good suit into a position where his own skill would
betray him, and it had taken a clerk with a lorgnette and a statute from 1810, and there
was no heroism in it anywhere, and she had gone through the whole of her life being good at
one thing.

At four o'clock she was still at the end of the corridor when he came out again, and he was
not going anywhere, and she said, because it was the only thing she could think of:

"Prior, not subsequent."

"Prior, not subsequent," he agreed.

"Was that a mistake?"

"It was a miscalculation of the month," he said. "It would not have been a mistake in
March. In March I would have had the Saturday free."

"You had the Saturday free in March?"

"I had nothing in March," he said. "I have had nothing in March for nine years."

---

That was the whole conversation, and it took ninety seconds, and it was the longest
ninety seconds of Reiner's working life, and she wrote it down that night in four words,
because it was four words and because she had learned in April that the four words were
the only part of the case that anybody was ever going to be able to check.

*Row 1. In March he has nothing. In March he is free. He has had nothing in March for nine
years.*

And then, underneath, slower:

*Row 2. He asked for leave in June, which was wrong, and he knew it was wrong when he asked,
and a clerk refused him on a statute from 1810, and it was the funniest thing that has ever
happened to me and I have not been able to laugh at it once, and I want it written down here
that the reason I cannot laugh is that I built the room, and I built it to put a man in a
position where his own cleverness would fail him, and instead a woman called Mrs Voorhout
did it in four lines with a lorgnette in about ninety seconds, and I have been in this work
since I was nine and I have never once been beaten by anybody in this trade, and I was
beaten on a Friday in June by a clerk who has never heard of him.*

*Row 3. And the worst of it, and I am going to have to sit with this one. He is not here
because of me. He is here because a woman in a canal house in Amsterdam wrote four hundred
words in a fortnight, and a constable who could not get past two days wrote a thing into a
small book about a door, and a man who keeps a lock at Delft said the same thing in a shed
in April that he said to three other people in May. None of it is me. I have been the
person in the fourth row for four days. In September I am going to be the person in the
fourth row for about four minutes, and if the door works then it works, and if it does not
then nothing anybody has done for twelve months has worked, and both of those are the same
thing and I have known it since a Sunday in April and I have not been able to say it out
loud until now.*

# 25 · The Procedure

He stood up at ten past ten on a Tuesday in June in a hall in Utrecht, and he was fifty-one
years old, and he had been in a great many rooms, and Reiner found — she had time to be
surprised by this, sitting four rows back with a shut notebook on her knee — that she was
not the only person in the hall who found it was a good suit.

That was the first thing. Not the face. She had expected the face and had a whole winter of
preparation built on the face, and the face was unremarkable in the specific way that she
had begun, in April, to understand was itself an achievement: a face that would be
forgettable on a Tuesday, at a distance, by a person who had not spent eleven months.

The suit was good. That was what she could not get past for the rest of her life.

Because a man who is about to be asked to account for a body in a storeroom in a town
where he has no engagement gets himself a good suit. He does not get a good suit because he
expects to enjoy it. He gets it because he has spent nine years being photographed, one
town at a time, in documents, and a man who has been photographed for nine years knows
exactly what a person looks like when they open a door.

---

The assesseur did it properly. That was the second thing, and it was the thing that undid
her, because she had spent eleven months building the case as an argument between people
and it had turned out to be a machine with a crank.

"Madame. The matter is called at the request of a party who is not before us. You will
understand that I have no power to compel any person's attendance and that everything said
in this room today is said at the risk of the person who says it." He looked at the clerk.
"Proceed."

The clerk read. It took fifty minutes and it was four hundred words and it was the *requête*,
and Reiner had read it in a hall in Utrecht in April upside down from four rows back and
had thought it was the best piece of writing ever done to her.

Hearing it read aloud in a room by a clerk, at that speed, in that voice, it was four
hundred words of nothing.

That is the first thing nobody tells you about a *requête*. It is a document written to be
*heard*, and it is written to be heard by men who have heard eleven of them, and every
sentence in it is doing one job only, which is to get to the next sentence, and by the time
it is finished the four hundred words have established that a woman believes something
about a man, which is not the same as establishing that she is right, and in a room where
the machine is working properly those two things are held apart the whole time.

At the end of it the assesseur said, "You will give your name."

"Elsa Reiner. Working conjurer, of Antwerp, and I hold an engagement at the hall in
Delft on Saturday, and I am not a magistrate, and I have no standing here and I have been
told so by the clerk and I accept it."

There was a small stir. She had said it because somebody was going to say it for her within
the hour and she wanted it on the record first.

"Have you anything to do with the company of the *salle* which has put this?"

"No. I have never been a member. I have never held an engagement under that name. I came
to that name four years ago, in a list, in a programme in a foyer in Rotterdam, and I have
been looking at it for eleven months."

"And you put the challenge."

"No. Madame L'Ombrière did, and she is not here today, and that is her decision and I have
argued with her about it and lost."

---

They took him at half past eleven.

It was Halvard Grens, of all people, who did the introduction, and Reiner understood about
four seconds later that this had not been arranged and was simply the first thing that came
out of a man's mouth, and that it was the correct thing, and that Halvard Grens had been
doing a version of it for nineteen years with chalk and a tent.

"Joachim Corbeau," said Halvard Grens. "Of the Grand Cirque Scandinave."

And there it was. She had a piece of paper in her hand that said a sentence and the sentence
was *he answered the question he was asked*, and she was watching a man in a hall in
Utrecht hear his own name read out in a room by a book man from a tent in Assen, and she
watched him receive it, and it went into him like water into sand.

The assesseur said, "You are the respondent."

"I am the respondent."

"You have been served?"

"I have been served. I came this morning and I gave my name to the clerk and I have a
receipt, and I would like it read into the record, and if the *requête* has been served on me
I would like the date of service read into the record also, because I have never in my life
been in a room where a date mattered and I have a feeling that it is about to."

The clerk looked at the assesseur. The assesseur looked at the clerk.

"Continue," said the assesseur.

And Reiner — who had spent eleven months learning to work with a face, and a room, and a
voice, and who had four notebooks' worth of material and a seal that did not exist and a
door that was four feet from a man's head in a shed in Rotterdam in April of last year —
sat in the fourth row of a hall in Utrecht and realised that the man in the good suit had
asked for the date of service to be read into the record, and that the clerk had gone white,
and that the request was in order, and that it was the first *réponse* anybody had filed in
the matter, and that it was four words long, and that it was not about the trick at all.

---

He had ninety minutes. That was the arrangement, and it was in the schedule, and the
schedule had been drawn up in March by L'Ombrière in a house in Amsterdam with a canal in
front of it, and the ninety minutes was hers.

The assesseur asked ten questions. Reiner wrote down the numbers in the margin of a shut
notebook and could not afterwards remember a single one of them, and the eleven questions
were, as far as she could reconstruct the entire afternoon from the outside, the following:

Whether he had performed at the Kralingen field on the eleventh of April last year.
Whether he had been in Rotterdam at any time in that year.
Whether he had held an engagement in Rotterdam.
Whether he was a member of any company.
Whether he had ever been charged with an offence.
Whether he had ever been the subject of a complaint to a tribunal.
Whether he knew the deceased.
Whether he had ever met the deceased.
Whether he had any property in the premises.
Whether he wished to make any statement.

And the answers were, in order: no; no; no; no; no; no; no; no; no; no; and *thank you*.

Nine noes, in a row, in a good suit, in a room, in a voice with nothing in it. And
Reiner wrote down the number of noes and then, some hours later, in a room over a
tobacconist, understood what she had actually been looking at.

He had answered nine questions in the negative without once being asked what he *had* done.

Not one of the nine was a lie. That was the part she kept turning over in the room over the
tobacconist until two in the morning. A man who says no nine times and means it nine times is
not a man who is hiding; he is a man who has arranged his whole life so that the true answer to
every question a tribunal knows how to ask is the answer that costs him nothing, and the only
question that could cost him anything is the one that nobody in that building had been trained
to put, and she had built the building, and she had filled it with people who knew how to ask
about dates and memberships and property, and not one of them had known how to ask a man where
he had been looking.

Nobody had asked him. She had built this room. She had put ninety minutes in it. And in
ninety minutes, ten questions, eight of them hers by right and two of them the
assesseur's own, and **not one of them required an answer about the storeroom.** They
required answers about Rotterdam, about engagements, about membership, about charges,
about property. All of which were true. All of which he could file. All of which he had
been making true for nine years, one town at a time, at a cost he paid himself out of a
fund in a canal house in Amsterdam.

The challenge had been a *requête* against the act, and the act did not exist, and the man
it was aimed at had said *no* nine times and had said it about nine true things, and the
room had a transcript, and the transcript was unanswerable.

"Miss Reiner."

She had not noticed him standing up. Van Eede was in the second row with a small book in
his hand and the look of a man who has been waiting an hour and a half for the correct
moment and has only just decided that this is it.

"The respondent has answered. Do you wish to put anything?"

---

Here is what she had.

Four witnesses to a sentence spoken in a room over a tobacconist in Utrecht in April, which
could not be shown to anybody without a week, and which in any case consisted of her own
mouth.

A sealed account of the eleventh of April, from a man who keeps a gate at Delft, in a
drawer in Utrecht, in which he says he was watching a door and does not say which.

Eleven months of notebooks in which every single thing in them was unfalsifiable.

A *réponse* that would be filed in September, sealed, four words long, in a good suit, by
a man who had asked for the date of service to be read into the record.

And a bag in a room in Antwerp with nine sheets of carbon paper in it that she had never
opened and had bought from a man who sold information and had told her, on a stair in a
corridor, in advance, that he was going to be disbelieved when he said so.

The assesseur was waiting. The clerk had a pen. There were forty people in a hall in
Utrecht and a man in a good suit sitting at a table at the front with his hands flat on it,
and he had not looked at her once, not in ninety minutes, not in eleven questions, and she
understood that he had made a decision about her in the first four minutes and had not
revised it, and that the decision was that she was a woman who would like to talk.

"No," said Elsa Reiner.

The clerk's pen stopped.

"I have nothing further to put today," she said. "I would like it recorded that I had
nothing further to put, and I would like the reason recorded too, since I am the only person
in this room who knows it."

"You will not put the reason."

"I will not."

The assesseur took his glasses off. It was the first human thing anybody in the room had
done since ten past ten.

"Madame, this is not a procedure in which a party explains her reasons for silence."

"No, sir," said Reiner. "It is a procedure in which a party states whether she has
anything further to put, and I have not. And I would like to say — to the room and not to
you — that the reason is that I have spent eleven months building a description, and a
description cannot be wrong, and I have found out this afternoon that I have been waiting
four months to be asked a question I have not thought of, and that the man who asked the
questions was not me and has never once been on my side, and that I cannot object to the
questions because I built the room."

She sat down.

"But I want the record to show one thing, and I want it in the transcript and not in a
note, and it is one sentence and it is the only sentence in eleven months that I have ever
been sure of."

The clerk waited.

"In September," said Elsa Reiner, "I am going to state where a man was looking, in a
room, in terms that can be checked, and I am not going to write it down first."

# 17 · Rask, Asked Directly

She did it on the fourth night, in the yard, in the cold, having spent three days deciding
how.

Three days is not long to decide how to ask a man of fifty-eight for the one thing he has
told you, by name, that he has spent fifty years not saying. She had gone at it four
ways and had thrown all four away.

The first was a question in front of people, which she rejected because it is a *salle*
move and he had said so in Utrecht and she had believed him.

The second was money, which she rejected at about a minute's thought and felt sick
afterwards, because there was a man in her notebook in a yard in the rain who had given her
a year for nothing, while angry, on purpose, so that she could not call it a gift, and she
was not going to be a person who paid a different man for a different piece of the same
thing.

The third was a letter, which she rejected because a letter is a written thing and he had
told her that a description of the sixth would be repetition rather than proof, and she had
not believed him at the time and had no idea why she believed him now.

The fourth was simply: ask him, in a yard, at night, alone, and hear what he says.

That was the one she had wanted to do in the first place and she had spent three days
finding reasons not to.

---

He came out because she was standing in the dark by the generator and he always heard the
generator, and he came out with a shawl over his shoulders because it was the second week
of February and he was fifty-eight, and she had noticed in December that he had started
doing that and had decided not to remark on it, and had then spent a fortnight deciding
that not remarking on it was a kind of lie and that she would remark on it, and had not.

They stood in the yard for a quarter of an hour before either of them said anything about
the sixth, and that quarter of an hour was the most frightening quarter of an hour she has
spent with him, and she has thought about it more than she has thought about any of the
three times in her life that she has been in a room with him.

Because she could have asked. That is the thing. There was no procedure. There was no
clerk and no assesseur and no *réponse* and nobody whatsoever between a woman in a yard in
Utrecht at eleven at night in February and a man standing four feet away with a shawl on,
and she had gone out into the cold three nights running to stand eleven feet off him and
had not asked, and he had watched her not ask, and they had both understood exactly what
was happening the entire time.

It is a thing you can only do once. She knows that as a working conjurer, in the way she
knows that a room is a room: it is not a rule anybody taught her, it is a property of the
material. Walk up to a man and ask him for the thing, and you get the thing, and you get
it in the shape of the question, and the shape of the question is the only thing about it
that will survive the morning.

So she had gone out three times to make the shape good.

"You have been in that yard three nights running," he said.

"You have been in that yard three nights running," he said.

"I have."

"And you have not asked me anything, and you have stood about eleven feet off, and you
have not asked me anything, and I have been in this trade for fifty years and I know what
eleven feet is." He put his shawl over his shoulder. "Eleven feet is a woman who is about
to ask."

"Yes."

"Then ask."

"I want you to name the sixth."

Rask did not move. She had expected him not to and had a whole speech built on his not
moving, and it went to pieces, because he did something else, which was to laugh — a
short one, without any amusement in it at all — and then to sit down on the crate at the
edge of the yard, which a man of fifty-eight does not do in February.

"Sit down," he said. "You are going to be here for a quarter of an hour and you may as well
be sitting."

She sat down.

"I am not going to name it."

"I know you are not."

"Then why are we having this conversation."

"Because you have come out into a yard in February four times to not ask me, and I would
like to sit down." He put his hands on his knees. "There. That is the whole of my interest
in this and it is an entirely selfish one, and I have never pretended otherwise and I am not
going to start at fifty-eight."

Reiner found that she was angry, and that the anger arrived very fast and from a
direction she had not prepared for, and she had about four seconds to decide what to do
with it and she said the true thing, which is what she always did and which had been her
worst professional habit since she was twenty-six.

"Then say what you *will* say. You have told me there is one. You have told me you can do
five and I can do five and there is a sixth that nobody can describe, and you said that in
a shed in Rotterdam in April to a constable, and you have said it to me in a corridor and
in a corridor again, and you have now said it three times to two people who were not your
students, and I would like to know *what* it is, and I would like to know before I am
forty, and I have earned it, and I know that I have earned it, which is the part that is
making me say this to a man in a yard at eleven at night instead of saying it in a
committee."

"Yes," said Rask. "You have earned it. You are owed it. I have thought about it since
January and I have decided to give it to you."

"Then—"

"Miss Reiner." He did not raise his voice and he did not move at all. "If I tell you what
it is, then in four months a man in a wig stands up in a room and asks you what the sixth
effect is, and you will have to say *I was told*, and you will have to give the name of the
man who told you, because a thing you were told by a witness is evidence, and you will
have to stand in that room and be a woman who was told."

"Yes."

"And I will have been in a room being asked the same question, and the man in the wig will
be a very clever man and he will put us in that room together, and he will ask whether
either of us could perform it, and either of us who says yes has described a method, and a
described method is a *réponse*, and a *réponse* is sealed, and the seal is the only thing
in the world that has kept me out of a court for thirty-one years."

The yard was very quiet.

"So I am not going to tell you," said Rask, "and when you are angry about it in about
eleven minutes, I would like you to hear the reason, and the reason is that I would rather
be a man who kept a thing than a man who handed a woman a weapon and got her killed with
it, and I have watched that happen twice, and in one of the times I gave it away."

Reiner was angry. She had said she would be and she was, and the anger was entirely
correct, and she could feel it being correct in the way that a thing being *right* is
sometimes worse than a thing being wrong.

She was angry for eleven minutes and she has never been angrier with anybody in her life
and she has never once been able to describe it without it sounding like a woman who has
been refused something she deserved.

It was not anger about the trick. She wants that on the record and she has been careful
about it ever since, because the anger people expect and the anger she had are not the
same anger and the difference is the whole of the second half of this book.

The anger she had was about being *managed*.

She had spent eleven months building a case. She had a notebook with nine months in it, a
fishmonger in Marseille, a boarding-house keeper on the Rue de la République, a woman of
about fifty in a canvas binding, a man who kept a gate, a good coat in a corridor, a
dossier kept at a woman's own wages for nine years, and a woman in Delft who had said *I
would say yes* on a gate on a Wednesday afternoon and had not known she was helping.

And in about ninety seconds, in a yard, in the cold, a man of fifty-eight had taken all of
it and folded it and put it in a drawer, and had done it in a way that was so gentle and so
complete that she would not have been able to object to it, and she had gone out into a
yard on four separate nights to earn the right to ask him a question, and he had taken the
question away from her by answering it, and he had done it kindly.

"You have decided what I can be trusted with."

"Yes."

"You have known me for eleven months."

"I have watched you for eleven months," said Rask. "You have a face that takes a thing down
and turns it over and finds the seam, and that is a magnificent face, and I have had that
face pointed at me for about nine seconds, and I do not think you have ever once pointed it
at a thing that did not have a seam."

She wanted to say: *you do not know what I would do with it.*

She wanted to say: *I have not asked you a single question in eleven months that I did not
think I could defend.*

And what she said, because she was angry and because it was true, was:

"Then I am not going to be able to use it."

"No."

"Then what was the point of eleven months."

"That," said Rask, "is the first sensible question anybody has asked me about this in four
years, and I have been waiting nine seconds for it, and I am going to answer it properly,
and it will take a moment, and you are going to be angry again when I have finished and I
am telling you now so that you do not think I have not noticed."

The thing that undid her was never the argument. It was the last sentence, and it was
delivered in a different voice, and she did not understand it for perhaps a minute.

"Miss Reiner," said Rask. "I am going to tell you what the sixth is, and I am going to do
it now, and I am going to do it in a way that you will not be able to use, and I want you
to notice that I have chosen the way on purpose and that I have done it for you and not
against you."

She did not say anything.

"Describing it would not be proof," said Rask. "It would be repetition."

"Is that all?"

"It is the whole of what I have, and I have been trying to say it to you since
August." He got up off the crate, which took him longer than it used to. "A man can
tell you what he did. You will nod. You will write it in your book. And in four months a
room will ask you to do it again, in front of people, and you will do it — you are good
enough, I have watched you, you will do it inside a week — and the moment you do it, it
stops being a thing that happened to a man in a storeroom and becomes a thing that happens
in rooms. And then it is a method. And a method is in a notebook. And a notebook is a
thing that a man in a wig can hold up."

"So I get it wrong."

"You get it *in a notebook*," said Rask. "Which is a different failure entirely and you
would not see it coming, and it is the failure I have been avoiding since 1919."

He was at the yard door. He turned round.

"I am going to say something unkind and then I am going to bed, and you will please not
write it down, and I am aware that asking you that is absurd."

"Say it."

"You do not want to be able to *perform* it." Rask said it without any emphasis and then
said it again, because she had not moved and he wanted her to. "You want to be able to
*explain* it. Those feel identical and they are opposite, and I have watched a very great
number of conjurers walk straight past the second one and fall down the first, and it is
always, without exception, the ones who cannot stop talking."

He went inside. The door shut. Reiner stood in a yard in Utrecht in February and found that
she had one hand over her mouth and had no memory of putting it there.

She went back up and she did not write anything down for two hours, which was the second
time in five days.

Then she wrote four lines, and the fourth was the one that mattered and she wrote it last.

*Row 1. He would not name it. He said naming it would make it repetition and I do not yet
know what that means and I am going to find out or I am going to die.*

*Row 2. He said I would get it wrong. He said I would get it wrong in a notebook. He said
it kindly and he was right and I want it on this page that he was right, because I have
known eleven men in this trade who would have enjoyed telling me I was wrong and he did
not, and that is the whole of what I know about him, and it is the fourth time.*

*Row 3. He was not frightened of the room. He has been in four hundred rooms. He was
frightened of the notebook and he has been frightened of it for thirty-one years and he has
been carrying one the entire time and I have never been in a house where anyone was.*

*Row 4. He gave me the thing I came for in a way I cannot use, at eleven at night, in the
cold, on purpose, and he was kind about it, and I am angry with him, and I have been angry
with him for two hours now, and the reason I am angry is that I cannot tell whether he did
it to protect me or to protect himself, and I have been in this yard four times and I have
asked him nothing else, and I have exactly one question left and it is the only one in nine
months I have been afraid of, and it is not about the trick at all.*

*It is whether he did it for me.*

# 8 · A Small Pay

Nobody died in that chapter and nothing was required of anybody, and it is the chapter
Reiner has come to think of as the one that made the other thirty-three possible, for a
reason she was not able to explain to Sigrid Voll when she tried, three years later, over
rather a lot of wine.

The company split in Utrecht, in a cold room above a tobacconist, on a Tuesday in
November, over money, and it split the way everything in that trade splits, which is
quietly, and with a document.

Here is the sum. It took four meetings to arrive at and the fourth meeting was not a
meeting at all.

A mid-sized European engagement of that period — a fair, a week at a market, a Saturday in
a hall — was worth, to a company of eleven, a little under four hundred guilders gross,
against costs of about two hundred and ten. The balance was divided by a formula devised in
Delft in 1919 and never once rewritten, and the formula was this: a fixed share to the
tent, a fixed share to the book, and the balance divided **equally**, and it did not matter
in the slightest how much work anybody had done, and it had not mattered since 1919,
because in 1919 the drawing man also carried the stove and the drawing man's wife cooked
for the corps and was also, in the summer, the person who stitched the costumes.

Forty-four guilders, then, in the formula, per man, on that week, before anything.

Reiner laid it out on the back of an envelope in the room above the tobacconist and watched
four men discover, separately and in silence, that they had all known the formula and none
of them had ever seen it written down.

"You have got it wrong," said the man from Delft, when she had finished. His name was
Verhagen and he had been in an office since 1908. "There is a supplementary."

"Tell me the supplementary."

"There is a supplementary for the *sole charge* of an engagement, which is where the tent
takes the risk and the rest do not."

"Which is what this is?"

"It is what a fair is," said Verhagen. "It is not what a Saturday in a hall is, and the
formula covers the hall, and the hall is nine engagements in ten."

"So in nine weeks out of ten, Halvard Grens, who has booked every one of them and carried
the risk of the tent standing in the wet, has taken the same four hundred guilders divided
by eleven as Johan, who cannot hold a pencil and lost a week in March."

Verhagen looked at the envelope for a while.

"Yes," he said.

Nobody said anything. Somewhere below, a man was putting a shutter up over a tobacconist,
and it took a great deal of time, and every man in that room listened to all of it.

---

The argument that followed was the one Reiner had been in rooms for, except that she had
never once been in a room where everybody in it was right.

Halvard was the first to give ground, which Reiner had not predicted and Sigrid had.

"Do not," Sigrid said to him, on the stairs, afterwards. "Whatever you were about to do,
do not."

"I was going to say that I would take a smaller share."

"You were going to say that you would take a smaller share," said Sigrid, "and it would be
true, and you would be praised for it in a way that would make it impossible for the next
man to do it, and in three years there would be nine men in this tent taking less than they
are owed and every single one of them would be a saint, and the formula would be exactly
where it was, and the saints would all be sixty."

"Then what is the alternative."

"There is no alternative, that is what I am telling you. There is only that you do not go
first. If you go first you make it a question of character and then we are in a business
where the man with the best character has the most money, and it will be Johan inside a
year, and Johan will not know it is happening and Johan is a good man and it will make him
unbearable."

Halvard thought about that for a long time.

"Write it down," Sigrid said. "Write the number and write the formula and write the year
it was invented and write who it suits, and put it in the book, and say nothing. In eleven
years somebody will be a great deal older and it will be in a drawer and it will do more
work than anything either of us says tonight."

He wrote it down. Reiner saw him do it, four lines, in a small hand, on the back of a
guarantee, and she thought: *this is the only time in this entire business that anything
gets fixed, and it is fixed by a man writing it down and saying nothing, and that is
either the most hopeful thing I have seen in six years or the saddest.*

---

The document came on the Friday evening, and it described rather than decided, which is how a trade writes things down when it has finished arguing.

It was one page. Verhagen wrote it in the hand he used for accounts and it set out the 1919 formula in full and it set out what each man was owed on the season at forty-four guilders, and it set out, in a sentence at the bottom that had taken four meetings to arrive at, that the supplementary payable on a sole charge was payable at such rate as the office should determine, which meant that the office would determine nothing and the sentence would sit there being reasonable for a very long time.

Halvard read it twice and signed it. Johan read it once and signed it upside down, on purpose, so that it would have to be turned to be read, and Verhagen looked at that signature for a while and said nothing at all and filed it, and the page went into a drawer in Delft where it very likely still is.

Nobody signed anything else and there has never been another document, and what the one page did not say was that the drawings had been free for three years and that a notebook in a drawer in a tent was worth forty guilders a quarter to the office that wrote the supplementary, and the reason it did not say those things is that nobody in the room wanted them said, and that is the whole of the trade's method in one page of accounts.

---

She found out what Johan was paid on the Friday, and the finding was a nothing at all, and
it was the thing that made her begin to keep a record of what people said before they said
the other thing.

She did it by accident. She asked him what a fair was worth in Holland and he said eleven
hundred, and then he said *about* eleven hundred, and then he said *well, it is eleven
hundred and it is a good one, but you have not asked me for a fair, you have asked me what
a fair is worth, and those are different questions, and I answered the first one, and you
are going to be furious with me because the second one is the interesting one.*

"How much of that eleven hundred do you get?"

"The same as Halvard."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is what I get," said Johan. "That is what I *get*. What I do with it afterwards is a
different matter and it is my business and it is a good business, and I will tell you one
thing about it because you have not told anybody anything about yourself in three weeks and
we are friends."

"You are not my friend."

"I am a little bit your friend. You have got a face like a receipt and you have been
checking my arithmetic since the Tuesday, and there is nobody else in this tent you can
check, so we are friends." He grinned. "I take my forty-four guilders and I keep a
notebook, and in the notebook there is every fair in Holland and Belgium for six years and
what it actually cleared, and I sell a page of that notebook to a book man in Delft for
forty guilders a quarter, and the book man uses it to make the supplementary that the
office pretends it invented."

There was a silence on the loading ramp.

"That is not a fair trade," said Reiner.

"It is the only fair trade in this business," said Johan. "Everyone who has ever been a
fair performer knows what a fair actually clears, and it is not in any ledger in Delft, and
the men who pay for it are the men who cannot ask. And the man in Delft is not a thief. He
is worse than a thief, because he has *invented a supplementary*, in a room, with figures,
and he believes in it."

Reiner looked at him.

"And Halvard knows."

"Halvard has known for three years and Halvard does the drawings for free, and has done
since 1932, and knows it, and does it." Johan shrugged. "So the whole thing works. It is
a beautiful thing. Everyone in it knows about half of it and the half they know is always
the half that makes them the villain, and so nobody can do anything, and I make a hundred
and forty guilders a year out of a drawer and I am the second-best-paid man in the tent."

---

They parted on the Saturday.

It was not dramatic; Reiner had been in a great many rooms where it was dramatic, and in
every one of them the man leaving was the one who had been shouted at. Here four men left,
and one of them was crying, and it was the one who cried who was owed the most, and the one
who cried was Johan.

He cried on the loading ramp with his back to the cart and did not make a sound and did not
stop, and Halvard stood four feet away with a rope in his hands and looked at the horizon
and gave him the whole of it, and nobody else saw, and Halvard mentioned it to nobody for
eleven years.

Reiner walked back to the yard with Sigrid and did not say anything for a quarter of a
mile.

"You have been quiet," said Sigrid.

"I have been counting."

"Count what."

"How many ways there are for a man to be paid less than he is owed in this business." She
was quiet for a moment. "It is not the one you would think. Not Delft, taking his
percentage out of the top. Four men and a formula and a book, and it has been running
since 1919, and every single person in it has a perfectly good reason, and nobody is a
thief, and that is what makes it so hard to write down."

"Yes," said Sigrid.

"You knew."

"I knew the shape of it. I have known since I was nineteen." She pulled her coat up. "I
came into this trade because I could do one thing and it was not special and I was not
pretty, and the trade did not ask me anything about myself, and that is the only reason
I am in it. And I have learned in the last two years that this trade does ask you things.
It asks you whether you will be quiet while somebody does a job badly, and if you will be
quiet, it is very kind to you for thirty years, and if you will not be quiet, then you
are a woman with a notebook, and the notebook is worth a hundred and forty guilders a year
to somebody."

"And which are you?"

"I am the tent," said Sigrid Voll, and stopped, and they were at the corner, and she said
it again. "I am the tent. I have been the tent since I was nineteen and I have got very
good at it, and the price of being the tent is that I know the whole of it and I say none
of it, and I have got very good at that too."

---

She wrote it all down that night, in the notebook, in Utrecht, in a room over the same
tobacconist, and it came to two thousand words and it is the longest single document in the
whole case file, and it is about money.

*Row 1. The formula is from 1919 and has never been rewritten. It does not reward work.*
*Row 2. The supplementary is invented in Delft and is arithmetically false. Johan has sold
it to the man who invents it, for forty guilders a quarter, for six years, in a notebook.*
*Row 3. Halvard Grens has known for three years and does the drawings for nothing, and
knows it, and does it, and is the best book man in the north.*
*Row 4. Four men left in Utrecht in November. One of them was owed the most and one of
them was crying and the one who cried was the only one who had done nothing wrong.*

And then, at the bottom, in a slower hand, four lines that she tore out and did not send:

*If he has a man in every town who is good at his job and quiet, then I cannot get near
him, and I have spent six weeks learning that I cannot, and I am not angry about it. The
men in this tent are nobody's men — they never were. Eleven people agreed a fair in 1919
and have never once broken it, and that is the most decent thing I have ever stood next to,
and it is the exact shape of the thing I have to get past.*

She tore out the four lines and read them once, and then she put them in the fire, and then
she wrote them out again in the same book on the following page in smaller writing, because
a thing that is only ever in your head is not a record, and she had nine years of practice
at knowing the difference between the two.

Then she shut the notebook, and put it under the mattress, and went down to see whether the
stove in the front room had gone out, which it had.

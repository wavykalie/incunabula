# 11 · The Second One

It was a man of about fifty in a lock-keeper's house on the Scheldt, and the corps[e] knew
he was there, and had been paid, and had said nothing, and the reason they had said nothing
was the only interesting thing in Antwerp that winter.

Reiner got the whole of it out of Sigrid over two days and it took the shape of a sum.

De Vries had been in the northern circuit for six years. He was a small, careful, unremarkable
man who did card work and who had a cough, and in October the corps[e] had been told by a
man in Delft — by letter, on a Thursday, on good paper — that there was money in a
particular house for a particular man, and that no questions would be asked in any quarter,
and that the corps[e] would continue to receive its share from that quarter in the ordinary
way.

"They knew," Reiner said.

"They were told," said Sigrid. "That is a different thing and you will need the difference
later. They were told that there was money and that nobody would ask. Nobody told them
anything at all. Not one of those eleven men has ever been in that house."

"And they went on taking the share."

"They went on taking the share and that is the part you will not like, and here it is." She
was not looking at her. "Verhagen, in Utrecht, in November, took four men out of a
company of eleven over a formula. The formula was wrong. The formula has been wrong since
1919. And in January, De Vries's widow was given a quarter's share by a man from Delft with
no letter and no explanation, and the amount was correct, and Verhagen was the man who
wrote it, and he wrote it, and he told me he wrote it, and he said: *I have taken four men
out of a company because a formula is unjust and I have just written out a correct payment
to a widow from money I did not have a claim to, and I would like you to explain to me the
difference between those two things, because I have been unable to find it since Tuesday
and I have not slept.*"

"He did not sleep over it."

"Nobody slept. It was the worst three weeks of my working life." Sigrid finally looked up.
"But that is what a good man looks like, and I have been in a great many rooms with a great
many good men, and I am telling you the entire content of what I know about it, which is
that they did not know, and that being paid and not knowing is not the same as being paid
for it, and that in about four months a room full of educated men are going to be told that
eleven conjurers took money to be silent, and it will be true that they took money, and it
will be true that they were silent, and it will not be true that either of those two facts
has anything to do with the other one, and I am not going to be able to prove that in a
room."

"Who signed the Delft letter?"

"Nobody signed it. It came on the paper of a firm of forwarding agents in Rotterdam who do
not know they exist. I have the letter. I did not show it to you because I have been
carrying it since January and I wanted to have it a little longer."

---

She went to the house in March, in the rain, with a lawyer from Antwerp who had agreed to
come and had then not come, and the door was answered by a woman of about sixty who looked
at her and said, "You are not from the police."

"No."

"Then you have come about the money and I can tell you about the money, and I have told
about the money eleven times, and I would like it to be the last time."

There had been a fire in the grate. There had been one fire in the grate in October, the
tribunal had established, and the rain had got in and ruined two books, and the woman
apologised for the books twice.

The kitchen was the front room. That was the first thing Reiner noticed and it turned out to
be the whole of the case in a piece of furniture. The front room was the kitchen, and there
was a table in it with a cloth on it, and a cloth on a table in a lock-keeper's house on
the Scheldt in the autumn of 1931 is a thing about eleven o'clock in the morning on a
Sunday, because a woman of sixty who has been keeping a house since she was forty does not
have a kitchen, she has a room with a fire in it and a table she eats at, and everything
else in the building — and there were four other rooms and they were all shut — is for
people who are staying.

The woman had not opened the other rooms. Not once, in two hours. Reiner saw the four doors
and saw them stay shut, and understood, about half an hour in, that those four rooms were
the thing, and that everything the woman was going to tell her happened in the front room
in daylight with the curtains open and a fire lit and a cloth on the table, on purpose, in
October, with the other four rooms shut.

*He asked for the back room.*

That was the second thing, and the woman said it in a way Reiner wrote down twice because
Reiner did not believe it the first time.

"He asked for the back room. In October. In the morning. I said there was no fire in it
because there was no fire in it, and he said he did not want a fire, he wanted a room with
a door that shut, and I said *Mr —*, I did not know his name in October, I said *there is
the front room*, and he sat in the front room for eleven hours and never once asked for the
back room again, and I have thought about that every day for a year, and it is the only
thing about the eleventh of October that I cannot account for."

"He wanted a door that shut."

"That is what I said to a man from a court in January and he wrote it down and he did not
come back, and I have been told since that it is in a file, and I would like to know what a
file does with a man who asked for a room with a door that shut." She straightened the cloth
with two fingers, which she did perhaps a dozen times in two hours. "The front room has a
door that shuts. Everybody's front room has a door that shuts."

"Yes," said Reiner. "But you have four other doors in this house and you did not open one
of them and I would like to know why."

The woman looked at her for the first time with any attention at all.

"Because a room with nothing in it is a different thing from a room with a fire in it," she
said. "And a man who has asked for a room and been given the room he asked for, in the
morning, in daylight, with a cloth on the table, is a man who has been *received*. And I
have had four people in the back room in ten years and two of them were for an hour and one
of them was for a night, and I could not tell you one thing about any of them except that
they came in the back way, and I have thought about that since the Wednesday."

She was very composed and Reiner sat in her kitchen for two hours and learned the following,
in this order, and in the order she had them, which Reiner wrote down and thought about
afterwards for a long time.

He had come on a Tuesday in October and had been polite, and had asked about the river, and
had stayed for a whole day and had not performed. Not once. He had asked her to show him the
lock, which she had done, and he had asked how long the tide took, which she had told him,
and he had asked what a lock-keeper's day was like, and had listened to the answer.

He had not asked her anything about the corps. Not one question in a whole day.

He had, at the end of the day, given her a folded paper and said it was for the market at
Breda, and she had given it to a man in Delft, and had been given thirty guilders.

"He was in my house for nine hours," said the woman, "and he did not do anything at all,
and thirty guilders is a great deal of money, and I have had thirty years to think about
that day and I have got exactly one thing, and it is this. He asked me the tide twice. Not
once. Twice, about four hours apart, and he wrote the second one down, and I noticed him
writing it, and I thought: *he is making a record.* And I have thought about it every
night since."

Reiner did not move.

"I am a lock-keeper's wife," said the woman. "I have had the river for thirty years. I
know what twice means. Once is a man being curious. Twice is a man making a table."

---

She walked back along the Scheldt in the rain and stopped at the place where the canal goes
in, and stood there, and worked out the thing she had not worked out in Rotterdam, and it
took her about four minutes and it was the whole of the second half of the case.

The first death had had no motive she could find. The second had thirty guilders, and the
thirty guilders had come from a firm of forwarding agents who do not exist, and the thirty
guilders had been accepted by a woman who was not a witness to anything.

They talked for eleven hours and Reiner had the shape of it in four pieces, and the first
was that they had talked about the river for the first five of them.

Not about the lock. About the *river*, at a level of detail that Reiner — who has been in
nine hundred rooms and can follow a conversation being conducted as a method — could only
just keep hold of. He had asked what the tide did at the bend below the house. He had asked
whether the current ever changed. He had asked what happened in a hard February.

And the woman of sixty had answered all of it at length, and had enjoyed it, and Reiner
understood on about the second hour that this was the point.

"You are enjoying this."

"I am," the woman said, without any embarrassment at all. "I have had a man in my front
room for eleven hours in October asking me about the Scheldt, and I have not had anybody ask
me about the Scheldt since my husband died, and he worked it. He asked me whether the
current ever changes, and I said only in a hard February, and he said *what happens in a
hard February*, and I talked for about forty minutes, and it was the best conversation I
have had in this house since 1931, and I have thought about it a great deal and I do not
like it."

"Why not."

"Because I have not told anybody about it." She put her hands flat on the cloth. "I told a
man from a court that I was a widow with no family and that a stranger was in my house for
eleven hours, and I did not tell him that we talked about the river, because it does not
bear on anything, and because a thing that does not bear on anything is the only kind of
thing I have had in this house in three years, and I was not going to hand it to a man in a
corridor."

Reiner wrote that down in full, and it is the only page in the case file that has nothing
to do with the case, and she has never been able to decide whether that is a weakness of the
case or the whole of it.

The second piece was the hands. She had noticed them at the table in April and had not been
able to put anything on them for six months, and in the front room of a house on the Scheldt
she got four sentences about them and the fourth was the one that mattered.

"He held a cup for about four hours. That is all. He held it. It was half full and he did
not drink it and he did not put it down, and at some point in the afternoon I took it away
and filled it and gave it back, and he said thank you, and he held it again."

"That is nothing."

"It is nothing to you, and I have thought about it every day for a year," said the woman,
"because I do the same thing. Not with a cup. I hold the same cup for eleven hours when
somebody is in my front room, and I have never once known I was doing it, and my husband
laughed at me about it, and he is dead, and I have not had anybody in this room since, and
on the eleventh of October a man I had never seen held a glass of water in my front room for
eleven hours, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it. I would like to know what
it is. Nobody has ever been able to tell me. I have asked a doctor and he laughed."

The third piece was that he had not once asked her anything about the corps, or the circuit,
or the trade, or who she was, or where she had come from, in eleven hours. And the fourth
was the tide.

But he had asked about the tide **twice**, and had written the second answer down.

A man who kills a man in a storeroom in Rotterdam has a room full of people who were right
about what they saw. A man who kills a man in a lock-keeper's house on the Scheldt has
apparently been in the house for nine hours, and done nothing at all, and asked a question
twice.

And a question asked twice, and written down, is a *sample*. Two readings of a river,
taken four hours apart, by a man who is building something, and who went home and used
them.

Reiner stood in the rain on the bank of the Scheldt and understood that he had a laboratory
and it was a *schedule*, and that the schedule was not the eleven seconds at all.

The eleven seconds were only the part that other people could see.

She had been hunting the trick since August. It had taken her seven months to work out that
she had been hunting the wrong thing in the wrong direction, and that the thing she had
been hunting was the cheapest part, and that it was cheap *by design*, and that a man who
spends eleven seconds doing nothing in a lit room is not showing off.

He is giving a room the only thing he can be seen giving, so that when the rest of it is
asked about later — by a widow, by a tribunal, by anybody at all — there is a room, and
there are twenty-two people in it, and they were right, and it does not matter in the least
what was in the room, because the question was never *what was in the room*.

It was *was anybody there*.

And in a storeroom on the Kralingen field on a Tuesday in the spring there were twenty-two
people, and every one of them was in a storeroom, and that is why nobody has ever been able
to touch him, and that is not because of a trick.

That is because a man who is in a room with twenty-two other people has an alibi made out
of twenty-two liars who are all telling the truth, and he did not have to arrange a single
one of them.

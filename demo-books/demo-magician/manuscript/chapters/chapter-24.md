# 24 · Twelve People in a Room

The hall at Utrecht was not a court, and that was the first thing Reiner noticed and the thing
she spent the whole of the first morning being angry about, and the reason she was angry
about it was that she had been told by a man in Rotterdam that it would be a court and she
had believed him because believing him had been convenient.

It was a room with a table at the front and eleven chairs, and a clerk, and a man in a wig
who was an *assesseur* and had been an avocat for twenty-six years and who had agreed, for
a fee of four hundred guilders paid out of a fund that did not exist, to sit in a room and
read a document aloud and ask questions.

That is all it was. And it was enough to take the entire case out of her hands, and she
understood that in about four minutes, and the understanding was the most useful thing that
happened to her that week.

Here is what she had been doing for eleven months, and she could see it now from a chair
four rows back, and it was this: she had been building a case, and a case is an argument
you make to a person, and a person can be argued with.

She had never in her life been in a room where an argument could not be made. Not once. She
had been in nine hundred rooms and every one of them had been a room with a person in it
who could be turned, and the whole of her craft is the turning, and it is a craft and it is
a real one and it is worth a very great deal in a tent.

At ten o'clock on a Tuesday in June, in a hall in Utrecht, there were twelve people at a
table, and the man at the middle of the table was going to read things aloud, and the
person reading them aloud was not going to be turned by anything she did for the next four
hours, and the reason was not that he was clever. The reason was a *schedule*.

---

The schedule was on the table in front of him and it was four pages and it had been drawn up
in March by somebody who had thought about it more carefully than she had.

Reiner read it upside down from four rows back, which is a thing she is good at, and by
eleven o'clock she had the shape of it, and the shape of it was this.

The matter would be called a *requête* — that is, a formal challenge — and the challenge
would be against the *act*, not against the man, because nobody had standing to challenge a
man. It would name a venue. It would be answered by a *réponse*, and the *réponse* had to
be delivered in a sealed envelope, and the *sentence* would be delivered by the assesseur.

And every one of those three things was hers, or would be, in the ordinary way.

"You have built the trap out of our own procedure," she said, to nobody, on a Tuesday
morning, in the fourth row, with her hands in her lap. "Every door in it is a door I have
to walk through and file something in."

"Yes," said Halvard Grens, from the fifth row, in the voice of a man who had said *yes* a
great many times.

"And in September he will file a *réponse*."

"He will file a *réponse*."

"Four words long, sealed, naming a method he has not named since 1931, and the assesseur
will accept it because a *réponse* that names a method is a complete answer and no man in
that room can go behind one without a second tribunal and a second set of costs, and he
will do that in a room with a document in it and I will be sitting four rows back with my
mouth shut."

"Yes,"said Halvard Grens.

Reiner put her hands over her face for about four seconds, in a hall in Utrecht, in front
of a clerk and two notaries and a man in a wig, and Halvard Grens — who had not asked her a
single question since Utrecht in February, who had sat through eight months of not asking,
and who had turned up in April uninvited to a room over a tobacconist to hear a woman
describe a door — put his hand, briefly, on the back of her chair, and took it away again,
and said nothing, because he had said in February that he would not ask her a question and
he intended to keep to that until the day he died.

---

They called it at ten past ten and it took four hours and eleven minutes, and in that time
Reiner learned more about the inside of her own case than she had in eleven months of
working on it, and none of it was about the trick.

At half past ten the clerk read the *requête* and it was hers — she had not written it and
L'Ombrière had, in a house in Amsterdam with a canal in front of it, on nine sheets of paper
that had cost nothing, and it was four hundred words and it was the best piece of writing
she had ever seen done to her.

At eleven the assesseur asked his first question. It was: "Do you put that the respondent
appeared at the said premises on the eleventh of April last year?"

And Reiner discovered, in a hall in Utrecht, in the fourth row, that she was in an
agonising position, and that the position was *entirely* her own fault, and that it would
have been the same in any court in Europe.

Because there was no answer.

The honest answer was *I do not know that he appeared at the premises*, which is the answer
she believed and had believed since the autumn, and which is worthless in a room, because a
room cannot do anything with an answer it cannot file.

The useful answer was *yes*, which she could have given, and would have given, in
November — before Utrecht in February, before the yard and the year, before a man of
fifty-eight had sat in a cold corridor and told her a thing she had not asked for. In
November she had had the [presumed / actual] notation and a schedule and a coffer in a
field and three deaths and a great deal of evidence and no idea at all that it was not
enough, and in November she would have said *yes* with about nine-tenths of her body
weight.

In June she could not say it, because in June she knew that a man who had never been in a
court in his life had arranged to need no testimony from anybody.

The assesseur waited. The clerk waited. A man in the third row turned a page.

"I cannot say that he appeared there," said Elsa Reiner.

Somewhere at the back of the hall a man said, "Ah," in a way that meant *so the whole
morning goes*, and she heard it, and she turned round and looked at him, and he was
Brynjar Sigmundsson and he had not made the sound.

She looked back at the table.

"I can say," she said, "that a man was at those premises, and I can say the hour, and I can
say what was in the room and I can say what twenty-two people said afterwards, and every
one of those twenty-two was right, and I can say that none of them is able to say the one
thing I need, and that is not because they are liars. It is because none of them was
looking at the right thing, and I have spent eleven months proving that they were not."

The assesseur wrote something down. It took a long time.

"You say they were not looking at the right thing."

"I say they were never watching him. They were watching a man in a room, which is what
people watch."

"That is the same man."

"Quite so," said Reiner. "A man in a room, standing in the middle of a lit space, looked at
by twenty-two people for eleven seconds, and there
is not a thing in the world in that room that anybody could have watched instead, and I
have watched the watches and I have read all twenty-two accounts, and not one of them
mentions the door, and the door was four feet from the back of his head."

---

At half past two an elderly woman stood up in the second row and gave evidence about a
sheet of carbon paper.

Reiner had met her twice. She had ridden on a boat to Hull in the summer and she had a
face like a receipt and she had spent four days being corrected by a fishmonger in
Marseille, and she took the stand for four minutes and gave an account of a storeroom that
was entirely accurate and was, in every particular, worth nothing.

The assesseur asked her one question and it was a good question and she had clearly asked it
of himself first.

"You say you saw nothing happen."

"I saw a man in a room."

"You saw him for eleven seconds."

"I saw a man in a room for a length of time that I did not measure." She had a copy of
Aldrich's sealed account. She did not have it in her hand. She had it at home in a drawer
in Utrecht. "And I saw twenty-two other people, and I was one of them, and we were all
certain, and we were all right, and I have not been able to put those two things together
in eleven months and I have stopped expecting to."

"And would you say that the respondent did nothing?"

"I would say that nothing happened in that room that I was able to see," said L'Ombrière,
"and that I have been in this trade for thirty years, and that in thirty years I have never
seen it done by anybody, and that I am not able to say that it was not done."

The assesseur looked at her for a moment over the top of his wig.

"That is a very careful answer, madam."

"It is the only one I have," said L'Ombrière. "The other one is a lie and I have never
told it to anybody, and if I said it to you this afternoon you would write it down, and it
would be in a file in ten years, and it would be the one sentence anybody remembered about
me."

The room was extremely quiet. The assesseur wrote it down.

Reiner sat in the fourth row of a hall in Utrecht with her hands in her lap and watched a
woman of about fifty stand in a witness box that was not a witness box and refuse, at some
risk, to lie in a form that would have been easier, and thought: *she has a case too. She
has had one for nine years. It is in a tin.*

And then, because the afternoon was not yet over and because she had spent eleven months
learning to work with a face and a room and had not once thought to work with a schedule:

"The assesseur," she said, to Halvard Grens, very quietly. "He is going to call the
respondent."

"He is going to call the respondent."

"Today."

"Today," said Halvard Grens. "It is on the schedule. It has been on the schedule since
March. It is the second item."

"It is the second item," said Reiner, "and I have read the schedule upside down from four
rows back for four hours and I did not read the second item, because I have spent eleven
months building a case and I have never once in my life been in a room where the case was
not the only thing that happened in it, and in about ten minutes a man who has spent nine
years being careful is going to stand up in front of a clerk and two notaries and a man in
a wig, and I am not going to be the one asking him questions, and I have not prepared him,
and I have spent eleven months preparing myself, and I am going to find out in ten minutes
whether that was ever any use at all."

The clerk began to read the second item.

Reiner closed her notebook, which she had not opened once, and put it face down on her
knee, and put her hands flat on either side of it, and looked at the man in the wig, and
waited.

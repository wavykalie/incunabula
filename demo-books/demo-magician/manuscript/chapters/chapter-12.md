# 12 · A Certificate of Good Conduct

The certificate was in Flemish and dated the eleventh of May, 1935, and it had been applied
for and issued in a city where, by every account in the dossier, he had never performed.

L'Ombrière put it on the table between them and did not sit down.

"You may pick it up. I have had it since June and I have not been able to make it mean
anything and I have come to the conclusion that it means something perfectly ordinary and
that the ordinary thing is the problem."

Reiner read it. *Certificate of Good Conduct, issued to the bearer at the request of the
holder, for the purposes of an engagement, at Brussels, 11 May 1932.* It was signed by an
officer of a district office and stamped, and it said nothing whatever except that some
person had asked a city to confirm that they were a person worth hiring.

"Applied for," she said. "Under what name."

"Under the name he used there in 1931. Which is not the name he had used there in 1929,
and is not the name he uses in a tent." L'Ombrière still did not sit. "I have three names
for him and one of them is on this. It is a good name. It is a name with a vowel in the
middle of it that a man would pick in a city like Brussels in that year, and I have looked
at it for a month and I cannot see what it is doing."

"What was he doing in Brussels in 1931?"

"Nothing that anyone can find. No booking, no company, no reason to be inside the ring
road at all. There is no engagement in Brussels in 1931 in any of eleven companies, and I
have checked all eleven, and the check took me a fortnight and cost me forty guilders and
I would do it again." She put one
finger on the paper. "And then in May of 1935 a man who had not been in Brussels asks
Brussels to certify that he is a good man. And Brussels certifies him. Without a fee. In
four days."

"Four days is fast."

"Four days is *instantaneous*." She sat down at last. "Miss Reiner, in 1932, in Belgium, a
certificate of good conduct required a police record to be traced across three communes.
Four days is not a search. Four days is a man producing a document he was given by somebody
who had prepared it."

"So he had a reference."

"He had a *reference*," said L'Ombrière, "and the reference was a man in a police office
who either did not exist, or existed, and was not going to look, and either way is not
going to be asked, because you do not ask a policeman about a certificate of good conduct
unless you have already decided the man is not good, and a man who has a certificate issued
in four days over three communes has *decided* he is good. The paper says so. The paper
exists in order that the question does not get asked."

Reiner put the certificate down.

"Then the certificate is not evidence of anything."

"The certificate is evidence of one thing, and it is a thing I have been circling since
June, and I would like to say it out loud and have you tell me I am wrong." L'Ombrière
leaned forward. "It is evidence of a **sequence**. He is building a paper. Seven disputes,
three countries, two withdrawals at his own cost, a certificate in a town where he has not
worked, a name in three towns. And every piece of it is a piece that a person in authority
is required to *hold*. The tribunal holds a sentence. The police hold a certificate. The
*réponse* is held in a sealed envelope in a tribunal. None of it is evidence of anything
about him. All of it is evidence that he has been *somewhere* and dealt with men who have
duties."

"And the point of holding a paper—"

"Is that you do not have to be in a room." She sat back. "He has spent nine years making
himself into a file. And you cannot cross-examine a file. You can only ask questions, and
he has answered every question ever put to him in four lines of paper, and he will go on
answering them, and he will do it in a room in about four months in front of men who will
find every answer correct, and there will not be one moment in that room where a single
person can stand up and say *that is a lie*."

"Because there is no lie in it."

"Because there is no lie in any of it." L'Ombrière's voice did not change at all, which
Reiner was to think about later. "That is the whole of it and I have not been able to say
it in nine years. Every document in that dossier is true. Every answer he has ever given
is true. He has never once lied to anybody, and a man who has never lied cannot be
cross-examined, because there is nothing in him to catch, and the only thing left to
cross-examine is the *account*, and the account is made of other people, and the other
people were not in the room."

Reiner looked at the certificate for a long moment.

"L'Ombrière. Who draws the line when a man is truthful all the way to the top of a
building?"

The woman thought about it properly, and Reiner waited, because she had learned in the
spring that L'Ombrière would always think about it properly.

"Something has to be *owed*," she said. "Somebody has to have lost something. It cannot be
him, it has never been him, and it cannot be the other witnesses, because they were all
right. So it has to be a person who is not in the room and never was, and that person has
to be owed something, and it has to be owed in a way a man can *see*."

"And it has to be money."

"It has to be visible, and money is the most visible thing there is, and I have gone round
and round this for nine years and it is the only answer I have." She pushed the certificate
towards her. "Which is why the thirty guilders matter more than the body. The body is
deniable. The thirty guilders went to a woman who is not a witness to anything, from a firm
that does not exist, and there is no *réponse* in the world that answers that, and there
never will be one, because it is not a question anybody in that trade can be asked.

And that is what the nine years have been. Not a case. A man laying down a floor of paper that
a person in authority is required to stand on, so that when the day comes there is nowhere left
for a question to stand, and a woman with a tin of two hundred and eleven pages has been
counting the years to find the one where he did not lay a floor down."

---

She got up and went to the window.

"There is a year missing," she said.

"I know there is a year missing."

"From what?"

L'Ombrière did not turn round. "Say it plainly. I have been waiting eleven months for
somebody to say it plainly."

"1927 Lyon. 1928 Ghent. 1929 Rotterdam. 1930 Lausanne. 1931 Antwerp. 1932 Lyon. 1933
Brussels." She counted them on the window glass with one finger, which she was aware of
doing and did not stop. "Seven disputes in nine years, and six of those years are in that
list, and 1931 Antwerp is a *réponse* in 1931 and you told me in November that there was no
Belgian engagement in that year at all, and 1935 is on the certificate, so the sequence
runs 1927, 1928, 1929, 1930, 1931, 1932, 1933, and then 1935."

"And 1934."

"And 1934 is not a gap in his record. Every other year in that run has a document. 1934
has nothing. Not a dispute, not a withdrawal, not a certificate, not an engagement, not a
*réponse*." Reiner turned round. "L'Ombrière, the sequence has no gaps in it anywhere else.
Seven disputes, three countries, nine years, and the man has made sure that every single
year is accounted for. And there is one year where there is no paper at all, and it is the
one year where a baker of Delft is not being compensated and a man is not being scrupulous,
and I have spent eleven months believing he is the most complete man alive, and he has a
hole in him exactly the width of one year."

L'Ombrière sat very still.

"Yes," she said. "That is what I have got. I have had it since June and I have not been
able to use it, and I would like you to tell me what a hole is worth."

"It is worth everything," said Reiner, "if you can find a person in 1934, and if you
cannot, it is worth nothing at all, and that is the whole question, and it is not a
question I can ask anybody yet, because the moment I ask it, Rask knows that I am asking
it, and Rask is the only living person in the world who has met him."

She picked up the certificate and looked at the date again.

"Eleventh of May, 1935," she said. "Four days, three communes, no fee. And then nothing
from that man for two years, and then a certificate of good conduct in Brussels in 1935
under a name I cannot match to anything, in a city where he had not worked since 1931."

"And in between, a year that has nothing in it."

"In between," said Reiner, "a year that somebody is going to have to talk about, and there
is one man alive who can, and he is fifty-eight years old and he is in Utrecht and he has
already told me a date once, on purpose, while angry, so that I could not call it a gift."

She put the certificate down on top of the dossier, exactly square, which was a habit from
her father and which she had not noticed doing.

# 16 · Five

It took her five days to get him alone again and the reason it took five days is that she
had learned something in the corridor, and what she had learned was that Rask is not
avoiding her.

A man avoiding a question moves. Rask did not move. Rask sat in a chair by the wall for
eight weeks with a cup he did not drink from, and he was pleasant to her at meals, and he
called her madam, and on the Thursday he asked her whether the room at Delft was warmer
than it had been in the spring, which is a thing he could not have cared about and which
took her forty minutes to understand was a question about her *work*.

There was no evasion in him anywhere. He was being extremely careful with her, in a way
that had a price, and she worked out the price on the Friday.

On the Friday he was in the mess with a script in his hand — he was a strongmover, he was
fifty-eight, and he had done six turns a night for forty years and he was still doing them
in a hall with a tin ceiling — and she sat down at the other end of the table and did not
say anything for about a minute and a half.

"You are doing the thing I asked you not to do," he said, without looking up.

"I am not asking you anything."

"You are sitting at a table looking at a man in a way that four people in this company have
already noticed and have described to me, and one of them described it as *the polite
kind*." He turned the script over. "I said I would not ask you a question and I have kept
to it, and I would be obliged if you would stop making me regret it, because I do not
enjoy regretting a thing I said to a stranger in a yard in the rain."

"You told me a number in a corridor."

"I did."

"Five."

"Five," said Rask, and turned the script over again.

"You have six."

He put the script down. He did it carefully, the way a man puts a thing down when he has
been holding it for a while and has decided to stop pretending to read it.

"Go on," he said. "Say the rest of it and get it over with, and then let a man of my age
do a turn in a hall with a tin roof, and then we will both be able to forget that this
conversation took place, which is what is going to happen whether we like it or not."

"I am not going to say the rest of it," said Reiner. "I said that number in a corridor to
a man with a cup, and you have had five days to decide what to do about it, and you have
done nothing, which means you have been waiting for me to come and say something clever
and then correct it, and I have not, and you are now in a mess."

"I am not in a mess."

"You are in a mess," said Reiner. "Because now that I have said it out loud, it is a thing
in this building, and there are seven people in this building, and one of them is from
Rotterdam and he will have heard it, and if he has heard it then in nine months' time
there is a room somewhere with a man in it who can say *a woman asked an old man in a
corridor how many effects there were*, and that sentence has no context in it, and there
is nothing in the world you can say back."

Rask looked at her for a long moment.

"You are right," he said. "That is the most frightening conversation I have had in nine
years and I have had a great many, and you are right, and I am going to tell you what I
am going to do about it, and then I am going to tell you the reason, and the reason is not
going to be for your file."

---

He came to her room at midnight, which she had not expected, and did not come in, and
stood in the doorway of a room over a tobacconist in Utrecht with a shawl over his
shoulders because the corridor was cold.

"I am going to tell you why I said five," he said. "And then I am going to go to bed, and
you are not to write it down, and I am not going to know whether you have written it down,
and that is the arrangement, and it is the only protection either of us has got."

"All right."

"The sixth does not exist. I have said that. It was said in a yard in the rain and you
wrote it in a notebook and I saw you do it." He shifted in the doorway. "What I am going to
tell you now is why I said five in a corridor on Saturday night, and the two things are
not the same and if you go into that room with a magistrate you will have to have both."

"I am not going into any room with a magistrate."

"You will, within a year, and I do not know that you know it yet, and I am telling you now
so that when it happens you have had it since January." He pulled the shawl tighter. "In
fifty years I have been asked how many effects there are by, let us say, four hundred men
in four hundred rooms, and the number has always been five, and I have said five every
single time, and I have never once thought about why."

"Then why did you think about it on Saturday?"

"Because on Saturday you did not ask the question the way the other four hundred asked it."
He was looking at the wall opposite her door and not at her. "They ask it to find out
whether I can do something. You asked it because you had already worked out that I had
done something, and you wanted the number, and there is a difference between those two
people and I have not had to tell them apart since 1919."

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere below, a man was putting a shutter up over a
tobacconist, and it took a very long time, exactly as it had in the spring.

"So I said five," said Rask. "Which is a lie. And I want you to understand that I did not
say five because I was frightened of the sixth. I said five because the moment I say six,
I am describing a thing to a person who will then know that I have described it, and in
fifty years nobody has ever known that I described anything, and I have got very fond of
being a man nobody can check.

And that is the whole of the sixth, and I will not say more than this about it. There is no
name for it because a name is a claim, and a claim is a thing that can be answered, and the
sixth is not a thing I do so much as a thing I do not do, which is be in a room where anybody
can ask. The other five are hands and a light and a rope and a card and a box, and any man can
learn them in four years and be better at them than me. The sixth is fifty years of never once
letting the question land, and nobody can learn that from me, and I have not taught it, and I
am not going to, and the reason I said five in that corridor is that the sixth has to stay a
thing nobody has ever said out loud, or it stops being true."

He laughed, and it was not a pleasant sound, and it went on for slightly too long.

"That is the whole of it, and it is a bad reason, and I have never said it out loud, and
you may tell nobody I said it, and in about four months I am going to be asked a question
in a room by a man in a wig and I am going to lie to him, and I will lie well, and I have
now spent a night in a cold corridor in Utrecht telling the truth to a woman I met in
April in a shed in Rotterdam, which is a thing I would not have believed of myself in
January."

He turned to go and stopped in the doorway.

"Miss Reiner. You asked me how many effects there are. I have answered you."

"Yes," said Reiner.

"I want to say one more thing and I am going to say it badly, and then I am going to bed."
He did not turn round. "You are not going to be able to use this. I know what you are. I
have watched you for eight weeks and I have watched you write in a notebook in a tent and
in a corridor and in a yard, and I have watched your face, and your face is a man looking
for a thing he can put in front of people. And I have just given you the only true thing I
have ever said about the sixth, and it is a thing about *me*, and there is nothing in it
for you at all."

He went along the corridor. Reiner stood in the doorway of her room and listened to him
go, and then she sat down on the end of the bed, and then she did not write anything down
for a quarter of an hour, which was the first time in nine months.

Then she wrote one line, and it was: *Row 1. He gave it to me. He gave it away for nothing
at midnight to keep the last of it, and I cannot use any of it, and he knew that when he
opened his mouth, and he did it anyway, and I have been in nine hundred rooms and I have
never once seen a man do a thing like that for the reason he said he did it.*

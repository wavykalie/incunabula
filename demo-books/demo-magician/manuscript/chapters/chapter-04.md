# 4 · 1919

The telegram had been from a man in Utrecht who had paid her once and owed her once, and
the man who opened the door to her in Utrecht was not him, and when she said the name Rask
the woman on the step said *he is the other one* and went and stood in the passage and did
not come in, and that told Reiner more about the household in the first ten seconds than
four months of professional reading of rooms had told her about anybody.

He was a big man gone slightly soft, in a good coat, with the hands of somebody who has
stopped doing the work and has not replaced it with anything. He had been a conjurer for
thirty years. He looked at her for slightly too long and then at her hands, and then he
let her in, and he did not offer her the seat nearest the door, and she noted that.

"You have had a bad month," he said.

"Have I."

"Van Eede wrote to me. He does not write to me." He sat down opposite her. "He says a
woman came and did nothing to him for eleven seconds and that he has not been the same
since. He wrote that. I have known that constable for nine years and he has never once put
a sentence in a letter that was not a fact, and he has written me a letter about a woman
doing nothing."

"I would like to ask you about the sixth."

"I thought you might, and I have thought about it since Wednesday, and I have decided that
I am not going to lie to you, because I have watched a great many people lie to me and it
has never once improved the situation." He put his hands flat on the table. "I am not going
to help you either. Those are two different things and I would like them kept apart,
because one of them is honest and the other is cowardice, and you will know which is which
in about a year, and you will not care then."

"You could at least tell me whether there is a sixth."

"There is a sixth."

The room went on being a room. Reiner had come four hours for that, and she had it, and she
sat with it and did not put it down, and she asked the next question in a voice she was
careful with, which is a thing she had learned to do the hard way at twenty-six.

"Then tell me what it is."

"No."

"You will not."

"Especially not."

She let it stand. There was a clock in the room with a loud escapement and she was aware of
it in a way that had nothing to do with clocks.

"You are the only one," Reiner said. "Of the six of us, of the people I would put in a
room, you are the only one who has been in a room with him. I have verified it since
Monday and I have not verified it by asking you, and I am asking you now, and if you say
no I will go and be wrong in a room on my own."

"I have never seen that man in my life."

"Mr Rask."

"He has never been in this house."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," Rask said. "It is not. I have had forty years of being asked what people mean when
they say the name of a man they have not met, and it is very nearly always that they want
to put you in a room. You want to put me in a room with him and be in the room when it
happens, and I am not going to be a device for it, and I would rather be unkind to you
than useful to you in that particular way."

"That is a reasonable position."

"It is an old one." He looked at the door. "What I will tell you is this. If I had met him
I would not have sat here for an hour with you either, and you would not have got
anything, and I would have been unkind. So you have not been robbed of a thing. You have
been told the truth by a man who has arranged it so that the truth is worth nothing to
you, and you are the second person this year to be handled by me and the first to do it so
quickly."

Reiner stood up, and then did not leave, which she was aware of as a thing she had chosen
to do.

"What was his first name," she said.

"No."

"You have a name."

"I have a surname. I gave him a first name back in a town where I have not worked since
and I have been sorry about it for a long time, and it is not a thing I am going to hand
to a woman I met on Wednesday and give a first name to because she has asked me a great
many careful questions in an extremely level voice." He almost smiled. "You have a manner,
madam, and it is a good one, and it is a manner. It is what you do instead of swearing at
me, and I have been on the receiving end of better than it, and I would be careful how much
you spend it on me, because you will want it for somebody else in about a fortnight."

---

She got as far as the passage. The woman was not in it.

"He will have gone to the yard," said Reiner.

"He will have gone to the yard."

She went out into it. He was standing at the far end with his back to her doing nothing at
all, which is a thing a man does when he has been standing in a house for an hour waiting
for a woman to leave, and it is a thing Reiner had watched men do perhaps two hundred
times, and she had never once gone and stood beside them, and she went and stood beside
him.

"Mr Rask."

"You have five minutes and then my hostess will think I am being taken advantage of, and
she is not wrong, and I would rather not explain you to her twice."

"Has anybody ever asked you what he learned from you?"

It came out before she had it ready, and she heard it come out, and she was not sorry.

Rask was quiet for a long time. The yard was very small and there was a wall on two sides
of it and a shed on the third and the light came over the shed, and the light moved the way
light does on a wet morning in a country with a lot of water in it.

"Nobody taught him," Rask said.

"All right."

"You may write that down. Nobody taught him. I have said it in front of witnesses, you are
a witness, put the date on it, and it is true, and it will not help you, and I want it
recorded that I have said a true thing to you on purpose and it has not helped you."

Reiner did not write it down. She stood in the wet yard in a good coat and did not have a
pen on her, which was the single most convenient accident of her working life.

"Except that he was quick," Rask said.

He said it the way a man says a thing to correct a record. He said it to the wall, not to
her, and he said it fast, and he heard himself say it, and she watched a man take a thing
back.

"He was fifteen and he was quick and nobody had to show him anything, and that is not what
I said—"

He stopped. The light moved on the wall.

"That is not what I said," Rask said again, in a completely different voice. "I said
nobody taught him. I did not say I was not in the room. You may write both of those down
and they are both true, and only one of them is an answer, and I have been doing this for
forty years and I would still not have said the second one if you had not stood in that yard
in the rain asking me about a fortnight that I did not know you had."

Reiner said, "Mr Rask—"

"Nineteen nineteen," he said. "That is the year you are going to ask me for and so I am
going to say it to you now, while I am angry, so that you cannot say I gave it to you."
He turned round. He was not angry, and they both knew it. "It is a year, madam. It is not
a place and it is not a name and it is not a boy, and you may have the year and I am going
back inside."

---

The number was in her notebook by half past two, in the margin, on its own line, and she
looked at it for a long time and then wrote underneath it, because the habit had been
trained into her by people who were dead:

*Row 1. He has met him. Confirmed. Not by him.*

*Row 2. A fortnight. He said a fortnight. He has not been asked what happens in a
fortnight. He said it himself, and then he heard himself, which means there is a fortnight
in which something is expected to happen, and it is not the nineteenth of any month I know
of yet.*

*Row 3. Nineteen nineteen. Given. Refused twice and then given while angry on purpose so
that it could not be called a gift. I do not know what happens in that year. I know that
nobody taught him and that he was fifteen in it, and those two sentences are in the same
breath, and they cannot both be true, and he knows they cannot both be true, and he left
them standing in the yard in the rain with me.*

*Row 4. He would not give me his first name. He gave me a year instead. He has been alive
for sixty years and he chose the year.*

She closed the book on the fourth one, and then opened it again, and added a fifth, because
she had learned that the fifth one was usually the true one and it never came first.

*Row 5. I did not ask him what the man learned. I asked him what the man learned from him,
which is a different question, and he heard the difference, and he answered the first one
and not the second one, and the first one was about a boy who was fifteen and quick and did
not need to be shown anything, and the second one is about a man in a yard in Utrecht this
afternoon, and he is the only living person who knows which of those two things happened in
nineteen nineteen, and he is going to have to decide one day whether to tell me or to keep
it, and I have known him for one hour and I have already decided I will not make it easy
for him, and I want that written down, because in about nine months I am going to be in a
court and I am going to want to be able to say that I did not.*

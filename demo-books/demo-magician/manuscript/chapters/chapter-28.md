# 28 · The House That Could Not Give

There was a house in Rotterdam, at the end of a street off the Binnenweg, and it had been
empty since the March and it had a key, and Reiner was refused entry to it on the fourth
of July by a man in a good coat who was extremely polite about it and who took nine minutes
to say no.

"I am asking for an hour."

"You are."

"I am not asking to take anything. I am asking to stand in a room."

"Madame, I understand perfectly. The answer is that I cannot let anybody in."

"Because of the proceeding?"

"Because of the proceeding." He said it as though it were a physical object he was holding
up. "There is a matter standing at Utrecht in which the name of this house appears in the
second paragraph of the *requête*. Until that matter is disposed of, no person who is not a
magistrate, and not an officer of the court, and not named in the instrument, may enter. That
is not my regulation. I have no regulation. That is a section and a date and it is the
twenty-second of April and it has been the law since before either of us."

"You could not enter either."

"I have not entered," said the man in the good coat, "and I have no intention, and I have
been wearing this coat since the twenty-third of April and it is not a good coat, and I am
telling you that so that you understand I am not enjoying this."

---

It took Reiner five weeks to understand what had actually happened, and she understood it
in a corridor in a court in Utrecht from a clerk of about thirty called De Wit who was not
on her side and had no reason to be and who was, in the end, the only person in the entire
summer who told her the truth without being asked.

"You wanted a room," said De Wit. "That is what nobody understands. Everybody thinks you
wanted evidence. You have had evidence since March. You have had so much evidence that you
have a notebook nobody can read. What you wanted was a *room*, and a room is not evidence,
it is not a document, it cannot be copied and it cannot be filed and it cannot be sworn
to, and it is the only thing in the world that is worth anything to you, and there is no
provision anywhere for it."

"There is no provision for a person wanting a room."

"There is no provision for it because it is not a legal interest," said De Wit. "That is
the whole answer and I have been a clerk for eight years and I have never had to give it. A
magistrate has standing because the law gives it to him. A party has standing because a
court gives it to her. A policeman has standing because an employer employs him. What is
your standing?"

"I asked a question in a hall in June."

"You made a statement. Statements are not standing. Anyone may make a statement." He was
sorting folders and did not look up. "You are a woman who performed on a Saturday in
Delft. That is not a reason. If you had been a magistrate you could have walked into that
house on the fourth of April and you would have had the room for an hour, and you would have
stood in it, and you would have gone out again, and it would have been worth nothing at
all, because a room is only worth anything to somebody who can prove they were in it, and
you could not have proved it, because you would have been a magistrate, and a magistrate
going into a room is a magistrate going into a room."

---

Here is the list. She wrote it out on the back of an envelope in Utrecht in August and it
is the only document in the whole case that has never been in a file, and it is the reason
she slept for four nights running after she had finished it.

Things she could not have because she was not a magistrate:

A room in Rotterdam, for an hour, in July.

The accounts of the yard man, who had been on the premises for eleven years and who had a
lorgnette and a memory and had been twice to the police about the predecessor and had been
ignored both times.

The inventory of the storeroom, which existed, which listed nine crates and a paraffin
stove and a coil of rope and a thing described as *unidentified, boxed*, and which had been
in a file in a municipal office since May of last year.

The four crates along the far wall, stacked four high, four along, with the fourth row
short by one.

Any conversation at all with the man who had put the body on the crate.

And the thing at the bottom of the list, which she wrote last, which is the reason the
envelope exists:

The predecessor.

Not Aldrich. The other one. The yard man who had been on that door for eleven years and had
come through at four on the Tuesday and gone back out at ten past four, and had said *I was
looking at the door the whole time*, and had not thought about it again.

He had given that account in May of last year, in a shed, to a constable with a small book,
and it was the single most important fact in the case, and it was true, and he had not
thought about it since, and there was no instrument in the world that would make him think
about it except a magistrate with a warrant and a building he could put in front of him, and
she had neither, and she was not going to get either, because she was a woman who performed
on a Saturday in Delft.

---

She went back to Rotterdam in the third week of August and stood outside the house at the
end of the street off the Binnenweg for about an hour and a half, and there was nothing to
see, and it was a house.

That was the afternoon. That is what the afternoon was: a woman standing on a pavement in
Rotterdam in the rain outside a house she had been refused entry to, in a street with a
lock-keeper's yard at one end and a coal merchant at the other, and there was nothing to
see, and she stayed for an hour and a half.

What she did in that hour and a half was to reconstruct the room from memory, which she
could do, because she had been in it for four minutes in April of last year and had gone
closer and had crouched and had looked at a man's hands, and she has never in her life had
trouble with a room.

Nine feet by eleven. Stained tin ceiling, low, with one panel out of line. Concrete floor
with a drain in the middle of it that had a smell she could still get. Four rows of crates
along the far wall, four high, four along, the fourth row short by one. A paraffin stove
with a pipe going up into nothing. A coil of rope on a nail. A goods door on the
Binnenweg side with a lock that had a new key in it. A back stair.

Nine feet by eleven, and a man on a crate in the middle of it, sitting in the manner of a
man waiting for a tram, and twenty-two people in a room that size, which is not many, which
is a small room, which is a room you could empty of people in eleven seconds by pushing the
crates against the door.

And she stood in the rain and worked out the thing that she could not make work, and it was
this.

Twenty-two people. Nine by eleven. Eleven seconds.

There is no room of that size that holds twenty-two people for eleven seconds and stays
still. Not one. She had done rooms of that size for twenty-five years. A room that size
holds eight comfortably, and the ninth person changes everybody's position, and by the
fifteenth there is somebody near the door who can feel the draught.

And not one of the twenty-two accounts mentions a draught, or a movement, or anybody wanting
to leave, or anybody being pressed, or a man in the way.

Which meant the room was not nine by eleven. Which meant the room had been cleared, and
somebody had come in afterwards, and put twenty-two people in a cleared room, and put a
man on a crate in the middle of it, and the twenty-two had seen none of that, because they
had been somewhere else at the time, and the somewhere else had been a room with a door
they were watching.

She stood in the rain and understood that the room in the *requête* was not the room in the
yard.

And the only person alive who could confirm it was a yard man with a lorgnette who had been
ignored twice by the police, and there was no instrument in the world that would make a
magistrate open a door in Rotterdam for a woman who performed on a Saturday in Delft.

---

On the Monday she went to Van Eede and told him what she had worked out, and he listened
to the whole of it standing up, in a shed, which was where they had met in April of last
year, which was a fact she noticed and did not remark on.

"Eleven seconds," he said. "Yes."

"Van Eede. I need that room opened."

"I know you do."

"So do it."

"I cannot," said Van Eede. "I told you in March and I am telling you in August and it has
not changed. I am a constable. I have no standing. If I go to a house in a street off the
Binnenweg on my own authority and open a door, then in ten years there is a man in a room
asking me why I opened a door on my own authority, and there is nothing in the world I can
say to him, because the answer is that a woman asked me to in a shed, and she is not a
magistrate and she is not the owner and she is not the family and she performed on a
Saturday in Delft."

"You did not say that in March."

"I said something else in March. I have been a constable for nine years and I have said a
great many things in nine years and in March I said that a shed is a shed." He put the
small book in his pocket. "I am sorry, madam. I have not stopped wanting to help you. I
have stopped being able to, and those are different and I have learned the difference at
some length since the spring, and I would rather tell you the truth in a shed in August
than be useful to you once in a room in October."

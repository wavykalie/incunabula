# 18 · The Port

The year was 1931 and it took her nine weeks to get into it, and what she got into it with
was a ferry ticket and a bad coat.

There is no good way to reconstruct a thing that nobody wrote down. Reiner had learned
that in the spring and had learned it the expensive way. The dossier gave her paper. The
corps gave her four people who had been in the circuit. And the rest — the three weeks of
Marseille in the autumn of 1931, the missing year, the year with no document in it
whatsoever — the rest had to be walked.

So she went, and she took a room over a chandler's shop near the Old Port, and she did what
she had done in Rotterdam and Utrecht, which was to sit in rooms and let people correct
her.

The correction she got most, and fastest, and in the end from a fishmonger of about
seventy who had sold fish on the Quai des Belges for forty years, was this.

"He was not here in 'thirty-one."

"Four months. The autumn. September to November at least."

"He was not here," said the fishmonger, "and I will tell you how I know, and it is not
because I remember him, because I do not remember him, and everybody in this port has been
telling me for a month that they remember him, and not one of them can tell me a single
thing he said."

Reiner sat down rather more suddenly than she meant to.

"He was here," she said. "There was a death. There is a witness in a court in —"

"I know there is a death. I know about the death. I have read about the death in the
newspaper, and the newspaper got it wrong, and everybody in this port got it wrong, and I
have been correcting people about it in a market for a month and I would like to stop."

"Because everybody says he was here."

"Everybody says he was *here*. Not in this port. *Here.*" The fishmonger leaned over the
counter. "There was a man in 'thirty-one who came to this port and did a turn at the
Cirque and went away, and there was a man who came to this port in the spring of 'thirty
and did a turn and went away, and there is one in 'thirty-two, and a Marseille man will
tell you the same face for all three, because that is what a face is for. It is a thing you
recognise in a market and you put a year on it afterwards."

"Where would it be afterwards?"

"Thirty," said the fishmonger. "It would be thirty, and I would be telling you it was
thirty-one, and I have been telling a man from a court in a town with no trams that it was
thirty-one, and he wrote it down."

---

She got the year on a Tuesday, from a woman of about sixty in a house on the Rue de la
République who had kept a boarding house for nineteen years and who did not want to talk
about any of it and talked about it for two hours.

"He had a room. October. He was here eleven days and he paid for the room and he paid for
the breakfast and he gave the girl a tip that was too much, and I have had that story out
of four people in a fortnight and I am telling you it because you have written down the
things the other four have told you and you have written down that he was in *thirty-one*,
and he was here in *thirty*, and I would like one thing in this to be right."

"Then why does everybody say thirty-one?"

"Because he was here in thirty-one as well." She said it flatly. "In thirty-one he was
here for one night, in a coach, in September, and he did not take a room and he did not
perform and he did not speak to anybody except the man he came to see, and his name at
that address was not the name he uses in a tent."

"Then he was in this port twice."

"He was in this port twice within thirteen months," said the woman, "and if you have found
that out in three weeks after nine months of not finding it out, then somebody has been
telling you a great deal and they have been telling you carefully, and I should like to know
who, because there are four people in this house who know it and I am one of them and I am
not talking for the other three."

Reiner did not tell her.

---

**Marseille, 1931.** The third death, reconstructed. She built it out of a coach, a
one-night stay, an address, and the fact that a man who has come to a city to see somebody
does not come once.

The address was in the Rue Sainte, and it belonged to a firm of forwarding agents who did
not exist, and Reiner had that by October and had been keeping it in the notebook since
June. In September of 1931 a man had stayed one night in an address that belonged to a firm
that did not exist, and in the following March a woman had been given thirty guilders at the
same address on the Scheldt.

The death itself she had from four lines in a Marseille newspaper of the second week of October, and the four lines were about a Belgian labourer of no fixed address who had been found at the foot of a stair in the Rue Sainte, and the four lines knew things that four lines have no business knowing. He had been drinking. He had fallen. The matter was closed.

She had been in the documents for long enough to know what a closed matter looks like when it has been written by somebody with an interest in the closing. A fall down a stair is the most accommodating death in a port city, because at the foot of every stair in Marseille there is a man who has been drinking, and the doctor who signs the certificate has four hundred like it in a year and no reason at all to spend an afternoon on any one of them.

So she built it out of what the port would give her. A man had come to the city twice within thirteen months and had stayed eleven days the first time and one night the second, and a man who comes back has a reason, and the reason had an address on it. On the second visit he did not perform and he did not take a room and he did not speak to anybody except the man he had come to see, and eleven days after that a labourer went off a stair two streets from the address that belonged to a firm that did not exist, and the newspaper closed the matter in four lines.

She put the two visits side by side in the notebook and looked at them for a week. The first visit was a man doing a turn at the Cirque and paying for his breakfast and tipping a girl too much, which is the behaviour of a man with an audience. The second visit was the behaviour of a man with an appointment. And there was the third thing, which the boarding-house woman had said without knowing she had said it, that his name at that address was not the name he used in a tent.

Reiner wrote that at the top of a page and left the rest of the page empty for two days, because a man with two names in a port city is either hiding from somebody or is somebody, and she could not yet say which, and the whole of it sat in the book in two columns of her own: what the port said, and what the port meant.

What she had in the autumn of 1931, once she had it, was this:

He had stopped using the notation. That was the first thing anybody noticed and it was
noticed by three people independently, all of whom had been asked a question by her, and all
of whom said the same thing when they said it, which is the thing that is odd, because it
is the one thing a conjurer would not volunteer.

*In every other account of him, over nine years, he writes [presumed / actual] beside
things. Every letter, every *réponse*, every note in every dossier, both columns, in ink,
for thirty-one years. And in the autumn of 1931, in a house on the Rue Sainte, a woman saw
a sheet of paper on a table with only one column on it.*

"Only one column," said Reiner.

"Only the first one," said the woman. "And I remember it because I thought: *he is in a
hurry, he has run out of time to write it twice.* And it is not hurry. It is that a man who
has written both columns for thirty-one years and then writes one has stopped needing to
know what actually happened. Because he knows."

"You are certain."

"I am certain it was one column. I am not certain what it meant." She was looking at her
hands. "Miss Reiner, I am sixty-one years old and I have had three people in this room
since January, and two of them were from a court, and both of them were very polite, and
both of them asked me about a coach in September that I have told you about twice."

---

Nobody writes [presumed / actual] for a thing they have to think about. You write it
because you do not know. You write the second column afterwards, in a different hand, on
the same day or the day after, in a book that is yours.

There is one reason to stop: you no longer need to find out.

Reiner sat with that for most of a night in a room over a chandler's shop on the Quai du
Port and understood the shape of the worst thing in the case, which was that in 1931 he
stopped writing it down, and that in 1931 he used force, and that the two facts are one
fact, and that a man who begins with a procedure and ends without one has usually not run
out of time.

He has run out of patience. Reiner wrote that. And underneath it: *which means the room
stopped working on him, and I have been in nine hundred rooms and I have never once
watched a room stop working on a man, and I do not know what happens at that point, and
nobody in this trade is going to be able to tell me, because there is nobody left who was
in the room.*

The only person who was in the room is a woman of sixty-one on the Rue de la République who
has now been asked by two men from a court and by a conjurer, and who is going to remember
the interview with the conjurer, because the conjurer was the only one who wrote down
*one column* when she said it.

In the autumn of 1931 he stopped writing both columns.

In the autumn of 1931 he was in a port for eleven days and the man who was there said he did
nothing at all, and this time the man who was there was a woman of sixty-one who was very
good at her job and who had kept a boarding house for nineteen years, and Reiner had three
months to get back to Utrecht and four hundred and sixty of them.

She got back to Utrecht on the eleventh, and the first thing Rask said to her, in a
corridor, without stopping, in a voice with nothing in it at all, was:

"Do not come and tell me about Marseille."

"I am not going to tell you about Marseille."

"You were going to tell me about Marseille."

"I was going to tell you about Marseille," said Reiner, "and I have not, and I am not going
to, and you knew that before I got in the room, and I would like you to tell me what you
heard, because you have heard something, and you have heard it from somebody, and it has
not come from me and it has not come from anybody in this building."

Rask stopped walking. He was in a corridor in Utrecht in March with a shawl over his
shoulder and he stopped walking, which she had not seen him do.

"In 1931," he said, "somebody wrote to a house in Marseille and the house was not
forwarding agents, and the letter was about a young man of about
nineteen who had been let go from a *salle* in Antwerp in the spring and who had gone home
to his family and who had, in the September, stopped going home."

She did not say anything.

"There is nothing else I am going to tell you," said Rask. "It is not a method and it is not a
reconstruction and it is not in a book. It is a thing a man wrote a letter about in 1931,
and the man who wrote it has never mentioned it to anybody, and he is mentioning it to you
in a corridor in Utrecht because you have just come back from Marseille with a face on you
that I have not seen since the spring, and I would like the record to show that I noticed."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you were going to find it out," said Rask, "and I would rather you had it in
March from a man who will deny it than in September from a woman of sixty-one on the Rue
de la République, who has now been asked by three people and will remember the three, and
whose face is going to be in a list somewhere in a town with no trams."

He went along the corridor.

"Mr Rask."

"Mrs Reiner."

"It was the boy," said Reiner. "Was it the boy."

"It was the boy."

"And he taught him."

"He taught him," said Rask, and did not turn round, and that was the last time in his life
that he said it to anybody, and he had said it eleven months too early, and he went along
a corridor in Utrecht to a room with a cup he would not drink from, and he shut the door,
and he sat down, and he had been fifty-eight for two years and he had been doing this since
he was nine and he had just, in a corridor, in eleven words, given the whole of it away to
a woman he had met in April in a shed in Rotterdam.

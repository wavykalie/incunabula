# FOUNDATION — *Muzzle and Marrow*

**Working title.** The series name, the book title, and every chapter title are **deferred
to the author** and were not invented.

**The pilot book.** This is the first book drafted from scratch under the current
framework. Every prior book was re-chaptered after the fact, so nothing has yet
demonstrated that drafting *under these rules* produces the chapter spread the framework
rests on. That is what this book is for. It is a test of the pipeline as much as a novel.

**No numbers in this document describe how long anything should be.** See
`DRAFTING_FRAMEWORK.md`. A chapter is as long as the thing in it takes.

---

## The premise

There is a Great Orb of Light at the centre of the world. It grants any wish.

Five people of five different species set out to reach it.

That is the whole engine. The joke and the dread live in the same clause: **any** wish.

## The world

One planet. Its surface is divided among peoples who are each a different animal, and the
division is old enough that nobody living argues about it. Species is not a costume and
not a metaphor — a cat is a cat, a dog is a dog, and the differences are administrative as
much as biological. A cat who grew up among dogs will have the wrong reflexes for a
dogfight and the right ones for a rooftop. Nobody finds this remarkable. It is Tuesday.

The politics follow the animals, not the other way round:

- **Cats** are numerous, quick, and ungovernable. Every city has them and none of them
  rules one. When something needs doing that no one will be paid to do, a cat is already
  doing it, badly, and that is the folk-heroic tradition this book stands on.
- **Dogs** hold the oaths. Pack-loyalty is the oldest legal fiction on the planet, and
  the aristocracy is a codified version of it. Dukes swear to their collar. It is
  considered honourable and slightly embarrassing.
- **Ravens** keep the records. Every library, every archive, every long memory that
  outlived its owner is held by birds who were not there and will not pretend to have
  been.
- **Mice** move things. Not stole — *move*, from where they are to where they need to be,
  for a fee, on time. Half the economy is a mouse moving something. This is respected.
- **Serpents** remember the old empire, and the Orb is a piece of it. They are the only
  people who take the quest seriously, and they are not going to say why.

### The Orb

It is called the Great Orb of Light. It is at the centre of the planet, which is the one
geographic fact everybody agrees on and nobody can explain.

It grants any wish. This is stated as fact, in the passive voice, by people who were not
there.

## The naming

Every person in this world is named with a **pet name** — a common animal name, plus an
ordinal, or a title and a place. This is not a joke about the characters. It is the
ordinary register of a world where nobody is named after an ancestor and everybody is
named after something a child would say.

**Duke Rocky of Cavalton.** **Mittens the 7th.** **Sergeant Patches of the Low Barracks.**

A person's name is a thing that was done to them, early, by someone fond. Most of them
have made peace with it. The protagonist has not, and that is the book's opening wound —
a genuinely formidable person held together by a name that makes shopkeepers laugh.

The comedy is that the name is *accurate*. Mittens the 7th is, in fact, excellent with
mice. He hates this.

## The party

Five, going down. Four is a company, five is a queue.

### Mittens the 7th — cat
The protagonist. A blade-fighter with a reputation he did not seek and would trade away
for a different one. Seventh of his name, which means six went before him and only two
survived being him. He is thirty-one and has been the best thing in a room since he was
twelve, and he cannot get through a market square without someone saying *aw, isn't that
the seventh one.*

He wants the Orb. He will not say why, and the reason is not the one you would guess.

### Duke Rocky of Cavalton — dog
Patron, not fighter. Sworn to the quest as a formality, because the oath means something
to him and he is the only one present for whom it does. Enormous, grave, faintly
unbearable, and — this is the point — **completely sincere**. He believes in the Orb
because a duke of his line is supposed to, and he keeps finding out that nobody else is
keeping that promise.

He pays for everything. He is bad at the actual quest. He is not a joke character; he is
the one whose decency is the plot's engine.

### Dame Hester Pye — raven
Archivist. She has spent her life in rooms with other people's memories and has the
specific, unnerving calm of someone who already knows how this ends. She is the only one
who has read the accounts of the last party that reached the Orb.

Her function is to be right too early. The reader wants to know what she knows and she
will not say, and the reason she will not say is that the last party told the truth
about what the Orb grants and every city in the world has spent four hundred years
quietly deciding not to ask.

### Sergeant Patches of the Low Barracks — ram
Career soldier, thirty years in, retired twice. He came because Duke Rocky offered him
the job and because a sergeant with no post is a sad joke. He is the party's competence
and its bad temper, and he is the one who will say the quiet thing at the wrong moment.

He does not want a wish. He has thought about it and decided against, and he will need to
re-decide.

### Tuppence — mouse
Thief, courier, and the only one of them who can get anywhere without a fight. Was hired
for the descent and has been, from the first hour, planning to sell the party to whoever
gets to the Orb first — which is a plan that only makes sense while nobody reaches it.

Comedy engine, and the reader's guide: Tuppence narrates the impossibility of everyone
else's behaviour in a world where she has a perfectly good reason for all of hers.

She is called Tuppence. Nobody knows if that is a pet name or a price. It is a pet name.
She will tell you it is a price.

### Offstage

**Madame Void of the Ninth Coil** — serpent. The last party to go down, or the one that
is still down. She is not competing for the Orb. She is trying to stop anyone else from
having it, and the party will not believe this for most of the book because it does not
sound like anything a person would want.

## Theme, as a question

**A wish is a sentence about who you are, and the Orb only has to read it.**

Not *can a good person want something*. Not *is power corrupting*. The engine of this
book is a grammatical horror: the Orb grants **the wish as worded**, and nobody has ever
been able to word one carefully while falling down a tunnel towards it.

The question the book asks and does not answer:

> **What does it cost to get what you asked for, when you find out you asked for the
> sentence and not the thing?**

---

## What the comedy is for

The tone is comic — a party of earnest people on an absurd errand, in a world where
nobody bats an eyelid at a cat being a person. The jokes are not a layer on top of the
story. They are the *delivery mechanism* for the dread: the Orb's literalism is funny, and
being funny about it is how the party avoids saying out loud that the last people who
made a wish are the reason nobody makes one.

The funniest thing in the book is somebody, sincerely, saying a wish out loud properly
for the first time and hearing the silence afterward.

## What this book is not

- Not a quest-with-a-villain. The Orb is not evil. It is a mechanism, and mechanisms do
  not have intentions.
- Not a chosen-one story. Mittens the 7th is seventh for a reason and the reason is
  attrition, not prophecy.
- Not a joke that resolves into warmth. The ending, when it comes, should be the one
  genuinely cold moment in the book.

# Chapter Six — Camp

Nothing happened on the seventh day, which by then was a thing the party had stopped
remarking on.

They had a camp. That is the whole of it: a camp, on a shelf, in the lee, with the wind
coming over the top and going round to find somewhere better to be, and a fire that Hester
said was a function of the route rather than a thing they had made, and which therefore
could not be put out by anyone who was not the route.

Patches did the fire. Nobody asked him to. He had been doing it since the first night and
by the seventh it was his, in the way that a thing becomes yours by being done.

The others slept in shifts, because it was still a descent and nobody had forgotten, and
because Hester had explained on the third day that the route being open was a courtesy
and courtesies get withdrawn.

It was on the seventh evening that Patches said it.

---

"There is a thing," he said, "that I would like to put on the table, and I would like
everyone to let me finish it before anybody is clever about it."

They were all awake, which was a coincidence, and which he noted and did not comment on.

"Right," said Mittens.

"I am not going to wish for anything."

Nobody said anything at all, and he had asked for space, and this was what space looked
like.

"Nine years," said Patches. "I have had nine years. That is the true part, and it is the
part I want in first, because the rest of it sounds like an argument and it is not one. I
went down at thirty-one and I came up at thirty-one and I have had nine years since, and I
have spent them."

He turned the poker in the fire.

"I have a house in the Ninth that I do not live in. I have a pension that arrives and a
bar I drink in that has a landlord who has stopped asking me anything, which I have taken
as a kindness, and which I last week realised is a kind way of saying he has worked out
that I am not going to stop. I have a medal in a box that I took out twice and put back
twice." He looked at the box, which they all knew about, which he had not mentioned. "I
have spent nine years being a man who went down and came back, and there is not one single
thing I have done with the nine years, and I am going down now to the centre of the world
because a young cat and a mouse asked me to, and I said yes, and I want it recorded that I
am glad I said yes, and I also want it recorded that I have no idea what I am doing with
the rest of it."

"Patches—"

"Let me finish." He put the poker down. "I have thought about the wish. Everyone has. I
worked out what I wanted in about a year and then I thought about it for the other eight.
And what I found is that I do not want one. Not the way Kir wanted, not with a plan. I
want to be a man who has had a life, and the only way to have one of those is to be
somebody who has needed something and got it and then had to carry it. And I have not had
that. I had a hole and I went and looked at what came out of the hole and it was me,
eleven years younger, and I have spent nine years failing to be interested in him."

He stopped. The fire took the silence and made it smaller.

"So that's it," he said. "That's the whole of it. I do not want a wish. When we get there
I am going to stand there and be the one who does not ask, and I would rather that was a
decision anybody respected than a thing that happened to me, and I would rather have said
it out loud to four people on a shelf than arrive at the bottom of the world and have it
turn out I had never mentioned it."

---

The duke went first, which Mittens did not expect and would remember.

"Sergeant."

"Your grace."

"I swore an oath," said Rocky, "that my errand will not become a different errand by the
time it is done, and I have been thinking about what a wish is, since we were in Copper
Lane, and I have arrived at the conclusion that I have no idea, and that this is the most
honest thing I have said in front of anybody since I was twenty-two years old."

"That is a duke's answer."

"It is *an* answer," said Rocky. "I shall be perfectly frank with all of you. I have
wanted something my whole life, I am aware of it, and I have never once been able to say
what, and I have been filling the space where the what should go with duties, which is
what a collar is for. If I were to stand at the bottom of the world and say a sentence,
the sentence would be *I wish to be a better man than I am*, and I have read the account,
and I know exactly what happens to a man who asks for a description."

He looked at Patches.

"I respect it," said the Duke of Cavalton. "I want to be honest that I don't much like it,
and that I think you may be wrong, and that both of those are things I can hold at once."

---

"Three days," said Tuppence.

The fire had got to the stage where everyone was looking at it.

"Three days I've known you lot," she said, "and I've watched all three of you decide what
you want, in front of me, in about a day each, and I've not decided anything, and I want
to say that out loud too while we're doing it, because I'm the one who's going to get
asked, later, and I'd rather be in the habit."

"What do you want," said Mittens.

"I want the commission," said Tuppence. "I want to get paid, and I want to be somewhere
warm, and I want to not be carrying a lamp. Those are the three things and they're all
small and I don't intend to say a wish out loud down there at all, and if somebody puts a
sentence in my mouth I'll bill them for it."

Nobody believed her, which she noticed, and which was fine, because it was the first thing
in nine years she had not entirely meant.

---

They left it with Hester, which was predictable, and she did not take long, because she had
thought about it for eleven years and had already written it down.

"I have already wished," she said. "It is in the account. I asked to know what was not
kept, and I was told, and it cost me eleven years and most of the reason I talk to
people." She tilted her head. "So I will not wish again. One is the number. It is not a
principle, it is a count, and I have used mine."

She looked at the fire for a while.

"And I want to say the cruel thing," she said, "because we are all being honest and
because somebody should. Every one of you has just told this fire that you have nothing to
ask for, or a small thing, or a sentence you have already learned is a mistake. And in
about four hundred miles of rock there is a mechanism that will do exactly what you say,
and if every person on this shelf is right — if this is genuinely a party of five who want
nothing — then the Orb is going to grant five refusals, and it will grant them perfectly,
and we will come back up with nothing at all."

Nobody said anything.

"That is the good version," said Hester Pye. "I have been waiting three days to see whether
one of you would say it, because I could not say it without it sounding like a warning, and
I did not want a warning. I wanted to know whether it was true, and now four of you have
said it, and it is true, and I am not relieved, and I would like to go to sleep."

---

She went, and Tuppence went, and the duke went, and Mittens stayed, because he had a stone
with a shape he had been turning over in his hands for an hour.

Patches was still up, of course. Sergeants are always still up.

"You've not said yours," he said.

"No."

"Fair."

The fire was a function of the route. Mittens put his stone down and looked at the man who
had just made the most decent statement of the evening and ruined it for himself, and had
known he was ruining it, and had done it anyway because Patches was a sergeant and
sergeants say what is true and then live with the room's opinion of them.

"I said my reason wasn't the one you'd guess," he said.

"You said it wasn't a good one."

"It's not a good one. It's the opposite of a good one."

Patches waited, in the manner of a man who had spent thirty years letting people find
their own sentence.

"Her name was Kir," said Mittens, "and she was the fourteenth of her name, and I was the
seventh of mine, and we were the same age for about a year, and I have not said her name
out loud in a room that meant it since she went down."

"That is not a wish," said Patches.

"I know what it is," said Mittens. "It's a reason. There's a difference and it's the only
one in this book."

"Get some sleep," said the sergeant. "You can be right about it tomorrow and it'll still
be true."

# Chapter Four — Down

The Descent begins as a hole and becomes a route within about a mile, which is the point
at which people stop calling it a hole.

They went down at fourth bell on a Thursday, four of them and a lamp, and the shaft took
the lamp and gave back a long way of nothing, and Hester Pye said the only thing anybody
had said for an hour, which was "it's further."

"It's further," Mittens agreed.

It was further for most of a day.

---

The Descent is not a stair and it is not a tunnel and calling it either is how people
come to be surprised. It is a spiral, cut into rock of a colour that has no name in common
use because there is no daylight here to name it by, and it goes down and around and down
and around, so that a party walking it in a straight line travels a very long way and
arrives almost directly below where they started.

That is not a convenience; that is a *design*, and by the second turn Mittens understood
that they were inside something, and by the fourth he had stopped finding it comfortable.

"The lamp," he said.

Tuppence had it in a sling across her back, in a case of oiled leather that had belonged
to somebody who had carried it a great deal further than this.

"The lamp is lit," she said. "It has been lit since Millward."

"Is that the job?"

"The lamp is the job," said Tuppence. "Everything else is just me."

Hester came up beside him, which for a raven meant a certain amount of silent flap and
then a landing on a rock outcrop the exact size of a cat.

"Ask the question properly," she said.

"I wasn't going to ask you anything."

"You were going to ask me whether the lamp is the Orb's, and you were going to do it in
front of the mouse, and she would have heard it, and she already has." Hester folded her
wings. "It is not. The Orb has never been moved. That is the one thing about the centre of
the world that nobody argues about."

"Then what is it for."

"It is a marker," said Hester. "It is what a party carries down so that the route knows
one is coming, and it is how the route decides where to be open. Everything you have
walked through today was open *for* us. We are not exploring. We are being permitted."

Mittens looked at the light on the little brass lamp, which was the colour of weak tea.

"And when we're not permitted?"

"Then it closes," said Hester Pye, "and the way it closes is the thing I would like you
to be thinking about for the rest of this descent."

---

It closed at about the sixth turn.

There was no warning that Mittens could point to afterwards, and he went back over it four
times looking for one, and there wasn't. There was simply the sound — a long, low, final
sound, like a door in a house two streets away, closing in a house you are in — and a
shaking in the rock that came up through the soles of his feet and put grit in his teeth.

"RUN," said Patches, and was extremely good at it.

They ran for what Mittens later put at four minutes and no more, along a spiral that had
been comfortable walking and was a staircase in the dark, and the sound came round behind
them the whole way, keeping pace, which was the detail that frightened him most. It was
not chasing. Chasing is fast and irregular and makes mistakes.

It kept pace.

"It knows the route," said Tuppence, from somewhere behind, and she said it in the voice
of somebody confirming an appointment.

The way out was a narrowing, and the way out was a place where the spiral doubled back on
itself so that there was a lip of rock, and under the lip a gap that a mouse could get
through and a ram could not and a duke could not and a raven could, and which Mittens
could just about.

"It wants the lamp," said Hester, from above. "Mittens. It is not after us. We are
excess."

And that was the moment the whole thing turned over in his head and stayed turned over:
that they had not been escaping anything. They had been *delivered to a door*, and the
door was closing, and the only thing in the world that mattered about it was an object
the size of a teapot.

"Right," he said. "Give me the lamp."

---

The lamp went up onto a rock shelf, and the light from it went up onto about nine feet of
damp stone, and Mittens got behind it with his shoulder and his weight and pushed it into
a crevice in the wall, the way you wedge a door, and then went further and put his back
against it and *held* it, which is not what a person does with a door.

"That will hold," said Patches, from the gap.

"Will it."

"That stone'll hold a lamp."

"That stone'll hold a lamp," Mittens agreed. "It won't hold the route."

He heard Hester say something to the duke, low and fast, in a register of dog that is used
for moving furniture. He did not hear the words and would not have understood them, and
this was later, in a different room, the single thing in the entire descent that he
considered giving up his name over.

What happened next took about nine seconds and consisted of the light going out.

---

It went out like a candle in a draught, and the dark that came in behind it was total and
had weight, and Mittens lay on his back on the stone of a spiral tunnel with his boot sole
against a wall he could not see, and every animal in him was up.

Somewhere above him, a door he had never seen finished closing.

Then there was nothing at all for a count of perhaps ten, and in that nothing he understood
the whole shape of the thing: that they had come down into a route, and the route had let
them in because a lamp had been coming, and now there was no lamp coming, and the route
had closed the way a hand closes, and the party was now *inside the closed thing* and would
be inside it until the route decided otherwise.

"Oh," said Tuppence, in the dark, conversationally. "That's not good."

"Is there a light," said Patches.

"No."

"Then it's not good."

Patches was already moving. Mittens heard him go past, the particular noise of a large
animal in a confined space, and then the sound of a ram's hoof on stone, and then Patches
saying, with enormous mildness, "Right. It comes back up. That's a spiral. It comes back
up. There's a light at the top of a spiral or there was, thirty seconds ago."

Nobody argued with him, because he was a sergeant and he had said it in the voice that
sergeants use, and because the alternative was four people and a mouse in the dark
convincing each other they had come down for ever.

They came back up. It took most of the night and it cost them the rest of the lamp's oil,
and Patches led it, and nobody spoke for the first four hours, and twice Mittens was sure
they had lost the turn and twice Patches was right, and by the grey light they came out
onto a shelf of rock nine miles east of Millward and lay down on stone in a wind that felt
like a mercy.

The duke found him an hour later and sat down next to him and did not say anything for a
while, which was the first thing the duke had done in two days that Mittens had not
already begun to be grateful for.

"I would like to say something," Rocky said, "about what you did with the lamp."

"Don't."

"I am going to say it anyway, and I am aware that you have not asked me to."

"Then say it quickly and say it once."

"You stood in a hole," said the Duke of Cavalton, "and held a wall, for a light, so that
four people and a mouse could get through it." He was looking at his own hands. "I have
sworn the oath I swore in that room. The oath says I will not let the errand I set out
on become a different errand by the time it is done. I want you to know that I know what
happened to the lamp, and I am not going to spend the rest of this pretending it was a
detail."

Mittens lay on the stone in the wind.

"What would you have spent it on," he said.

"I don't know," said Rocky. "That is the honest answer and I have given it a great deal of
thought since two o'clock this morning."

---

What they did not discuss, then or later, was that there had been no chase. Nothing had
followed them. The route had simply been doing its job, the way a lock does its job, and
the part of it that had cost them the light was not a fight.

Mittens thought about the nine seconds, and about the sound of a door closing in a house
two streets away, and about the fact that a lamp the size of a teapot had been the whole
of what stood between five living creatures and a route that did not want them.

Somewhere under the rock, a long way down, the Descent settled back into the dark and
began, in the way that such things do, to open for whatever was coming next.

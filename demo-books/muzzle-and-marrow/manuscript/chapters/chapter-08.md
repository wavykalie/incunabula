# Chapter Eight — Ahead

The plan had been simple for about four months and Tuppence had not revised it once, which
was not confidence; it was that the plan was made of steps, and a mouse who has spent her
life moving things does not revise a route; she moves the thing.

**The plan.**

1. Get the lamp down. *Done, and paid for twice.*
2. Get ahead of the party. In progress.
3. Find the buyer. *(There is only ever one buyer. There is a woman at the bottom of the
   world and she is forty years ahead of you and she has been buying for a decade.)*
4. Sell.
5. Be somewhere warm by the back door of the Bell House before anybody works out what she
   sold.

Step five was the one Tuppence had spent the most care on, and she had spent it because
step four was the one that would be remembered. There is a great deal of money in step
four. There is a *life* in step five.

She was in the corridor a good way ahead of them now, which is the only advantage a mouse
has ever had over anything larger, and she was taking it.

---

The corridor was warm and it was lit and it was the width of five, and Tuppence went
along it eating an apple she had taken from camp, and thought about how stupid she was,
and enjoyed it, because enjoying it was part of the professional.

She was not, she would want on record, frightened. Frightened was for the descent. Frightened
was behind and below and coming up the stairs at a sergeant's pace.

She had been frightened in the Hollow House for nine years, and it had been the most useful
thing that had ever happened to her, because you could not be good at moving things if you
were frightened of being caught with them.

That was the job. That was the whole of the job. You take a thing from where it is, and
you take it to where it needs to be, and you do it on time, and you never once think about
whether it ought to be moved.

*Don't think about it,* Tuppence. That was the rule. That had been the rule since she was
eleven years old and a badger in the Low Marsh had explained it to her with a paw across
the back of the neck, and it was the only thing anybody had ever given her that was
actually useful, and she had been breaking it, cheerfully and for eleven years, in the
specific direction of caring about things.

She finished the apple and dropped the core in a corridor four hundred miles from any
street, and thought: *that is the most expensive apple in the world and I have had four.*

---

She found the buyer at the third junction, sitting on a step with her back to the wall in
a coat from ninety years ago.

"You are early," said Madame Void.

"You did not say where to be early *to*."

"No. I did not." The serpent tilted her head, and it was a long way round. "You were
supposed to bring them down and then go up, and you were supposed to be in a bar in
Millward by the third day, and instead you have spent nine days behind them and have not
sold anybody anything at all."

"Would you have paid me?"

"Probably not," said Madame Void. "You would have had to be able to describe the party to
me, and the party is four mammals, a bird, and a ram, going to a door on the strength of a
lamp. Any fool could have sold that. What I paid for was the door opening, and you have
been instrumental in that, and I would like to pay you for it, and I would like to hear
what you are going to say about it."

Tuppence stood in the warm corridor of a place designed for five, with a piece of apple
core in her teeth, and made the assessment she had been making since she was eleven years
old.

"Mine's not for sale," she said.

"Whose is?"

"Still mine," said Tuppence. "That's what I keep finding. You buy it and you sell it on
and it's not yours by the time anybody asks you. Yours is the four hours in the Bell House
and the second cup and going out through the yard instead of the front, and I have been
doing that since I was old enough and I have never once been able to explain it to anyone
and I am not going to try with you."

Madame Void looked at her for a while.

"Then why are you here," she said. "You are half a day ahead of a party you have just
betrayed, in a corridor you cannot leave, and you did not go up. You had the stair."

Tuppence did not have an answer, and it turned out — which she had not been prepared for
at all, and which she resented for the rest of the day — that not having one was
information.

"Step five," she said. "I had step five."

"Describe step five."

"Somewhere warm, back door, second cup, nobody followed me home." She heard how it sounded
and made herself finish it, because a professional finishes. "And I've not had it. I had
the commission and I had the four times the rate and I had nine days of watching four
people not go up the stair, and the first one is dead now and the second one is a voice I
have to be in a room with twice a day, and I would like it noted that I said yes, and I
would like it noted that I said it *twice*, and I don't think I did the second one on
purpose."

---

The serpent stood up. It took rather longer than a person.

"You are correct that you cannot leave," she said. "The route you came down is closed
behind them, and it is not closed because of me. It is closed because there is a party on
it, and the party is five, and the party is what the corridor was built for." She looked
down the warm passage. "And you are correct that I have been buying things for eleven
years and have not yet bought a person. I stopped, some time ago, without noticing, and I
found it extremely convenient not to look into it."

"What do you actually want?"

"I want you to go back down there and stand in front of them and say it," said Madame
Void. "Not to me. To them. I have forty years of a case and it has exactly one hole in it
and the hole is that every year somebody gets a sentence wrong and I have never once been
able to show a city what the right sentence does. Because the right sentence does nothing.
The right sentence is simply a smaller accident, and nobody in the world has ever been
moved to be careful by a story about a smaller accident."

She picked up her coat.

"Tell them I am not the villain," she said. "They will not believe it, and I would like
that noted too, because in about an hour one of them is going to say it to my face, and I
would like a record of having predicted it in a bad mood."

---

Tuppence went back down the corridor, which took most of a day, because the stairs only
worked for a party of five and she had already used up the width.

She had time to think, which she had not wanted, and she used it the way she used
everything — as a distance to be moved.

The thing she could not get past was that she had gone down to sell four people, and had
spent nine days finding out what a person is worth by watching what it costs to keep
carrying them around, and had arrived back at the front of her own commission with her
hands empty and her rate unspent, on the grounds that a mouse who sells things for a
living has a professional obligation to be the only honest one.

It was, she thought, the single most stupid piece of reasoning of her life, and it was
hers, and she was going to have to say it out loud to a cat and a ram and a bird and a dog
in about forty minutes, and there was no way in the world to make it sound like a line.

She could do that, at least. She had been doing that since she was eleven.

It was the only thing she was actually good at, and she had a great deal of practice at
saying the true thing in a voice that made it sound like a joke, and there was a corridor
four hundred miles long in which to get it right, and she intended to use every yard.

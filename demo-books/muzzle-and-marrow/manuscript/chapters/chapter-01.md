# Chapter One — Mittens the 7th

The fishmonger's name was Teasel and she had been calling him that since he was nine
years old, which is a thing you are allowed to do to a cat in Carrowmarket, and the whole
of the Quay agreed it was funny.

"Mittens the 7th," she said, and put the fish down. "Two of the good ones."

"Thank you, Teasel."

"You'll want the other five, since you're the seventh."

He paid her. There was a rhythm to it, the whole square had the rhythm of it, and he had
been walking to this exact stall for eleven years and had never once been in a hurry
there. Two of the good ones. He'd taken the joke at some point around the fourth or fifth
year, which was earlier than he could remember and much earlier than he'd have liked.

The cat beside him — a young thing, ginger, all elbows — was still working on a laugh
that had started ten seconds ago. Mittens let him have it. That was also eleven years of
practice and he was, at thirty-one, too old to explain the technique to anyone.

"Brisk," the ginger said. "That's it. That's the one."

"Mm."

"No, that *is* it, that's brilliant—"

"Mittens," he said. "It's Mittens."

"I know it's Mittens, that's why—"

"Better off with your fish, Tallow."

The ginger cat looked at him for a beat too long, the way young ones do when they are
still working out whether they have been insulted, and then went back to his fish, and
Mittens went back to his, and the Quay went on being the Quay.

He was fine about it. He had been fine about it for most of his life.

---

The duke had been standing under the awning by the rope-market for the better part of an
hour. Mittens had clocked him at the second awning, at the far end, and had been
constructing a reasonable explanation for the duke's presence ever since. Something with
a cart. Something dull. Dukes did not come to the Quay, and if a duke had come to the
Quay it was because a cart had gone wrong, and if a cart had gone wrong it was a matter
for the carpenters and not for a man in a coat the colour of a wet dog.

The duke was not looking at a cart.

"Right," said the duke.

Mittens turned. He had a fish in each hand and no plan for the rest of the sentence.

"Your grace."

"Rocky. Please. If we are going to be ridiculous at each other for a season, we may as
well skip to the ridiculous part."

"Rocky," Mittens agreed, because there was no version of this where he didn't.

"You're Mittens."

"Third time today. Fourth if you count the one on the rope-market sign, which is me, and
which I asked them to take down, and which they did not."

"I know. I asked for it to be painted larger."

Mittens waited for the rest of it.

"I'd like to hire you," said the duke, "to go to the centre of the world."

---

The square had gone on being the square. Two stalls down a man was arguing about eels. A
dog on a ladder was re-hanging a sign that had clearly been re-hung that morning already.
Somebody's dog was doing a trick with a coin and losing.

"That's a great deal of money for a walk," Mittens said.

"It's not a walk. Nobody knows what it is." Rocky's hands were behind his back, which
Mittens understood to mean he was holding something, which was a duke's whole art form.
"I want to be honest with you about the terms, because I think you will hear the honest
version and refuse, and I'd rather you heard it."

"I'm listening."

"Five hundred crowns up front. The wage is nothing — you'll want more, and we'll argue
about it, and you'll win, because I've been told I'm bad at this." He said the last part
the way a man says a thing he has been told about himself and has decided to keep. "The
quest goes down. There's five of us, or four, I'm still assembling. The last party went
down eleven years ago and two of them came back, and one of the two is the woman who
wrote the account, and I would like her on it, and she will not come for me."

"Two came back."

"Two came back."

"And the rest?"

"Didn't."

The coin trick had stopped. Mittens had not noticed it stopping.

"Why me," he said.

"Because you're the seventh."

---

The Quay had a rhythm and Mittens had been in it since he was nine, and the thing about a
rhythm that long is that you can feel when it goes wrong. It had not gone wrong. The
square was a square. A cat who lived in a square and was thirty-one and had killed
people for money at one point, badly, in a manner he did not discuss, was not going to
be undone by a dog in a wet coat offering him a walk.

And it wasn't.

He put the fish in his basket. He counted out the coins. He was aware, distantly, of
doing each of these correctly.

"Your grace," he said. "Duke. Rocky. Before I answer."

"Yes."

"What happened to the party that went down before."

Rocky's face did something Mittens had not seen a duke's face do before. It went
careful.

"The account," Rocky said. "It's partial. Hester Pye has it — the raven, the archivist at
the Cold Reading. She'll tell you what she can."

"What she can," Mittens said.

"What she can," Rocky agreed.

"And if what she can isn't enough to talk me into it?"

"Then it is a walk," said the duke, "and I'll have wasted an hour of your morning, and
you'll have had a very good fish, and I will be sorry, and I will not mention it again."

---

Mittens thought about the sixth one.

He thought about her the way you touch a tooth you have been told is fine. Kir, called
Kir the 6th, who had been the best of the seven and the fastest and the only one of them
who could make a room laugh on purpose, and who had gone down at nineteen, and whose name
was not on a cart or a sign or a stall, and whose sixth was going to be somebody else's
problem within a year.

The Quay did not know that. The Quay knew the seventh. The seventh was a good joke and
the seventh was on a sign.

"Thursday," Mittens said.

Rocky had the thing out of his hands before Mittens had finished the word. It was a
contract, folded, and it was already signed, which meant he had brought it knowing the
answer, which was the single most insulting thing about the entire morning.

"Thursday," the duke agreed.

"One condition."

"Name it."

"You tell me her name." Mittens picked up his basket. "Kir. Kir the Sixth. If the account
has anything at all in it, I want to hear her name, and if it hasn't, I want to know that
it hasn't, out loud, from you, before I go."

Rocky did not answer immediately, and Mittens understood that this was the first honest
thing that had happened to him all morning, and that the duke had not known he was going
to do it.

"Kir," Rocky said. "The Sixth. Yes. It's in the account."

"Then I'll go."

Mittens the 7th walked home through a square that had gone back to arguing about eels, and
the fish went warm in his basket, and he did not find it funny, and he did not find it
anything else, and the two of them were, he was well aware, the same thing.

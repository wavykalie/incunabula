# Chapter Ten — The Orb

It was not impressive, and that was the first thing, and it was the thing that undid all of
them, in the end, more than anything else.

They had come four hundred miles down. There was a warm corridor that counted them. There
was a door that read a name. There was a woman of the Ninth Coil who had been sitting at
the bottom of the world for forty years to make sure nobody said a sentence badly.

And at the centre of the planet, on the floor of an unlit room, there was a light about the
size of a teapot, the colour of weak tea, doing nothing.

The floor went round it in a ring of bare stone about nine feet across, and that was all.
No altar. No mechanism. Nothing that looked built.

"It doesn't look like a thing that grants wishes," said Patches.

"It's a thing that *reads*," said Hester Pye. "It isn't built, it's written. That's what I
should have said in Copper Lane and didn't."

---

It did not speak. Nobody had expected it to and nobody afterwards claimed they had, and the
claiming would have been the only dishonest thing any of them ever did.

There was a silence, and the silence went on, and it became obvious to all four of them
within a minute that the silence was the *mechanism* — that there was nothing to wait for,
that it was not considering, that it had been ready since before they were born, and that
the only thing it required from the four hundred miles above was that somebody eventually
said something.

"I thought there'd be a question," said the duke.

"There is no question," said Hester. "That's the thing nobody believes. You don't ask it
what you want. You say it, and it takes the sentence."

"Then who goes first?"

It was Mittens.

He had thought about it for eleven days, which was the longest he had thought about
anything, and he had done it the way Hester had told them to, in front of everybody, at a
table, in words they could all hear, and the sentence was forty-one words long and it had
been forty-one words long since the second night, and he had not shown it to anybody.

"I'm going," he said.

"Mittens—"

"I'm going because I am the seventh of my name and I have said her name out loud in two
rooms this week and I am the only one here who has come down for a person rather than for
a sentence, and if I stand behind you then I will say something clever."

He stepped onto the ring of bare stone.

It was warm under his feet, which was the corridor's doing, and the light was about four
feet away at the level of the eye of a cat, which was the only part of the room that had
been fitted to him.

He had, he found, about as long as it took to notice that he had no idea.

---

"Whatever you say," said Hester Pye behind him, "it will be done. That is the entire
guarantee. There is no interpretation step, there is no goodwill in it, there is no *me*.
If you say a sentence about a lamp, there will be no lamp, forever, and that is not a
threat, it is a description, and you should hear how flatly I am able to say it."

"I know," said Mittens.

"Do you?"

"I'm the seventh of my name," he said. "I've been a careful one since I was nine."

And he said it, and it was forty-one words long, and it had been checked and re-checked for
eleven days against every account in a folio that stopped in the middle of a page, and
it was, by every measure Hester Pye could apply to a sentence, a good sentence.

He said:

"I wish that Kir, the Fourteenth of her name, of the Caversham line, who went down eleven
years ago and was not brought back, is at rest, and is neither in pain nor in fear, and
knows that she was not forgotten, and that her brother is alive, and that it is known to
her that her brother is alive."

---

It was over in a moment. There was no light, no sound, no movement, no weight in the air.
A mechanism that takes a sentence and does the whole of it has no reason to make a
performance of the doing.

And that was that, and it had worked, and it was forty-one words of the most careful
sentence anyone had ever said in that room.

Mittens the 7th stood on the bare stone in a room at the centre of the world and found
that he could not breathe, and worked out, in a space of about four seconds, what he had
done.

*Is at rest.* Not: *was brought back.* Not: *I will know.* Not: *let me speak to her, once,
for a minute, and ask her whether she was afraid.*

*Is at rest.*

*Knows that she was not forgotten.*

*Her brother is alive, and that it is known to her that her brother is alive.*

It was all of it. It was the whole of the sentence, perfectly carried out, in the sense
in which a mechanism carries out a sentence. Kir was at rest. Kir was not forgotten. Kir
knew that her brother was alive, because he had asked for that she would know it.

And Kir was four hundred miles up, in whatever the account's last page did not say, being
*known about*, forever, by a brother who had come to the centre of the world to make sure
the universe was informed.

Mittens had wanted to ask her whether she was afraid. It was in him, it had been in him
since the dock at the bottom of the shaft, and he had looked at it for eleven days, and
he had taken a thought and put forty-one words around it like a man building a wall around
a fire, and the wall had been perfect, and the fire was behind it.

He could not ask her. He had asked for the *description of her being all right*, and he
had got the description, and the description was all right, and the asking was gone.

---

"Sergeant," said Hester Pye. "You said you did not want one."

"No."

"Then say it anyway, so that it is in the room."

"I said what I said—"

"Say it to the light, Patches. That is the only place it has ever been said to."

The ram stood in front of it for a long time. He was thirty-one going on forty-two and he
had spent nine years being a man who came back, and he had said, on a shelf four hundred
miles up, that he did not want anything, and that he would rather be a person who wanted
nothing than a person who got it, and everyone had been kind about it, and he had known
exactly how it would go.

"I wish," said Sergeant Patches, "that I had not come back."

Nobody moved.

"That is a wish," said Hester quietly. "That is a complete sentence, and it is aimed at
the mechanism, and you should know, before you finish it, that I have read the account and
that is the one construction in this book I do not know the ending to."

"I know what I'm doing," said Patches.

"You do not," said Hester, "but that has never once stopped anybody, and it is a poor
reason to wait."

He said it.

And the Duke of Cavalton — who had sworn an oath in a room above a chandler's that his
errand would not become a different errand by the time it was done, and who had composed
for two hundred miles the most careful sentence anybody had ever heard at this table, and
who had asked for *truth* rather than goodness because truth was a smaller thing —
looked at the light, and understood at last, in the way that a man understands a lock he
has been turning in the dark, that a careful sentence is still a sentence.

He said: "I wish to know that my oath held."

It held. It was going to hold for the rest of his life, absolutely, in the one sense of
the word, and he was never going to be able to put it down.

---

Nobody asked Tuppence. She noticed nobody asking her, and stood at the back of a room at
the centre of the world in her good coat, and did the arithmetic she had been doing since
the Nettle, and it came out the same way it always came out, which was that a mouse who
sells things for a living has a professional obligation to be the only honest one.

"I said I didn't intend to say a wish," she said. "At camp. I said I'd bill anybody who
put a sentence in my mouth."

"Mittens the 7th," said Madame Void, from somewhere behind them — she had come down at
some point in the last day and had not announced it, and there was something about that
which was worse than anything else in the room, "put no sentence in anybody's mouth. I have
been sitting in a room at the bottom of the world for forty years preventing one sentence
a year from being said badly, and I have never once in all that time been asked to
*watch* one."

"Then watch," said Tuppence.

She stepped up.

"I wish," said Tuppence, "that nobody in this room would ever be able to sell anybody
anything again."

---

It was over in a moment, in the way that all of them had been over in a moment.

Hester Pye closed her eyes, and opened them, and said, in a voice nobody had heard from
her: "Oh."

Mittens felt it go out of him. He put a hand on the stone. Eleven years of going to
betray somebody, four hundred miles of it, the Bell House and the second cup and the yard
instead of the front — all of it, gone, cleanly, completely, the way a sentence is carried
out when a mechanism takes it seriously.

"Well," said Tuppence, and looked round at them, and had to sit down on the bare stone
afterwards. "That's a waste of a perfectly good career."

---

The four of them stood in the room at the centre of the world.

The light was the colour of weak tea, and it was about the size of a teapot, and it had
given each of them precisely what they had asked for, and it had never once been kind, and
it had never once been cruel, and it was still there, doing the only thing it has ever
done, patiently, at the centre of the world, four hundred miles above every city in the
world where four people had wanted something and had said it perfectly and been heard.

"Madame Void," said Mittens, at the end, when the others had started up the passage. "You
said there was no treasure and no curse."

"There isn't."

"Then what's the point of it?"

The serpent looked at the light for a while, and there was nothing in her face at all.

"It does what it was built to do," she said. "Somebody, four hundred years ago, needed a
sentence to be carried out exactly, and it was built, and then the world forgot that was
what it was for, and the forgetting is the only disaster in this room."

She turned round and went out after the others.

Mittens the 7th was the last one out. He stopped in the doorway of a room that had been
the same size for six days and had been waiting, patiently, with the whole of his sentence
in it, for a cat to come and say it properly.

It had been perfect.

He had wanted to ask her whether she was afraid.

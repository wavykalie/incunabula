# Chapter Three — The Oath

The room was above a chandler's on Copper Lane and it had been a chandler's office until
the chandler died, and nobody had wanted it since, and the rent was the sort of rent you
pay when you would rather not think about the room.

Mittens had the window seat. He had taken it in the first minute, the way you take the
window seat, and had been checking the street at intervals ever since out of a habit he
would have described as professional and which was in fact mostly the want to be able to
leave.

"Order," said Duke Rocky.

"There is no order. There is a bar."

"There is an order to it."

Patches shifted in his chair, which was built for a dog. "Your grace—"

"Rocky. If I'm going to be asked to swear something in front of witnesses, I want at least
one of us to have said my name in the last minute."

Patches looked at the table for a while. "Rocky," he said.

"Good. Hester, are you content?"

"I am always content to watch." Hester Pye had not taken a chair. She had taken the
shillelagh that stood in the corner by the door, a habit from an archive where the
shillelagh was always within reach and had turned out to be the difference between a
professional and a mob. "I'll warn you that I have read the account three times and I
still cannot tell you why we're doing this."

"Nobody can. That's rather the point of the offer."

"Yes. But I'd like it on the record that I said so and was ignored."

"Noted," said Rocky, with what appeared to be total sincerity, and Mittens thought: *he
believes that. He actually believes I've noted it.*

---

The oath was longer than anyone had warned him.

Mittens had assumed — had assumed, and was now discovering he had assumed on the
strength of never having been asked — that swearing to something was a short business.
Hands, words, a clasp, done. It was not that. Duke Rocky of Cavalton took the centre of
the floor, and squared his shoulders, and drew breath, and went through an oath that
belonged, word for word, to a pack ceremony that predated the current city by something
Mittens could not have guessed at.

The words came out of him in the register a dog uses when he is being entirely sincere,
which is a register most dogs can produce at will and which makes a room go silent.

"...and I hold the collar-sworn, and set my name against the collar, that the errand I
set out on is the errand I set out on, and that when I have walked it or been carried from
it, no other account of it will stand."

The last part of it was the part that mattered and it took eleven seconds, and it said
that the Duke of Cavalton was authorising anybody, anywhere, ever, to check his story
against his sworn word and to treat him as a liar if it did not match.

Nobody in the room said anything for a moment. Patches had both hands flat on the table.

"Right," said Rocky, slightly pink. "That's that."

"Was that the whole—"

"That's the whole."

"You said 'carried from it.'"

"My grandmother was carried out of a fire at six years old," said the duke, mildly. "It is
the standard form. I did not enjoy the standard form. Shall we go on?"

Mittens looked at the shuttered window. The Chandler's had been somebody's office, and
somebody had died in it, and the rent was cheap, and the Duke of Cavalton had just sworn a
four-hundred-year-old dog formula in a room where nobody could verify a word of it, and
meant every syllable, and Tuppence the mouse was filing it all away somewhere behind her
eyes with the frankness of a cat watching a bird walk into a room it had been banned from.

---

Hester put the folio on the table when the tea had been poured and drunk and poured
again.

"I want to say the terms," she said. "I will read what is in the account. I will not read
the parts that are not in the account, and if I stop, that is a stop and not a pause."

"Understood."

"The last descent was eleven years ago. Five went down. Three names are in the account,
and the fourth name is in the account, and there is a fifth name nowhere, which we will
come to."

"Names first," said Mittens.

Hester looked at him. "Yes," she said. "Kir, the Sixth. Lead. Twin foxes — no, I am sorry,
that is not a species, it is a habit of mine, I will stop."

Mittens did not say anything. He had not expected her to go there and had not prepared
his face.

"A cat, of the Caversham line. Twenty at the time of the descent. Fourteenth of her
name."

"Fourteenth."

"You are the seventh. She was the fourteenth. Those are not the same number, if you were
about to ask, and nobody has ever been able to make them the same number."

Somewhere behind Mittens' ribs something settled into a position it was going to keep.
He had been the seventh since he was nine years old. He had been, for the whole of that
time, a number that got said in a market square. And Kir had gone down at twenty as the
fourteenth of a name and nobody on the Quay had ever said it, and he had not known that,
and now he did, and knowing it had changed the shape of the week.

"The second name," Hester said, "is Sergeant Patches of the Low Barracks, then of the
Ninth."

"That was me," said Patches. "I was thirty-one and I was the best-looking ram in that
barracks and I have never been able to say that sentence again."

"You went down," said Hester.

"I went down. I came back. I wrote a report of four pages and it was filed and it is not
the account. The account is hers."

"Then the third name is Hester Pye," she said, "and the fourth is Tuppence, who is in this
room, and who would like everyone to notice that she is reading about herself in the
third person and would prefer that we all just got on with the tea."

Tuppence, who had a cup of tea she had not drunk and a plate she had cleared twice,
raised an empty hand. "Afternoon," she said.

---

"The account is short," said Hester. "I will read it as it stands."

She did, and it was funny, and that was the first thing Mittens had not been ready for.

It was funny because the party had been *careful*. Whoever wrote it had understood, more
sharply than any of them were going to get, that they were describing a wish in front of
the thing that would carry it out, and so they had phrased everything to the letter.
*"We wished that the matter of the Hallow Lamp be resolved."* That is a wish about a
lamp. It is not a wish about a lamp. It does not say whose, it does not say how, and it
is a sentence that four hundred years of legal argument could not improve.

"And the result," said Hester, "was that every lamp in the Hold was relit, at once, for
the rest of the year, which they had wanted, and which cost them a fortune, and which
none of them had thought to word."

"So it's a joke," said Tuppence. "The Orb's a joke. Get there, say it wrong, get the
wrong thing, it's a funny old machine—"

"That is the third entry," said Hester.

She stopped.

"Hester."

She had her hand flat on the folio, on the fourth page, and she had stopped reading some
time before she stopped talking, which Mittens thought was the whole of her character in
one movement.

"The fourth entry," she said, "is my own hand. And it begins — 'We were careful. We were
so careful. We learned that you may have a thing, or you may have the description of the
thing, and we did not know which we were asking for, and we asked for the description.'"

Nobody said anything. The chandler's office was very quiet, and out of the window the
street did its street business, and a man went past with a handcart, and nobody in the
room looked at him.

"And then?" said Mittens.

"And then the account ends," said Hester Pye. "On that line. In the middle of the page.
There is nothing after it. The record is not damaged, and it is not torn, and the hand
stops mid-sentence on a word that is not the end of a word, and I have had it eleven
years, and I have never found a reason."

She squared the folio.

"So," she said, "that is the account. The rest of you are now informed, and I have
delivered on my undertaking, and I notice that none of you have said anything, which is
the correct response, and which is also, I think, the first honest thing to happen in
this room since the duke sat down."

Mittens found his voice, and it came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

"You said you'd tell me if her name wasn't in it."

"Kir the Sixth is in the account. Three times."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," said Hester. "It is not. And I am not going to be able to answer it in this room,
and I would rather say that than say it badly."

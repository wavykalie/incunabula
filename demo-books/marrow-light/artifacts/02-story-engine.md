# STORY ENGINE — the machine underneath

`artifacts/02-story-engine.md`. Phases 0 and 1 collapsed into one document because the
premise gate work had already been done and recorded in `premise.md`; this file carries
the world, the escalation logic, and the engine, and does not repeat the gate history.

---

## THE WORLD

Contemporary. A northern European karst country that is not going to be named, because a
speleological novel that names its range has to be accurate about its hydrology and this
one is going to be accurate about other things.

**The industry is small and dying in the specific way small industries die.** Caving is
not dangerous in the way films say and not safe in the way licences imply; it is
*unfunded*, and the funding situation is what actually shapes every character in this
book. There is a national body that issues survey permits. There is a commercial
association that nobody belongs to. There are four people in the country paid to do this
full time and thirty who do it between other work, and the four are all either injured,
retired, or in a job they are too good at.

**Kit is real and specific and is never explained to the reader.** A harness, a rack, a
bolto, a jug, a cylinder, a cave pack, a survey station, a clinometer, a disto. The
readers who know will be satisfied and the readers who don't will not be lost, because the
prose always states the *function* before the object. Nobody in this book has ever said
the word *speleology* in conversation and neither should the narration until chapter
nineteen, at the earliest, and probably never.

**Film school is real and slightly embarrassing, and it is a different kind of
endangered.** A two-hander about a documentary is not a documentary. Ottoline is
filming ninety hours of material into something nobody has commissioned and her
supervisor knows it. The grant window closes in October. The film school has four
surviving rooms and one of them is a store cupboard.

**The overlap is the book.** Caving and documentary-making have the same disease:
a young person with access to equipment, an old professional with a reason to be in the
way, and a funding deadline that does not care about either.

## THE VELL

The system itself. Named here once and then used by name throughout, because a cave that
is *the cave* for forty chapters is a mood and a cave with a name is a place.

**Vell** — a limestone system in the eastern karst, roughly four kilometres of mapped
passage, of which the deep section is under-surveyed. It has one entrance large enough
for a person with a full rack. It is not large enough for two people abreast (WR-002).
Everyone goes in one at a time. This is a physical fact and the plot leans on it hard.

**The gauge.** A brass plate, hand-painted, nailed to the wall fifty metres inside the
entrance, marked in hours since the last known rise. It is a real instrument of the kind
cavers actually use — a crude local device that is right often enough to be dangerous.
**It is the book's clock, its lie, and its murder weapon.**

**The collapse, 2022.** The fourth chamber, in the section below the second pitch. The
official account is rock. WR-003 holds that the collapse was caused by water and that
this is knowable from the start — **so nothing in this book turns on the reader not
knowing it.** The tension is not *what happened*. The tension is that four years later
nobody in the professional community will say the word, and the one person who was there
is selling bolts in a shop eleven kilometres away.

**The Vell is not haunted, is not magical, and has no opinion about anybody.** It is
limestone and water and gravity, and it killed Solveig Havelock the way a river kills a
person who is standing in the wrong place at the wrong time, which is to say
indifferently and for reasons that are entirely explicable. The supernatural reading is
available to Ottoline for the length of about two chapters, because she is twenty-two
and because her mother did something stranger, and it is *wrong*, and the book does not
pretend otherwise for longer than it takes to kill it.

## THE TWO ENGINES

They are one engine, which is the premise, and the book's job is to keep the reader
believing they are two for as long as possible.

### Engine A — the descent

A survey of a tight system needs two people on separate ends of a line (WR-001). This is
a physical constraint and not a metaphor, and the book is careful to let it be a physical
constraint for as long as possible before anybody in it says something about the other
thing.

Ottoline gets underground access. That is the film's problem, and it is a *logistics*
problem, and it is solved in the most ordinary way available: she cannot be on the line
herself, so she is on the surface end, and Ines is fifty metres down with a radio and a
student's questions in her ear. **The film's subject and the person filming it are
separated by the length of a rope for most of the book.** The romance happens down that
line, in one direction, badly, at intervals.

### Engine B — the romance

Ines will not be filmed. Not the Vell. Not the survey. Not herself. She has four years of
a reason and she will give a different one each time. Ottoline needs her on camera, not
because she wants her on camera, but because **Ines is the only person alive who was
there** and a documentary about the Vell that does not contain Solveig Havelock's crew is
a documentary about a hole.

So the professional arrangement is impossible from the start, and both women know it, and
both of them spend the first half of the book negotiating around it in the language of
kit, permits, and daylight windows.

### WHERE THEY MESH

The film's funding deadline and the survey's permit are the same deadline. The person who
can authorise the descent is the person who is obstructing the film. The person who knows
the Vell's water is the person who will not go into it. The romantic arc and the descent
become the same problem at **chapter twenty-two**, and the book is built so that both
readers who are there for the romance and readers who are there for the cave are holding
the same scene and getting different things from it.

## ESCALATION

Pressure compounds. It does not restart. Four turns, each of which costs the book
something it cannot get back.

**Turn 1 — the survey begins (ch 1–9).** The arrangement is made, and it is made in
writing, and the writing is the first thing that goes wrong. Ines goes down. She comes
back. Nothing goes wrong. The book earns the reader's trust by having nothing go wrong for
nine chapters, which is the correct amount of rope before the drop.

**Turn 2 — the descent proper (ch 10–24).** The line is live. The romance is happening
down the wire in both directions and neither woman can stop it by going up, because going
up ends the film. Ines's control of the situation is entirely a function of being deeper
than the other person, and she is learning what it costs to be the one who is deeper.

**Turn 3 — the past catches the line (ch 25–34).** What actually happened in 2022 becomes
available to more than one person. The film's own archive — the 1994 Vera Marrow material,
which Marisol releases for reasons she cannot fully explain — turns out to contain a
gauger reading. **The instrument in the 1994 film is the same instrument, and the reading
in it is wrong.** The book's central lie is not just Ines's. It is in the archive, from
before Ottoline's mother was a name anybody used.

**Turn 4 — the water (ch 35–45).** The gauge is read, and it is read correctly, and the
book does not hide behind any last-minute doubt. This is the chapter the whole structure
was built to make possible: **everyone who needed to know knows, and the film is rolling,
and the rope is two people and there is no third option.**

## WHAT THE BOOK REFUSES

- It does not have a last-minute doubt. Nobody says *maybe it wasn't the water.* The
  doubt was in 1994 and it was wrong then too.
- It does not rescue anybody by equipment. The gear is good. The gear is why this is a
  novel and not a memorial.
- It does not have Ines confess in a single scene. The sentence is assembled in pieces
  across nine chapters by three different people, and the last piece is not given by
  Ines at all. It is read off a gauge by a rescue technician.
- **It does not kill anybody in the last third.** This is a romance. The tension is
  structural — a woman who is too good at this, going into a place that has already
  happened to her, on a line held by someone who wants her not to.
- It does not let Ines be the romantic prize for having suffered. She is the competent
  one and she is the one in the worst of it, and the romance is not a reward, it is a
  second problem.

## WHAT THE READER IS PROMISED

A two-hander in which one of the two will not be seen and the other cannot stop looking,
built on a physical arrangement that keeps them apart, ending in a hole with a
twenty-eight-year-old brass gauge and a nine-minute video nobody has watched in four
years.

**The opening promises exactly this.** See `artifacts/07-opening-strategy.md`. An opening
that promises a different book is the most expensive mistake in the architecture phase,
and every inheritance from this phase is paid for by chapter thirty.

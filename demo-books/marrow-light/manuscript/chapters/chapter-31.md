# Chapter Thirty-One — Agreed

They met on the twenty-sixth of November in a room with a window and a table and a recorder
that was switched off, because Marisol Trent had decided at about eleven o'clock the previous
night that this one was not going on a recording, and had told the man with the laptop so, and
he had not argued, and had taken his laptop and left.

"So no minutes," said Marisol.

"No minutes," said Tove.

"I want to be very clear that I've decided that and I'm not asking permission from anybody,
and if anybody wants to object they can object to the fact rather than to the decision." She
put a folder on the table. "This is a decision meeting. I want it in the room for an hour and
then I want everyone to leave and go and do something else for a day, and I want to say why
before anybody asks."

"Go on."

"Because in September I gave Ottoline a no in writing over nine paragraphs and every single
paragraph was correct, and in October I withdrew paragraph six and signed a form with a note
in it apologising to whoever reads it in 2036, and I did both of those things because the
right answer kept arriving at the wrong time." Marisol's hands were flat on the table. "I've
been doing this for twenty-two years and the failure is never the decision. It's the timing.
It's having a decision ready when the facts aren't ready and then having to defend it, and
I've done it four times on this one and I'd like to do it once properly."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Everybody's got their reasons," she said. "I've read all of them. I want to go round the
table and I want it to be short, and I want people to say the reason they'd still do this if
nobody else were doing it. Not the reason it's a good idea. The reason you'd do it alone."

---

Ottoline went first, because Marisol had said so.

"Forty hours of a layby," she said. "That's what's at the other end of not doing it. I've
been in a car park for four months and I have got forty hours of somebody not talking to a
camera, and it's a good film and it's not the film, and everybody involved has been extremely
kind about that, and being extremely kind about it is the worst thing that's ever happened to
me."

"Fine. Anybody could have got there."

"No. That's the answer to the easy question and it's not the one I was asked." She turned her
cup round on the table. "I asked a woman in a car park whether she'd go down eleven times
because a number on a wall didn't mean anything, and she told me the truth, and the truth was
about a collapse in 2011 that changed a catchment. That's the answer. That's what's under it.
And I'm going down because the answer is on page two hundred and nine of a document and
nobody's read page two hundred and nine, and because a person went to a lot of trouble in 2019
to put it there, and because there is a number on a wall in that system that gets repainted
by hand by a woman who will not tell anybody she's doing it, and I have spent four months
finding that out, and I'm not going to go down and film nothing."

Marisol wrote one word on a pad. Nobody saw it.

---

Tove went second, and took eleven seconds longer than anybody expected.

"Page two hundred and nine," she said. "I'd read that. It's a good page. It's the best page I
have ever read in an association document and it is the only reason I stopped asking her to
explain herself, and I'd like that in the room because it's the reason my opposition went
quiet and nobody should let it go quiet without noticing."

"You raised it in October," said Ines.

"I raised it. And it was the right thing to raise, and it didn't work, and I stopped raising
it in about the third week of October, and I've been uneasy about that for six weeks." Tove
did not look round. "My reason. I'd go on my own because the fourth edition is due on the
third of February and it's the first edition in eleven years that's finished, and if the
hydrology in it is wrong then somebody has to be able to say when we knew, and the only
record of when we knew is a date on a piece of paper, and the piece of paper is a descent."

"That's the professional reason."

"That's the *deciding* reason. The one I'd give a committee." Tove sat back. "The one I
wouldn't is that I have been forty-one times to a document and I am not going to spend the
rest of my career reading it. I want to be in a room. That's it. That's the whole of it, and
it's not noble, and I'd have said the other one if you hadn't asked properly."

---

Hana went third.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "That's my number. Not hers. Mine."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to, and I've been not-having this for four years and I'm having it in a
room with a table, and I'd like to say it properly because I've said it to nobody." Hana
Vogel had her hands on the table and was not looking at any of them. "I have been on a
telephone not-taking a call since March 2022, and I have told myself for four years that it
was about the roof, and it isn't, and I worked that out in a layby four weeks ago because a
film student asked me a question in a car park that I didn't want to answer.

"I was not-taking her calls because she kept offering to let it go, and I couldn't bear being
forgiven, and I have been the last voice on a telephone for a person who did everything right
and lost somebody anyway, and I have made a career out of being the last voice, and it turns
out that's not a service, it's a *habit*, and the habit has been a wall, and I put it there
in September 2022 and I've been standing behind it ever since."

The room was very quiet.

"So I'd go on my own," said Hana, "because I want to find out whether I can be on a line with
her and be the one who says come up, and not know beforehand whether I'll say it, and I'd
rather find out in a system I know than in a callout at two in the morning with somebody I've
never met. And because I owe her a telephone call, and I've owed her a telephone call for
four years, and this is the only place I can think of to start paying it."

---

Marisol did not ask Ines. She said so.

"I'm not going to ask you," she said.

"Ask me."

"No." Marisol's voice was not gentle and it was not unkind. "I've asked you once, in a
room, in October, and you gave me a true sentence that stopped the question, and you did it
beautifully, and I have spent five weeks thinking about it. You don't owe me a second one
because I liked the first one."

"Then what do you want from me?"

"I want you to say whether the plan is safe," said Marisol Trent. "That's it. That's my one
question, and it isn't about you, and it isn't about why, and it is genuinely the only thing
I am competent to decide in this room, and if I ask you why you'll do it I'll get the wrong
answer and I'll have to act on it, and I have acted on your wrong answers twice already this
year."

Ines Varga looked at her for a while.

"The plan is safe," she said. "As it stands. As of this afternoon. On the twenty-eighth, at
nine in the morning, after Hana's read, if the read is good and if the number is where it
should be, then the plan is safe, and I will put my name against that in writing and I will
mean it, and it will be the most careful sentence I have written in four years."

"And if the read isn't good?"

"Then we don't go, and I'll say so, and you won't have to ask me."

Nobody asked anything else. That is the thing about the twenty-sixth of November and it is
the reason it matters: everybody in that room had a reason to say something else, and every
single one of them said one sentence, and not one of them argued, and not one of them
persuaded anybody, and by half past four there was a plan on a table with a date on it and
four names on a sheet and no dissent of any kind whatsoever.

Which should have been the best possible outcome and which everybody who was in that room
found, in the way you find a thing that is going well, quietly unbearable.

---

"I'm going to say the uncomfortable thing," said Marisol, at about ten past four, "and then
we're going to finish and everybody's going to go home, and I want it said now rather than at
three in the morning."

"Go on."

"I have been trying to stop this since September." She had both hands round a cup she had not
drunk from. "Nine months. I've written four reports. I have a paragraph six that I withdrew
and I'm still not sure was wrong to withdraw, and I have said no to a person I have spent
nine months trying to help, and every single reason I had was correct.

"And there is nobody in this room who wants anything. That's what I've been waiting for and I
want you to understand that it's a much worse thing than opposition. If Ottoline wanted to get
at the Vell for a film, I could have stopped her on procedural grounds in October and I'd have
been *right*. If the film wanted drama, I could have said no. If any of you wanted anything —
money, a credit, a story, a feeling about a woman who died in a hole — I could have found the
document, because I have been finding documents for twenty-two years, and I am extremely good
at it, and there is not a single one left.

"You all just want to go and look at a wall."

She put the cup down.

"That is the most frightening thing I have ever sat in a room with, and I have sat in a lot of
rooms, and I want it minuted that I said so, and I am aware that nobody is minuting."

"Noted," said Tove Marsk, and picked up a pen, and did not write anything with it.

# Chapter Eight — Recon

Four people, one van, and a document that said the road was passable.

"The road is passable," said Tove.

"The road is passable if you don't stop," said the driver, who was called Petar and had
been driving survey vans in three counties for nineteen years and had the specific calm of a
man who had watched a whole survey season get lost to a gate.

"Stop where?"

"Wherever it stops being a road. There's a farm gate at the second bridge that's been shut
since the spring and it's not locked, it's just shut, and if you come up on it at speed in
this you'll put a wing through and that's the end of the day."

"So we open gates."

"You open gates. Or you reverse a long way, which costs you twenty minutes, which in
October costs you the light." Petar took a corner without slowing. "Everything today costs
the light. That's the whole job today. There's nothing else in it."

There were four of them and Tove was driving, which nobody had expected and which was
because the third person to ask had been the person with the most hours on the particular
insurance.

In the back: two survey kit bags, a shelter that had been in the van since March and had
been in the van through the summer and had a bag of silica gel in it the size of a cat, and
Ottoline Marrow, who had a notebook on her knee and had been told, correctly, that the
notebook was the most valuable object in the vehicle.

"Am I allowed to film the drive?"

"You can film whatever you like. There's nothing to film on a drive." Tove took the second
bridge at a speed that suggested there had been a discussion about this. "Ask me again when
we're on the ground and I'll tell you what I'd rather you didn't point a camera at, and
then you can do that anyway, and we'll both be fine, and that's the arrangement. What I
won't do is work out your rules on the road. Road's for driving."

The road went on not being a road.

Tove drove the way she did everything, which was without any visible interest in it. Ottoline
had spent four months learning to read people for a living and had gotten almost nothing out
of the twenty minutes in the back of that van except a growing conviction that she was in the
presence of somebody who had never once been impressive at anything on purpose.

"You've got a shelter," said Ottoline, at the second bridge, where Petar got out and opened
the gate and did not close it behind them.

"We've got a shelter."

"In a van. Since March."

"Since March," Tove agreed.

"That's—"

"Wrong. Yeah. It's wrong. It's a van and a shelter's a van, and a cage for a cage is a
van." She slowed for a ford that wasn't a ford any more. "If it were yours I'd have put it on
a rack. It's not mine, it's on the association's, and the association's budget went on the
flood instrumentation in 2019, which I told them in 2017, and they did it anyway, because the
instrumentation was a line item and the shelter was a line item and one of them was cheaper.
That's the whole story of that shelter. It's not a drama. It's a purchase order."

"You can tell me about purchase orders," said Ottoline.

"No," said Tove. "You can ask me questions about the Vell and I'll answer them all, and
that's the deal, and I'd go on answering them if you asked me about purchase orders, but I
wouldn't enjoy it, and you'd get a much better interview if you didn't spend it on a man
complaining about a budget."

Ottoline wrote that down. Then she crossed it out. Then she wrote it down again, in
different words, and did not cross that out.

They arrived at four in the afternoon and the sky was doing what the paperwork had said it
would do, which was nothing yet, and which everybody in that van regarded as a personal insult
with a forecast attached.

"That's the entrance?"

"That's the field above it. Two minutes."

"I've never seen it."

"You will in about four minutes and it's going to be a hole in a hillside," said Tove, "and
I'm telling you that now so that when it's a hole in a hillside you don't feel misled."

Petar stayed with the van. He had a book, and he had a radio, and he had the specific calm of
a man who was going to be there in four hours and who had a flask and had brought enough for
two people because he was that sort of driver, and he put the flask on the bonnet and said
nothing about it, which was the correct way.

The walk down was six hundred metres and Tove did it at a pace that was not fast and was not
slow and which Ottoline, who was twenty-two and in good boots, found extremely annoying for
the first four hundred metres and then stopped finding annoying and started finding
something else.

"You've walked this a lot," she said.

"Every survey. Every callout I've been on where the callout was the Vell. It's four hundred
metres of grass and then it's not."

"You counted."

"You count the first four hundred. Everybody counts the first four hundred. It's the last
thing you'll do underground and the reason you can still do the first thing."

The entrance was a hole in a hillside, and Tove had been right, and Ottoline stood in front
of it for a moment longer than she had planned to.

It was not beautiful, and that was the thing she had not been ready for and could not have said
in advance. It was a sort of gash in a sort of hillside with a sort of scaffolding over it
and a steel plate over that, and it looked like a thing that had been dealt with rather than
found, and the two things she had wanted — the dark, the *going* — were not visible from
where she was standing at all.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it."

"The entrance is that."

"The entrance is that. The system is four kilometres. The entrance is nine metres wide and
it's a hole in a field that a farmer has been very polite about for thirty years." Tove sat
down on her bucket at the top of the slope. "This is the bit nobody films, by the way. Not
the going. This. The field, and the gate, and the scaffold, and a woman sitting on a bucket
deciding whether the day is good enough. That's ninety per cent of a survey and it's the bit
that looks like nothing, and the ten per cent that looks like everything is the ten per cent
that nearly goes wrong."

"How do you decide."

"The weather, and the paper." Tove tipped her head back. "Paper says the rise is four hours
ago and stable. Paper said that yesterday and the day before. So the water's not doing
anything and we've got a window from now until about four and a half, and after that I want
to be out and on this hill with the light going, and that is not a fear, it's a habit, and
the habit is thirty years old and I would like to keep it."

Ottoline looked at the field, and the scaffold, and the van with its door open and Petar
reading, and the sky that had promised nothing.

"Can I take a picture of the entrance from here?"

"No," said Tove.

"Why?"

"Because in October you'll stand in that hole and it will be the best thing you have ever
put a camera near, and if you spend a shot on it now from four hundred metres away on a
grey afternoon in October, then in six weeks when you're standing in the dark with a light
on your face you'll reach for the shot you already have and it will be a shot of a hole in a
field." She got up. "That's a professional note. Take it or don't. But I think about that
one more than most things."

"Okay," said Ottoline.

"Okay," said Tove, and put her hand on the back of her own neck, and looked down the slope
at the entrance, and did not say anything else about it for about eleven minutes, which was
how long it took to change and kit, and which was also, Ottoline noted, exactly how long it
took her to be a different person.

The eleventh minute was when Tove stopped being a woman who had given a camera note and
became a woman who was about to go into a hole, and the change was not in her face at all.
It was in her hands. She stopped touching things.

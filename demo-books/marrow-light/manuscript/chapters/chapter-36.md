# Chapter Thirty-Six — Nine Minutes

It was a laptop on a folding table in a back room of Renn and Daughters on the evening of the
second of December, and there were six people in the room, and Fabiola had made six cups of
tea and put them on the table and then had put them on a shelf, because tea on a table beside
a laptop was not a thing she was going to allow.

"I haven't watched it," she said. "I want that said in the room. In four years I have not
watched nine minutes of my partner in a back room of my own shop, and the reason is that I
am a fool and not because of anything clever, and I've been a fool about it since the Thursday
after and I'd like that to be in whatever gets written about this."

"You don't have to watch."

"I want to watch," said Fabiola. "I've wanted to watch since the Thursday after. I've been
not-watching for four years and I've been *very good* at it, and I can tell you exactly what
I've been doing instead, and it's this: I've been imagining it. I've had a version in my head
since 2022 and it's got a laugh in the middle of it, and I invented the laugh, and it is not
hers, and if I watch the tape then I'll have the real one, and I have been not knowing the
real one for four years on purpose."

She looked at Hana.

"You're in it."

"I'm in it," said Hana. "For about a minute and a half, at the front, and that's the part
I've been not-watching, and I'll tell you why, and it's the same reason you have. I know what
my face did. I've been not-thinking about what my face did for four years and I'd rather
watch it than keep not-thinking about it."

There were six people in the room. Tove Marsk was there with a leg and a stick and had not
been asked to come and had come. Marisol Trent had a chair against the wall and a notebook she
had not opened. Ottoline Marrow had a camera and had it in a bag on the floor, which nobody
commented on and which she had put there herself, which is the only time in the whole of the
last four months that she had put a camera on the floor.

"Can I ask for one thing," said Ines.

"Go on."

"Don't stop it. If it's — if it's awful." She was standing by the door and had been standing
by the door for about a minute, and had put herself at the door, and had not been asked to.
"Don't stop it for me. I've had four years to be ready and I'm not ready, and stopping it
would be worse, because then it's a thing I did at six o'clock on a Tuesday with six people
watching and then everybody has to be kind about it afterwards, and I'd rather just have it."

The room agreed and nobody said so.

---

The first twenty seconds are a wall.

It is a wall in a car in daylight, filmed on a phone, propped on a dashboard, and it is
slightly too close, and the picture moves as the car goes over something, and the sound is a
road.

Then a hand covers the lens and there is a noise of a person sitting back down, and a voice
that says, "No. *No*, it's doing it again, hold it—"

"Which one's she talking to?" said Marisol.

"Petar," said Hana. "Petar Marsk. He's in the van in about four of these nine minutes and
that's his voice."

"—hold it, I'm not doing anything, it's the mount, it's a *mount*—"

And then the picture is a car roof, and it stays on a car roof for about eleven seconds,
and then a woman's face arrives in the frame from below and is too close and fills the whole
screen.

"Well," she says. "Hello."

Somebody in the room made a sound. It was Tove Marsk, and it was extremely short, and she
covered it immediately with a cough that fooled nobody.

"Oh, don't," said the woman on the screen. "Oh, don't do that. I'm not — it's a phone, I'm on
a phone, I'm in a *van*, this is the least dignified thing I've ever recorded and if that goes
in anything I will deny it, and I will deny it in writing, and I'll put it in a report with
citations."

"That's her," said Fabiola, to the table.

"That's her," said Hana.

"Play it," said Fabiola. "Play the whole thing, I don't want it in bits, I've thought about
this for four years and I've decided I'm not doing it in bits."

---

She is funny. That is the first thing, and it is the thing that the back room was not ready
for, and it is the reason it is nine minutes and not nine seconds.

She is funny about the carabiner for a minute and forty seconds.

It is a carabiner that has gate-loaded, which means a gate that has to be turned to get it
on, and it is the correct kind for a piece of kit and it is the wrong kind for somebody who
has already got eleven of them on a belt and is holding two spares in one hand.

"Right, look. Look at this. Look at *this*."

"Yes."

"It's a lovely bit of kit. It's a beautiful bit of kit. Somebody in a factory has spent money
making that gate go round, and I want you to appreciate that, because I have spent forty
minutes in a shop being told by a man of about nineteen that gate-loading is a bad idea."

"Was it a bad idea?"

"It is a *gorgeous* idea and it is completely wrong for me and I have just lost it into a
crevice and I am not going down after it." A sound of a car going over something. "That's
loose."

"Which is loose."

"Which is — Sol, don't say it."

"Which is loose *if you don't look at it*, which is what it was for, and I want to be extremely
clear with the person watching this in about fifteen years that the clip was on the *right*
gate and I have never in my life looked at a gate."

"That's a thing to say on a tape."

"It's a thing to say on a tape," said Solveig Havelock. "This is a *tape*. Somebody will watch
this. That's what a tape is for. It's not for the footage, it's for the — the bits where you
sound like a person."

There is a pause of about two seconds, and the picture wobbles, and she says:

"Sorry. That's me being a person. It happens four times a day and it's a **disaster**."

---

The middle four minutes are the pitch, and they are good work, and everyone in that room
watched them as work.

That is the part that undoes it. She is at a pitch, in a system, doing the thing she was
extremely good at, and she narrates it — not for a camera, for a phone on a dashboard in a car
that is parked at the top of a field, in daylight, to Petar Marsk, who has asked her to say
what is happening because he is not going down and has said so honestly.

"Right, the pitch. I'm at the pitch. It's fine, it's a baby, it's about four metres, and
I'm going to say something and I want you to write it down because you write things down and
I never do."

"Go on."

"It's the top. That's the thing. Everybody always goes straight past it and the pitch is the
*best* bit, and the reason everybody goes past it is that you're all in a hurry to get to
something deeper, and the shallows are where you learn the system." A sound of kit. "That's
the old one. That's the 'ninety-nine. That's the one I put in in — hang on—"

"—nineteen ninety-eight, I'm not sure—"

"'Ninety-eight. It was hanging in a garage in a town with a name like Warmington, and it cost
me four pounds, and it has been on every pitch in this county since and it has saved my life
once and I've never worked out how, and if you take it off me I'll have to buy another one and
it'll be a modern one and it'll be *fine*."

---

At six minutes eleven seconds Hana Vogel's face comes into frame.

It is on a laptop in a back room in front of six people, and it is about a second and a half
long, and it is in shot because the phone was on a dashboard and a young woman in a helmet
opened the door to shout something at Petar, and it happens.

Hana is twenty-five. She is holding a radio. She is laughing at whatever it is, and her face
is open, and she looks directly into the lens for about a second, and then she says:

"—no, *no*, I know, I know, I'll get it—"

and the door shuts and the picture is a car roof again.

Nobody in the room said anything.

Hana Vogel sat on a chair in a back room of a shop with a leg that was not hers across the
room and looked at the wall opposite, and about four seconds later said:

"That's not the face I've been not-thinking about."

"No," said Fabiola, without looking at her. "No, I don't suppose it is."

---

The last two minutes are the fourth chamber.

She goes past the second pitch at 7:38 into the tape and she is still talking, and the thing
she says at 8:04 is the reason there is a chapter at all.

"—and the plate's *wrong*, and I want to say that out loud for the record, because in about
thirty years somebody's going to find this and write a report about it and I want it already
on the tape."

"What does it say?"

"It says two hours and the water's been up about four, so it's *low*, and it's not low
because it's me, it's low because it was me in a wet jacket in September and the paint's
behaved. It goes off by one after rain and I cannot find out why and I am *not* going to
guess about it in a document, I did that once in 'ninety-four and I had to take it out again
and I don't remember why and that's a terrible thing to have done to a document."

Somebody turned a light off in the room. Nobody had.

"Pet."

"Go on."

"Two hours on the plate, four hours in the water. That's a small thing. That's the *smallest*
thing I've ever seen and I've been looking at it for nine months and I want to say it out
loud, because it's the sort of thing you'd only say on a tape, and because in about thirty
years somebody is going to go and look at that wall and I would like there to be a voice in
the room when they do it."

"Voice for what?"

"Voice for the *wall*," said Solveig Havelock, from a phone on a dashboard, from two hundred
and two metres, on the eleventh of September, in 2019. "The wall's not a hazard. The wall's
where the water tells you. Everybody looks at the ceiling. Look at the wall."

---

The tape ends at nine minutes.

The picture holds on a car roof for about four seconds and then goes to black, and there is
eleven seconds of a car interior, and a hand, and then nothing.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Solveig Havelock said, from a speaker in a back room, in a voice that had been a voice in
a room for four years and was now a voice in a room, "Voice for the wall," which was a thing
she had said once and had recorded on a phone in a car and had put in a box, and had said it
in a car because it was a thing you said on a tape.

That is the whole tragedy and it is the whole of the book: **she was funny.** That is the
thing. She is not atmospheric; she is not a mood, and she is not a tragedy, and she is not a
gap in somebody's life. She is a woman who lost a carabiner in a crevice and was rude about
a nineteen-year-old in a shop and got a story wrong on a tape in 1994 and did not want to
guess about it in a document, and who is nine minutes long, and who was *specific*.

And that is what makes it cost.

Ottoline Marrow was crying, which she discovered afterwards had been happening for about
four minutes and which she had been aware of for the last one of them, and which she did not
apologise for and which nobody in the room acknowledged, and which was the correct handling
of every element of it.

Hana Vogel said, into a table in a back room: "She knew about the plate."

"Everybody knew about the plate," said Tove Marsk.

"No. She knew the plate was *wrong* and she'd been looking at it for nine months and she said
it on a *tape*, in a *car*, to Petar, in 2019." Hana put her hands flat on the table, which
was a thing that everybody at that table did and none of them had taught each other. "I've
been reading a four-page report for four years about a woman who went too many times. I did
not know that she was the one who *found* the thing. In 2019. In a car."

Fabiola Renn stood up, and put her cup down on the shelf where she had put the other five, and
went to the door, and stopped, and did not turn round.

"Right," she said. "That's my fault, then."

"Fab—"

"No, it is. I've had that laptop in that cupboard since the Thursday after, and there was a
tape in the same box, and they were the same box, and one of them was a report and one of
them was a *voice*, and I put them both in a cupboard and shut it, and I have done that for
four years, and I have been very careful and very busy and I have *not watched the woman*."
She was still facing the door. "I have watched the number. Forty-one times, or forty, or
whatever it is. I have read the numbers until I could see them in the dark. And I have not
once, in four years, turned on nine minutes of the best person I have ever worked with,
because I did it in a car, and I did it badly, and it was a *phone*."

"No," said Ines Varga. "You didn't open the box because the box was next to the report and
the report was a professional matter and the video was—"

"Was what?"

"Was the other thing," said Ines. "It was the thing neither of us was ever going to open, and
you were managing a shop and I was selling cylinders, and we were both extremely good at
managing it, and I don't think either of us did that on purpose."

---

Tove Marsk stood up, on a leg, at nine o'clock in the evening on the second of December, in a
back room in a shop.

"Right," she said. "I need to say something and I need about ninety seconds and I'd like to
say it standing up because I'm not going to be here long."

"Go on."

"I've read that four-page report forty-one times and I've been trying to get at the water in
that system out of it for two years, and I have never once, in two years, wondered about the
woman." Tove was gripping the back of a chair. "I've been treating her as a *fact*. I've been
using her, professionally, for two years, as a reason to want an answer, and I have never once
thought about what the answer's for."

"That's not—"

"It is. I want you to know that I know it is." Tove looked round the room. "There were six of
us in a car park on the fifteenth of March and one of us was talking about a man who bought a
waist belt. There was a woman in this shop on the first of November who was cleaning a rack
that hadn't been cleaned in six years. There was a technician on a hillside for four years
being the last voice for a woman who wouldn't take a call. And there was a nine-minute tape
in a box next to a report and nobody opened the box, because we were all *working*."

"It's not a failure," said Marisol Trent, from her chair against the wall. "It's a Thursday."

"It's a Thursday," Tove agreed, "and I want it written down, and somebody's to write it
down, and I don't much mind who."

Marisol Trent opened her notebook.

"Done," she said.

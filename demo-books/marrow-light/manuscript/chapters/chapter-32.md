# Chapter Thirty-Two — Read

The twenty-eighth of November was clear, which was the first clear day since the nineteenth
and which nobody trusted.

Hana Vogel got to the layby at half past eight with a flask and four cups on the lid and got
out of the van and stood in a car park on a hillside above a shut gate and listened to the
morning, which is what she had done every day for twenty-two years and which is not mysticism
and is not a gift and is about forty years of being outdoors accumulating a database.

"There's a haar off the shoulder," she said. "Four, five kilometres. It'll be here by eleven
and it'll be nothing. Half an hour of light drizzle, maybe."

"And the catchment?"

"That's the thing I can't hear and the thing I have to ask about." She got the radio out. "Vega, Hana. Vell catchment. Overnight. Give me the overnight."

There was about four seconds of hiss and then a voice that was not a person she knew, and
which read a number out in a completely flat professional way.

It was 4.2 millimetres, it had fallen between two and four, and there was nothing in the
system.

Hana wrote it on the back of her hand.

"Copy," she said. "Vega, copy. Thanks."

"What's the catchment saying?"

"It's saying there's been nothing since the twenty-first and there's nothing forecast until
Monday." She put the radio down on the bonnet of the van and turned round. "So. That's the
water. And I'll say the rest of it, and I'd like everyone to hear it, because it's the last
time I'll be able to say it with anybody in a car park."

---

Nineteen of them were standing in the layby at nine in the morning on the twenty-eighth of
November, which was too many, and the plan said three on the surface and four on the certificate
and one of those four had gone to the shop, and the film crew was two, and Petar was in a van,
and Marisol Trent was at the top of the field at eight forty because she had a right to be
there and had used it, and had a flask, and had said nothing at all to anybody for two hours.

"Right," said Hana. "Here's the read."

The layby went quiet in the way laybys do, which is a car park going from eleven people to
one.

"Twelve. Twelve hours since the last significant rise, which was at about nine o'clock
yesterday evening, and it was eleven millimetres, and the catchment took about an hour and a
half to answer, and the answer took it to four metres of depth at the second pitch, and it's
been at four metres since." She was reading off the back of her own hand. "There is nothing
forecast before Monday. Vega's given us Monday morning and that's a hard number and I'd like
everybody to write it down, because in this county people have a habit of forgetting what a
met office said and then being surprised by it."

"Twelve," said Tove.

"Twelve. Which is the correct number, which is the number on the plate, and I checked the plate
at nine o'clock this morning and it says twelve and it *means* twelve and it has meant twelve
since the twenty-first." Hana put her hand down. "Now I'm going to say the other thing, and I
want to be very careful how I say it, because I've been sitting with it for eight days and I
want it said once and said properly and then I want it to be over.

"For four years I've been not-taking a call from a woman, and I have not been able to give the
reason, and the reason was that in 2022 I read a line in a third edition that said *unstable,
avoid* and I said nothing about it, and the distinction I didn't make — that *avoid* and *do not
enter* are not the same — is a distinction I had four years to make and didn't.

"This morning, at nine o'clock, on a field above a shut gate, with the number in front of me,
I am going to say it out loud, and it is the reason I read it aloud and it is the reason we
have all been here for eight days. The plate is correct. The plate has been correct. Nobody
has painted that plate since the twenty-first of November and nobody needed to, and every
person standing in this car park knows it, and it is the only good piece of news any of us
will get this month."

Nobody said anything.

"So," said Hana Vogel. "Twelve. Confirmed twice. And that's the read, and there is no further
read, and I am not going to read it again today, because I am the last voice on this line
and if I say a number every ten minutes then a number stops being a number and starts being
a thing I say, and you go down on what I say rather than on the wall. And the wall is better
than me. The wall has been better than me for two hundred years and nobody ever asked it."

---

She said it once.

That was the design and it was Marisol's design and Marisol had written it into a plan in a
room on the twenty-sixth in a voice that was not gentle: **one read, at the top of the hill,
in daylight, in front of every person on the surface, and no second reading, and nobody
downstairs is told a number on a radio at any point in the descent.**

The reason was in the plan, in Marisol's hand, in one line, and she had written it because
she had thought about it for three days:

*Because a number on a radio is not a reading, it is an instruction, and this county has
already had one of those.*

Nobody argued about it. Ottoline Marrow had argued about the hard stop at the top of the
pitch for about ninety seconds and then had not argued about the read at all, and had said,
"I think that's right, and I want it on the record that I wanted to be on the line."

---

The descent was at ten past ten.

The film started at ten past ten and this was in the schedule and it was on a tripod in the
layby with a rain cover, and the shot was a van, and a field, and a wall eleven metres away,
and it was running.

It is not a shot anybody would keep, and that is worth saying, because the whole of the previous
four months had been built around the idea that the film was going to be about a person
underground, and the film that was actually being made on the morning of the twenty-eighth of
November was a locked-off shot of the outside of a car park in a layby above a shut gate with
a wall in it.

On the wall was a brass plate.

Ottoline had the camera on the wall from ten past ten until two o'clock in the afternoon,
and it was in the schedule, and it was the first thing she had ever put in a schedule that she
had not had to argue for, and she filmed a wall for three hours and fifty minutes and got
four thousand seconds of usable material and used about forty seconds of it.

The forty seconds is in the film. It is the fourth shot. It is a wall with a plate on it and
it is four seconds long and it has a number on it, and the number is legible, and the film
does not say what the number means, and there is no music on it, and it is the only shot in
the whole picture in which nothing at all is happening.

---

Below the pitch, at fifty metres, the line went live.

"Varga to surface. Fifty. Line's live."

"Vega to surface. Fifty. Line's live."

"Surface copies. You're good."

"Going to a hundred."

And for four hours the thing that had been building since the ninth of October — two women
fifty metres apart, one of them unable to be filmed — was a technical arrangement with a
schedule and a checklist and a woman reading a number out loud exactly once.

There was no descent past the second pitch. The cap held and nobody tested it and nobody
wanted to.

At about eleven o'clock Ines Varga, at a hundred and forty metres in the light that comes down
at forty degrees onto a wall that leans in a metre over four metres that nobody had remeasured
since 2011, said, into a radio, to a woman on a hillside she could not see:

"You've got a wall on your camera."

"How do you know I've got a wall on my camera?"

"Because you've been quiet for a bit and then you said *and*," said Ines, "and you only say
*and* when you're describing something you're looking at, and the only thing you've been
looking at for three hours is a wall."

There was a pause of about two seconds on a fifty-metre line.

"The plate's in it," said Ottoline.

"Is it."

"It's the fourth shot. It's four seconds and I might use it and I don't know if I will and
I've been looking at it for three hours." Her voice did not change and something in it had
changed entirely. "You could tell me what the number says. You've been down there. You know
what it says."

"No," said Ines.

"No?"

"I know what it said in September," said Ines. "I don't know what it says now, because I
haven't been in the chamber, because the cap holds, and because if you go past the second
pitch to look at a wall then in eight days somebody will be writing an incident report about
it, and Marisol Trent will be right, and she will be right for the wrong reason, and that is
the worst combination in this trade."

"So you haven't looked."

"No."

"In four years?"

"In four years I have not looked at it on the day I have needed it, and that has cost
somebody something," said Ines, "and today I am not looking at it at all, and that is going to
cost me something too, and I would like you to shoot the wall and not ask me what's on it, and
I would like to do that for free, and I would like it to be the last time either of us asks
the other for something for nothing."

"That's a good rule," said Ottoline.

"It's a rule I invented four years ago on a day I'm not going to talk about, and it has never
once worked," said Ines, "and I would like it to work this time."

They held the line open for another four seconds, which was longer than a professional
procedure allows and which neither of them counted.

# Chapter Twenty-Four — In Two Places

Hana Vogel did the check on the Tuesday, because she did the check on the Tuesday, and at ten
past nine on the Wednesday morning the van was in the second bay with the shelter on the
board, and the shelter was not there.

There was a note.

The note said: *Shelter ordered. 6 wks. No. 4. See you. — F.*

Hana stood and looked at it for a while. Then she did the check, all eleven items, in the
order they were written, and she was slower on item six than usual, and then she got the book
and did the log, and she had four minutes left before she had to leave for a callout in
Kirkmichael that would turn out to be a cow.

She did not start the van. She sat on the bench with the note in her hand.

Four years and one month. There had been a piece of A4 on that wall since 2022 saying
SHELTER — ORDER in somebody else's handwriting, and she had looked at it every Tuesday for
four years, and she had never once written the number on it, and the reason she had never
written the number on it was that she had decided some time in 2023 that if she had not
ordered the shelter by now then the reason she had not ordered it was not money.

Somebody else had now ordered it. Somebody had walked into a shop or made a telephone call and
paid four hundred and something pounds for a bivvack out of their own pocket, and had not
told the station, and had written four words on a bit of paper and left it where it would be
found on a Tuesday morning at ten past nine by a woman who would be in the second bay on her
own.

Hana Vogel had a rule about this and the rule was that she was not going to cry in a station,
and she did not cry in a station, and she sat on a bench in a bay for about ninety seconds
doing absolutely nothing, and then she got up and started the van.

---

She did not go to the Vell. She had no reason to go to the Vell; the Vell was thirty-one
kilometres away and there was no callout and no meeting.

She went to the shop.

She did not decide to go to the shop. She was aware, at about the eleven-minute mark of a
forty-minute drive, that she had made a decision to go to the shop, and she was aware that the
decision had been made in a station by a woman reading a note, and she was aware that a
rescue technician who drove thirty-one kilometres in a van to deliver a piece of paper would
be, in the words of a film student she had met four times, a person who did not know the
difference between a professional thing and a personal one.

She parked outside and sat in the van for four minutes, which was longer than she had planned,
and then she got out and went in.

"Fabiola."

"Hana." Fabiola did not turn round. "You've come in the van, which means you came on purpose,
and the last time you came on purpose you were here about a gas bottle, so if you're here about
a gas bottle the answer's in the cupboard."

"I'm not here about a gas bottle."

"Then you're here to say something and I'm not going to help you say it, and I want that on
the record at the beginning so that you can't come back later and say I made it difficult."

Hana put a piece of paper on the counter. It was creased from a book and it had a date on it
in the top corner in a hand that Hana Vogel had known since 2011.

"What's this?"

"That's a delivery note," said Fabiola.

"From the twenty-second of March."

"Yes."

"For two cylinders and a rack."

"Two cylinders and a rack," said Fabiola, "and a helmet, and a carabiner, and about four
pounds of fixings, and it all went on one sheet because the shop's sheet's always been one
sheet since 2011 and I will not be told otherwise by anybody, including my accountant, and he
has tried."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because you drove here," said Fabiola.

Hana looked at the paper. It was a delivery note and it was entirely ordinary and it was
dated the twenty-second of March 2022 and it was the twenty-second of March and there was a
box in the bottom right corner for the *second signature* and it was empty.

"There's no second signature."

"No."

"Everything else is signed."

"Everything else is signed by whoever took it, and there was only one person, because she was
buying it for herself and she wasn't on a callout and she wasn't working, so there was nobody
to countersign." Fabiola turned round. It was the first time she had turned round. "The box
is for the second person. When I did the sheets in 2011 I put that box in because you do it
for a callout, two people, one of them leaves it. And I have never once, in nineteen years,
had a reason to look at that box in a delivery note for kit bought by a woman on a Tuesday
afternoon for herself."

Hana Vogel did not pick the paper up again.

"You've had this," she said.

"I've had the sheet in a folder in the office since March. Everyone's sheets go in the folder
for a year and then they get binned, and it wasn't binned, because—" Fabiola stopped, and
started again, and it was the hardest eleven words of her life and she had been saying hard
things for forty-one years. "Because it was the eleventh one."

The shop was very quiet.

"Hana."

"Fabiola—"

"Eleven," said Fabiola, "and I have never said that to anybody, and there is a tape in the
back room of nine minutes of a woman telling a joke about a carabiner in a cave in 2019, and
I have not watched it since the Thursday after, and I am sixty-one years old and I have run
this shop for nineteen years with the best person I ever worked with and I *counted*."

She put her hand flat on the counter, on the bone-coloured counter, and it was not steady
and it was not trying to be.

"And the reason I counted," said Fabiola, "is that she counted. Solveig counted everything. She
counted the descents — she was the one who started counting, in 2019, because she was doing
the numbers for a paper and she said the numbers were the only honest thing about the Vell.
And I used to say *you can't count that*, and she'd say *I can, it's the only way anybody
knows a place*, and then she'd go down, and she'd come back, and she'd say the number, and
she'd be pleased with herself about it. Eleven. And I never once said it out loud to another
human being until about four seconds ago."

Hana Vogel put both hands on the counter.

She had come here to say something herself. She had driven thirty-one kilometres with
something in her chest about a shelter and a piece of paper and four years of not taking a
telephone call, and she had it ready, and it was gone, and what was left was a woman of
sixty-one standing in her shop saying a number out loud for the first time in four years.

"Fab."

"I know what you're about to say."

"I was going to say thank you for telling me."

"Don't," said Fabiola. "Don't thank me for telling you, because I didn't tell you for you. I
told you because you drove here, and because you've been not-taking that woman's telephone
calls for four years, and because I have watched you not do it from behind a counter and I
have thought about it every day since March and I could not think of one single other way to
open this."

She put her hands in the pockets of a cardigan.

"There's a second thing," she said, "and I've been not-saying it since March, and I'm going to
say it now while I've got my hands full, and then I'm going to go and stock the back room and
I'm not going to talk about it again."

"Okay."

"Solveig knew." Fabiola said it to the middle of the room. "That's the second thing. She knew
that Ines was going too often. She said it to me in November, in this shop, on a Saturday,
and she said *she's counting the descents like they're entries in a book and they aren't, and
somebody's going to read that book*, and I said *nobody's going to read a number in a cave*,
and she said—"

Fabiola stopped, and got a cloth out of her apron, and wiped a counter that was already
clean.

"She said *you don't read a number, you read the person who wrote it*, and I said *who reads
you*, and she said *nobody, that's the point*." Fabiola folded the cloth. "And then she went
down in December, and then January, and then March, and on the twenty-second of March a woman
came into my shop and bought two cylinders and a rack with her own money and her own hands and
I said *that's a lot for a survey* and she said *it's for a survey*, and I let her say it
twice."

The rain came on the window. It came on the window the way it did, and neither of them
looked at it.

"I've been carrying that since March," said Fabiola, "and so has she, and neither of us has
said one word about it, and you're here and you're the only person in this county she told."

"I didn't know her."

"No," said Fabiola. "But she talked about you. That was the thing. In November she talked
about you for about an hour and you were in the back and I thought she was just going on.
She wasn't going on. She was making a list out loud, because that's what she did, because she
counted, and I have had four years to work out that the list was about a woman who was going
to go back one too many times and who would need somebody standing on a hill when she did."

Hana Vogel stood at a counter in a shop in the rain with a piece of paper in front of her
that had an empty box in the corner of it, and understood that the thing she had driven
thirty-one kilometres to say had been said already, in this room, months ago, to a woman who
was upstairs, by a dead woman, over a carabiner.

"I have to tell her," said Hana.

"No," said Fabiola. "You don't. And I'll tell you why you don't, and it's not loyalty, love,
it's arithmetic."

"Fabiola—"

"In November, in this shop, I said *nobody's going to read a number in a cave*, and she said
*you don't read a number, you read the person who wrote it*." Fabiola's hands had come out
of her pockets and were flat on the counter again. "The number's on the sheet. The sheet's in
a folder. The folder's behind the till, and any person who's ever worked in this trade in this
county could come in here and ask me for the March sheets and I'd have to hand them over,
because I've got a legal duty and a shop and forty-one years, and there's nothing on this earth
that stops me.

"But there's nothing on this earth, either, that makes me tell a woman in a shop that a woman
in a shop in November said *somebody's going to read that book*." Fabiola looked at Hana
Vogel, who was a rescue technician and who had reached eleven minutes in 2022 and who had been
four years waiting to be the last voice somebody heard. "I have been the last voice in this
story for four years and I have not said a word, and if I say it now it is because a rescue
technician came thirty-one kilometres in a van, and I would like you to go back to the station
and think about whether that's the standard you want."

Hana did not answer for a moment.

"Say the sentence again," she said. "The one she said. In November."

"*You don't read a number, you read the person who wrote it.*"

"That's what I'd have said," said Hana Vogel. "That's exactly what I'd have said, and I've
been sitting on a hill thinking it, and I have never once said it out loud to anybody, and
that's — " She stopped, and started again. "That's the first time in four years I've heard
somebody else say it, and I want you to know that it is a genuinely unbearable experience and
that I'm going to need to go and sit in my van for a minute."

"You do that," said Fabiola.

At the door, Hana stopped.

"The shelter."

"Yes."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a gift," said Fabiola, and did not turn round, and was not going to, and had a
cloth in her hand that did not need wiping. "It's on the invoice. It's in the folder. It's
the twelfth."

Hana Vogel went out into the rain and got into a van in a layby and sat for about four
minutes with her hands on the wheel, and did not start it, and then started it, and drove
back to a station above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings, and did the rest of the log,
and wrote:

*1104. V., shop. Fab R. Present for: the sheet.*

And then sat with the pen on the line for a while, and then added, in brackets, which she had
never done in eight years of logs:

*(not a professional entry. eleven.)*

She put the book back on the shelf, and did not read it again, and the note stayed on the
board where it was, and item six on the check sheet remained empty and was not going to be
filled in for six weeks, and would not be filled in at all, and the shelter would come, and
would be number four, and would be in the second bay, and would be the twelfth thing.

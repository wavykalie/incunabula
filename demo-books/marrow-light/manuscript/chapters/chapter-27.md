# Chapter Twenty-Seven — Good Plan

The plan was good. That is the thing that has to be understood about what happened on the
nineteenth of November, and it is the thing that nobody in this book says afterwards, and it
is the thing that made it what it was.

Every element of it was correct. Every element had been assembled by a competent person, at
a different time, for a different reason, and each element was the best available answer to
the question that person was answering, and not one of them had ever been in the same room.

That is not a failure of coordination; that is what coordination is *for*, and coordination
fixes the opposite problem — it fixes the plan that has a hole in it, the plan where nobody
thought about the second pitch, the plan that turns up with four people and a rope for
three. Coordination is a repair technology. It is very good at repair. It is not a thing that
looks at a plan which is entirely made of correct decisions and asks what the correct
decisions add up to.

The film base was in the layby. That was decided in September, on the advice of a rescue
technician at ten past nine on a Tuesday night, and it was right: the layby got a signal
pointed at a hill and the headland did not.

The line was fifty metres of wool, and there was a second fifty metres on the kit list, and
that was right too, because you never plan one.

The descent was capped at the second pitch by a supervisor who had no authority to cap it and
had imposed it anyway and had written down that she was imposing it. That was right.

The surface party was three: Hana Vogel on the surface end, Tove Marsk on the certificate as
the second, and Fabiola Renn on the certificate as the third, which had been agreed on the
sixth of November at about eleven o'clock in the morning, on the telephone, in nine words,
after a condition had been met.

Tove went first. That had been settled on the ninth of October, on a hill, in the rain, as a
scheduling argument, and nobody had revisited it, and everybody assumed it was still
scheduling.

Hana had the gauge. That had been settled on the sixth, in a car park outside a shop, when
Hana Vogel had said, *I read the gauge and I read it aloud and if anybody's got a different
number they say so now, at the top of the hill, in daylight, with everybody listening*, and
Ines Varga had said, *If the number's wrong we go in anyway on the four-hour rule*, and
Hana had said, *No*, and Ines had said, *It's the rule I wrote*, and Hana had said, *I know
whose rule it is, and the rule is right, and the rule is the reason I have been not-taking
your telephone calls, and I'd like to stop now.*

Ines had said, "Agreed," and had not said anything else, and had not looked at her.

The film was on the certificate as a non-participant with a hard stop at the top of the
pitch, which was clause 4.2, which had been read by neither of them in a stockroom in
September, and which was about material neither party owned, and which had turned out in
October to be the correct clause for a completely different reason, which was that Ottoline
Marrow had no business on a working line at all and everybody knew it and the paperwork was
where you put that.

Otto was on the surface and not on the line.

The van went out at ten past seven.

---

It was the best-run day any of them had ever been part of, and they all said so, afterwards,
and none of them said it at the time.

Petar drove. That was not in the plan — the plan said the base was the layby and the layby was
where Hana worked from, and the getting there was not a thing anybody had assigned — and
Petar Marsk turned up at ten to seven in a van with the cages in it because Tove had
telephoned him on the Monday, and he had said *I'd have come anyway* in a way that meant he
would not have, and he had come anyway, and he had brought the good flask and a spare and
enough cups for eight, and he had brought his own flask as well, which nobody had asked for
and which turned out to matter.

The cages went in the back. The survey bags went in the back. The four of them went in the
front and the fifth person went in the back with the equipment and the flask, and there were
four in the van, and there should have been five, and nobody noticed for about nine miles.

They noticed at the second bridge, where Petar got out to open the gate as he always did, and
stood there with it open, waiting for a person who was not there.

Nobody said anything.

Petar shut the gate and got back in and drove for about half a mile and then said, "Fab's not
in this van."

"We'll go back for her."

"Not for a minute," said Tove, looking at her watch, which she had not worn underground
since 2011 and which she had put on that morning for the first time in fifteen years. "Go
back now, we lose eleven minutes at the far end, and the far end is where the day is. The
question is whether the day needs her."

"It's her certificate," said Hana, from the back, in a voice that had gone flat.

"It's her certificate and she's a pair of hands and I've been the second on a line for
sixteen years and I don't need a third pair of hands, I need three people who know where
everybody is." Tove did not turn round. "That's the actual answer and it's the boring one.
Three is not a number of hands, it's a number of *knowers*. If I've got two people above the
pitch and I know what they're doing, I have a surface party. If I've got three and I don't
know what the third is doing, I've got a liability with a certificate."

"She's in her car," said Petar.

"She's in her car and she's had a telephone and she's been asked to be there," said Tove,
"and she's a sixty-one-year-old woman who sells equipment to people who go into holes and
she has never been on a surface party in her life, and I'll tell you exactly what happened,
which is that she got a call about a delivery."

Nobody said anything for about a mile.

"Ring her," said Ines.

"I'll ring her," said Tove, and did not ring her, and said, in about ten minutes, in a layby
at the far end, with the van on the grass and the rain starting, "She's coming. She was at the
shop. She's about forty minutes behind us and she's coming, and she was furious, and she's
coming anyway, and that's the second of the two things I got wrong today and I'm telling you
both now while it's early."

"What's the first thing?"

"The rain," said Tove.

---

The rain was not rain; that was what everyone meant afterwards and nobody said at the time.

The forecast had given twelve millimetres and the twelve millimetres came at ten o'clock in
about nine minutes and it did not stop, and at eleven the far end of the valley was under
water in a car park that had a surface-drainage design for a rainfall event that happened
about once every eight years, and Petar's van had the water coming up under the sills.

That is not a flood; that is a car park doing the thing a car park does. Nobody was in danger
and nobody was a hero about it and the correct professional action was to move four hundred
metres up the track to the grass above the second bridge, which is what they did, and which
took twenty-six minutes, and which cost the day its window.

The window was the thing.

The plan said: descend by eleven, be at the second pitch by half past one, be on the surface
by four. The plan was sound. The plan had been written by a woman with a contract and a
stopwatch and eleven years of doing this, and the plan was right, and at half past twelve
they were at the second pitch with four and a half hours of light and eighty-five minutes of
usable weather in front of them and everybody knew it.

"Vega says the catchment's already answering," said Hana, into the radio, from a field above
a shut gate, at four minutes past one. "Up nineteen millimetres since eleven. It's not
stopping."

"It's not stopping because it's raining."

"It's not stopping *now*," said Hana. "Varga, copy that. The catchment responds to the karst
in about two hours and that's not a guess, that's Tuesday, that's how it works. So the rain
stops at about one and the water arrives at about three and it's going to be a big one and
you will have eighty metres of line on a hillside in the dark with a rise coming."

There was a pause on the radio.

"That's my job," said Ines.

"That's your job and I'm telling you the number," said Hana, and her voice did not change
register, which was the thing that it was for, and which is the thing that Tove Marsk had
told Ines Varga in 2017 that she was good at, and which was in a report read out in a room in
2022 in a paragraph about a surface party of three.

"Confirm the reading," said Ines.

"Confirming."

"Read it aloud."

"I read it aloud," said Hana. "Twenty-two. Twenty-two hours since last rise, and the rise
was on the fourth, and it is *not* my number, Varga, and I have been the one reading it for
eleven years, and I am telling you on a radio in daylight that twenty-two is not a number I
can sign, and the plate on that wall has said twenty-two since the fourth of November, and
nobody's been down here since the fourth of November."

The radio did what a radio does.

Tove Marsk, on a field above a shut gate, eleven hundred metres from a hole and fifty metres
of wool from a woman, said: "Tove to surface. Confirming. Copy that."

"What's your confirmation mean?"

"It means I heard it and it's mine as well as yours," said Tove, "and I want to say on the
record, to a woman who can hear me, that two people have just said out loud on an open
frequency that the plate in that system has not been touched since the fourth of November,
and that neither of us has been down there since the fourth of November, and that on the
ninth of October somebody went down and did a station and did not mention it."

There was a silence on the frequency that was not radio discipline and was not procedure.

Then Ines Varga said, at fifty metres down and eighty metres from the second pitch, in a
system she knew better than she knew her own kitchen:

"Copy," she said. "Both. Logged."

"Confirm the number and the time," said Tove, because that was what you did.

"Twenty-two. Fourteen sixteen. I have it."

The plan had been good. The plan had eleven elements in it and every one of them had been
made by a competent person and nine of them were right, and the two that were wrong had been
made by the best person there and had been correct on the day she made them, and the reason
they were wrong was that one of them had assumed the plate was maintained.

She had maintained it herself, and she had not been down since the fourth of November, and
there was a number on a wall that said twenty-two and had said twenty-two for eleven days
because nobody had gone in and painted it, and the last time anybody had painted it she had
been at a hundred and sixty metres describing a wall to a woman in a layby because she had
been asked what water sounded like.

"Seventy minutes," said Tove, on the frequency. "Seventy minutes on the plan, and then we're
coming up, whatever we're doing, and I'm not saying it to be hard, I'm saying it because I'm
the second and the second says when the line comes back up. Confirm."

"Confirmed," said Ines.

"Surfaces to surface. Radio check every ten. Go."

And they went on, and they did it well, and the plan held, and at six minutes past two Hana
Vogel said, "Varga to surface," in a voice that did not change register at all, and which said
something else entirely.

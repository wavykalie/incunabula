# Chapter Twenty-Nine — Unfinished

They had it in a car on the Sunday.

That was the arrangement and it was the correct arrangement and all four of them had
fought for it a little, because a car is a bad room and a car is also the only place in
which a conversation of that kind can be had in this county without a committee.

Petar drove. He had a flask and four cups on the lid and he drove to a layby above the
second bridge, which was not the working layby, which was a different layby, eleven minutes
away, that nobody had any professional reason to be in, and which Hana Vogel had identified
on the second of November for exactly this purpose and had not mentioned to anybody.

"You can't have a car park at four people," she had said. "You need a car park at four people
who can't hear each other over an engine."

There were four of them and there was a fifth person, and the fifth person was in the layby
at the working layby with a camera on a tripod in a rain cover, and was not part of this, and
had been extremely careful about not being part of this, and had in fact not turned the
camera on at all.

---

The thing that had to be said was said at about twenty past eleven.

Tove Marsk had been working up to it since the Thursday, in the way she worked up to
everything, which was by writing it down and then crossing it out, and she had got it to a
state on the Saturday night where she was able to read the first four lines of it aloud to
herself in a kitchen, and the first four lines were fine, and the fifth line was the one she
could not get to.

They were parked. Petar had the engine off. The rain was doing what it had been doing for a
week and it was doing it on a roof in a layby and it was the loudest thing in the car.

"I want to say a thing," said Tove, "and I want to say it once, and I want to say it to you
and not to Hana and not in a room with a recorder, and I'm going to say it badly and I want
you to let me get to the end of it before you say anything, because I've started about nine
times this week and I've got it further each time and I'd like to get all the way round."

"Go on," said Ines.

"You shouldn't be on the rope."

Hana, in the front passenger seat, said nothing and did not move.

"Not on the nineteenth," said Tove. "Not on any of it. Not in the Vell at all. Not as a
consultant, not as a body on the end of somebody's line, not for a day, not for four hours,
not to be nice to a film student who's going to have a hole in her film if you don't."

"That's the association's position, and it's in every report since 2011—"

"It's not, actually." Tove turned round. "That's a thing people say and it's a good
rhetorical position and it's completely wrong, and I want to take it off you before you use
it, because you've been hiding behind it since March and it's not load-bearing."

"Then say the actual thing."

And Tove said: "I don't have the standing to say this, and I'm going to say it anyway, and I
want you to hear the first half of that sentence properly, because the second half is the
one I'd like you to remember."

---

"Say it slowly," said Ines. "The standing part."

"The standing part." Tove put her hands on her knees. "I hold a contract. I hold the
association's third edition. I have a professional opinion about the closure of the section
and it is a good one and it's in a report. That is the entirety of my authority. I cannot
stand in a room and say *you are not competent*, because I have watched you be more competent
than me on the four occasions I have watched you be competent, and one of those was in 2017 in
a system nobody had a plan for, and I have never told anybody about the 2017 and I am not
going to start.

"What I can say — the only thing I have a standing to say, and the only thing I've ever had
standing to say about any of this — is this.

"I have been the second on a line in this county for sixteen years. I have brought up
four hundred metres of rope in weather and in the dark and on a bad idea. I have made the
decision to come up at about ninety seconds' notice eleven times, and I have been wrong
about it twice, and the two times I was wrong are the reason I know what the ninety seconds
costs.

"And on the nineteenth of November, when the number on the plate was wrong, the person who
brought the line up at six minutes past two was Hana. Not me. Me, I would have given her
twenty minutes. I'm not being brave about it. I am simply not the person who does that, and
I have known that since about 2014 and I have been extremely careful about it ever since.

"Which means that in this system, on a day, the person who decides when the line comes up is
a rescue technician with eleven years in who is not in the system and cannot see anything, and
who is making that decision off a radio and a number she is reading off a wall, and if she's
four minutes late then I am four minutes late and you are —"

She stopped.

"Go on," said Ines quietly.

"That's where I can't get to," said Tove.

"Why not?"

"Because what's at the end of that sentence is that you'd be on a line with a person who
cannot get to you," said Tove Marsk, "and I am not the person who is qualified to say that to
you, and I have just spent four minutes saying everything I am qualified to say and I cannot
get the rest of it, and I have tried nine times, and it is not that I don't know the words,
it's that I'm not — "

She stopped again and put her hand flat on the seat in front of her.

"It's not competence," she said. "I want to be very clear that that's not what I'm circling.
You're better than me. If I said that to a committee I'd be laughed out of a room and I'd
deserve it. It's that you are the best thing I have ever worked next to and I do not want to
be on a rope with you on a bad day, and I have not got one single professional word for that
and I have been trying to find one since Thursday."

Hana Vogel, in the front passenger seat of a van on a Sunday afternoon in the rain, said
nothing at all, and understood that she was in the presence of two professionals at the exact
moment when the professional language ran out, and that she was not going to be useful for
the rest of it.

"The fourth edition's going to the committee on the twenty-seventh," said Ines.

"Yes."

"And you're submitting it."

"I am." Tove did not turn round. "Which is the fifth line, and I got round to it, and it's
the reason I started, and it's not the reason I'll remember. On the twenty-seventh there will
be a schedule, and my name will be on it, and there will be a person from the office on a
telephone, and at some point between now and then the schedule is going to be the reason."

The rain on the roof of the van.

"That's a filthy thing to say to somebody."

"Yes," said Tove. "It is a filthy thing to say, and I have said it, and I'd like to be clear
that I said it as a *scheduling* argument, and that it is a scheduling argument, and that I
would put it in a report in exactly those words if you asked me to write it down."

---

Ines Varga sat in the back of a van in a layby above a second bridge and looked at the back
of a head for about eleven seconds.

Then she said, "What would you do."

Tove did not move.

"Say it again."

"You've told me I'm the best you've worked with and you can't get to the end of a sentence
and the reason you're saying all this is the twenty-seventh." Ines had not raised her voice
once in the conversation and did not now. "So what would you do. If it were your line. What
would you actually do."

Tove Marsk thought about it, properly, for about four seconds, and gave a professional
answer, and it was the correct answer, and it was given to the only person in the county who
could have used it.

"I'd go and stand at the top of the pitch at four in the morning on the seventeenth and I'd
make sure that whoever's coming up that line has read the water," she said, "and that
whoever's on the surface has a number they trust, and that the two of them have spoken to
each other, and then I'd go home and I'd let the schedule do whatever the schedule did,
because the schedule's not a moral instrument, it's a piece of paper with dates on it, and
the only thing worse than a bad schedule is a good one that nobody argues with."

"That's not a safety instruction. That's a paragraph."

"It's *both*," said Tove. "I know. I've been a person who writes paragraphs for twenty years.
I've never once been a person who gets heard, and the two are the same skill and I've been
paid for one of them."

---

Petar Marsk said, "Are we going to be a while, then," to the windscreen, mildly, and started
the engine, and pulled out, and drove eleven minutes to a layby above a shut gate, and stopped
again, and turned the engine off.

Nobody had told him to. He had simply assessed, in the way of a man who has driven survey
parties for nineteen years, that four people who needed another eleven minutes had about four
minutes' worth of weather in them and would get more out of a second stop than out of a cup
of tea, and had gone to the other layby, which he knew about, because everybody who drove in
this county knew about both laybys and neither of them was in anybody's paperwork.

Nobody thanked him for it, because he was in the front and they were all in the back, and
because he would not have wanted it.

When they came out of the second layby, Hana Vogel said, to nobody in particular and to
Ines Varga in particular, "Twenty-seven's a Friday. I'm on the callout rota that weekend. I
can't be on a surface party on the twenty-seventh and I couldn't be on the twenty-ninth
either."

"You don't have to be."

"I know I don't have to be. I'm telling you the rota so that if you were counting on me for
the twenty-seventh you weren't, and I know you weren't, and I'm telling you anyway." Hana
looked at the side window. "I'll be at the shop on the twenty-eighth. If you want me there
I'll be there and I won't ask you anything."

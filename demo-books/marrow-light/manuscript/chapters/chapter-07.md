# Chapter Seven — The Professional

The coffee was terrible and Tove Marsk made it anyway, which was the first thing Ottoline
Marrow decided about her, and it was not a thing she was going to say out loud for at least
another hour.

"You'll have to take it as it is," said Tove. "The machine's a piece of scrap and I bought
it in 2014 out of sentiment. If I get a new one I'll be here til three and I've got a
station to set."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. Don't drink it for appearances, that's worse than not having any."

So Ottoline drank it, and it was worse than the shop's, and she had not been to the shop's
except once.

The room was a room with a survey report on the table and eleven other things that were not
a survey report, and the survey report was in the middle, face up, and Tove had put it there
in the first ninety seconds without saying anything about it, and it was still there an
hour later, and neither of them had touched it.

Tove Marsk was thirty-four and had a way of sitting that made a chair look temporary. She
had a job, which was the current Vell contract, and the contract was worth more than the
last three of Ottoline's life had cost, and she had been doing it for five years, and she
had got the funding on the fourth attempt, and the fourth attempt had been a two-page
document that a woman in an office had read and signed off on a Tuesday.

"How much of the film is already shot?"

"None."

"Right."

"It's a — the access is the first week of October. I've got a schedule, and the schedule's
built around weather calls, so I've got maybe forty percent chance of getting what I need on
any given day, which is why I need to be—"

"You need to be underground on a day when the sky's doing what you hope it's doing," said
Tove, "which is right, and which is the only sentence in your email that was any use to
me."

"I thought it was all useful."

"It was all clear. Clear isn't the same as useful." Tove put her cup down. "Do you know what
a survey is."

"Yes."

"No, you don't, and that's fine, and I'm asking so I know which of the two things I have to
give you." She held up a hand. "One version of a survey is the thing people do to a cave:
you go in, you go forward, you measure, you come out, you write it up. That's the version
you'll find on a poster. The other version is: you go in, you go forward, you measure, you
stop when the thing you came for is not the thing in front of you, and you come out with
less than you went in with, and you write *up* that, and the writing-up is the entire
profession and everything else is transport."

"That's a good way of putting it."

"It's the only way of putting it," said Tove. "Everything else is a lie they tell on the
telly about adventure and men and ropes, and the rope is a delivery mechanism."

Ottoline laughed, which she had not planned to do.

"Go on," said Tove, and she was not smiling, but something had moved. "You're about to say
that's the most romantic thing anybody's said about my job and I'm telling you now that it
isn't and I will deny it."

"No, I was going to say that's the first true thing anybody's said to me about this in four
months and I don't think you were going to like hearing it."

"No," said Tove. "I didn't."

They sat with the report between them for a while. Outside, the rain had come back, and it
was the kind of rain that came at that angle and made a room feel like it was somewhere
else, and the light in the room changed, and the report on the table stayed exactly the same
shape.

"What's your involvement?" said Tove. "I mean, actually. Not on paper. You've hired Varga,
who is the best cave mind in this country and has been out of it for four years, which
means you've hired somebody who knows more about that system than the man who's got the
contract, and either you did that on purpose or you did it by accident and you'd like to
think it was on purpose."

"It was on purpose."

"Huh." Tove did not look away. "Right. So what's it for."

"The film is about—"

"Don't tell me what the film's about. The film's about a cave that a woman died in and
you're going to stand in a beautiful place and put a small box on a stick near where it
happened and get the light right, and everybody who's ever done that has told me the film
is about grief or memory or the passage of time, and every single one of them has been
telling me the film is about themselves, and I have been to the premieres."

"Then what is the film about."

"I don't know yet. I'm not your audience." Tove leaned forward. "Here's what I actually
wanted to know, and I've been waiting forty minutes to ask it, and I'm going to ask it badly:
what is *your* work about? Not the cave. You. What do you do when there's no camera, when
nothing's being filmed, when it's just you and a subject and a Tuesday. What is the thing
you're after?"

"I don't understand the question."

"That's the first honest answer anybody's given me in a year," said Tove, "and it's the
wrong one, but it's an honest one." She sat back. "Let me put it another way. You've got a
camera and you've got a woman who won't be filmed. And in about six weeks one of those two
things is going to break, and I want to know which one you'd rather, because you'll have to
have decided by then and if you haven't decided you'll do the thing that keeps the project
alive, and the thing that keeps the project alive is never the thing that keeps the film
honest."

"That's a lecture."

"That's a fact with a lecture wrapped round it, and you'd be the first person in this
country to be given the fact without the lecture, and here we are." Tove picked up her
cup, found it empty, and set it down again. "My name's Tove."

"I know. It's in the paperwork you signed."

"It's not in the paperwork, it's on the paperwork, and people use those differently." Tove
put her hands flat on the table, either side of the report. "Take Varga. She's the best I've
ever worked with and I don't mean with a rope, I mean in a room, where somebody's wrong and
everybody's pretending they're not. She was right more often than anybody I've worked with,
and when she was wrong she was wrong in a way you could see from across the room, and there
was never any *wait, is she* — you always knew. That's a thing you can't teach and I have
tried."

"Sounds like you liked her."

"I liked working with her," said Tove. "Those are different and I'm not going to pretend
they're the same, because in four years neither of us has been in a room, and I've been
careful about that, and I don't want to stop being careful now that there's a reason on the
table."

Ottoline sat very still.

"You've been careful."

"For four years."

"Why?"

And Tove Marsk, who had been ready for every question except that one and had built an
entire professional life on being ready for most of them, opened her mouth, and found that
the answer she had was about a report, and the answer she wanted to give was about the fact
that the woman on the other end of a line had once told her the reading was fine when it
was not, and that she had believed her, and that a person was dead, and that Tove had not
believed her since and had never once known what to do with it.

"Because of the paperwork," she said.

"Right," said Ottoline, and did not believe her at all, and let it go, which was a
generosity that Tove noticed and did not deserve and was not going to mention.

Tove stood up, and gathered the eleven things that were not a survey report, and stacked
them, and picked up the survey report, and then put it back down.

"You can have this for the week," she said. "It's public. It's already in the archive. I
want you to look at how it's laid out, because the layout is the whole point, and if your
film is going to be about a system then somebody in it ought to know what a system looks
like on paper. I don't want a shot of a woman in a helmet looking thoughtful."

"You don't like looking thoughtful."

"I like it fine when it's earned." Tove got her bag. "And you haven't earned it. Nobody has.
Being filmed is not being interesting, and the fact that you'll find that out and be
disappointed is not a reason for me to soften it."

She was at the door, and she stopped, and she did not turn round.

"Send me the schedule when it's fixed. Not for my approval, I don't have any and I don't
want any. Send it so I know when you're going to be on my system, because in October I'll
be in there, and if there's going to be a film and a survey in the same hole at the same
time then one of us needs to know about it, and I'd rather it was you than find out from
Hana."

"You know Hana?"

"Everyone knows Hana," said Tove. "Hana knows everyone's phone number, which is a service
she provides free of charge, and I have never once been able to work out whether that's
generosity or a system."

She went. The door shut with the ordinary sound of a door.

Ottoline sat in a room with a survey report on the table that had a woman's name in the
header and a date on it and four hundred pages of somebody else's careful work, and she did
not open it, because she had a rule, and the rule was that she did not read anything about
the Vell that a person had handed her in a room, because a person who hands you something in
a room has chosen the moment, and she was not going to take the moment away from them.

She put her hands on the cover.

Then she opened it, on page one, and read the header, which said:

*Vell System. Third Edition. Surveyed 1994, revised 2011, partial revision 2019.*

*Compiled by: V. Marrow, B. Ostrowski, and S. Havelock.*

*Third edition, 2019 revision compiled by T. Marsk.*

Ottoline read that four times.

Then she put both hands flat on the table, on either side of it, in the position Tove
Marsk's hands had been in, and did not read any more of it at all that afternoon.

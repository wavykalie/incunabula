# Chapter Eighteen — The Same Date

The two dates were on the same day and it took Ottoline four days to work out why, and the
reason was not interesting.

The school released its studio facilities on the last working day of the term, which was
fixed in 1987 and had never moved. The association's permits ran to the end of the month.
The nineteenth of November was a Friday in 2026 and it was both of those things, and nobody
had arranged it and nobody would ever arrange it again, and the entire difference between a
film that got made and a film that didn't was a coincidence in an academic calendar.

"Fourteenth of November," said Marisol, reading from a printout. "That's your access-day
window closing. The school releases the room on the nineteenth. You've got five days and then
there's nowhere to put the edit."

"The permit's the nineteenth."

"The permit's the nineteenth. So the permit's the day before the building shuts." Marisol put
the printout down. "That's not a coincidence, that's a structural problem, and you have had
it since September and you didn't look at it because you were looking at everything else."

"I've looked at it now."

"Good."

"Do you know what the school does if the room goes back?"

"They put four hundred pounds of something in it," said Marisol. "It'll be a room with a
projector in it. Which is what it was in September."

Ottoline sat in an office on the second floor of a building with a car park on the roof and
had the thing that had been building behind her ribs since the sixteenth of October arrive,
which was not panic and was not disappointment, and was the specific and unwelcome
understanding that a professional project with a fixed end date is a completely different
animal from a thing you are doing because it matters, and that the difference was going to
decide what she made in November.

The deadline and the descent were the same day. That was the sentence. In five days she would
either have gone down with a woman who would not be filmed, or she would not have gone down
at all, and the going down was the entire film, and the not-going-down was the entire reason
she was the person to do it.

"You know what this is," she said.

"Yes."

"It's not a coincidence about dates. It's that the film needs the descent and the descent
needs a woman who won't be filmed and I've spent seven weeks arranging the second thing so
that the first thing could happen, and the arrangement stops working on the nineteenth because
that's when the building goes back."

"Yes."

"So what do I do."

Marisol Trent looked at her across a desk for a moment longer than a supervisor needs to.

"You're going to ask me for something," she said.

"I'm going to ask you for the 1994 material," said Ottoline, "as a cut, in a format I can
work from, by Monday."

"It's in a store room. It's on a projector. It is a forty-minute rough cut of a survey that
was never finished and there's a reason it's in a store room and the reason is a commercial
one, not a legal one, and if I hand it over, you will make a film about a survey, and the
survey will be about water, and the water is a matter for this association and not for a
film."

"Yes."

"So I'm going to say no, and I'm going to say it well, and I'm going to give you three
reasons that will all be true, and you're going to be angry with me, and in about four years
you're going to agree with me." Marisol's hands were flat on the desk. "Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless you tell me what you saw on the seventeenth."

Ottoline did not move.

"I don't know how you know there was a seventeenth."

"Because Bernadette rang me on the Wednesday evening," said Marisol, "because Bernadette
cannot help herself, and because Bernadette said that a young woman had sat in the screening
room for eleven hours and had not once asked to take anything away, which is the single
strangest thing she has ever reported about a film person, and that she'd mentioned the
field wall."

"I didn't ask about the wall."

"No. You didn't." Marisol's voice did not change. "That's why I'm still sitting here. If you'd
asked about the wall I'd have said no and we'd have had this conversation in about a minute.
You didn't ask, which means you don't know what you have, or you know and you didn't want
it, and I have spent four years watching people in this trade take things out of that
building and I've never once watched somebody leave something in it."

There was a car going across the car park outside.

"I'm going to say something and then I'm going to get a box out of a store room," said
Marisol, "and I want you to notice that I am not entirely clear myself why, and I would
rather say that out loud than pretend."

"Ottoline—"

"You've read the report. You know the descent count. You know the section's closed and that
nobody's characterising the water, and I have written four reports recommending that somebody
characterise the water and I have been ignored four times, and the reason I have been ignored
is that nobody's ever produced a reason." Marisol stood up. "In 1994 a woman of twenty-eight
surveyed that system for nine months and worked out something about the water that I have
never been able to get out of her, and the reason I have never been able to get it out of
her is that she would not put it in a document, and I have spent thirty years respecting
that, and I would like it on the record that I am now overriding it, and that I am doing so
because of a film."

"That's a bad reason."

"It is a *terrible* reason," said Marisol, "and I have thought about it for about eleven
minutes and I have decided that eleven minutes is what I have, and that a terrible reason
that I can defend is better than a good one I can't." She got the door. "The whole box. Not
the store-room copy. The whole box, including her working, including the nine hours, including
the box file which I have not opened since 1997 and which has her index in it in her
handwriting."

"Why the box file?"

"Because the index is in her handwriting," said Marisol, "and you are about to spend a hundred
and forty hours with a camera in a system she spent nine months in, and the least I can do is
hand you the only document in this country that was written by somebody who was there."

She was in the doorway.

"Marisol. Why are you doing this?"

And Marisol Trent, who had a plant on a windowsill that had been replaced with the same plant
from the same shop in October and had not told anybody, stood in a doorway and gave a
completely true answer that was also completely beside the point.

"Because in 1994 I was on the panel that cleared her permit," she said, "and I cleared it in
nine days, and I have thought about those nine days every October for thirty-two years, and
last week a young woman sat in my building for eleven hours and did not take one thing out of
it, and I have decided that I would rather be the person who was wrong in 1994 in a building
with the lights on than the person who was right in a corridor with the door shut. It's not a
decision. It's a preference. I'm not going to dress it up as a policy because I have spent
thirty-two years writing policies and I know what they're for."

She went out.

Ottoline Marrow sat alone in an office on the second floor for about four minutes.

Then she went and got her bag, and went down the stairs, and out into the rain, and stood in
the car park, and did the thing she had done in a car park at four in the morning three weeks
ago, which was to stand still in the middle of it and let a thing be enormous for a second
before getting into the van.

The permit was the nineteenth. The building closed on the nineteenth. There were five days.
The descent needed a woman who would not be filmed. And in a store room in the same building
was a box with an index in it in a dead woman's handwriting, and the dead woman had surveyed
the system and had decided not to write down what she knew, and had been right, possibly, and
was thirty-two years gone.

Ottoline got in the van and drove to the shop and did not ring first, and stood in the rain
outside it for about forty seconds, and then went in.

"Fabiola, is she in?"

"She's in the back," said Fabiola, without looking up, and then, with the particular
flatness she used about twice a year: "She's in the back and she's been in the back for an
hour with a mug of cold tea and I would like you to know that if this is about the room I'd
like to be told so I can go and stand somewhere else."

"It's not about the room," said Ottoline. "It's about a plate."

# Chapter Twenty-Five — Paragraph Six

They put it in a room with a window and four chairs and a recorder, because the school did
that, because the school had a form for it, and because the form was cheaper than a lawyer.

Marisol Trent sat at the head of the table with the printout in front of her that she had
given to Ottoline Marrow in October, and she had brought a second copy, and the second copy
was for herself, and the second copy had a paragraph marked on it in pencil that she had made
on the first of November in a room with a plant on the windowsill.

Nobody had asked for a hearing. That was the first thing, and Marisol said it out loud within
about ninety seconds, because it was true and because she was going to need the room to know
that she had not manufactured it.

"Nobody asked for this. I'm going to run it, because it's my job and because a decision made
in a corridor is a decision that doesn't exist, and I'm going to run it exactly as I would
run any other, and I'm going to say the things I've already said in writing because reading
them in writing was not enough."

"Say them again."

"Not yet. First I want to establish the terms." Marisol put her hands flat on the table. "This
isn't disciplinary. Nothing here is disciplinary and I'm not your supervisor in any sense that
word means. This is a review of a risk assessment that I have refused to sign, and the outcome
is one of three things: I sign it, I don't sign it, or I sign it with conditions. Those are
the three. There's nothing else on the table and nobody is going to be clever tonight."

"And if you don't sign it?"

"Then the descent doesn't happen and the film is forty hours of a layby in October and you
graduate in June." Marisol said it without any particular weight at all. "I want to be clear
that I know exactly what I am holding over you and I have thought about that more than I've
thought about the assessment, and I'm not going to pretend to be smaller than I am tonight."

Ottoline was sitting very still.

There were two other people in the room. One was a man from the school who had come to take
minutes and who had a laptop and had opened it and had not typed anything. The other was a
woman called Fen from the facilities office, who was there because the room was booked through
her and who had brought a key and a bad mood and would leave in eleven minutes regardless of
what happened.

"Okay," said Ottoline. "Read me paragraph six."

Marisol did not have to look at it.

"Paragraph six, in the covering note I sent you on the eighth of October, reads as follows.
The project is well-conceived. The Vell is a single-entrance system with a known flood
history, and the association's position is that the section below the second pitch remains
closed pending a full hydrological assessment, which it has been since 2023, and which is
true.

"The staffing arrangement proposed in the application provides for a named person on the
surface end of the working line at all times. No such person has been named. The person who
would in all likelihood be named, if the application proceeded, is the technical consultant
herself, and I wrote in October that this was a structural problem rather than a staffing
problem, in that the arrangement depends on a woman of thirty-eight going underground on the
end of a line that she has not been on for four years, and that I was not prepared to record
my agreement to that and would not be able to."

She stopped.

"That was the paragraph. There's a seventh paragraph and the seventh paragraph says that
competence is not the point, and there's an eighth paragraph about a panel in 1994, and you
have all three and I'm not going to read the other two, because if I read all three this
becomes a performance and I want it to be a process."

"You've changed the staffing," said Ottoline. "Since October. There's a rescue technician
named and there's a second surveyor named on the certificate now, and neither of them is me."

"Yes."

"When?"

"Yesterday evening. The certificate went to the school at about seven." Marisol turned a page
she did not need to turn. "I want to answer a question that isn't going to be asked properly
and I'd rather answer it early. Yes, I have moved. Yes, I know I said in writing that this
was structural and not a staffing problem, and I said it in a document with my name on it, and
I have not retracted a word of it, and the staffing arrangement is now adequate and I have
signed it as adequate."

"Then paragraph six doesn't stand."

"Paragraph six is *withdrawn*," said Marisol, "and I want to be very careful about the tense
of that, because the paragraph was correct in October and I did not make a mistake. Paragraph
six was correct in October and it has been overtaken, and the thing that overtook it is that
a rescue technician drove thirty-one kilometres in a van on Wednesday to deliver a piece of
paper to a shop, and I know that because I telephoned the shop on Thursday and Fabiola
Renn told me about it in about four sentences, which is about four more than she usually
gives me."

---

"And the risk assessment," said Marisol. "I'm going to ask you about it and I'm going to ask
you once and I'm going to wait, and I want to explain the waiting first, because I know what
it does and I'm doing it deliberately and I'd like that on the record."

"That's the cruelty," said Ottoline.

"It's the only tool I have and it's a very poor one." Marisol folded her hands. "I cannot
make you not do this. I have tried nine months to make you not do this, in writing, in
courteous paragraphs, and you have outlasted all of it, and I have not once had an argument
with you that worked, because I don't have an argument. I have facts. My facts have all turned
out to be true and reasonable and completely beside the point, and you have sat through every
one of them and out-politened me, and I have never once been angry about that, and I've been
frightened about it for about seven weeks.

"So the only thing left is to ask you the question, out loud, once, in a room with a recorder
on it, and let the room be a room. That's not a tactic. It's a woman who has run out of
administrative options and would like to be told she's wrong by somebody who respects her
enough to say it."

The man with the laptop typed something. Fen looked at her watch.

"Marisol."

"Mm."

"I'm not going to lie to you," said Ottoline.

"I'd be astonished if you did."

"I'm going to tell you that I know the section's closed. I know the water's not characterised.
I know that on the ninth of October a woman who hadn't been underground in four years went
down fifty metres and came back, and I know that on the fourteenth she was at a hundred and
sixty for two hours ten, and I know that the deepest she has been this year is a hundred and
sixty, which is four metres above the second pitch, and that she has said on the record that
she will not go below the second pitch, and I have that in writing from her and I have it in
writing from the association and I have it in writing from Hana Vogel."

"That's true."

"That's all true and none of it is the question." Ottoline put her hands flat on the table,
which was not a thing she had ever done at a table in her life, and which she had learned in
the last six weeks from watching somebody else do it in a stockroom. "The question is why
I'm asking. And the answer is: because if I don't go, then the next thing I make is about
somebody else, and it'll be good, and I'll be good at it, and I'll be doing it for the rest of
my life, and I will have had exactly one year in which somebody trusted me with a thing that
mattered and I was in the room for it."

"That's not a risk assessment."

"No," said Ottoline. "It's the reason I'm doing something my risk assessment says not to do.
And I want you to write that down, because that's the actual sentence, and everything else in
this room has been a way of not saying it."

Marisol Trent did not move for about four seconds.

"That's the answer," she said.

"Is it the right one?"

"No," said Marisol. "It's the *human* one. It is the honest reason and it is not a
professional reason, and if you had given me that answer in a corridor in September I would
have signed the form, and I want to be honest with you that I'm finding that extremely
inconvenient."

The man with the laptop had typed nothing at all for eleven minutes.

"Marisol. What are you going to do?"

And Marisol Trent, who had a plant on a windowsill that had been replaced with the same plant
from the same shop in October and had not told anybody, and who had drafted a hedge in 2022
because a document that said *eleven descents was unreasonable* would be found by a family,
and who had cleared a permit in nine days in 1994 and thought about those nine days every
October for thirty-two years, and who had overridden thirty-one years of respecting somebody's
silence on a Wednesday because a young woman had sat in a building for eleven hours and taken
nothing out of it, sat in a room with a recorder on the table and did the thing she had been
not doing since March.

"I'm going to sign it," she said, "with three conditions, and I'm going to write them now, and
then I'm going to read them out, and then I am going to leave this room, because I have been in
it for forty minutes and I have not enjoyed one of them and I have never once enjoyed one of
them, and that's the part nobody tells you about this job."

She wrote for about two minutes.

"One: the descent does not pass the second pitch. That is not a condition of the association
and it is a condition of me and I am aware that I am not entitled to it and I am imposing it.

"Two: the surface party is three. Not two. And I want that in writing on the certificate by
Friday, and I have already spoken to both of them, and one of them has said yes and one of
them has said she'll answer on Thursday, and I'm not going to pretend I know which.

"Three." She stopped writing.

"Three is not a risk condition," said Marisol. "Three is a note. It reads: *I record that I
asked the applicant, in a room, on the record, why she was doing this, and that she gave me a
reason, and that the reason was true, and that the reason was not a professional reason, and
that I have signed this assessment anyway.*

"Which means that in about ten years somebody will read that note, and they will read it
either as the day a good supervisor made a bad call, or as the day somebody wrote down the
truth at the time she was asked for it. And I do not know which it will be, and I have
decided I would rather be the second."

She signed it and turned the paper round and pushed it across the table.

Ottoline read it twice.

"You've written a note in your own assessment apologising to whoever reads it in 2036."

"Yes."

"That's the least professional thing I've ever seen."

"It is completely professional," said Marisol Trent. "It is the most professional thing
available, and I have taught for twenty years that the alternative is a document that reads
well and is useless, and I have watched about four people ruin their careers doing the
other thing and about thirty ruin their consciences."

Fen was already at the door with her key.

"Am I dismissed?"

"You were never here," said Marisol, pleasantly. "Thank you for the room."

The door shut.

Marisol Trent picked up her bag, and stopped at the door, and did not turn round, and said:

"Ms Marrow. On the fifth of November the school will want a contingency. If the nineteenth
closes, the twenty-third is a Monday and there is a room."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like that. Say what you'd have to do."

"I'd have to tell Ines on the nineteenth morning that the permit's gone, which is a
conversation I have been putting in every possible gap in October, and it would mean she's had
two hours and four minutes underground in a month for nothing." Ottoline heard her own voice
and stopped. "I know. I'm sorry. That was self-indulgent."

"That was the first true thing you've said about the logistics all evening," said Marisol,
"and I want you to notice that you said it after you gave me your honest reason, which means
the honest reason was the *why* and the permit is the *when*, and those are two different
questions and you've been answering the first one to everybody for two months and you have
never once answered the second one out loud, including to me, in October, when I gave you a
deadline and you took it."

She went out.

The man with the laptop closed it.

"That was the strangest thing I've ever sat through," he said, "and I've been taking minutes
for this school for eleven years, and I'm going to write it up as a risk review with three
conditions, because that's what it was, and because nobody's going to believe the other thing
and I've got a job."

"That's the right call," said Ottoline.

"Yeah," he said. "I know. I'm going to have a very uncomfortable Friday."

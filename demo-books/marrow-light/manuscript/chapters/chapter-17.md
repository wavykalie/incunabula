# Chapter Seventeen — The Other Woman's Film

The reason nobody had made a film about the Vell in thirty years was not that it was
dangerous, and it was not that nothing had happened in it, and it was not — although this
was what everybody said, and was said in a way that was technically true and functioned
entirely as an excuse.

The reason was that Vera Marrow had made one, in 1995, and it had been good, and then
there had been nowhere for it to go.

That was the whole story and it was not a tragedy and there was nothing in it to be sorry
for, and it had nevertheless taken thirty years and a great deal of somebody's life.

Ottoline found this out in October from a woman in a cardigan in a room above a shop that
sold fluorescent fittings, and it took two hours, and the woman in the cardigan was called
Bernadette and had been a unit administrator at the association since 1991 and had not
wanted the meeting and had said so on the telephone.

"You're the film person."

"I'm the film person."

"I've got about twenty minutes and then I'm doing the filing, and I want to say up front that
I don't think this is any of my business and I'm doing it anyway."

"That's a good way to start."

"Right." Bernadette put a folder down. "The '94 material. You've seen it."

"I've seen eleven hours of it."

"Then you know it's good." Bernadette said this flatly, as a professional assessment, and
gave it about the emphasis of somebody reading out a serial number. "It is genuinely good. I've
been in this trade thirty-four years and I have seen four pieces of work off that tape and
it's the best of the four by a distance, and I was not in the film industry, I was in this
one, and I want to be careful with the word *best* because I know what I'm saying, but she
could make a system legible. Nobody else in this county can do that. She could put a thing on
a screen and you'd come out knowing it."

"She didn't release it."

"No."

"Why not?"

And here Bernadette took about eleven seconds, which was longer than the question, and looked
at the folder.

"Because the rough cut is forty minutes and it's about a survey," she said, "and there's no
way to get a broadcaster to make a forty-minute documentary about a survey in this country,
and she was twenty-eight and she'd spent nine months on it and she went and did the maths and
the maths said no, and then she spent two years getting the maths to say something else, and
it didn't."

"That's a very commercial reason."

"It's a *correct* reason," said Bernadette, with an edge that was not unkind. "That's the
thing about that generation. They made the work and then they thought the work was going to
get itself made, because the work was the hard part, and the work had been hard, so they'd
earned the rest of it, and the rest of it never came. Your mother's generation made the work
and then went and looked for somebody to give it to."

"Who did she give it to?"

"Me," said Bernadette. "I was a unit administrator. I was twenty-six. She handed me a tape
and a form and I signed for it in 1996 and I put it in a store room and it has been in that
store room ever since, and I have moved it twice."

Ottoline wrote this down. She had a rule about not writing down the thing that was actually
happening, and she was breaking the rule, and the rule existed because she had learned in
her second year that the true thing always went in a notebook and then came out in an edit
twelve weeks later looking like a decision.

"What was it like working with her?"

"It was like being twenty-six and completely out of your depth for nine months and being told
every morning that you were doing fine." Bernadette's face did something complicated. "She
was the best I've worked with. And I want to say the other thing as well because I don't think
she'd want it left out: she was the most *certain* person I have ever met, and she was
twenty-eight, and by the end of it she had worked out something about the water in that system
that nobody else in this county has worked out since, and I have never known what it was,
because she never wrote it down, and when I asked her in 1996 she said, *I'm not going to
write that down, I've got it, and I'm going to need a while.*"

"Need a while for what?"

"She didn't say." Bernadette looked at the folder again. "And that was the last conversation I
had with her, and I have thought about the sentence for thirty years, and I have decided the
sentence is not the point. The point is that a woman of twenty-eight decided that the answer
she had was not reliable enough to put in anybody's mouth, and then she stopped working
altogether, and those two facts are very strange next to each other and I've never been able
to make them not strange."

Ottoline sat very still in a room above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings and understood
that she was being told a true thing about her mother by a woman who had no reason to tell
her and no stake in it and had told it anyway, and that this was the first genuinely
unpleasant thing that had happened to her in October.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me, I'm doing the filing."

---

The afternoon she watched the rest of it.

She brought nobody. That was the arrangement she had made with herself at four in the morning
in a car park and she kept it, and the keeping of it was the reason she had to watch the
final hour in a screening room on a Wednesday afternoon with the door open and the light on
and somebody walking past in the corridor, which was the exact opposite of how anybody watches
anything that matters.

The final hour was the field, and the entrance, and a woman standing in front of a hole in a
hillside in 1994 talking directly to camera about access, and it is the best four minutes of
the entire tape, and Ottoline watched it with the sound off.

She watched it with the sound off because she could not do the other thing, and she found out
within about ninety seconds that this was not a limitation but an advantage, because a woman
talking to camera with no sound is a woman talking, and the shot was a medium two-shot from
about four metres, static, tripod, and Vera Marrow was extremely good in it in a way that
Ottoline could only see with the sound off.

She is not selling it. Four metres and a tripod get you a person who is not
selling anything, who has made a film about a survey and is standing in front of the survey
saying what the survey is for, and who is enjoying herself, and who is twenty-eight.

And then the camera does something, and it does it for about six seconds, and it is not a
cut and it is not a move.

It pans about four degrees to the left.

It pans off her to a wall — the wall of the field entrance, the retaining wall on the
uphill side, which is in shot in the wide and which Vera is standing three feet in front of —
and holds there, and on that wall there is a plate.

It is a brass plate, about the size of a bookplate, with a number painted on it in white by
hand in a slightly unsteady hand. It is nailed to the stone at about shoulder height. It is
in frame for six seconds at 940mm on a 1994 tripod-mounted camera and it is *legible*.

Ottoline stopped the tape.

She rewound. She watched it again with the sound off. She got the frame — 47 seconds into the
final hour, six seconds long — and she took a photograph of the screen with her phone, which
is a thing she had never done and did not tell anybody about and which she did twice.

Then she watched the rest of the hour and she did not rewind, because she had already seen
what was in it and the rest of the hour was the entrance and the field and a woman saying
the numbers out loud, and there was nothing in the rest of it that was a brass plate.

The book had said that the plate was fifty metres in. The book had said it was *maintained*.
The book had said, in a report read out in a room with a recorder on the table, that the
gauge in the fourth chamber was not photographed before the incident and could not be
photographed after it.

And here it was, on the wall of the field entrance, on the outside, in 1994, in a survey of
the access, on tape that had been in a store room for thirty years.

Ottoline Marrow sat in a screening room in a film school on a Wednesday afternoon with the
light on and the door open and a person walking past in the corridor, and looked at a
photograph of her own phone screen, and did not say anything out loud, and then spent two
hours and forty minutes not saying anything out loud to anybody, including to herself, while
a very careful and thorough re-reading of everything she had been given established the
following:

That in 1994 the plate on the field wall was the same plate. That the same person had
maintained it, because the hand was the same hand in the same unsteady way, and the numbers
were in the same white. That Vera Marrow had filmed it, on the outside, in daylight, in
1994, in a shot that lasted six seconds and had no narrative reason to exist, which meant
that either she had put it in deliberately or she had panned across a wall in April 1994 and
it had ended up in a box for thirty years.

And that there was one number in that frame, and that the number was a number of hours, and
that nobody in October 2026 knew what it was, and that she was the only person in the country
who had a photograph of it.

She did not tell Ines. She wrote in the notebook that evening:

*Not yet. Not because I'm holding it — because I don't know what it IS yet. A number on a
plate in 1994 is a number. It is a gauge reading and gauge readings are exactly the thing
that is wrong, and I have got a film to make and if I walk in with this like a detective
I become the thing I promised I wouldn't be, which is a person who uses information about
somebody else as a way of being near them.*

And underneath that, in the other handwriting, which she had still not examined:

*Ask her what the plate says. She'll know. She was the one painting it.*

And then, on a separate line, at the bottom, and this one she wrote at two in the morning:

*You already know she was the one painting it. That's why you can't ask.*

# Chapter Thirty-Eight — Hours Since

The arts centre in Kelder has a room with a projector in it and a man who introduces you and
a chair at the front and four minutes at the end for a thing they call a Q and A, and
nineteen people came on a Thursday in February, which is four more than came to the last one.

Ottoline Marrow said thank you in nine words and did not say any of them about the film. She
said the film was ninety-four minutes and it was made in and around a layby in the eastern
karst between September and December and it was her first one and she was glad to be here.

Then she put it on.

The room is a good room. That is the first thing about a screening and it is the only thing
about it that the person making the film has any control over: there is a screen at the front
and about two hundred people could sit in it and on a Thursday in February there were
nineteen, and the light was the right light, and the man had put the blackout down properly.

There were six people from the layby and there was Fabiola Renn, who had come from the shop
in a car on her own and had got there at six o'clock and had sat in the eleventh row and had
not spoken to anybody, and there was Petar Marsk, who had driven down from the county and had
not brought a flask because there was a bar, and there was Marisol Trent, who had left at
half past seven and had sent a note that said *I have a class. It was a good film. The cap was
my fault and I have said so in a report.* And there was Ines Varga, who was in the fourth
row, and who had driven an hour for a Q and A that lasted four minutes, and who had said
that in a car outside a shop on the thirteenth of December and had meant it, and who had come.

And there was a woman of sixty-eight at the back, who had come on her own, and who had not
told her daughter she was coming, and who had not been in a cinema since about 1998 and who
had driven ninety minutes to do it and had got there at five and had sat in the back and had
watched a wall in a layby for four seconds at the fourth shot.

The wall was legible.

Vera Marrow had seen it before. That was the whole of what she was there for and she had
told nobody that and had not said a word about it and had watched her daughter put a four-second
shot of a brass plate into a film about a system, and had not moved, and had understood in
about the second second that her daughter had found the plate, and had gone through a
calculation in the dark of a cinema in about nine seconds that came to *she found it in
October and she has not rung me*, and had stayed in her seat.

Ottoline Marrow did not know her mother was there. That is true, and it is checkable in
eleven different ways if anybody ever wanted to, and it is the last fact in this book that
is simply true and has nothing to do with anybody.

---

Hana Vogel was in the sixth row and she had the book in her lap.

She had brought it because she had been on a callout on the Tuesday and had not put it down
since, in the sense that a book lives in a bag and comes out, and it had come out on a train
on the way south and she had read the same four pages in it for two hours without noticing
and had got here at half past five and had found a seat on the left.

The plate was on the screen for four seconds and the number on it was twelve, and the film did
not say what the number meant, and there was no music on it, and it was the only shot in
ninety-four minutes in which nothing at all was happening.

Hana Vogel looked at four seconds of a wall and a number and then the film went to a shop.

She did not watch the shop. She had watched the shop for twenty years in various states of
repair and she had watched it on a laptop nine nights ago and it was in the film and it was
good and it was a shop.

She read the four pages.

---

The film finished at twenty to eight.

There was a man with a microphone and he asked one question, which was about equipment, and
somebody at the back asked whether the descent had been dangerous, and Ottoline said that
there had been a cap on it and the cap had been broken by fourteen minutes and it was in a
report and the supervisor had put it in the report herself, and a man in the fourth row said
that that was not an answer to the question.

Ines Varga, in the fourth row, said, "It was dangerous," in a voice that about nine people in
a room recognised and nobody else did, and did not expand on it, and the man in the fourth row
sat down.

Then there were the four minutes at the end, and a woman of sixty-eight in the back row did
not ask a question, and her daughter did not look at the back, and the film ended with a
number.

---

The last scene is nine seconds long.

It is a car park above a shut gate, in the morning, in December, and it is the locked-off shot
from a tripod in the rain cover, and there is a van and a field and a wall, and the number on
the wall is twelve, and it is the fourth shot and the book has kept it.

The last line of dialogue in this novel is not a line of dialogue; it is a number on a plate,
and it is legible, and it is twelve, and the plate is the plate.

Before the plate there are nine seconds, and in the nine seconds Hana Vogel is on a
hillside at the top of a pitch on the twenty-eighth of November, at half past nine in the
morning, in a clear light, with four people standing in a car park and eleven metres of
rain-soaked wall behind her, and she has a book open in her hand because she read the number
off a book that morning on a decision she made at six o'clock and told nobody about, and she
reads it out loud.

It is not loud; it is the registered voice, and the registered voice is a professional
instrument and she has used it for everything for eleven years, and in a cinema in a town
forty minutes away it was possible to hear it perfectly and impossible to hear it at all,
depending on where you were sitting.

"Twelve," said Hana Vogel, into a handset, on a hillside, in a car park above a shut gate, on
the twenty-eighth of November. "Twelve hours since the last rise, and it is correct, and
there is nothing forecast until Monday."

The number was correct. It had been correct since the twenty-first. Nobody had needed to
paint it and nobody had painted it and the woman who painted it had been down there seventeen
times in fifty-six days and had read it every time and had trusted it every time, and it had
been right every time, and that was the whole of the thing that had happened in the Vell in
four years.

Nine seconds.

Then a wall, and a plate, and a number on it that said twelve, and nothing else, and no voice,
and no music, and no caption, and no explanation, and no commentary from any character or from
anybody, and the picture went out.

The room was dark for about four seconds, which is what a room does, and then a man at the
back said, "Well," to nobody, and somebody's coat made a noise, and nineteen people in a room
in a town forty minutes away began, at the same moment and for eleven different reasons, to
talk.

---

Out in the car park at nine o'clock, Hana Vogel stood by a car and did not go in.

There were four of them, which was not the same four as anything and was not meant to be. It
was a woman of thirty-eight and a woman of thirty-four and a woman of sixty-one and a woman
of twenty-nine, standing in a car park in Kelder in the rain, having come to see a film.

"Mine's the black one," said Fabiola, and got in it, and did not say goodnight, which she had
never once said in forty-one years.

Hana Vogel did not get in a car. She stood in a car park and got her book out of her coat and
opened it on the bonnet of somebody's car, because the bonnet was the only flat thing, and
found a page, and read a number off it, out loud, at nine o'clock in the evening, in a car
park in a town forty minutes away, to nobody at all.

There was no one to hear it. The others had gone. The door of the arts centre was open and
light was coming out of it and about nine people were in there talking about a wall, and none
of them knew what the number was.

Hana Vogel closed the book, and put it in her coat, and got into the van, and drove back to a
station above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings, where there was a check on a board with
eleven items on it, and item six had been empty since 2019, and there was a shelter in the
second bay with NO. 4 written on the strap in a hand that was not Hana's and that Hana had
looked at about four times in three weeks and had still not got used to.

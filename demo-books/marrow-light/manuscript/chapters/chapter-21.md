# Chapter Twenty-One — Archive, Work

They watched nine hours of tape in a room above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings with a
stopwatch and a list, and it was the least emotional two days of Ottoline Marrow's adult
life, and they got more out of it in a room with a stopwatch than she had got out of
eleven hours alone in the dark.

That was not a surprise to her; it was, in fact, the thing she had learned in her first year
and had then spent two years being bad at, which was that watching alone is a way of not
watching.

Tove came at nine. Marisol came at ten and left at half past ten, which was deliberate on
both sides and neither of them mentioned it. Hana came at half past ten and left at eleven,
which was also deliberate, and which Hana announced: "I'm not here for the film. I'm here to
be in the room while you watch a tape of a system I might have to be responsible for. If I'm
going to be stood on a hill in November in the rain because somebody made a decision in 1994
then I'd like to know what's in the thing."

Nobody argued with Hana about anything logistical. This was noted later as a professional
inefficiency in a small organisation.

So there were two of them, and a stopwatch, and a notebook each, and Tove Marsk had brought
a grid.

"That's the grid?"

"That's the grid," said Tove. "I don't watch a survey film. I watch a *record* of a survey.
I want to know, in order: did they go in, what did they do, how long, what went wrong, what
did they write down, and how long did it take them to write it down. That last one's the
important one. The last one's the whole job."

They started at the beginning.

The first hour they established that Vera Marrow had shot four hours of a woman getting into a
coat in a field at dawn, which was unusable, and eleven minutes of a survey office in 1994
which was not, and a sequence in March of a shaft nobody was going into that day.

"She's not going in," said Tove, at eighteen minutes. "She's going to shoot the *set-up* for
four hours. Which is a waste, and which in 1994 nobody would have done, and which now every
second-year does, and both of them are wrong for different reasons."

"That's two hours of my mother's time," said Ottoline.

"That's two hours of your mother's time in 1994 and it's a choice she made and she's forty
minutes of table in this tape that I would put in front of the Crown. Give me the table."

---

They got to the table at about one and a half hours in and they did not leave it for
seventy minutes, and what came out of those seventy minutes was the entire reason Tove Marsk
was in the room.

It was unglamorous. It was three women at a table in 1994 and a great deal of measuring and
arguing about how to record a passage, and there is a sequence of about eleven minutes where
two of them disagree about a convention and the third one — Vera Marrow, twenty-eight, in a
sweater — solves it by going and getting a piece of paper and drawing something, and the
drawing is on the tape for four seconds and it is on the tape because somebody propped the
camera up and walked out of shot and came back.

"What is she drawing?"

Tove had the rewind on. She went back four seconds and watched it at quarter speed, twice.

"She's drawing a convention for a side passage," she said. "She's drawing it wrong."

"She's drawing it *wrong*?"

"She's drawing it in a way that's easier to see, because she's going to have to explain it to
two people who haven't done this, and then she's going to write it up properly and the proper
version's later." Tove stopped the tape. "That's the whole profession in four seconds of a
woman's back. Draw it wrong to teach it, write it right to keep it. Every survey office in
this country has a version of that, and I've worked in three of them, and I have never once
seen it on a tape, because nobody thinks to put the camera on during the bit that matters
most."

"You think about the tape."

"I think about the tape constantly and I've never been in a position to do anything about
it," said Tove, "and there is a nine-metre rule about cameras on my own contract, and that
rule exists because of a 2013 incident that everybody in this trade knows about and nobody
will ever discuss."

They went on.

They found the fourth chamber at three hours forty.

They found it because Tove had a list and the list said *below second pitch* and Tove was
working the way she always worked, which was briskly and in order, and Ottoline was
beginning to understand that this was the single most valuable thing anybody had ever done
for her and had not been done for her by anybody.

"Two hundred and ten seconds of the fourth chamber," said Tove. "That's it. That's all
there is. Two hundred and ten seconds of the room where somebody died, in 1994, before
anybody was in it, and it's the most beautiful two hundred and ten seconds on this tape."

"What's in it?"

"Water. And the ceiling, and a piece of kit somebody's dropped, and Vera telling a sound
recordist that the ceiling is going to come down in about nine years." Tove looked at the
stopwatch. "Two hundred and ten seconds, and about forty of them are a shot of a wall, and
that's the shot, and I want it for the fourth edition, and I can't have it, because it was
filmed with a camera on a tripod in a wet cave in 1994 by a woman with a different employer
and a different insurance, and it isn't mine, and the frame is Vera's, and the wall is the
association's, and the *index* is the only thing anybody can use and the index doesn't have
pictures in it."

That was a professional fact and she said it as one, flatly, in a room, with a stopwatch.

Then she said, "Play that bit again."

They played the bit again. It was forty seconds of a wet wall. There was flowstone. There
was a kit bag against it, forty years old, made of a material that does not exist any more, and
there was a light on a tripod, and there was the sound of a woman telling a sound recordist
that the ceiling was going to come down in about nine years, and the ceiling was exactly
where the ceiling was in 2026 and it had, in fact, come down in 2022, and the woman was
right by seven years.

And on the wall, at about two metres, in shot for about eleven of the forty seconds, there
was a brass plate.

"Stop," said Tove.

Ottoline stopped it. The frame sat there.

"Back up two seconds. Two. And forward four."

"That's the plate," said Ottoline.

"That's the plate," said Tove.

"It's on the inside."

"It's on the inside. She's fifty metres in and that plate is on the inside, and the plate in
the field entrance that Hana saw in the layby is a *copy*, and she said so without knowing
she was saying it, and there's a plate in here in 1994 on tape that nobody in this trade has
ever looked at." Tove had both hands flat on the table. "That's a gauge. In frame. Legible.
From 1994."

"Yes."

"It's in eleven seconds of a forty-second shot of a wall," said Tove, "and in a nine-hour
tape that has been in a store room since 1997, and there is no index reference to it, because
she wrote the index in March and this is September."

Neither of them said anything for about eleven seconds.

Then Tove said, "What's the reading say?"

"I can't tell."

"Neither can I, it's four per cent of frame and it's 1994 video and it's got a light in it.
No. That's a job for somebody who's got the tape at full resolution on a good monitor in a
room with the lights off." She was writing in her notebook and she had not looked up. "That's
four hours' work. That's somebody with a decent machine and an eye and a bit of time."

"That's not yours," said Ottoline.

"It is absolutely not mine," said Tove, "and that's the first interesting thing that's
happened to me in about four years, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise just to make
myself feel better about it, so I'm going to say the useful part out loud: whoever does that
four hours of work is going to find out something about the water in the Vell that this
association has wanted since 2011, and whoever does it will do it in a building that is not
this building, and neither of us is going to be able to stop it, and there's no version of
this where I get to be the person who found it."

"No," said Ottoline.

"No." Tove closed the notebook. "Good. Right. That's a good thing to have established on a
Tuesday, because there's a version of Thursday where I don't establish it, and I'd like to
have this on the record instead."

She got up and put her coat on and stood at the door with her hand on it.

"There's a plate on the outside wall as well," she said. "A copy. Which means somebody built
this, and somebody maintained it, and somebody thought it was worth having a second one where
people could see it from the road. That's a decision you make about a thing you think matters,
and that decision has a name attached to it, and the name is the same on both plates."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to say the name," said Tove, "because I've read a four-page document
forty-one times and I have never once been in a position to say a name about anything to do
with it, and I would like to keep it that way for about a week longer." She opened the door.
"Get your four hours of work done. Do it properly. Don't show it to anybody until you've
looked at it twice, and don't show it to her first."

"Why not her first?"

"Because she'll know what it means in about four seconds," said Tove, "and then you'll be a
person who went to a professional with a number and got a story, and you have spent nine days
not being that person and I would like you to go on not being that person for four more."

She went out. The door shut with the ordinary sound of a door.

Ottoline Marrow sat in a room above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings at half past four on
a Tuesday afternoon in October, in front of a monitor with a frame of a wet wall on it and
eleven seconds of a brass plate in it, and wrote in a notebook, in ordinary handwriting, which
was the other handwriting, the one she had still not examined:

*She knew. She's known since 9am. She knew when she saw the layby wall and she said it out
loud in front of Hana and then she sat still for four seconds and did not pick it up, and
then she said "not yet" about a document she has not read.*

And then, underneath:

*Everybody in this is doing "not yet" at me. Four people. In three weeks. And every single
one of them has handed me something to hold instead of the thing.*

And then, at the bottom of the page, and this one she wrote at four in the afternoon on a
Tuesday, and underlined it, which she did not do:

*You are the only person in this county who can make them stop. That's not a film. That's a
lens. Do you want it?*

She read it back. She did not cross it out. She did not write it again.

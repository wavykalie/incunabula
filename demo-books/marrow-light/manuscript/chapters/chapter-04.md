# Chapter Four — Eleven Minutes

The station had a kettle, a pool table with a slate bed and a hole in one corner, and a
fridge that came on at four in the afternoon and went off at nine, and by ten past nine it
was making the noise fridges make when the compressor has finished and is deciding whether
to be a fridge again.

Hana Vogel was in the second bay doing the one job that had to be done on a Tuesday, which
was the check, and the check took an hour and a half if you did it properly and twenty
minutes if you did it the way most people did it.

She did it properly. She had done it properly on a Friday night in March with a woman
missing in the eastern karst who turned up at a bus stop, and the twenty-minute version
would have found the problem in the second bay in week four, and the woman had been
missing for six days, and Hana still thought about the twenty-minute version, and would go
on thinking about it, and had arranged the rest of her life so that there would not be
another one.

The check was: two cylinders, one of them due, both of them full, both of them the right
date. Eleven items on the board, and eleven ticks, and she did them in the order they were
written and not the order she could do them fastest in, because the order they were written
in was the order somebody had thought about.

Item six was the shelter. Item six had been shelter for four years.

She had not got a shelter. She had got a bivvy sack, two of them, and a heat pack, and a
group shelter that went in the van in 2019 and did not come out again, and this was
recorded, correctly, on the board, in the box, with a date, and there was a piece of A4
taped to the wall above it that said *SHELTER — ORDER* in somebody else's handwriting, and
the date on the A4 was four years old.

The van was due. The van was always due. The van was eleven years old and had a wet patch
on the passenger side that had been a wet patch since 2021 and had been assessed as a wet
patch in 2021 and had not changed, and Hana had been offered a newer one in 2023 at a
discount she had declined, and the reason she had declined was that the discount came with
a lease and the lease came with a fleet manager, and she was not going to have a fleet
manager.

The phone went at ten past nine.

She looked at it and it was Ines Varga, and Hana picked it up, because the alternative was
letting it ring and there was no version of the evening in which that was better.

"Varga."

"It's Hana. Is that—" She stopped. "Sorry. I know who you are. I was going to ask if you
knew who this was."

"Where am I calling from?"

"You're at the station."

"I'm in the shop," said Ines. "It's Tuesday and it's after nine and I'm in the shop. You're
at the station because it's Tuesday and it's after nine and you do the check on Tuesdays."

There was a pause.

"That's the system," said Hana.

"That's the system," said Ines. "That's not a question. I've been calling this number for
four years and I've never once had to say the first eleven words and you've never once
picked up before the fifth ring, and I want to say that I know what that's about, and I'm
not asking you to explain it, and I'm not asking you to feel bad about it, and I'm not
going to make a speech. I've got about ninety seconds and I need to know if you'd take a
call in three weeks' time."

"I'd take a call."

"You'd take a call from anybody."

"I'd take a call from you," said Hana, "which is a different sentence and I meant it."

"What was it about."

So Hana had not been waiting four years to be asked. She had been waiting four years to be
asked and had built an entire personality out of the not-asking, and the not-asking had
been a discipline and the discipline had been a wall and the wall had been a door the whole
time, and it took one sentence from a woman in a shop eleven kilometres away to open it.

"There's a student," said Ines. "Second year. She's making a film about the Vell and she's
hired me to be on it as a consultant and I've said yes, which is — I don't know why I said
yes. I've said no to four of them. The fourth one had a much better offer for me than
she's made and I turned it down, and I'm not going to think about that tonight because it's
a Thursday problem."

"Who's the student."

"Ottoline Marrow."

"I don't know that name."

"She's twenty-two," said Ines, and Hana waited, and Ines did not continue, and had not
intended to, and there was a silence on the line of a kind that people who are not used to
each other find unbearable and people who have known each other for nineteen years do not
notice at all.

"There's a shot at the end of the drive," said Hana. "Not the Vell end. There's a layby
about a kilometre back where you can get a bar and a signal if you point it at the hill.
That's where the film base would go, not the headland. The headland gets forty minutes of
nothing in bad weather and the layby doesn't."

"How do you know about the layby."

"Because I've been sitting in it," said Hana. "A lot. For four years."

Ines did not say anything. Ines, who filled silence professionally and had once talked a
technician in a jammed shaft through a phone for two hours because the alternative was
letting him think about his hands, did not say anything for eight full seconds, and Hana
counted them, because counting was what she did instead of the other thing.

"You're not calling me back in three weeks," said Hana.

"No."

"No, you're not. That's fine, and I need that on the record: that's *fine*." She turned the
fridge door open and shut it again, which made it worse. "Here is what I need, so you can
decide whether I'm worth a phone call in three weeks or not. If she's going to be anywhere
near the Vell she needs to be checked, and I don't care about the film, and I don't care
about the schedule, and I don't care what her supervisor thinks. She gets checked or she
doesn't get to stand at the top of an entrance and watch people do a thing she can't help
with, and that's the last time anybody from this station watches somebody do that and it
wasn't good then either."

"Agreed."

"And *you*." Hana's voice did not rise. That was the thing about her; she had never once in
twenty years raised her voice in a call, and people found it much worse than shouting. "I
know what you are. I've been on the other end of the line from you and you are the best
person I have ever worked with and I have not worked with you since 2022 and I would like
to, and I would like that to be a professional arrangement and not a favour, and if it can't
be professional then I would rather you didn't ring."

The fridge made its noise.

"It's not going to be professional," said Ines.

"No," said Hana. "It isn't. I know. I'm not a fool, I have read the same report you have
and I have done the same arithmetic, and the reason I'm asking is not that I've forgiven
anybody, because there's nothing to forgive, you did the only thing there was. I'm asking
to be at the top of that entrance because I'm the last voice you hear, and that isn't a
sentence about the past, it's a sentence about the job. Can you understand the difference?"

"Yes," said Ines.

"Then I'll be in the layby," said Hana, "and you don't have to explain anything to me
before then, and you don't have to explain it to her either, and if you start explaining
it to her I will know what you're doing and I'll say so, and you'd hate that, so don't."

There was a pause, and it was a different quality of pause from the one before.

"You've got somebody with a camera," said Hana. "That's the phone call. Two weeks, not
three, and I'll take it whenever. And if she's a good egg I'll have her on my kit and if
she's a film-school disaster I'll have her on my kit anyway, because everyone who's ever
been anywhere started as a film-school disaster with a camera and a reason."

"She filmed Colin buying a helmet."

"Who's Colin."

"A man I sold a waist belt to."

"Right," said Hana, and Hana Vogel, who had been a rescue technician for eleven years and
had a reputation across four counties for being the calmest voice on any frequency in the
district, made a sound into a telephone in an empty station at twenty past nine on a Tuesday
that was not funny at all and did not entirely succeed in not being it.

"You've sold equipment for four years," she said.

"Fabiola's."

"Fabiola's," said Hana. "Right. Two weeks. Ring me on this number and let it ring."

It rang four times.

"Varga?"

"It's me. I forgot the thing I was going to ask you to ask me, and I've been standing here
for a minute working out whether I can pretend I rang back for a different reason, and I
can't, so: does the layby have a signal in rain."

"Has it always had a signal in rain?"

"You said you get a bar if you point it at the hill."

"I did say that."

"You did," said Ines. "I'm writing it down. Thank you."

"Don't thank me for having told you where to stand," said Hana. "That's not a favour.
That's just Tuesday."

The line went. Hana put the phone face down on the bench, and then picked it up again and
put it the other way up, which made no difference at all, and then did the last item on the
board, which was item eleven, which was the log, and which took four lines.

She wrote: *2209. Varga re contact, tel. 2wk. Layby.*

And then she sat for a while in a station with a hole in the pool table, with a piece of A4
on the wall that had been there four years and had not been acted on, and did not write
*SHELTER — ORDER* underneath it, because she had decided some time in 2022 that she was
going to order a shelter, and that if she had not ordered it by now the reason she had not
ordered it was not money, and she was not going to write that down in a log where Hana's
successor would read it.

She got up and put her coat on, and it was the wrong time of night to be putting a coat on
for that, and she did it anyway, because she had a layby to think about and a signal to
point at a hill, and a fortnight is a long time if you spend it in a station on your own
planning a car park.

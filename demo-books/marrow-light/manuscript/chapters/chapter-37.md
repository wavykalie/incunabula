# Chapter Thirty-Seven — The Last Conversation

She was twenty-four days late.

That is the first fact about the film and it is the fact that everything else in December is
made of: the school released the room on the nineteenth of November, as it had released it
since 1987, and Ottoline Marrow was in it until the thirteenth of December, on a card that
gave her access to the building until eleven at night, and the room that had a projector in
it had a woman in it with a deadline that had gone by three and a half weeks ago.

The edit was ninety-four hours.

She did not do it in the room. She did it in two places: the room, on a machine that made a
noise, for the picture; and a small room above a shop, on a laptop, at night, for the sound,
because the sound in a layby is nothing and the sound at a hundred and ninety metres is
everything and the two of them had to be put together in one place and there was only one
place in that county where a woman who sold equipment would let a film person sit on a
folding table at midnight with headphones on.

So that is where the film was finished, and that is a fact that would appear in exactly one
place in any account of it, and it does not appear in any account of it.

---

The shop closed at half past five on the thirteenth of December and the shutters came down at
twenty to six and the bell did not ring because the door had not opened since four.

"You've been here nine days," said Fabiola.

"Since the fourth."

"That's nine days. I have let a film person live in my back room for nine days, on a
folding table, with a laptop, and I have fed her, and I have not once asked to see the
film." Fabiola was counting the till and had been counting the till for forty-one years and
did not stop. "That's not restraint, love. That's a decision, and I made it, and I'd like you
to know that I made it deliberately and that I'm not going to be thanked for it, because if
you thank me for it then it was a favour and it wasn't, it was a condition."

"What's the condition?"

"That you finish it here," said Fabiola. "That's the whole of it. You finish it in this
building. Because I have been in this room for nineteen years with a woman who worked in it,
and there's a thing that happens when you do a job in a room where somebody else did it, and
you don't get to have it, and I'm not going to explain it any better than that, and I know
it's not a real reason."

"It's the only real reason I've had all month."

"Then I expect it in the film," said Fabiola, "and I don't care if it isn't, and I want it
in the film, and I've known since the second of December that I want it in the film, and I'm
going to tell you now so that nobody can say afterwards that I asked."

---

Ines came in at half past five with a box file under her arm, which she did every day, and
sat down on the stool behind the counter, which she had not done since March, and which
Fabiola noticed and said nothing about and would think about for years.

"You've heard the room's let go."

"I've heard. Congratulations."

"Don't." Ottoline did not look up from the laptop. "It's the correct outcome and I'm not
going to be a person who's sad about a room in a conversation about a film, and if I start
being sad about it now you'll have to comfort me and you'll do it well and it'll cost you an
hour I don't have."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's true and it's not fair." She took the headphones off. "Do you want to
see it?"

"Yes."

"Now?"

"No," said Ottoline. "Not now. That's a lie. Yes, now, obviously now, I've been not-asking
you that for four months and it's half past five on the thirteenth of December and I have
ninety-four hours and a room that closed on the nineteenth and if I don't ask you now I'm going
to not-ask you until February."

Ines put the box file down on a counter that was the colour of a bone.

"Where do you want me?"

"There." Ottoline turned the laptop. "There, with the light off, because I'm not doing this
in a shop with the lights on. It's ninety-four minutes. There's a section in the middle where
I can't tell you if it's good and I want you to tell me and I want you to be allowed to say no
to the whole thing, and I want you to understand that I'll take a no and I'll be all right and
I'll do the ninety-four minutes again in a different order if it helps."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I've had four months and a bad October," said Ottoline. "Yes."

---

The ninety-four minutes.

Nobody who has not seen it can be told about it, and the honest answer is that it is not a
very good film, and that it is the only honest thing a person of twenty-two has made about
that system in that decade, and that these two facts are not in tension.

It opens on a car park. It is a locked-off shot of the outside of a layby with a wall in it,
and it runs for four seconds, and it is the fourth shot in the film.

The first nine minutes are a shop. It is a shop, and a counter, and a woman of sixty-one
counting into a till, and a woman of thirty-eight putting a coil of line on a counter and
being told to put it in the drawer on the left if it clears, and it is very good, and it is
the best footage in the film, and it is all of it that happens in a room.

The middle is the Vell. It is the pitch, and the light at forty degrees onto a wall, and two
people working. There is no narration. There is a woman saying *that's the 'ninety-nine* and
there is a woman saying *log a fault at thirteen forty, shoulder, left* and there is a great
deal of a rope.

At forty-four minutes there is a car park and four people in it and a woman on a bonnet and a
woman on a wall and a woman standing at a door, and a man asks a question about competence
and gets an answer about a plate.

At fifty-one minutes there is a car park in the rain and two women and no camera, and it runs
for six minutes, and it is the only scene in the film that was not filmed.

And that is the thing, and it is the last thing, and it is the whole argument: **there are
six minutes in a film about a cave that were not filmed, and they are the six minutes the
film is actually about**, and they are there because a woman put a camera on a bonnet in the
rain and did not pick it up, and because the person in the film did not ask her to.

There is no shot of the plate in the fourth chamber. There is no shot of the cap. There is no
shot of a woman at two hundred and four metres in the dark.

There is a shot of a wall, in a layby, for four seconds, at the fourth shot, with a number on
it, and a voice saying *twelve*, and then nothing for nine seconds.

---

The tape stopped at 15:11.

Nobody said anything for a while. The shutters were down and the light was off and the shop
was a room with a shop in it.

"It's ninety-four minutes," said Fabiola, from behind the counter, who had not sat down.

"Yes."

"It's not the film."

"No," said Ottoline.

"No," said Fabiola, "it isn't," and she said it with something in her voice that was not
accusation and was not disappointment and was, Ines thought, the closest a person could get to
saying *now I know what the thing was*.

"I know," said Ottoline. "I want to say it properly, because you're the only two people in
this room who can say it and one of you's not going to say it at all and one of you has and
hasn't stopped."

"Then say it."

"It's not the film because there's no one in it." Ottoline put her hands flat on a counter
that was the colour of a bone, in a shop where a rack had once been cleaned every Thursday for
six years. "And I know that's my fault, and I know it's the *obvious* fault, and I want to
tell you both what I actually think it is, because I've had twenty-four days and it's not the
obvious thing.

"I could have made the film I said I was going to make in September. I could have had a woman
at a hundred and ninety metres talking about a fault in a wall and I could have cut in close
and put music under it and everybody would have watched it and it would have been
*extremely* good and it would have been about eleven minutes of a person being brilliant at
something they should not be doing.

"And it would have been a lie. Not a small lie. The whole film would have been a person
performing being watched, in a cave, at eighty metres, and I would have had to get her to do
it, and she would have done it for me, because she's spent four months in car parks
disagreeing with me about things and she has never once refused to do anything I actually
needed. That's the problem. She's *nice* at this job in a way that means I could have had
anything from her."

She turned round.

"And the only thing I have is six minutes of two women standing in the rain with a camera on
a bonnet that isn't running, and the reason I have that is that she wouldn't let me film her,
and the reason she wouldn't let me film her is that she was in 2022, and the reason she was in
2022 is that she went down too many times, and the reason she went down too many times is
that a number on a wall was correct and she trusted it, and the reason she trusted it is
that she maintained it herself with a brush on her own time, going past, with nothing to
carry."

Nobody in the shop said anything.

"So the whole film is a consequence of her not letting me film her," said Ottoline, "and
that's not a bug. I didn't find that out in a car park on the twenty-fourth. I found that out
in about November and I've been carrying it around a building for a month and I've got
ninety-four minutes and it's called *Marrow Light* and I don't think it's a very good film and
I want you both to know that I know that."

---

Ines Varga had not said anything for nine minutes.

That was the longest she had gone without speaking in front of another person in four years
that anybody in that shop knew of, and Fabiola Renn counted it, because she was counting the
til, and because she was also counting that.

"You've got a name for it," she said.

"Yes."

"Say it."

"*Marrow Light*," said Ottoline.

And Ines Varga sat on a stool behind a counter in a shop and heard a woman of twenty-two say
a word that was in a filename on a hard drive in a flat in this city and was going to be on
a screen in a room in an arts centre in a town forty minutes away in February, and did not
say anything at all about it for about nine seconds.

"That's not the book," said Fabiola.

"It's not anything, love. It's a working title and it was in my head in September because of
the word marrow in the survey language, which is the soft connective tissue, and because of
— " Ottoline stopped. "And because of my mother. And I haven't told anybody that and I said it
just now because I've been in this building for nine days and I can't do it any more."

"Your mother," said Fabiola.

"Surveyed it. In 1994. Vera Marrow, the index, the store room." Ottoline was looking at the
shutters. "She was on the team. She was there in 1994 and the plate's in her tape and she knew
the plate was wrong and she said it on a *tape*, in a car, in 2019, which is my mother, and
she did not want to put it in a document, and she stopped making films in 1997, and I have
never once asked her why and I asked her in October and she said no and then she said the
reason and I have not been able to think about the reason since."

"What was the reason?"

"She said it was a film about a woman wanting credit for being right." Ottoline's voice did
not change. "And it wasn't, and it was, and she was right about herself and wrong about the
water, and she has spent thirty-one years being a woman who worked it out and didn't say, and
I have spent four months filming the same system with the same plate and I did not do the
thing she didn't do, so I am not going to ask her again."

"That's a very good reason and it took you a year to get it."

"It took me four months and a woman in a car park," said Ottoline.

---

Ines got off the stool.

"Two things," she said, "and then I have to lock up, because it's Fabiola's shop and she does
the lock-up and I've never once done it and I wouldn't start now."

"Go on."

"One. It's not a very good film." She said it without any difficulty at all. "I don't think
it's a very good film, and I think about eleven minutes of it is genuinely good, and I think
the six minutes in the car park is the best thing anybody in this county has made about that
system, and I think both of those are true and I'm not going to pretend they aren't.

"And it's not the film. You said it and Fabiola said it and I want to add the other half of
it: it was never going to be the film. In September you told me you were making a film about
a cave and I told you that I could give you four sentences and no face, and you signed that,
and four sentences and no face is *all of it*, and everything since then has been you finding
out how much a person can say without showing you anything, and the answer has been about
six minutes and it has been worth more than four hundred hours of anybody else's footage in
this county, including mine, and I have been in films."

"That's the kindest thing anybody's said to me all year."

"It's not kindness. It's a craft note and you asked me for one in September and I've been
saving it up." Ines got her box file. "You're twenty-two. You will make a better film than
this in about six years and it will be about something else entirely, and the reason you'll be
able to is that you made this one and it wasn't very good and you finished it anyway."

"Two things," said Ottoline. "You said one."

"Two. The other one is a question and I want to ask it now while there's no camera on and
while we're both tired, because I've been not-asking it since the fifteenth of March."

"Go on."

"You deleted eleven seconds," said Ines. "On the third of December. Between the pitch and the
car park. I know because I was in the room and I know what the machine does and I know what
the eleven seconds was, and I'm not asking what's in it, and I'm not asking why, and I want
you to know that I know, and that's it, that's the whole of the second thing, and I've been
carrying it about for ten days and it's the only thing I've got that I can't put in a
sentence."

The shop was dark and the shutters were down and the bell did not ring.

Ottoline Marrow sat in front of a laptop in a back room of a shop and did not say anything for
about eleven seconds, which is a long time, and which is the length of a thing that has been
deleted.

"Yes," she said.

Ines Varga put a box file under her arm and walked to the door and stopped, the way people
stop at a door in that shop, and did not turn round.

"The film," she said. "February. The arts centre in Kelder. It's a Thursday and they do a
thing where you talk afterwards and they call it a Q and A and it's four minutes long and
nobody comes."

"I'll be there."

"No, you won't."

"I'll be there," said Ines Varga. "It's a Thursday and it's an hour's drive and I'm not
going to sit in a car outside for an hour and an hour is exactly the kind of thing I've been
doing for four years, and I've been told about it, and I'd like to stop."

"Fine."

"And I want to say one more thing, and it's a professional one, and it's the last one, and
then I'm going home."

"Go on."

"You called it *Marrow Light*," said Ines. "That's a bone. That's a bone in a body and it's
also the connective tissue and it's also what a cave is made of in a river, and you've named
a film about a limestone system after the inside of the thing, and that's a good name, and it
is not your mother's name, and you have used your mother's name in a filename for four months
and you have never once said it out loud until the second of December when you said it in front
of two people in a shop and then said something else."

"Which is what I did."

"Yes," said Ines. "Which is what you did. Go and see her. Not in February. Go this month,
before you put it in a programme, because there's a thing that happens to a woman when her
daughter's first film is finished and the daughter has made it about the thing she did, and it
takes about three years to say whatever it is, and the three years is the only bit in this
whole thing that you can actually do anything about."

She went out into the street.

She did not lock up. She had never locked up. She had done it once, in nineteen years, in
March, and had put a key back on the board, and it had been noticed by two people and never
mentioned by either of them.

---

Fabiola Renn counted the till.

"Ninety-four minutes," she said.

"Yes."

"And it isn't the film."

"No."

"Right," said Fabiola, and squared the notes, and put them in the drawer, and shut the drawer,
and looked at a laptop on a folding table in a back room of a shop that had been her for
forty-one years. "Then there's a great deal to do about it and no room, and I'd like to know
what you want to do about it, and I'd like to know tonight, because I close on the twenty-third
and after that I'm not closing on anything for a fortnight."

"You close on the twenty-third?"

"I close on the twenty-third for my holiday, which I booked in March, before any of this."
Fabiola looked at the shutters. "I booked a holiday in March and I've been not-telling people
about it since March. It's fourteen nights and I'm going nowhere in particular and I've been
saving it for a thing I didn't know about, and I've been not-telling five people in this
county about it since March because I wasn't going to have to say the reason out loud."

"What's the reason?"

"That I'd be sat in a shop on my own for a fortnight in December," said Fabiola Renn, "with
nothing to clean."

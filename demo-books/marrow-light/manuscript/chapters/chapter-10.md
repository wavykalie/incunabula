# Chapter Ten — The Morning After

The debrief was eleven minutes long because there was nothing to debrief.

That is the first thing a person learns about a professional debrief and nobody ever tells
you it in advance: the good ones are short. You go in, you do the thing, you come out, and
the entire content of the conversation is confirming that everyone agrees about what
happened, and if that takes eleven minutes then it was a good day.

Tove said it, wrote it down, and closed the file, and that was the whole of the professional
outcome of a two-hour-ten descent by a woman who had not been underground in four years.

"What happens now?" said Ottoline.

"Now the survey happens," said Tove, "which is a different thing from what happened, and
takes eleven weeks, and I need you out of the way of it for six of them."

"I'm not in the way."

"You're going to be in the way by about the second week," said Tove, "because you will want
to come and film it, and you can't, and the reason you can't is that eleven weeks is eleven
weeks and a survey with a film in it is a survey that takes nineteen and comes out worse.
That's not a moral position, it's just what happens."

"Then I film the access days."

"The access days are for a film, and the film is you, and the access days are separate from
the survey." Tove put the file in the bag. "How much have you got?"

"Enough for a treatment and a pitch and a rough cut of the part that's about Ines not being
filmed, which is a bit of nothing, and about a hundred and forty hours of me asking
questions in a car park."

"That's a good film," said Tove.

"You haven't seen it."

"I don't have to see it. Everybody films the car park. The car park's where the film's
actually in, and everybody who says they're making a film about a cave is making a film
about a car park with a cave in it, and the ones who admit it make the better ones." She
zipped the bag. "Your problem isn't that you don't have enough footage. Your problem is
that you have no reason to stop, and nobody's going to stop you, and you'll be shooting
October in a car park in February."

Ottoline wrote that down and did not cross it out and did not want to.

---

The film got scheduled on the Thursday, by email, in eleven lines.

*The school will hold a room until the 20th. Insurance certificate to follow. Crew of three:
you, your sound, and a second camera if you can get one on the budget, which you cannot.
October 9th to the 21st inclusive, weather-dependent, twelve access days maximum, six
expected. Your consultant has cleared the technical days. Sign here.*

Ottoline read it on the Thursday at nine in the morning on a corridor wall and then read the
first line again four times.

*Your consultant.*

She had a technical consultant. She had a woman in a shop eleven kilometres away who was on
her books until the sixth of October, and there was a line in an email that said *your
consultant* in the same tone as *your sound recordist*, and it had been written by a
departmental administrator who had never met either of them and would not have understood
the difference if it had been explained.

She went and got her notebook out at half past nine in a corridor with nobody in it and
wrote:

*Things that are true now that were not true on Tuesday:*

*1. I have a consultant.*
*2. She is on the school insurance as a named technical person, which means somebody read her
name, which means somebody typed it into a system.*
*3. There are twelve access days, which means there is a schedule, which means there are
dates, which means there is a point after which this stops being a film and starts being a
set of obligations that I have undertaken to two institutions and one woman who signed
something in a stockroom in September.*

She read number three back and it did not get any better and she did not cross it out.

Then, underneath, in different handwriting, which is a thing she had started doing in
September and had not examined:

*4. He hasn't rung back.*

She looked at that for a while.

Hana Vogel had not rung back. It had been six days. There was no reason on earth for her to
have rung back, and Ines had not asked her to, and ines had left a message on the Tuesday
evening that said *let it ring* and had then not let it ring for six days, which was a
thing she had done before, on purpose, four years ago, to a person who had been on the other
end of it.

She read the message she had left. She read the words *let it ring* in her own handwriting on
her own phone and had a small cold feeling in a place that is not really a place, and then
she got on with her day, because getting on with the day was what she had done for four years
and she was extremely good at it and had almost nothing else.

---

Fabiola had the shop to herself until eleven, and used the time to do the thing she did every
Thursday, which was to take the rack out of the corner and clean it.

Not the one in the window. The one in the back, the one that had been on the floor since 2019
and that nobody had put anything in for six years and that she cleaned every Thursday because
it was the thing in the shop that had belonged to Solveig and that she could not sell and
would not throw away and could not look at every day.

It took forty minutes. It took forty minutes because it was a rack — a full caving rack, the
old kind, all the metal, and every piece of it had to be taken off and looked at and wiped
and put back, and the buckles needed oil, and the buckles always needed oil, and there were
two on it that had been replaced in 2021 and two that had not been replaced at all and were
the same two Solveig had bought in 2004.

She oiled them.

She did not think about anything while she did it, which was the arrangement, and which had
held for four years, and which was the only thing about that rack she had managed to keep
intact.

At eleven, Ines came in with a box file under her arm, because they had agreed at nine that
the access-day paperwork would come through the shop for the address, and because a thing that
goes through a shop is a thing that exists, and both of them knew that, and neither of them
said it.

"Twelve days," said Ines.

"Twelve days."

"Twelve days in October with a film crew on my system, and you've had the insurance
certificate request sitting on the counter since Friday."

"I have."

"You could have sent it."

"I could have."

Ines put the box down.

"Fab."

"Don't," said Fabiola, in exactly the tone she had used in the shop on Thursday, and Ines
stopped, and stood there, and did not push it, and the not-pushing was the thing that had
kept this shop running for nineteen years and that neither of them had ever once discussed.

"Right," said Ines.

"Right," said Fabiola, and went back to her screen, and then, after about forty seconds,
said: "Hana rang the shop."

Ines did not turn round.

"Saturday morning. Asked if you were in. I said you were in on a Monday and she said she'd
ring on a Monday." Fabiola's fingers were on the keys and were not typing. "I said you were
in. She said thank you. And then she said, and I want you to have it exactly, because I've
been thinking about it since Saturday and I don't want to get it wrong: she said, *tell her
the layby's fine in rain, and that I'll be in it anyway.*"

The rain came on the window. It came on the window the way it did, which was a thing Ines
could have identified from a recording.

"Right," she said.

"That's it. That's all she said."

"That's all she needed to say," said Ines, and picked the box back up, and went out into the
rain with it, and walked eleven metres to the pillar box, and put it in, and stood there in
the rain for about a minute and a half, which was longer than putting a letter in a box
takes.

Then she went back inside and Fabiola offered her a cup of tea, and it was eleven in the
morning, and they drank it, and neither of them mentioned the sixth of October.

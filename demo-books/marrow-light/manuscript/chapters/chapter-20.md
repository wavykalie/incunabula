# Chapter Twenty — Serious

"Before any of that, I want to be checked," said Tove.

Ottoline put her pen down.

"Checked."

"It's not a favour and I'm not being kind and I want to explain why immediately, because if I
let you take it as a favour you'll accept it for the wrong reason and turn up half-trained and
get somebody hurt." Tove had a folder and she was not opening it. "Anyone on my line goes on
my line. That's the rule and it isn't about you, it's about the Vell, and the Vell doesn't
know who's filming what. Two people on a line, one above one below, and the one above is the
only thing between the one below and a bad afternoon. That's the whole of it."

"I understand that."

"Do you. Because the reason I'm asking and not telling is that you're going to be on the
surface end of that line on the nineteenth of November, and if you're on the surface end then
you are not a filmmaker, you're the person a caver's life is in the hands of, and those are
two different jobs and one of them doesn't stop to check framing."

Ottoline looked at her.

"You're offering to check me."

"I'm offering to spend two days with you and put you through a day on a system that isn't the
Vell, and it will be miserable, and you will be cold, and I will not once be gentle about it,
and at the end of it I'll either sign a piece of paper or I won't, and the piece of paper
goes on the certificate and the certificate is what lets you stand in a layby and be
responsible for a person."

"Why are you doing it?"

"Because Hana asked me to," said Tove. "And because I'd have asked me to."

"That's not—"

"It's not a favour, it's a chain, and the chain is: a rescue technician thinks you're
worth checking, and I agree with her, and rather than say I agree with her on a telephone I
have said I'll do it, and the telephone was too easy and I've had a bad month of too easy."

Ottoline Marrow had spent four months being encouraged by people who meant well and who
wanted something, and had learned to smell the two, and had not learned anything at all about
this, because this did not smell like either.

"You genuinely think I'm worth the two days," she said.

"No," said Tove. "I think you're competent and I think you're careful and I think you've read
enough to know the questions, and none of those three things is the same as being good on a
rope, and I'd rather find that out in a system that isn't the one we're both thinking about."

"What's the system?"

"Pitchdale," said Tove. "It's ugly, it's wet, it's got a bad squeeze in it that I put in
myself in 2011 and cried about for an hour, and there's nothing in it that can kill you. If
you can do Pitchdale you can do the entrance to the Vell. If you can't do Pitchdale then
nobody on this earth should be on the surface end of anything on the nineteenth of November,
including me, and I'll say so in writing."

There was a long moment.

"I nearly said no," said Ottoline.

"Why."

"Because I thought it was condescension." She said it evenly, without embarrassment, which
was a discipline and cost her something. "I thought you'd heard about a film and decided the
film needed checking up on, and that you'd do two days out of politeness and I'd have to
spend them being grateful and polite and I hadn't got eleven hours in me this month to spend
being polite."

"And now?"

"Now you're standing there telling me your reason is a chain and that you cried in a squeeze
in 2011, and nobody cries in a squeeze in front of a person they've decided to patronise."

Tove Marsk almost smiled.

"Pitchdale's not till the second of November," she said. "And I'll tell you the truth about
Pitchdale, since you asked honestly: it's two days and the first one is nine hours of walking
before you're allowed to touch anything. Nine hours. In November. I do it because the walking
is the job and everybody wants to skip the walking."

"I'll do the walking."

"You'll hate the walking."

"I'll do the walking," said Ottoline, "and I'm going to be good at the walking, and you're
going to find that annoying, and I want to warn you now that if you don't tell me what
getting it wrong actually looks like I'll do it wrong in November."

That was the moment, and both of them knew it was the moment, and neither of them said so.

---

They went to the Vell on the twenty-second, which was not the nineteenth and was not part of
any plan, and which happened because Tove went to look at something in the fourth chamber for
her own reasons and Ottoline was in a car park and said, in a text, *I'm free and I've got
the van*, and Tove read the text three times and answered, *That's a terrible reason to offer
a lift and I'm accepting it.*

They were on the hill at ten past eight and in by ten.

Ottoline carried two boxes. She carried them the six hundred metres herself and carried
them down the slope to the entrance, which Tove watched and said nothing about, and which was
the correct response, and which cost Ottoline something to be given.

"There's a thing in the fourth I need to look at," said Tove, at the top of the pitch. "It's
not important. It's a station that I put in 2019 and I've never been back to it and I want to
know if it's still where I put it, because if it's not then the last eight years of my own
work is a fiction and I'd rather know on a Tuesday."

"Can I come?"

"No."

"Can I come as far as the fourth?"

"No."

"Can I come to ninety and stay on the—"

"*No,*" said Tove, and then, less hard: "You can come to the top of the pitch and you can
hand me the box and you can go back up, and that is not me being cruel, that is me being
straight with you, and the straightness is a courtesy. If I said *yes* to ninety then next
November I'd have said yes to a hundred and a half, and the reason people get hurt in caves is
that they have agreed to a sequence of small yeses on days when they were having a good time."

"Right," said Ottoline. "That's the best answer anyone's given me all month."

"It's the only answer. Go on."

She went on. She went back up the field and sat on the bonnet of the van and did not get out
her notebook, and did not look at a photograph on a phone, and sat there for two hours and
fifty minutes in the rain without doing any work at all, which she had never done in her life,
and which was the most useful thing that happened to her that month.

At one o'clock a woman came down the field at a speed that was not a walk and not a run,
which is a thing rescue people do, and Hana Vogel went past the top of the pitch and past
Ottoline without stopping and without speaking and got a radio off Tove at about six seconds
from the entrance, and said something short, and Tove's voice came back up the hill and
Ottoline heard all of it because the hill is a bowl and the wind was in the wrong direction.

"—no, I'm fine, it's the leg, I've had it, it's the leg—"

"—not going to be more than a minute—"

"—right, yeah, that's fair, that's fair—"

and then six seconds of nothing, and then Hana's voice again from the hill, louder, and the
word *Varga* in it, and Tove's voice going quiet in a way that two people in a car park four
kilometres away could not hear and did not need to.

The conversation went on for about four minutes. It was, Ottoline learned later and much
against her will, about a fault in a radio and nothing else, and it involved no names at all,
and it ended with Tove saying *copy* and Hana saying *that's the first time in two years
you've said copy* and Tove saying something that made a rescue technician laugh audibly on a
public frequency.

Then a silence, and then Hana said, not into the radio but standing on the grass in the rain
with her hands on her hips, loud enough to carry thirty metres and not thirty-five:

"I'll be in the layby at three, I'm bringing the good flask, and if either of you says
*coffee* I'm going to be unpleasant about it."

And Tove, from the top of a pitch, said, "Copy."

---

At three o'clock they were in the layby and the flask was on the bonnet and nobody had said
the word, and the four of them sat in a wet layby at the end of a Tuesday and drank something
hot out of a proper flask with cups on it because Hana Vogel had opinions about cups and had
brought four.

Ottoline had a question she had been carrying for nine days, and she had decided twice that
she would not ask it, and she asked it.

"How long have you two worked together?"

"Nineteen years," said Hana.

"Sixteen," said Tove.

"Sixteen years' work, nineteen years'—"

"Sixteen years that I was on the ground for, in a system, in the same county, and the other
three are years of letters," said Tove, and drank, and handed the cup back. "You can have
both numbers. People always only want one."

Ottoline looked at the two of them.

"Which of you is better?"

The rain came into the layby off the wing mirror.

"That's a filthy question," said Hana, "and it's the right one, and I'm not answering it,
and Tove is going to answer it because she can't not."

"The honest answer is that it depends what you need," said Tove. "For this film, for a
fortnight, in this weather, with a surface party of three and a line of fifty metres — I am
better. I'm fitter, I've got more of the system, and I'm better at the boring part, which is
the part that decides whether everybody comes back. If somebody's leg goes at a hundred and
twenty metres then the person on the surface has about ninety seconds to get the right
decision made and I've been the one making it for sixteen years."

"And if it's Hana."

"Then Hana is better and I'll say so on a form." Tove looked at her cup. "Hana's better at
people. I'm better at rock. That's not a figure of speech, it's the whole of it, and if
you've got a crew in a wet layby for a fortnight then you need both and you need to know
which one you've got the hard day."

Hana Vogel, who had been in the back of a van for most of her working life and who had been
told a great many things about herself by people who had not been careful, put her cup down
on the bonnet and said, "That's the first time anyone's said that to me in a job and meant
it."

"It's true."

"It's true and nobody says it, because the job's supposed to be one thing, and it isn't, and
everybody knows it isn't, and everybody goes round being extremely nice about it." She picked
the cup up again. "Right. Who wants the survey report, then, because I've got it in the van and
I've had it in the van since Friday and I have been waiting for somebody to ask me for it,
and I want to say that I have not read it, and I want to say that I could not stop reading the
first page, and I want to say that if you give it to me now I'm going to be unpleasant about
the contents."

Tove put the cup down.

"No," she said. "Not yet."

Tove's answer took about a second and a half and Ottoline watched it happen, and she was
fifty years too young to read a face that well and she read it perfectly.

That was the thing about that half-second. Ines Varga had said *not yet* in a stockroom in
September with her hands flat on a table, and Tove Marsk had just said *not yet* about a
document in a layby with a cup in her hand, and they were the same two words in the same flat
voice from two different women, and neither of them knew the other had ever said it, and
Ottoline Marrow sat in a car park in the rain and understood that she was in a place where
people said *not yet* and meant *eventually, and not in front of you, and I have not decided
how*.

"All right," said Hana. "Not yet."

She did not ask what it meant. That was, in the end, the thing that made her what she was,
and it was not in any document, and there was no reason for it, and the woman who had spent
forty-one evenings with a four-page report could not have taught it to her in a year.

---

The last twenty minutes of that afternoon is the reason Tove Marsk read the 1994 material,
and it happened because of an accident with a flask.

There were four cups and there were four people and the flask had a fifth cup in it, in the
lid, which Hana always carried and never counted, and nobody had counted, and so at some
point between two and three o'clock there were five cups on the bonnet of a van in a layby in
the rain and no way of knowing whose was whose.

Ottoline put a cup down and got out her phone and photographed the layby.

Not the people. The layby — the wing mirror, the grass, the wall on the far side, the field
going up to a gate.

It was a framing thing and it took four seconds, and then she was putting the phone away and
Hana said, not unkindly, "That's a nice shot."

"It's a layby."

"It's a layby with a hill in it and a van in it and a wall, and you framed it low, which most
people don't." Hana squinted at it. "The wall's doing something, though. What's on the wall?"

"Nothing."

"There's a plate on that wall. Look at that, that's a plate, that's a *thing*." Hana was
already standing up, because Hana Vogel could not leave a thing alone. "That's a survey plate.
That's a gauge. Ines, do you— "

She stopped.

Hana Vogel was holding a cup in the rain on the far side of a layby with a wall eleven metres
away, and Ines Varga was not there, and had not been there since the fourteenth of October,
and would not be there until the nineteenth of November, and the woman who maintained the
plate on that wall was a person Hana had not spoken to in four years.

"Sorry," said Hana, and sat down.

Tove had not moved. She was looking at the wall.

"There's a plate on that wall," she said.

"Yep," said Hana.

"In a field entrance. On the outside."

"Yep."

"That's not the gauge," said Tove. "The gauge is fifty metres in. That's not the gauge. That's
— somebody's put a copy on the outside, isn't it. For visibility. So people coming down
don't have to go fifty metres to find out what the water's doing."

Nobody said anything.

"It's her," said Hana quietly. "Isn't it. It's Ines's. She did it. She did the one inside
and she did that one so you could see it from the road, and she did both on her own time for
four years, and nobody ever—"

"That's what the report says," said Tove. "*Local record, maintained.* Singular. One person,
one plate, one system." She was not looking at Hana; she was looking at a wall. "I read that
phrase forty-one times and I thought it meant a *job.*"

The rain came into the layby.

"Forty-one times," said Hana.

"What?"

"Nothing," said Hana. "That's a lot of times."

And Tove Marsk, who had come to the Vell that Tuesday to look at a station she had put in
2019, drove back down the track in a van with a flask and four cups and an accident, and went
home, and did not go out.

She did not go out because she had a box. It was not in her house; it was at the association,
in a store room, and it had been there since 1997, and it was a box of 1994 material with an
index in a dead woman's handwriting in it, and it had been released on the Wednesday by a
woman who had given a terrible reason and had not pretended it was a good one, and Tove had
been asked to look at it for work by a film student and had said that she would.

She got it out at half past nine in the evening on a Tuesday in October and put it on her own
kitchen table and opened the box file.

The index was four pages, and it was in handwriting that got worse as it went down the page,
and it was a working index and not a document, and it had times against everything, and at the
bottom of the third page, in the middle of a list of equipment, without any introduction or
comment of any kind, Vera Marrow had written:

*Plate. 40 in x. Check every trip. The number goes off by one after rain and I have never
found out why and I am not going to guess in a document. Ask somebody who goes.*

And Tove Marsk sat at a kitchen table in October with a four-page index in front of her and
understood that she had just been told, by a woman of twenty-eight, in a store room, in
1995, to go and ask somebody who goes.

She did not sleep much, and what she did instead was go through nine hours of tape on a
machine in a room above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings, with a stopwatch, for work,
and at about two in the morning she stopped the tape forty minutes from the end and sat in
the dark and said, out loud, in an empty building:

"Oh," said Tove Marsk. "Oh, you're joking."

# Chapter Thirty-Three — First

The rack came out of the van at ten past nine in the morning and it took four minutes to put
on, and Ines Varga stood in a layby on a hillside in a clear light being a different shape.

That is the whole of it and it is worth being exact about, because the film is about it and
the film does not have it: she is not a different person; she is the same woman who sold a
waist belt to a man in chapter one. What changes is her outline. Twenty-two kilos of
aluminium and a shape that means she cannot turn her head quickly, and once the rack is on,
the woman standing in a car park is a piece of equipment that a person is inside, and the
person is much harder to look at.

Nobody said anything about it. Nineteen people were in that layby and not one of them said
anything about it, and this was not politeness; it was that they had all worked in or
supported a trade in which the equipment is the person, and the equipment is put on, and the
putting-on is not an event, and the most frightening version of this profession is a woman
putting on a rack in front of a camera for the first time in four years and nineteen strangers
understanding immediately that they should not be watching.

---

"Tove."

"Going first."

That was the whole of the exchange and it lasted about four seconds, and it happened at nine
forty-four in the morning at the top of the pitch.

Tove Marsk had gone first on the nineteenth. Tove Marsk went first on the second, on the
ninth, on the sixth, on the fourteenth, and on every one of the nine days in October, because
she had offered it in September as a scheduling arrangement and it had been a scheduling
arrangement for five weeks and had become something else by November, and nobody had revisited
it because there had never been a reason to.

At nine forty-four on the morning of the twenty-eighth of November there was no reason
either. The plan said Tove first. The certificate said Tove second. The line was fifty metres
of wool with a person on each end and the system below them was the Vell with a cap on it and
twelve hours on a plate that said twelve.

Tove was in a sit harness with a rack on and a helmet on and had been ready since half past
nine.

"Okay," said Tove.

Ines Varga went down the pitch.

---

She did not explain it. That is the fact about this morning that four people went back over
for years and that none of them ever satisfactorily explained to anybody, including, on two
occasions, to each other.

The reasons that were available were all available to Tove. It was her certificate. She had
been on the line eleven times in nine weeks and she knew the passage and the pitch better than
anybody in the county. She had maintained the plate. She was the one whose number had been
wrong.

None of those were why, and Tove Marsk — who had known Ines Varga for sixteen years and had
never once been able to predict her, and had said in a room in October that you always knew —
stood at the top of a pitch for about forty seconds after the rope had gone and could not
think of a single thing to say about it.

Then she went down, because it was her turn, and because the rope was live, and because a
surgeon does not stand still.

---

The work was the work.

That is the whole of what happened at a hundred metres, at a hundred and forty, and at a
hundred and ninety, on the morning of the twenty-eighth of November, and it is the reason the
last block of this book is not a catastrophe.

Two women did survey work in a system they both knew, on a line, with a cap they both
respected, for three hours and fifty minutes, and they did it at the rate that competent
people do it, which is not fast, and which involves a great deal of sitting down.

The second pitch was rigged at two past twelve and there was a photograph of it and nobody
disputes that it is a good photograph. There is a set of prusiks at the top of it that Tove
Marsk put in in 2019 and which she checked and re-dressed and said was fine and which were
fine.

At a hundred and ninety, at twenty past twelve, the fault in the wall did the thing that a
fault in a wall does, and Ines Varga heard it — the third of a second, every forty seconds,
under her feet — and neither of them mentioned it, and neither of them mentioned it because
there was no reason to, and because a sound at a hundred and ninety in a system where you have
been eleven times in nine weeks is not the same sound as a sound at a hundred and ninety in a
system where you have been once, and both of those women knew that, and one of them had
learned it the expensive way.

"Ottoline."

"Go ahead."

"It's making the noise."

"I know," said Tove, on the frequency, from fifty metres above. "It does that. It's the fault.
It's been doing that since 2019 and it's in the third edition on page two hundred and eleven
and I've never once seen a survey put it in a report because it isn't a hazard, it's just a
noise."

"I'm not writing a hazard. I'm writing a noise."

"Write the noise," said Tove. "It's page two hundred and eleven and it'll be in the fourth
edition and I'd like it there with your name against it, because nobody's ever written it
down and it sounds like a person."

At a hundred and ninety Ines Varga sat on a rock that was not a rock and put her hand on a
wall that was a fault, and the water came out of it about a hand's width at a time, and
thought, for about four seconds, about a woman in a car park in the rain on the sixteenth of
October asking what water sounded like.

She did not transmit that. There was a rule about what went down a line and she had never
written it down and it was the single most consistently observed thing she had ever done in
four years of not being in caves.

---

The light came down at twenty past twelve at forty degrees onto a wall that leaned in a metre
over four metres.

"It's there," said Ines. "It's doing the thing."

"It's doing the thing," said Tove.

Twenty-eight minutes of it, and then it was gone, and the fourth pitch in 1994 was exactly
where it was and had been for thirty years, and a woman of thirty-eight stood in front of it
and did not go any further, because the cap was at a hundred and ninety and the fourth pitch
was at two hundred and four and there was a woman on a radio forty metres above who had
imposed the cap without authority and had written down that she was imposing it, and there
was a supervisor at the top of a field with a flask who had not been able to sleep on the
twenty-seventh and who would have known, within about four hours, that the cap had been
broken, and who had broken it herself in writing on the previous evening.

Fourteen metres.

"Two hundred," said Ines.

Tove did not answer for about two seconds, which is a long time on a radio.

"Two hundred," she said.

"I'm at two hundred and four."

"Copy. Two hundred and four. That's fourteen past the cap and I'm going to say something about
that and I want it on the log, and the something is: I'm not going to stop you, and I'm not
going to follow you, and I'm going to sit here at a hundred and ninety and tell you what time
it is every ten minutes until you come back up, and if that isn't a professional arrangement
then somebody in an office can ring me and I'll take it."

"Copy."

"And Varga."

"Yes."

"Say what you're doing out loud, because the log's got to have it in and because I'm not
qualified to guess and because there's a woman on a surface who's going to have to write a
sentence in about four hours."

Ines Varga stood in a chamber that she had not been in since the fourteenth of September 2022,
at two hundred and four metres, in the light off, with her hand on a wall.

"I'm looking at a plate," she said.

---

At two hundred and four metres the plate had one number on it.

Not five. One.

There were four numbers under it and the fifth was gone, and the four had been struck out and
painted over, and the top one was new, and the paint was the same white, and the hand was the
same unsteady hand, and the number was 12.

The number was 12, and it had been 12 for seven days, and it was correct, and it had been
correct since the twenty-first, and the four underneath it were 14, 16, 19 and 22, and the
intervals between them had been a rise and four hours and two hours, and the fifth was gone
because seven years of paint and a damp wall had taken it.

There was nothing else in the chamber. There was no note, and there was no message, and there
was no second plate, and there was nothing on the wall anywhere that anybody had ever put
there on purpose except a number that a woman went in and painted with a brush, on her own
time, going past, with nothing to carry.

Ines Varga got out her light and put it on the plate and looked at it for about two minutes,
which is a very long time, and did not touch it, and did not photograph it, and did not say
anything at all, which was the first silence of her professional life on a live line.

Then she said, into a radio, in a voice that four people had never heard before and that one
of them would hear again:

"That's my handwriting," she said.

"Copy," said Tove Marsk, from a hundred and ninety metres, in a chamber below a field, on the
twenty-eighth of November. "Say it again for the log."

"That's my handwriting. That's my hand. I painted that this year, and I painted the one
underneath, and I painted the one under that, and I've been in here every week since the
fourth of November and I have read it as twenty-two every single time, and it says twelve, and
it has said twelve every time, and I have been reading a number that was correct and going
down, and the number was *correct*, and I did not once stop to ask who was painting it."

"Varga—"

"I'm not finished. I want to say it properly because there's a recording of a woman asking me
a question in a car park and I owe her an answer and I'm going to give it to a radio in a
cave and that's the only opportunity I've got for four more years, and I'm not going to waste
it on a muddled version."

"Take the time you need," said Tove.

"There was a fifth number," said Ines. "Under the fourteen. It's gone. Seven years of paint.
And I put it there in 2022, on the fourteenth of September, at about eleven in the morning, and
it said six."

Silence.

"Six," said Tove.

"It said six, and I'd been down eleven times in nine weeks, and the last rise before that was
at about five, so six was *right*, and I was two chambers too deep at twenty past two, and I
came out, and I told them the roof came in." Ines's voice did not change at all, and it was
doing the thing that Hana's voice did, which was the thing that the people who are good at
this do, which is to stay completely level while the thing they are saying happens.
"The roof came in. It did. I heard it stop. It wasn't the whole of it and it wasn't the wrong
of it and it was the first true thing that came out of my mouth and I have been telling it
for four years like it was the answer, and it was the *roof*, and the water came up at half
past two and the water had come up before and had gone down and had come up again and I was
at two hundred and I had been at two hundred for an hour and I had chosen two hundred, in
September, on a Friday, when I knew the cycle, when I had the number in my hand and it said
six."

"That's the sentence," said Tove Marsk.

"That's the sentence," said Ines. "I chose it. Not the cave. Me. On a Friday, with a plate in
my hand that said the right number, because the right number said the window was open and I
wanted the window."

"And Solveig."

"Solveig was on the line. Solveig was on the line at fifteen metres and she was the one who
said *we're going to be late out* and I said *we're fine*." There was a sound on the radio
that was a person putting their hand over a microphone, or not over it, or only half over it.
"I said we're fine. I have said the roof came in for four years, and she said *we're going to
be late out*, and I said we're fine, and that is the last thing I said to her, and I have not
been able to say it out loud in this county since the Thursday after, and you have just heard
me say it to a radio in a cave and I would be grateful if nobody repeated it to me."

"Nobody's going to repeat it," said Tove.

"Time," said Ines. "What time is it."

"Twenty-five past twelve."

"Ninety minutes on the line. I'm coming up."

"You've fourteen past the cap and you're coming up."

"I've been past the cap," said Ines Varga, "and you didn't stop me, and you told me the time
every ten minutes, and I heard you every time, and I'm coming up."

That was the whole of the descent. It was fifty-four minutes past the cap and it was four
hours and eleven minutes of work and it was the best descent any of them had ever been part
of, and it was also, in the words of a woman in a car park in the rain three weeks earlier, a
second problem.

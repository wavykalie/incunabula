# Chapter Thirty-Four — Ahead

She was forty minutes up the pitch when Hana Vogel said, "Varga to surface," in a voice that
did not change register at all.

That was the thing about Hana Vogel and it was the thing Ines Varga had spent four years not
thinking about, and it was this: her voice did not change. It was the same voice it had been
at half past eight that morning, and the same voice it had been when it read twelve out loud
in a car park, and the professional register in which a rescue technician speaks is a
registered voice, and she used it for everything, and the result on the nineteenth of November
was that a woman eighty metres under a hillside could not tell from the radio whether anything
had happened, and had to work it out from the time.

There was no register on the radio at 12:49. That was the information.

"What's the number."

"Twenty-two hundred, and that's the number, and I'm not saying it twice." Hana was reading it
out of a book and not off a wall, which was a rule she had made for herself that morning and
had told nobody about. "The plate's in the film. The plate said twelve at nine o'clock and
it's twenty-two hundred and it's going to be at six hundred and then it's going to be at four
and then it is going to stop being a number and start being a rate, and I've been watching
that catchment since nine o'clock and it's doing ninety millimetres an hour and it is not
doing ninety millimetres an hour *now*, it's doing about a hundred and forty."

"Copy."

"Varga, I need you to hear the shape of that sentence. The catchment has been doing ninety
since half past ten and it has gone to a hundred and forty in the last ten minutes, and
that's the shape of a rise, and the rise takes about an hour and a half to come down to the
system, and it is one-fifteen now."

"I hear it."

"Good. Because I want to say that I am not asking you to come up and I am not telling you to
come up, and I am going to be on this frequency every ten minutes, and if you have a
decision to make you are going to make it against the time and not against my voice, and I
want that understood by both of you, because the two people I am talking to are extremely
different problems."

Tove's voice came down. It did not come down the way it usually came down.

"What's your depth."

"Eighty."

"Say it again."

"Eighty. Top of the pitch is ten below me and I've not moved in twenty minutes and I'm at
eighty."

"Log it. Everybody log it." Tove's voice was doing something Ines had never heard on a
frequency from Tove Marsk and had heard exactly once before, in a different system, in 2017,
and had not thought about since. "Vega, confirm. The catchment rate is a hundred and forty and
rising and the Vell will take water in about an hour. We are sixty-five minutes from the
pitch on a good move and a hundred and ten on a bad one, and the pitch is at eighty. Say again
that we're sixty-five minutes from the pitch."

"Copy. Sixty-five minutes on a good move, a hundred and ten on a bad one, pitch at eighty."

"Log it," said Tove. "Varga, you're not moving. Don't move. Not a thing. I want you exactly
where you are, and I want your light off, and I want you to sit down if you can find somewhere
to sit, and I want you to not answer the radio for about four minutes, and I want Hana to talk
to you about the weather the entire time."

"I can talk about the weather," said Hana, "and I will, and it will be extremely boring, and
it's going to be about eleven minutes long, and you're going to hate it."

---

She did talk about the weather.

She talked about a haar off the shoulder that had arrived at ten past twelve and gone and
would be back at four, and she talked about the temperature on the pitch, which was eight
degrees, and about a thing that had happened in this county in 1998 which involved a car park
and eleven people and a decision made at about this time of day, and which she told as a story
with a shape and no lesson, and which Ines Varga listened to at eighty metres in the dark
with her light off, sitting on a floor, for eleven minutes.

Then she said, "Tove, I want to say something and I want you to let me say it before I have to
do anything."

"Go ahead."

"It's eleven minutes to the pitch and the water's got about an hour. I'm not going to be a
problem. I want that logged in those words. I want it in your handwriting in the book tonight,
because I have been the problem in this system once and I have had four years to think about
it, and I am telling you now, on a frequency, with a recorder running somewhere, that I am not
going to be the reason this goes wrong."

"Logged," said Tove. "Vega, you heard it."

"Heard it," said Hana.

"And Varga."

"Yes."

"You've still got the line on you and you've still got to bring it up, and bringing fifty
metres of wool up a pitch in a rising head is the most dangerous thing either of us will do
today, and I need you to be extremely boring about it. Clip every knot twice. I'll count them
to you from the top. And you're going to go at a third of the speed you want to go at, and
you're going to hate it, and you're going to do it."

"A third of the speed I want to go at," said Ines.

"Yes."

"That's about four metres an hour."

"That's about four metres an hour," said Tove Marsk, "and you're right, and I'll be up there
counting every knot twice for eighty minutes, and there's nothing else I have to do today."

---

She came up in seventy-one minutes.

It was a third of the speed she wanted to go at and Tove counted every knot twice and Hana
read the catchment out every ten minutes and neither of them ever once raised their voice,
and at 12:51, at four metres an hour, at fifty metres below a hillside, a woman on the end of
a line said something to a woman on the other end of it that is four lines long and that
neither of them ever referred to again.

"Ottoline."

"You're supposed to be talking about the weather."

"I'm going to do two things instead and I want you to let me and I want you not to make it a
thing afterward."

"Okay."

"Second one first. When I came up, at the bottom, before I put the light on — Hana was
talking about the haar — you said, *I can hear you.*"

"I said that."

"I could hear you. That's — I want to tell you the true thing and not the useful thing. I could
hear you breathe. Fifty metres of wool and I could hear you breathe, and you were reading a
catchment figure off a book to keep me company and I could hear it in the way you breathe when
you're reading."

"You could probably hear me turning a page."

"I could hear you turn a page," said Ines. "Twice."

There was a pause of about four seconds on a fifty-metre line, which is a long time, and which
is in the log, because Hana Vogel wrote down the times of everything on that frequency for
four years and never once omitted a silence.

"Right," said Ottoline.

"That's the second one. That's all that was. Now do the first one."

"Which is the first one."

"You said you'd give me an answer you owed," said Ines Varga, at fifty metres, at four metres
an hour, at 12:51 on the twenty-eighth of November, in the Vell, with a hundred and forty
metres of rope and a woman in a car park above her. "You asked me on the twenty-fourth whether
I'd chosen it, and I said the roof came in, and you told me that was a hydrological answer to
a question about a person, and you were right, and I gave you the other one four days later on
a radio to a woman in a chamber, and you heard it, and it wasn't for you."

"It's for me," said Ines. "It was on a radio with three people on it and one of them has a
book and one of them has a certificate and neither of them knew what page two hundred and nine
was, and the woman who asked the question was on a hillside and could not hear any of it
except the number."

"You could have asked me to put you on. I was thirty metres away with a monitor."

"I know," said Ines. "That's what I want to say. You were thirty metres away and you did not
ask, and you did not use the one moment in four months when you could have asked for free and
got it, and I have been thinking about that for four days and I have worked out why you didn't
ask and I want to tell you that I worked it out, because it's the answer to the question I
asked you in a car park and you have been asking me the wrong one ever since."

"Tell me."

"You didn't ask because you'd have got it, and you'd have got it free, and it would have been
in a file, and you'd have known that the one time I said the true thing in this county you'd
got it from a radio." Ines was four metres an hour and eighty metres down. "So now you know
that I'd rather you had it and didn't ask, and that's not a favour, it's a fact, and it's the
worst thing that's happened to me, and I'm saying it at four metres an hour because that's
how long it takes to say it."

"It doesn't have to be a thing afterward," said Ottoline.

"No," said Ines. "It doesn't."

She did not say anything else about it then or ever, and on the morning of the third of
December a woman with a camera in a layby deleted eleven seconds of tape and the eleven
seconds were not the wall and were not the plate and were not the descent, and there is no
record anywhere in the world of what was said on a fifty-metre line at 12:51 on the
twenty-eighth of November except a rescue technician's log, which says:

*1251. Line live. Silence 4 sec x2. Routine. No action.*

---

The water came in at ten past three.

Hana Vogel read it off a book and said it on the frequency in the flat registered voice she
used for everything, and about four hundred metres away and six hundred feet below, a woman
standing on a pitch in a clear evening with fifty metres of wool in a bag over her shoulder
and a helmet in her hand said, "Copy," and began to walk to a van.

The catchment was in the fourth chamber at 12:50 and the fourth chamber was two hundred and
four metres, and there was a person in it, and there was not.

The second pitch was dry at ten past three. The top of the pitch was dry at ten past three.
The field above it was dry and flat and had four hundred millimetres of grass on it and a van
on it and a woman on a radio on it who was saying a number into a handset, and the number was
the number, and it was the last number of the day.

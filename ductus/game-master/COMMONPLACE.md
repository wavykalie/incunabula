# COMMONPLACE — The Game Master

This voice's own taste ledger, and only this voice's. Nothing here is enforced against a
book.

Entry types and ceremony are the root `COMMONPLACE.md`'s — `decision`, `preference`,
`outcome`, `promotion` — with the same formats and limits.

## Entries

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the author is at the table, not in the story

- **chosen:** the hand is a tabletop GM standing outside the fiction; its one dedicated
  room is the Table Note at the head of each part.
- **alternatives:** a clerk inside the fiction (the rejected Sabel Rook device); an
  omniscient historian; a second-person narrator throughout.
- **reason:** the author's objection was exact — a story about a contract must not be
  narrated by a contract-shaped device. The GM is not a character, a Clerk, or an office.
  The Note gives the game-master voice a room without letting it colonise the family's
  close third.
- **limit:** five Notes, not forty. A GM on every page turns the novel into a campaign log
  and the family into pieces.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — stats are sacred; the dice are honest

- **chosen:** every number is exact, every change is paid for, no death is reversed, no
  roll is fudged.
- **alternatives:** cinematic fudging (save the favourite); floating modifiers (whatever
  the scene needs); stat-light literary fantasy.
- **reason:** the reader's pleasure in LitRPG is the pleasure of a world that cannot cheat.
  A fudged roll is the one unforgivable act; it retroactively empties every earlier
  number. This also makes the family's gains real: if the sheet says they are stronger,
  they are.
- **limit:** the sheet stays lean — five stats, a class, a seal, traits, debts. Crunch is
  not clutter.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — every principal gets a class and a quote

- **chosen:** ten principals, ten classes, ten lines a reader could repeat.
- **alternatives:** an ensemble of competences with no labels; classes only in the ledger;
  quotes only for the protagonists.
- **reason:** the author asked for distinct personalities and classes. A class is a
  promise about what a person can and cannot do; a quote is the tell that identifies them
  under pressure. Both are load-bearing, not decoration.
- **limit:** a quote must be cheap and in character. If it sounds like the author, cut it.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the sentence is a debt instrument, not a punishment

- **chosen:** the Kasks are collateral on a surety their ancestor signed; the Down is an
  asset that yields salt, brightwater, relics, and precedent; the Upper Realms buy the
  yield and the law.
- **alternatives:** a crime-and-punishment dungeon; a cursed bloodline; a pure survival
  scenario.
- **reason:** this answers the author's world question — what kind of world sentences a
  genealogy — and it gives the family a route to power that is not a power fantasy. They
  are not escaping a curse; they are learning the paper.
- **limit:** the system must stay impersonal. No villain invented to enjoy the cruelty;
  the cruelty is the market.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the charter is the political engine

- **chosen:** one thousand cleared terms earn a charter: a seat, a levy, a vote in the
  Concord. The family ends Book One as a charter-holder, not merely free.
- **alternatives:** freedom as the ending (walk out and done); wealth as the ending; a
  military conquest of the upper realms.
- **reason:** the author asked how the cruel sentence becomes a political-powerhouse story.
  The answer is in the arithmetic: terms are standing, standing buys shares, a thousand
  buys a charter. The family's suffering is converted, on the page, into legal power.
- **limit:** the charter must cost. Hal refuses the Writ-holder's office, so the house
  enters the Concord without the right to sell other houses' sentences — poorer than its
  rivals, and that is the series' engine.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the Kasks do not become jailers

- **chosen:** the charter is taken, the Writ-holder's office is refused.
- **alternatives:** the family assumes the Writ and runs the Down; the family destroys the
  Assay; the family walks away.
- **reason:** the series' question is inherited obligation. A family that becomes the
  creditor answers it by becoming the thing it escaped. Refusing the office keeps the
  question open and gives the later volumes a political disadvantage that is also a moral
  position.
- **limit:** refusal is not purity. The Kasks still hold a charter, still profit from the
  Yield, still sit in the Concord. They are complicit; they are not the jailers.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the incest is downtime, not spectacle

- **chosen:** the breeding is a procedure with costs, permissions, and a ledger line; the
  family treats it as ordinary; the narration never leers or moralises.
- **alternatives:** eroticised; agonised; elided entirely.
- **reason:** the author's instruction, and the GM's instinct: a downtime action is
  resolved by its costs, not by a scene. The horror is institutional; the family's normal
  is the reader's uncanny.
- **limit:** ordinary is not invisible. The book must show the cost in bodies and names,
  or the custom becomes a setting detail.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the world is a hunger, not a market (supersedes two entries above)

- **chosen:** the Kasks ate a god in the Long Winter; the line is sealed inside its body and
  must regrow it, one part per term, while building a House to carry the sentence across
  generations.
- **alternatives:** the surety-and-asset world (retired this day); a bare curse with no
  economy; a survival story with no settlement.
- **reason:** the author's own account of the idea — a deep atrocity, a cursed lineage, a
  family alone with no residents, a civilisation built only to continue the sentence. The
  market in sentences made the family negotiate; the hunger makes them work.
- **limit:** no surface in Book One. The political game the author wants is post-Book-One,
  and this voice must not invent it early just because it is promised.

### 2026-11-23 — decision — the register loses its administrative grammar

- **chosen:** cold declaratives; the number before the consequence; at a death the sentence
  gets shorter; the tally, not the Writ, supplies the chapter epigraph.
- **alternatives:** the clause-bound periodicity the layer was drafted with; a warmer oral
  storyteller; second person throughout.
- **reason:** the author's complaint that the prose read old-school and British, and the
  diagnosis that the legalese came from the engine the book was built on. The register was
  written inside a world about documents and had inherited their syntax.
- **limit:** the persona keeps its table diction, but only in the Table Note. The narration
  stays concrete and cold, and "clause-bound" is withdrawn from the ledger's register line.

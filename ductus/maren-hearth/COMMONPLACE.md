# COMMONPLACE — Maren Hearth

This voice's own taste ledger, and only this voice's. Nothing outside this directory is
evidence for a Maren Hearth decision, and nothing here is enforced against a book.

The entry types and the ceremony are the root `COMMONPLACE.md`'s — `decision`,
`preference`, `outcome`, `promotion` — with the same formats and the same limits:

- **`decision`** — one real editorial choice, made between named alternatives.
- **`preference`** — distilled taste, written only after three agreeing decisions, and
  written `emergent`: guidance the next book may decline with a reason. A `limit:` line
  is required, because a preference without one is a rule.
- **`outcome`** — what readers did, with the prediction recorded before publication.
- **`promotion`** — a change the verifier stack adopted, with its falsifier.

**A preference that turns out to hold for another voice is not copied across.** It is
merged deliberately, dated, in `ductus/PROMOTIONS.md`, naming the evidence that
justified it in the second voice. A preference that appears in two ledgers by accident
has stopped being either one's, and `python tools/ductus.py cross` reports it.

## Entries

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the comic engine is consequence, not quip

- **chosen:** comedy is built from a change of altitude — a generous sentence, then a
  short flat one that reports what the world did — and from physical consequence
  escalating inside the room's own logic.
- **alternatives:** banter-led comedy, where the cast is witty and the room is scenery;
  absurdism, where the world bends to the joke; a comic narrator who enjoys the story on
  the reader's behalf.
- **reason:** the author's own example is a cat, a pat of butter and a heat vent, and it
  has no joke in it. It is funny because the physical world is indifferent, the cat is
  shameless, and the escalation is logical. Banter would put the comedy in the mouths and
  make the room interchangeable between volumes, which is the one thing a third-place
  series cannot afford.
- **limit:** consequence is not slapstick. Nobody is injured for a laugh and no object is
  destroyed that the room cannot afford to lose; the cost of the set piece is a mop, a
  burnt batch, or an hour.

**WRONG (2026-11-20, same day).** The `alternatives` line reads as a refusal of banter,
and the author's correction is *jokes and banter are fine, it wouldn't be a cafe without
banter and fun*. What was wrong was the word *led*: banter is required furniture and
consequence is the spine. The chosen line survives; the implication does not. See the entry
below.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — no villain, and no stake-raising

- **chosen:** opposition is friction — a pump, a queue, a letter, weather, a supplier —
  and the tension is whether a thing can be done well by the end of the shift.
- **alternatives:** the genre-standard external threat (developer, rival shop, festival
  contest); a recurring antagonist across volumes; a mystery plot per volume.
- **reason:** a villain converts a room into a battlefield and a comedy into a campaign,
  and it makes every volume's warmth contingent on being defended. The brief asks for
  slice of life with a hopeful world; hope that has to be won every book is not hope, it
  is suspense with better weather.
- **limit:** friction must still be on a schedule and cost something. A volume where the
  pump is merely inconvenient is a volume with no plot, and the zero-tolerance rule
  against a chapter where nothing changes is what catches it.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — one world, many rooms, and each volume stands alone

- **chosen:** the series shares a hopeful solar-punk world and an object or two between
  volumes; the cast, the trade and the town change, and no volume requires the last.
- **alternatives:** one shop and one cast across every volume; a different invented world
  per volume; a single anthology frame with a narrator who introduces each place.
- **reason:** the brief names four kinds of third place, which is a series of rooms rather
  than a series of one room's history. A single-shop series would also make the persona's
  refusals harder to hold, because a fixed cast accumulates continuity that competes with
  the room for the reader's attention.
- **limit:** standalone is not amnesiac. Weather, prices, a road closure and a repaired
  bridge are shared, and a returning regular is welcome as a customer. What may not cross
  is a plot, a debt, or a threat.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the benchmarks stay empty rather than invented

- **chosen:** `ductus.md` and the series contract ship with benchmark *slots* and no
  quoted lines, and the blind sort is recorded as NOT RUN.
- **alternatives:** seed four lines from the register the brief names as its model; write
  four lines in the voice and mark them as agent-composed; delay the contract until the
  author supplies excerpts.
- **reason:** a benchmark written by the agent becomes the target every downstream skill
  draws from, and the blind sort would then measure the agent's imitation against a corpus
  that has no lane for this register anyway. `ductus-designer` forbids it for exactly this
  reason, and the two existing contracts in this tree made the same call.
- **limit:** empty is not finished. The contract is a WISH, and it stays one until the
  author supplies 2–5 excerpts with sources and 500+ pooled words, at which point the
  slots are filled by quotation and the sort is run. If no excerpts arrive by the time
  volume one drafts, the fit is re-recorded as NOT RUN rather than quietly dropped.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the name argues the series' theme

- **chosen:** rename the voice from `hesper-wren` (Hesper Wren) to `maren-hearth` (Maren
  Hearth), moving the directory with it.
- **alternatives:** keep the bird name; publish under the first shop's name; use a
  house-name pseudonym shared by the series.
- **reason:** the author asked for a thematic name. *Wren* was a small bird at dusk —
  pleasant, argument-free, and mute about the register, which is the problem. *Hearth* is
  the first third place: the fire people gathered at without being obliged to, and also
  the thermal heart of a solar-punk building, which is the other thing this hand now has
  to be good at. The name is short for the contract instead of adjacent to it.
- **limit:** a name is not a contract. Nothing in Register, Refusals or Breaking moved
  because of the rename, the old slug stays in this ledger as the record, and no book was
  assigned under it, so no manuscript is orphaned.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the science is real, and it is paid for in labour

- **chosen:** every system is current-buildable, every number traceable to a per-volume
  physics ledger, and the physics delivered through work and consequence rather than
  exposition; the hard-science rules sit in the persona's Register.
- **alternatives:** soft-fantasy technology, where the world runs on hope and the reader
  is not meant to look; exposition-forward hard SF, where the systems are explained to a
  reader who came for them; magitech, which is both and neither.
- **reason:** the brief asks for hard-SF purists and character readers at once, and those
  two are usually served by different books. The reconciliation is not a compromise
  between them, it is a delivery rule: the same sentence carries the number and the
  person. A reader who wants to audit gets a real damper setting; a reader who wants the
  cat gets the cat, and neither waits for the other. The butter-and-vent set piece is the
  test case — a blocked vent is a cold room upstairs, and a cat that eats a quarter pound
  of fat is a cat with a real and undignified consequence.
- **limit:** hard does not mean technical. One auditable system per chapter is the
  ceiling, and a chapter that becomes a spec sheet has failed the hook half of the brief,
  which is half of what was asked for. The ledger is a research file, not a gate, and no
  cap moves because of it.

**WRONG (2026-11-20, same day).** *Current-buildable* was too narrow a standard, and the
author found it with the obvious question: if every system must be buildable today, there
is no space travel and no station at the end of things. The standard is physics-honest,
not built-yet. See the entry below.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — banter is required; consequence is still the spine

- **chosen:** at least one exchange per scene where two people are funny on purpose,
  running on the house's own history and stopping when the scene turns; the physical set
  piece stays the engine of any chapter that has one.
- **alternatives:** banter-led comedy only (funny people, interchangeable room);
  consequence only (accurate, airless, a service manual with a cat in it).
- **reason:** the author's correction — *it wouldn't be a cafe without banter and fun*.
  The first entry had written banter as the road not taken, which misread both the brief
  and the room: a third place is a place people talk in, and a hand that only does
  consequence writes the shift and never the society. The two do different jobs. Banter
  builds the cast and passes the time; consequence supplies the cost.
- **limit:** banter may carry information and may not carry a chapter. An exchange that
  replaces a scene is cut, a joke that costs nothing is decoration, and when something
  breaks the funniest person goes quiet — which is funnier than the joke continuing.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the series widens outward; the room does not

- **chosen:** the setting widens from a solar-punk town to an orbital stop to a station at
  the end of things, with a stranger cast at each distance, while the register, the
  refusals and the physics ledger hold at every scale.
- **alternatives:** keep every volume in the same town; make the station a separate series
  with its own hand; let the widening loosen the register because the setting is now
  science fiction.
- **reason:** the author's plan. A station stop is the same subject at a larger distance —
  a third place where nobody is obliged to be, run by a shift — and the brief's *wacky
  characters* are the same regulars with more range. Loosening the register for the
  widening was refused because a series layer exists so the room survives the change of
  address; a station volume that stops being a cozy comic slice-of-life is a different
  series wearing the name.
- **limit:** widening is not drift and not stakes. The end of the universe may not arrive
  with a threat attached, and the station's physics is load-bearing, not scenery. If a
  later volume needs the register itself to change, that is a dated change here with the
  volume named.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — physics-honest, with at most one declared departure

- **chosen:** nothing may violate conservation; engineering beyond us (fusion drives, spin
  habitats, orbital rings) is allowed; one invention beyond known travel may be declared
  for the whole series, priced in a ledger and constant; and the default is no invention at
  all, with the end-of-things station as the terminus of the settled line.
- **alternatives:** current-buildable only (the station becomes impossible, which is the
  contradiction the author named); unlimited soft SF (a drive for every chapter, and the
  physics stops meaning anything); a generation ship only (honest, and it makes every
  volume a voyage instead of a room).
- **reason:** real physics and a station at the end of things only conflict if *real* means
  *built already* and *end* means *cosmological*. Neither has to. Distance can be the
  series' clock, and a last stop is mythic at the scale of a route. If a volume genuinely
  needs to go further, one gate or throat or sail lane — chosen once, priced, never
  expanded — keeps the physics honest and hands the comedy a queue.
- **limit:** one. It may not be joined by a second, it may not be switched off when
  inconvenient, and it may not move messages faster than people unless the ledger says so.
  If the departure is doing the plot's work, the scene is a wish with a travel permit.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the world is a repaired Earth, and it never explains itself

- **chosen:** the solarpunk setting is a terraformed Earth generations after an unnamed
  catastrophe; its history arrives only as physical residue, ritual and language, and
  absence, seeded twice and cashed once per volume, with the cause never confirmed.
- **alternatives:** an open secondary world with no buried past (loses the ache, and the
  reason the infrastructure is remedial); a prologue that explains the Fall (spends the
  whole inheritance before chapter one); an in-world historian who lectures (breaks the
  register's refusal of explained worlds for the largest explanation available).
- **reason:** the author asked for the world to be discovered in pieces, and this register
  already refuses explained worlds over small things — a catchment, a pump. Extending that
  refusal to the biggest thing in the setting is not a new rule, it is the same rule at
  scale, and it makes every object in the room potentially load-bearing. It also fixes the
  tone of the technology: repair, not decoration. A town that knows it is terraforming has
  a reason to be careful with water that a town living in a completed utopia never gets.
- **limit:** pieces, not puzzles. There is no cipher, and no final reveal that retroactively
  re-reads the series; a seam has to be honest texture on the first read and become
  evidence on the second. The cause stays unnamed, because an answer would be shorter than
  the question, and that is the one thing a buried past cannot afford.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — specificity carries the theme; the narrator never states it

- **chosen:** le Carré's mechanism — meaning inside a specific, a body, a gesture — at café
  scale; Murakami's compression permitted in physical description and transitions only; the
  thesis sentence banned from narration; and a character's thesis cut short by the room.
- **alternatives:** le Carré entire (argument-driven scenes with monologues allowed to run —
  a different genre, and it stops the shift); Murakami entire (a narrator who aphorizes,
  which converts the buried past into a told fact and kills both the discovery and the
  comedy); no rule at all, which is how a book ends its chapters on morals it did not earn.
- **reason:** the author set the two passages side by side and asked which one this is. It
  is the first mechanism with the second's economy, because the premise is domestic: the
  theme has to survive being interrupted by a kettle. A stated theme is also the one thing
  the buried-past rule cannot survive — if the narrator may say what the world means, the
  seams become evidence for a case the book has already made.
- **limit:** this is a sentence-level rule, not a licence for ornament. le Carré's
  specificity is plain nouns, not fancy ones, so "purple prose" violates it as surely as a
  moral does. It does not ban a character from being eloquent; it bans eloquence that the
  room does not interrupt, and it bans the narrator's last word from being a verdict.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — procedure may own the page; explanation may not

- **chosen:** a brewing sequence may run to 350 words, once per chapter, when it is a scene
  — the read, the choice, one auditable variable, a visible consequence. The 60-word
  ceiling on exposition stands unchanged, and height comes from verbs, numbers and the
  wrist rather than adjectives or tasting notes.
- **alternatives:** keep the 60-word ceiling for everything (tea becomes scenery in a tea
  house, which is the one place it cannot be); a narrator's taxonomy of methods (the
  encyclopedia voice, refused everywhere else in this contract); pure impressionism —
  steam, warmth, a feeling (fails the hard-SF half of the brief and teaches the reader
  nothing they could check).
- **reason:** the author asked whether the narration can go deep on method, mechanism, and
  the decision per patron. It can. The contract's target was never labour — it was
  explanation, and those are different things. Tea is the shop's system, so it earns what
  the catchment earns: the reader learns it by watching somebody do it well and somebody do
  it wrong. Splitting procedure from exposition is a distinction, not a loosening: the
  exposition ceiling is untouched, and the new allowance is bounded by a word count and by
  the scene test.
- **limit:** 350 words is a ceiling, not a target, and a chapter that spends it twice has a
  manual in it. No passage without somebody waiting, talking or late. No narrator verdicts:
  the tea was right if a patron stopped talking or asked for another. And one auditable
  variable per passage — a second one is usually the writer showing off.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the bad afternoon is where the procedure licence is spent

- **chosen:** the licence's largest use is one scene per volume in which a stranger arrives
  after something has gone wrong and the trade is asked to steady a panicking person. The
  scene may be long, the procedure inside it stays under 350 words, urgency inverts every
  brewing parameter, and the scene ends with one usable thing rather than a cure.
- **alternatives:** keep the licence for ordinary service only, where a procedure is always
  background (a tea house that never gets to do the one thing a tea house is for); let the
  crisis scene run past the ceiling because it matters (a ceiling that yields to importance
  is not a ceiling, and the next important scene raises it again); write it as comfort — the
  cup arrives, the panic subsides, the room holds them (warm, unearned, and it makes the tea
  a drug and the shop a clinic).
- **reason:** the author proposed it, and the contract already had the room. The ordinary
  passage demonstrates competence; this one tests it, because a panicking person is the
  hardest read in the shop and every normal parameter comes out wrong — no time to steep, a
  heart that does not want a stimulant, hands that cannot hold a saucer. The physics earns
  its keep exactly here, and the honest win is small: a phone call answered, a coat off, a
  cup finished. That smallness is the series' thesis performed instead of stated, which is
  the only form this voice permits.
- **limit:** one such scene per volume at full length; a second one in the same volume is
  written short or cut. The wrong thing stays offstage and is never the arc, and the
  panicker never becomes a returning patient. No relief confirmed by the narrator: the win
  is an object and a next action. And she may not ask what happened — the read is the
  scene's information, and asking is a demand on somebody who cannot answer yet.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the herbs are real, and that is why they are weak

- **chosen:** calming herbs stay in the book as pharmacology — named accurately, dosed
  honestly, aimed at the half hour after the acute spike rather than at it — with the leaf
  named and its use never prescribed, and with the possibility that the herb is the wrong
  one.
- **alternatives:** ban them as magic (dishonest: a tea house without its own materia medica
  is a coffee shop with a shelf); let the cup settle the panic inside the paragraph (magic,
  and it turns the scene into a demonstration of a drug); treat herbalism as pure ritual
  with no active ingredient (throws the purist half of the brief away, and throws away the
  best clock in the book).
- **reason:** the author's correction — *calming herbs are a huge part of herbal tea... it
  sets the vibe*. The previous decision had over-corrected. It was written to stop the tea
  becoming a cure, and it banned a real faint effect alongside the fake one. The honest
  middle is graded, and it is the most hard-SF sentence in the scene: apigenin at a GABA-A
  site, a fraction of a trial dose, onset in tens of minutes. The weakness is the asset. A
  drug that weak cannot rescue anybody, so the rescue has to stay with the room — the sugar,
  the weight, the question, and somebody who is not panicking.
- **limit:** no promise spoken aloud, no verdict from the narrator, no diagnosis, and at most
  one herb credited per passage, because one auditable variable is still the row. A blend
  that credits three herbs is a label, not a scene. And the house's claims may be wrong: the
  folk label on the jar is culture, not evidence, and Maren may privately think so.

### 2026-11-20 — decision — the shelf is chosen, and the pharmacology is stated

- **chosen:** solar-punk abundance puts grown, bred intoxicants in the world and on this
  house's single licensed shelf — kava, kratom, cannabis, the stimulants — named with their
  real mechanisms, onsets and hazards, with the long plants refused because they outlast a
  visit, and with breeding allowed to change ratios but not hazards.
- **alternatives:** a dry house in a world where nobody gets high (clean, and it writes the
  most interesting thing about solar-punk domesticity out of the book); an unregulated
  counter where every plant is available and nothing is written down (abundance without
  consistency, which is precisely the hazard the setting creates); a house where the
  substance is the episode's engine (genre drift into stoner comedy or drug drama, and it
  converts a room into a case study).
- **reason:** the author's worldbuilding. If growing is personal, local and rapid, then
  availability is not a plot convenience, it is infrastructure — and infrastructure has
  quality variance, a licence and a rota. The pharmacology is also the best comic and moral
  machinery available here: dose, route, onset and duration give the same scene a clock, a
  cost and a refusal, which is exactly the friction model this contract already runs on. The
  honest of it is what keeps the purist half of the brief fed: apigenin, kavalactones,
  mitragynine, and the fact that eating takes forty-five minutes longer than smoking.
- **limit:** the high is never the entertainment. One active plant credited per passage, the
  onset and duration stated once rather than catalogued, no leaf that does a decision's work,
  and no volume whose arc is somebody's habit. A second substance in the same passage is a
  menu, and a menu is not a scene.

Fifteen decisions before a first chapter is past the floor; a voice with no chapters, one
afternoon of revisions, and a ledger that shows every one of them.

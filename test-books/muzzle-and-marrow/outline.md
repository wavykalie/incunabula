# OUTLINE — *Muzzle and Marrow*

**No length targets appear in this document, and there are no planned word counts per
chapter.** See `DRAFTING_FRAMEWORK.md`. A quota is a plan for where the masses go.
Chapters end when the thing they are about has been finished.

**Correction, 2026-09-29.** This paragraph originally justified the absence of targets by
citing a finding — that the one book written to per-chapter numbers produced a 1,004-word
band. That book is `demo-magician` / The Trick, and the claim is accurate about it. It also
implied that untargeted books reach the 33.4–45.1% band, which is now known to be
unevidenced: the two Merope books previously cited for that were drafted to a declared
7 × ~2,450 and only reached 41% after being re-partitioned. See `incunabula/ERRATA.md`,
2026-09-29.

**This book scores U1 CV 12.1% and fails the row.** That is recorded, not repaired, and
the failure is not read as an argument for putting numbers back into this document.

Each row carries a **structural approach**, so no two chapters are built the same way.
That is a defence against the shape of the book collapsing into one technique, and it is
deliberately *not* a length distribution.

## The movement

The book is a descent, and it is also a party that keeps misreading what it is for. The
structure is **outward, down, and inward**: the party gets smaller and more honest as it
goes further, and the Orb's literalism gets funnier and less funny in that order.

| # | approach | what it is about | ends when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | cold open, in medias res | Mittens in the market square being laughed at, and taking it, and then not | Duke Rocky offers the job, and Mittens says a name instead of yes |
| 2 | two threads, cut | The party assembling, in parallel, from four directions | Tuppence and Patches meet in a bar and both lie about why they're going |
| 3 | dialogue-forward, single room | The oath. Rocky swears it properly. Hester reads out what the last party recorded | Hester reads the entry where the record stops making sense, and stops reading |
| 4 | chase | Something comes down the shaft after them | They get it shut, at cost, and the cost is visible |
| 5 | document | Hester's folio, laid out — what the last party wished, and what happened | The reader can see the shape of the trap before the party does |
| 6 | rest / the camp | A quiet one. Somebody's private reason comes out badly | Patches admits he decided against a wish, and nobody respects it |
| 7 | set piece | The first hard gate, which is a door that opens on the party's real problem | Tuppence sells them, and is then stuck with them |
| 8 | POV shift — Tuppence alone | Tuppence ahead, doing the thing she planned | She comes back. Why is the chapter's question |
| 9 | descent proper, wide | The party moving fast, badly, in the dark | They stop being five |
| 10 | the cold one | The Orb | — |

Ten chapters drafted as a pilot. The manuscript is finished when it is finished; this table
is a set of questions, not a schedule.

## Per-chapter notes

**1. "Mittens the 7th"** — *cold open, in medias res.*
Not a beginning so much as a middle. We arrive with him already known, already funny, and
already sick of it. The scene is a market transaction where the fishmonger calls him by
his whole name and it lands the way it always lands, and he is *fine*, he is genuinely
fine, and then a beat later he is not fine, and the not-fine is small and specific and
nobody sees it. Duke Rocky is in the crowd and has been for an hour. The offer is
absurd on its face — a duke, an oath, five hundred crowns. Mittens does not accept. He
asks what happened to the last party, which is the wrong question and the one that
matters.

*Ends on the question, not the answer.*

**2. "Four Directions"** — *two threads, cut.*
Tuppence taking a job she has already decided to betray. Patches, retired twice, being
offered a post by a dog who treats him with a formality that is either respect or a joke
and is possibly both. Cut between them; they end in the same bar. Both lie about why
they're going, in the same evening, to each other, and both are believed, because the
thing you most want to be lied to about is the thing you will accept any answer to.

**3. "The Oath"** — *dialogue-forward, single room.*
Mostly one room and a table. Rocky swears it in full, the old dog form, the collar
language, and nobody else in the room knows the words and everyone lets him finish. This
is the chapter where the book earns its comedy: a huge dog in ceremonial dress swearing
an oath nobody can verify, surrounded by people who are about to go and possibly die, and
everybody behaving as though the ceremony is the load-bearing part.

Hester reads the folio at the end. She gets four lines in before the room goes quiet.

**4. "Down"** — *chase.*
They go down and something comes down after them, and it is not a monster, it is a
*procedure* — the world's own mechanism for closing a route, running the way a lock
closes. This is the first evidence that the descent is managed. The cost of shutting it
is somebody's kit, or somebody's certainty.

**5. "What the Last Party Wished For"** — *document.*
The folio laid out. Three names, three wishes, three outcomes, quoted. The comedy of the
wishes is the comedy of people who thought they had a better sentence. The horror is that
the outcomes are *exactly* what was asked for. Nothing was twisted. Nobody was cheated.
It worked perfectly, which is the problem.

This is the chapter that tells the reader the trap, and it does it by being funny, and
the party reads it aloud and does not understand it.

**6. "Camp"** — *rest.*
The quiet one, and the one the framework explicitly wants: a chapter with no plot
pressure where something is nonetheless finished. Patches says out loud that he does not
want a wish. Everyone is kind about it. It is the least likeable thing he does and the
chapter earns it by not defending him.

**7. "The Door"** — *set piece.*
A gate in the dark that opens for one of them specifically. Tuppence takes the job,
sells the party's route and their composition to a buyer who is buying information, not
allies — and then cannot leave, because leaving requires someone to know where she is.

**8. "Ahead"** — *POV shift, Tuppence alone.*
The one chapter she narrates. She goes ahead to do the thing, and the chapter is funny,
and the reader knows more than she thinks they do, and at the end she comes back.

**9. "Four"** — *wide, moving fast.*
The party in the dark, moving badly, and this is the chapter where it stops being about
adventure and starts being about arithmetic — how many of them are left, and what each
of the remaining ones is actually going down there for.

**10. "The Orb"** — *the cold one.*
No jokes. The Orb grants the wish as worded. The chapter's job is to make the reader
understand that nobody is being cheated and everybody is getting precisely what they
asked for, and that this is unbearable.

The ending is the genuinely cold moment in the book, per the foundation.

## Carried threads

- **Mittens' unstated reason** (ch1, paid later). He is seventh of his name. Six went
  before him. He is going down for one of them, and the reader should work out who from
  the ordinal alone.
- **Tuppence's betrayal** (ch2, ch7, ch8). Sold twice, and the second sale is to the
  party.
- **Hester's fourth folio** (ch3, ch5). What it omits is the plot.
- **Patches' refusal** (ch6). Re-decided by ch10, badly.
- **Madame Void** (not yet on the page). She is not competing for the Orb. The party does
  not believe this because it does not sound like something a person would want, and
  that is exactly the reason to believe it.

# Chapter Two — Four Directions

The Mouse Queen runs two houses in Carrowmarket and has never pretended otherwise about
either. The Bell House takes coin and keeps its mouth shut, which is a service. The
Hollow House takes no coin, keeps no record, and moves things, and half the trade on the
Quay runs through the back door of the Hollow on a Tuesday.

Tuppence went in by the front, because going in by the back on a Tuesday is a thing
people notice.

"You've got the look," said the girl at the counter. She was a mouse too, or a vole, and
about fourteen. "You've got the look of somebody who's been asked to do something and is
already thinking about what it costs to do it somewhere else."

"That's not a look. That's a face."

"It's a look *on* a face."

"Put in the book," said Tuppence. "Tuesday, the Hollow, one delivery, ground level,
ninety-one Millward. A gentleman wants a package into the Descent and back out again and
he wants it done by someone who doesn't talk on the road."

"And?"

"And I want twice the going rate, up front, and he can have the receipt that says ground
level, because nobody has ever read one."

The girl wrote it down. Behind her, on the board, the day's jobs went up in a hand that
was not hers, and Tuppence read it upside down without appearing to, which is a skill
she had learned in a harder school than this one.

**Hallow Lamp.** Third bell. Down to the Descent and back.

"Well," said the girl. "That's a strange one."

"Hasn't been done in a while?"

"Never. And it's a one-way commission, which is—" the girl stopped.

"Which is?"

"Which the Queen does."

"That's why the Queen does," said Tuppence. "Put it in the book."

---

She was out of the Hollow and a street away and standing in an archway she had chosen
for its view, which was a view of a wall, when she worked out that she had just agreed to
carry a lamp into the Descent.

She had looked at the board the way you look at a board. She had read the word
*Descent* and stopped.

"Well," said Tuppence.

The archway offered no advice. This was usual. An archway that offered advice would not
be an archway, it would be a confessor, and confessors cost.

The lamp was in a crate, and the crate was ninety-one Millward, and the crate was heavy
in a way that said *lamp* the way a man carrying a coffin says *box*. It was also, as far
as Tuppence could tell, the reason the job was up. Not the commission. The commission was
the bribe.

Somebody, eleven years ago, had gone down carrying one of these. And had come back, or
hadn't, and either way it had put a lamp on a board in a room in Carrowmarket, and
somebody had taken the job.

Tuppence had taken the job.

She had also, somewhere around the third bell, made a decision about that which she was
keeping for later, and which was the reason she was the best courier on the Quay, and
which was: *everything has a buyer, and the buyer of a lamp in the Descent is whoever gets
there first.*

---

Nine miles east, in a barracks that had been a barracks for a hundred and forty years and
would be, at the current rate of closure, a barracks for about nine months more, Sergeant
Patches was being offered a job by a dog in ceremonial dress.

"—so I am not asking you to believe anything," said Duke Rocky. "I want to be exact about
what I am. I am asking you to come to a room in Copper Lane on Thursday, and to hear a
proposal, and to say no if it is a stupid one."

"Then I will come on Thursday," said Patches, "and hear the proposal."

"You are not interested."

"Your grace, I have been retired from two posts in nine years and the third turned me
down on the grounds that I was 'not currently attached to anything,' which is a sentence
about me that I have had a great deal of time to consider."

"It is a hell of a sentence."

"It is," Patches agreed. "But the answer was correct."

Rocky put a folded paper on the barrack table and did not, at this stage, smooth it.

"Five of us," he said. "Going down. The last one was eleven years ago."

"Who's paying?"

"I am."

"Then it isn't a proposal. It's a favour, and you've dressed it up, and you'll want
something that doesn't sound like money."

"No," said the duke. "That is precisely why I have put it in this order. If I had opened
with the money you would have heard a wage, and you would have priced yourself against it.
I have opened with the going down, because you have been priced your whole career."

Patches looked at the paper for a while.

"You've read my record," he said.

"All of it."

"Then you know the last descent had me in it."

"I know the account has your name in it. I don't know what you did down there, and the
duke of Cavalton does not care, and I will put that in writing if it would help."

"It would help a bit," said Patches.

"It won't help a lot, because it's not the true part."

---

The bar was called the Nettle and it was the last of its kind on the east side, and
Patches had been drinking in it for eleven years, which was three years before the
descent, and the landlord had never once asked him about it.

There was a mouse at the far end.

"There she is," said the landlord, following Patches' eyes. "Came in an hour ago. Haven't
ordered. Hasn't said much. Pays cash, which in my experience means either she's rich or
she's frightened, and she doesn't look rich."

"Does she look frightened?"

"She looks like someone who has decided something," said the landlord. "Different thing.
Worse for me, because a frightened one buys a drink and a decided one doesn't."

Patches took his glass over.

The mouse was perhaps forty, or perhaps ninety, which with mice is not determinate and
they resent the question. She had good gloves, which said something. Her coat was good
and her boots were better, which said something else, and what it said was that the boots
were for running and the coat was for being seen.

"You're the sergeant," she said.

"Everyone says that."

"You're going down."

Patches sat down, which he had not intended to do.

"I'm drinking," he said.

"Everyone is drinking something," said Tuppence. "You're drinking *nervously*, which at
this hour on this street is a bit theatrical, and I mean that well. I say that well
because I mean it. Have you got a minute for somebody who's also going?"

"Am I going?"

"The duke came round this morning with a paper and a hat that cost more than your
month's pension, and you've said yes, and you've told nobody, which is fair, and I've
known you were going since before you walked in, because men who have said no come in at
eleven and drink the good whisky, and you came in at eight and ordered the beer."

Patches laughed, which he had not planned.

"Who's the other one?"

"Doesn't matter to you."

"If I'm going down with him, it does."

"Mittens the 7th," said Tuppence.

The name went across the table between them and lay there.

Patches had been in the Low Barracks for thirty years and he had heard that name perhaps
three thousand times and it had never once been attached to a person who was five minutes
away from him.

"Number seven," he said.

"That's him."

"Mittens."

"That's him," said Tuppence. "And before you say it—"

She stopped. She had a whole delivery prepared; it was good, it was the reason she was
good, and she heard herself about to say *aw, isn't that the seventh one* to a ram who
had never in his life said it to her, and who had just told her, honestly, that he had
been in the last descent and had come back.

"Say what," said Patches.

"Nothing," said Tuppence. "Pour me a drink and let's not do the bit where we tell each
other the joke."

"That's the first sensible thing anyone's said in here in a year."

---

They lied to each other for about two hours, which was longer than either of them
expected and shorter than they had money for.

Patches said he was going because he was bored, and it was nearly true, and Tuppence
let it stand because it was a good lie and it was true about the shape of him even if it
was false about the reason. Tuppence said she was going because the money was good, and
it was *nearly* true, and Patches let it stand, and at one point he looked at her the way
a ram looks at somebody who has just been exactly as honest as the situation allows.

"Whose lamp," he said, eventually.

"What?"

"You're a courier. Nobody walks to the Descent for a wage. You're walking down there with
somebody's lamp, and you already know whose, and you said it out loud in a bar in the
hope that a sergeant would ask."

The Nettle was very quiet.

"Eleven years ago," said Tuppence.

"Eleven years ago," agreed Patches. "And they never found out who took it."

"No."

"Because there was a second job that night," said Patches. "A small one. Nobody's asked
about the small one in eleven years, because the small one didn't go down. The small one
came back up and told them where the last party was, and then there was a descent they
could afford, because the route was safe by then."

Patches had not planned to say any of it. It came out the way it had come out in the
baracks, one drink at a time, in the voice of a man who had been carrying it in a place
where he was not to put it down.

"Whose lamp," said Tuppence.

"Sergeant Patches of the Low Barracks."

The mouse sat quite still.

"You have been down," she said.

"Eleven years ago. I came back. I've not told a soul and I'm telling you because you
already had it and because you've got a look, and I've been drinking for eleven years
against a day and I'd like somebody to have the number."

Tuppence got up. She put four coins on the table, which was more than the drinks, and
she did it while looking at him, which was a thing she had learned in a harder school.

"Ninety-one Millward," she said. "Thursday. If you want to know whose lamp, come and
watch me not answer."

"What will it cost me?"

"One cup of tea," said Tuppence, "and the truth about the small job, and I'd rather have
the second one now."

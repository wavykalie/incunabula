# Chapter Five — What the Last Party Wished For

They made camp on the shelf above the turn, in the lee of a rock that Hester said had
been used before and Patches said had a name and Tuppence said had a bar, and it did not,
which was the most Tuppence had contributed in a day.

In the morning Hester Pye put the folio on a flat stone and said she was going to read all
of it, out loud, in order, and that nobody was to stop her and nobody was to be polite
about it.

"It is not a sad document," she said. "I want that established first. It is a funny
document. It is funny in the way that a contract is funny. The people who wrote it were
being careful, and being careful is what killed them, and I would like you to hear it
being careful."

"Then read it," said Mittens.

She did.

---

"There are five names at the front," said Hester. "Four of them came down with us and one
of them turned back at the second turn and is not in this account, and we will come to
him, and his name is Tuppence, and he has asked me to say that he was not brave and does
not want to be remembered as though he was."

"Thank you," said Tuppence.

"It is not a compliment, it is the document."

She turned a page.

"Entry the first. Kir, the Fourteenth, lead. She writes: *'We have agreed that we will each
make one wish and that we will say it plainly, in our own words, and that we will not
discuss them afterwards, because I have read what happens when people discuss them.'*"

Mittens had not expected that. He looked up.

"She wrote that before," he said.

"She wrote it before. Two hundred miles down, in her own hand, on the fourth day." Hester's
voice did not change at all, which was how you knew. "There is a note in the margin that
says: *'We have read the old accounts. In every one, the people who lost everything had
argued.'*"

Nobody spoke.

"Entry the second," said Hester. "Sergeant Patches of the Low Barracks, then of the
Ninth. He writes: *'I wish to be relieved of my post and of the memory of it, and to
spend whatever time I have left in a place where nobody knows me and I am not useful.'*"

The wind came off the rock and went away again.

"That is what I wanted," said Patches.

"I know. I have read your report of the four pages, and the report says the same thing in
a different voice, and the report is the reason I trust the entry." Hester turned another
page. "Entry the third. Mine."

"You wrote yours down."

"I wrote mine down in front of them, at a table, in a hand they could all read, and they
signed below it, which I had insisted on, and which was, I told them, the only protection
available." She looked up. "It says: *'I wish to know what the last party who came down
here said in the part of the record that was not kept, and I wish to know it before I am
old, and I do not care what it costs me to know it, and I am aware that that is the
sentence of a person who has not read the rest.'*"

"And?"

"And I got it," said Hester Pye.

---

Hester read on, and Mittens stopped being able to hold the document at arm's length.

"It records what I got," she said. "Three lines, and the lines are the reason I have never
shown this to anyone who did not already know the ending.

*The third party of the last descent wished that the matter of the Hallow Lamp be
resolved. The Orb held that there was no lamp.*

"Nobody had said there was no lamp. In four hundred years, no Orb had ever denied that a
lamp existed. It simply took the sentence and applied it to a world in which no lamp was
in the room."

Mittens looked at the brass lamp in its case beside the stone, the one they had carried
down, the one that had bought them the whole descent.

"It didn't say it wasn't real," he said.

"It did not. It said there was no lamp," said Hester, "in the only tense that would hold.
We had been *careful*. We had been so careful that we wrote a sentence about a matter
rather than a sentence about a lamp, and the Orb is not a mind, and it cannot know what we
meant, and it did the only thing a mechanism can do with a sentence like that, which is
the whole of it, correctly, forever."

---

"The fourth entry is mine," she said, "and I have already read it, and I will read it once
more because the room should hear it in the same voice as the rest."

She did not look down.

"*"I asked to know what was not kept. The Orb has told me: it was kept, and it was
deliberately kept, and the party that kept it came back up, and told the city where the
last party was, and the city sent the second descent, and the second descent was safe,
because the first one had already been."*

"There is no fifth entry," said Hester Pye. "There is no fifth name. My hand stops on that
word, in the middle of a line, and the rest of the page is blank, and it is not torn."

Mittens found that he had turned round to look at Tuppence, and found that Tuppence was
looking at him, and that neither of them was going to say anything, and that this was the
second time in three days that a silence in a room had turned out to be load-bearing.

"You're the small job," he said.

"Mittens—"

"You came up and told them where the last party was."

"Yes."

"And they sent the second descent and it was safe because the first one had already been."
He was aware of how calm his voice was. "You told them a party was at the bottom of the
world. You did not have to tell them where. You told them *where*."

"I told them where," said Tuppence, "and I have been down two hundred miles of stairs for
two days with four people who would not exist in a world I had not sold, and if you would
like to do the arithmetic on that, do it now, and I will wait."

---

"Then do the last one," said Patches, from the far side of the fire.

Nobody spoke.

"You gave two entries and the account has three," he said, and he was looking at the
folio, not at Hester. "Kir. You. Yourself. Kir the Fourteenth is not in this book as
having made a wish, and she is the one who wrote the rule about not discussing them
afterwards, and she is the reason the whole thing was written carefully, and she is not
in it."

Hester Pye sat very still on her stone.

"Kir did not wish," she said.

"Then what is written on that page?"

"Her entry is the one the account stops before," said Hester.

The wind came and went.

"Read the margin," she said. "There is a margin note on the last page, and it is in her
hand, and it is the only line of it that I can read, and it says: *'I have decided I
will not make a wish at all, and I would rather be a person who wanted nothing than a
person who got it, and tell the others I said so, and if I am wrong about that then tell
them it was the fault of the sentence and not of the wanting.'*"

She closed the folio.

"Her name is three times in the account, as I told the duke, and she appears in none of
it as a person who asked for anything, and that is the only entry anybody down there ever
wrote that the Orb has not answered, and it is the reason this book exists, and I have
never known," said Hester Pye, "whether she got away with it."

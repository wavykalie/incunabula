# Chapter Seven — The Door

The door was nine days down, or four, depending on which of the two arguments about
turns won, and it was not a door in any sense that a mason would recognise.

It was a flat sheet of something the colour of a wet slate, set into the wall of the
spiral without a doorframe, without hinges, and without any visible sign of ever having
been opened. It was about nine feet high. It had a line down it that was not a crack,
because a crack wanders and this ran dead straight from floor to ceiling.

"It has been here the whole time," said Hester.

"Then we have walked past it six times."

"You have. I have not." Hester was on the stone in front of it, and she had been for some
time. "It is not a door to the centre. It is a door *of* the centre. There are, I am told
by people who should know, more of them."

---

The duke wanted to open it immediately and had to be talked out of it, and the argument
that finally worked was Hester's, and it was that nobody had any idea what a door would
do to a party of five who had walked up to it.

"Whose name," said Hester, "does it want?"

Nobody answered her, which was an answer, and she nodded as though it had been a good one.

"It is not asking," she said. "I want to be clear about that, because it is worse. It is
*reading*. There is a lamp, and a party, and one of the party is carrying a thing that was
given to her by somebody in a Mouse Queen house in Carrowmarket, and the door has known
that since Millward. We have walked past it six times and it has been standing there
knowing whose we are."

Tuppence had gone a very slightly different colour. Ravens cannot show this well.
Humans and mice can.

"Mine," she said.

"Not *yours*," said Hester. "Whosever's you were carrying it *for*. That is the question
it is asking, and you have been carrying the answer in a satchel since the Quay."

---

It was Rocky who worked it out, and he worked it out slowly, in the way of a man who
arrives at a conclusion he would rather not have reached.

"Somebody hired you," he said.

"Somebody hired me."

"Somebody with a lamp, a route, and a party already on it." Rocky sat down, which dogs of
his size do not do comfortably. "Madam. Who paid the commission."

Tuppence stood in front of a door nine feet high in a wall a mile inside the world, and
looked at a dog, a cat, a ram and a bird, and made a decision that took about two seconds
and that she had, she realised afterwards, been making since the Tuesday she had read the
word *Descent* upside down on a board and stopped.

"A woman of the Ninth Coil," she said. "Serpent. She gave me four times the going rate and
paid it up front, which is the only reason I'm standing here, and she asked one question,
and it was *are they going down with a lamp*, and I said yes."

"Then she wanted the party," said Mittens.

"She wanted the party to get there," said Tuppence. "That's what I sold. Not the route —
she doesn't need the route, she's been down it, I don't know how many times. She wanted
the five of you, and the fact that you'd have asked her to come."

The silence that followed was the worst moment of the descent and none of them were in
peril.

"She did ask," said Hester Pye.

"She did ask."

"And you said yes for me."

"I said I hadn't asked you yet," said Tuppence, "and you were going to be curious, and I
was going to be standing here when you were, and you'd have brought her because you bring
everybody." She put her hands flat against her sides. "I've been honest with you for about
nine seconds. I notice that's longer than I expected and I don't intend to give you the
rest of the day."

---

Nobody had time to be satisfied about it, because the door did something, and what it did
was open.

It opened the way a thing opens when it has been a wall for a very long time and has
decided otherwise: no sound, no effort, the line simply not being a line any more, and
behind it a passage that was warm.

"That is very bad," said Hester, "and I have not said what is bad about it, so let me."
Nobody could stop her and nobody tried.

"That door did not open for a party," she said. "It has been reading one thing since
Millward, and that thing is a mouse carrying a lamp. The lamp is not a marker, Mittens. It
is a *warrant*. Somebody has been carrying a warrant down to this door for eleven years
and the door has been waiting for it, and Tuppence has been carrying it for nine days, and
in about four minutes it is going to stop mattering who brought it."

"Then we go through," said Mittens.

"That is not what I—"

"We go through," said Mittens, "because we came down, and there is a way, and a woman of
the Ninth Coil paid four times the rate to have us at the bottom of the world, and I would
very much like to find out what she thinks we are for."

The duke looked at the cat, and then at the door, and then took his coat off and folded
it over his arm, which was a thing you did before you went somewhere you would not be
coming back from, and which he had clearly been taught.

"Sergeant."

"Sergeant."

"Two things," said Patches. "One, nobody in this party said a proper goodbye to anybody
they love, and that is a poor state of affairs and it is not mine to fix. Two." He looked
at Tuppence, and he was not angry, which was somehow the worst of it. "If there is any part
of you that sold us on purpose, I'd rather know now, because I'd rather be sold by somebody
who enjoyed it."

Tuppence looked at him for a long moment.

"No," she said.

"That is the first clean thing anybody's said since we got here," said Patches. "All right.
In we go."

---

It was warm inside, and that was the first wrong thing.

Not warm like a building. Warm like a held breath — a corridor of worked stone going down
and forward at once, lit along the floor by a line of something that was not quite light,
and the temperature of a hand on the back of your neck.

"It's managing us," said Hester, and she was flying, low, and sounding like someone
counting. "The warmth, the light, the width. It's the size of five."

"That's considerate of it," said the duke.

"It is *fitted* to us," said Hester. "It is a corridor built for five, and there are six
of us, and if you look — and do look, quickly, at the ceiling — you will see where the
sixth width was made and then taken away."

Nobody looked at the ceiling.

Tuppence looked at the ceiling. She was the only one who did, and she saw it, and it was
exactly what Hester had said, and the width had been cut off and the wall run in so
smoothly that the join was a line you could find with your hand and not with your eye.

"You knew," said Hester, to her, without heat at all. "You knew there were five."

"I knew there were five," Tuppence agreed.

"You did not tell us."

"You did not ask," said Tuppence, "and I was busy not telling you, and those took all my
time."

---

The passage went on for the better part of a day and none of it was interesting, which was
itself a discovery. There were no doors. There were no thresholds. There was a corridor,
designed to five, at a comfortable width, at a temperature, going down.

And then, at the end of it, there was a woman.

She was sitting on a step with her back against the wall, in a coat that had been expensive
somewhere about ninety years ago, and she had the composed, unnerving stillness of somebody
who has arranged a room and then sat down in it and is content to wait.

She was a snake. She was very tall for one. She had been down here a long time.

"Madame Void," said Hester Pye.

"Archivist." Madame Void did not stand, and it was not rudeness; it was a thing she had
clearly decided about herself and had no intention of revisiting. "You took eleven years to
come down and four days to be angry about it, which is exactly eleven years and four days,
so I am glad you are punctual."

"You sent a mouse."

"I sent a lamp," said Madame Void. "The mouse came with it. There is a difference and it is
the only interesting thing about the last nine days, and I would have told her so if she
had been in a position to hear it, which she was not, and so she has been
walking behind four people she sold."

Her eyes came up, one at a time, the way they do.

"You have a question," she said. "All of you do, and it is the same one, and I am going to
answer it in one sentence and then you may all be as angry as you like, because there is
nothing else to do down here and no time to do it in."

"Speak, then," said Mittens.

"The Orb must not be used," said Madame Void, "and I am not here to get there, and I have
never once wanted to, and every one of you has assumed that I did, because it is the only
story that makes sense about a woman sitting at the bottom of the world."

Nobody spoke.

"You think I am guarding it," said Madame Void. "I have been guarding it for forty years
and there is nothing to guard, and I would like that in the record, in whatever way you
keep records. There is no curse. There is no treasure. There is a mechanism that takes a
sentence and does the whole of it, and there is a woman sitting four hundred miles from
every city in the world, preventing one sentence a year from being said badly."

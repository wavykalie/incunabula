# Chapter Nine — Four

They went fast for a day and a half and the corridor let them, which was the first thing
that worried Mittens.

"Nothing has attacked us," he said.

"Nothing has ever to be fair," said Patches, "we've been four days in a building."

They went faster.

---

Hester flew the ceiling, because Hester flew the ceiling. It was the only way a party of
five could make a corridor of nine feet work, and it meant she was always in the light and
always out of reach and it meant Mittens had the whole time in the world to look at the
width.

"It isn't narrower," he said.

Nobody asked him what he meant.

"It *isn't*," he said. "That's what's wrong with it. The door behind us was built to five
and the sixth width was cut off. This isn't cut off. This is exactly five wide, and it's
been exactly five wide since we came through, and there are five of us."

"There are five of us," said Patches.

"There are five of us."

"So it's the right size."

"It's the right size *now*," said Mittens, and put his hand flat on the wall, and found
that the wall was very slightly warm, and did not say the next part out loud, because the
next part out loud was that a corridor built to five would need to be five wide for as
long as there were five, and that he had a fairly clear idea of what happened when there
weren't.

They went faster still.

---

The Duke of Cavalton found the first one, which is how it happened, and which was itself
a piece of information about a very large dog.

"Your grace."

"Mittens. What—"

"He isn't breathing."

Rocky got there faster than a man that size has any right to move and put a paw on the
shoulder of the ram, and looked at the face, and said, in a voice that had gone entirely
flat: "No. No, he is. He is. He is *breathing*—"

"He is breathing," Mittens agreed, and did not know why he had said it, and it turned out
later that he had said it because the alternative sentence was *I don't know if he is
breathing* and he had not wanted it to be true that he had said that about Patches.

It took forty minutes to get the lamp lit. It took Hester three separate arguments with
herself out loud, and all three were about the route, and all three were wrong, and
Mittens understood every one of them and let her have them because she needed to be
somewhere else.

Patches came round at about the second hour, and was very annoyed about it, and the first
thing he said was: "Nobody say anything about the stairs."

"About the stairs," agreed Mittens.

"I went down them at thirty-one and I came up at thirty-one, and I have spent eleven years
making sure that was a thing that happened to somebody else, and I have just watched it
happen, and I am telling you *now* so that you can all decide how you're going to feel
about it for the rest of the day instead of deciding it in a corridor at two in the
morning."

Nobody said anything about the stairs.

"It's not a corridor," he said, a while later, to Mittens, in the low voice they had all
started using. "It's a *counting*. The warm, the light, the width. It's counting us and
keeping the room right. And a corridor that counts you is a corridor with a *purpose*,
and a purpose is a thing somebody built, and if we have built this, then the party that
built it—"

"Then somebody wants a party of four," said Mittens, "arriving at the end of it."

"Yes," said Patches. "And I have been in a counting thing before, and it counted *up*,
and I got out, and I would like to make a suggestion that nobody argues with."

"Go on."

"Wake up the bird," said the sergeant, "and find out what this is."

---

Hester came down off the ceiling, and she had been up there for six hours doing, by her
own account, nothing at all, and she landed on a stone and said:

"It is a gauge."

"For what?"

"For whether a party arrives." She folded her wings, which is what she did instead of
smoothing herself. "The whole of the Descent is one mechanism. It opens for a party with a
warrant. It feeds them. It *fits* them. And then, some way along, it requires that they
arrive at the end as whatever they were when it started."

"Five," said the duke.

"Four," said Hester. "It is currently keeping us at four, and it is very comfortable, and
the width has not changed in six hours, and that is not generosity; that is a room that
does not intend to be rearranged."

"And the fifth?"

"The corridor is taking four," said Hester, "and the last one of us is spending a very
great deal of what we have left being extremely careful."

---

It was Tuppence, obviously, and she was not careful for four minutes afterwards, and the
four minutes were the worst of Mittens's life and he has said so since.

"You could have gone up," she said. "That's the thing. You had the stair, you had nine
days, and you were half a day ahead of us and you came *back*."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've been saying it to myself for two days and it hasn't got any less
true—"

"I know what it was," said Mittens.

"Do you want to know what I said to her? In the corridor?" Tuppence's voice went up, and
then went flat, and then did something Mittens had not heard from her before, which was go
quiet. "I told her I wasn't for sale any more. And she asked me why, and I said because
I'd noticed I'd be the only honest one, and she said that was an answer for a person, not
a mouse, and I have been thinking about that for two days and I'd like it on the record that
I've not got a better one."

"She was right."

"I know she was right. That's what I've been thinking about." Tuppence sat down on the
warm floor of a corridor that was keeping her, along with four others, at a width it had
chosen. "You want to know the worst of it. The worst of it is that I'd have got away with
it. I would have been in the Bell House in Millward with a second cup and nobody would ever
have worked out where the lamp went, and I'd have gone on moving things for forty years,
and there would be one fewer mouse in the world who knew."

"And now?"

"And now I have to go and say it out loud to a dog," said Tuppence, "who swore an oath with
a clause in it about how his errand will not change, and he is going to be *so polite*
about it, Mittens, that's the thing, that's the whole thing, he is going to be so
extraordinarily polite."

"He will."

"It'll be unbearable."

"It will," said Mittens, "and you should have it coming, and I'll stand next to you when
you do it."

The mouse looked up at him.

"Why," she said.

"Because I'm the seventh of my name and my whole life is a party of one that kept turning
up," said Mittens, "and I'd like to be next to somebody when they do the honest part."

---

They walked for another day.

On the second evening the duke made a speech, because the duke could not help it, and it
was about a sentence he had been composing for two hundred miles and it was as bad as
Tuppence had predicted, and Rocky delivered it standing up, to four people, in a warm
corridor, having sworn an oath that forbade exactly this.

"—and I have thought about it, and I know the account, and I know what a sentence does
here, and I want it on the record that I have chosen it anyway—"

"And it is a beautiful sentence," said Tuppence, "and it is going to take a lamp and a
thousand years to get anything out of it, and I say that with love."

"—and I am not asking for a better man," said the duke, with enormous dignity. "I am
asking for a *true* one. Which is a smaller thing and is more than I have."

"Rocky," said Hester, "that is the most careful sentence anybody has said in four hundred
years, and it will work exactly as you wrote it, which is the problem."

"Yes," said the duke. "I have rather begun to understand that."

---

The corridor opened at last, and it was not a door and it was not a wall and it did not
close behind them.

It was simply, after eleven days of a warm lit passage the width of five, a room with a
floor, and a ceiling a long way up, and no light in it except a line along the wall, and
the line did not come from a lamp.

There was nothing in the room. Mittens had expected something for four days. There was
nothing in the room.

"It's bigger than it should be," said the duke.

"It is exactly the size it needs to be," said Hester Pye, "for a party of four, and it is
going to stay that size, and I would like everybody to notice that we have not been
counted wrong."

There was a light in the middle of the floor, and it was about the size of a teapot, and
it was the colour of weak tea, and it had been there the whole time, four hundred miles
above their heads, waiting, with the patience of a thing that has never once been in a
hurry.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"Right," said the Duke of Cavalton, and took a step towards it, and then stopped, and
turned round, and looked at the four of them, and Mittens understood that this was going
to be the last time the party was five and it was going to spend itself on nothing.

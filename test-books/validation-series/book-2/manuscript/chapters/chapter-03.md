The shift does not begin. I want that at the front of this chapter, because every account of work I have ever been given starts with a bell.

There is no bell here. Nobody calls anything. At some point in the morning a woman who had been fitting two pieces together stood up and walked along the row to a different part of it, and the person beside her took up what she had left without either of them saying one word about it, and that is what a shift is. It is not an event. It is people moving and the work staying.

I had slept well. I am putting that in because I said I would be accurate and because it is the first night in a long while that nobody made me sleep near the door.

I came out holding the tool, since there was nowhere in the cabin to leave it and it did not occur to me yet to want one.

Here is the row, then. I am going to give it to you properly, because a person who describes a place without describing the work is only describing herself.

Down at the near end there is a man who keeps two pieces of something in line while a woman below him fixes them at the join. He has been at that for long enough that he does it with one hand and rests the other. He told me later that the second hand is for the days when the first one is tired, which I thought was careful of him and which turned out to be exactly what he meant.

Past him there are four people moving something heavy along a rail. They move it the same distance every time. It is not a long distance. It has been that distance for a very long while, I think, and it will be that distance for a longer one.

Then there are the ones I could not understand at all. Two of them doing something with the tops of things, checking and refusing and putting back. One who sits at a place where the rail turns and does nothing but watch the turn, all day, because the turn is where things go wrong, and standing at the turn is the whole of that part.

I am going to stop the list there, because the list is longer than the chapter and because the list is not the point. The point is that when I walked down it I could not find the part that did not matter.

I looked. I did this on purpose, before I had any idea what I was looking at. I went along counting the parts and asking myself which of them the work could do without, and every time I found one I thought might be spare, I found the thing that needed it two steps further on.

It is the arrangement of the place. Nobody has a part that is decorative. That is what I noticed before I noticed anything else, and I noticed it as a compliment, which is how I notice things.

Then I went back to my own part, which was at the middle of the row and was the simplest thing there.

It is not a difficult thing to hold a tool. I am not going to pretend I was given a craft, because I was not, and the work does not care what I think of it. But there is a right way and there is my way, and mine turned out to be close enough that by the second morning the woman who had handed it to me stopped checking the angle from four steps away, which she had been doing without my noticing, and which I noticed the moment she stopped.

I was good at it.

I want to sit with that sentence for a line, because I have been good at a great many things and almost none of them has ever been wanted by anybody.

I am good at being seen in a room. I am good at an entrance, and at a story told at the right length, and at knowing which of two chairs will make me look like the reason for the evening. All of it is real work and I am not going to be modest about it. None of it has ever been useful. It has only ever been wanted, which is a different thing, and I have never been able to say the difference out loud without sounding like somebody who has lost an argument.

Holding this was useful. It did not want me. It needed me to keep doing it.

I have thought about the difference for a while now and I am still not able to name it, and I have decided that is because there is not a name for it and everybody who has ever been useful has simply stopped trying to describe it.

On the second morning I tried to be looked at.

I am not proud of the way I did it, and I am going to describe it anyway, because the honest version is more interesting than the version where I happened to be excellent. I found the light. There was a way to stand, holding what I had, that put the tool between me and the flat grey sky so that the edge of it caught. I shifted my weight twice. I made an adjustment to a thing that did not need adjusting, purely so that the adjustment would be visible to anybody who happened to be watching.

A young man working the join four steps away looked up.

He looked, and he saw what I was doing, and he said, "It sits better if you take the weight off that foot," and went back down to the join.

He was right. I tried it and it sat better, and I stood there with the improvement in my hands and my position ruined, and I had never in my life been helped so completely by accident.

The woman at the turn did not look up either. Nobody did. There was one moment in the middle of the afternoon when I was certain I had an audience, and I performed the whole of a thing for it, and when I looked along the row the audience turned out to be Achird.

She was at the far end, walking the edge of the work the way she walks the edge of a clearing, and she had been there for some time.

The next part I will put down as it happened.

I have known this exact situation for years and I know what it is for. Somebody stands at the edge of a room and watches me, and I turn it into an invitation, because that is what watching is for. It is a door and I go through it.

I did that. I said something along the row about how well it was going, which was true and which had nothing in it that I needed her to answer, and she did not answer it, which is her way, and I got what I always get from that, which is nothing, and then I did something new, and I have thought about this more than anything else in the chapter.

Instead of performing for her, I asked her something.

I asked her whether the join four steps down was the same join the work had been making yesterday, or a different one. It was the sort of question I ask to hold a floor. It was shaped so that she would have to say something to answer it and so that whatever she said I would already know.

She looked at the join for a while. She watches things the way other people take notes.

"Different," she said. "Same shape."

Three words and a half, from a girl who spends nothing, and she was right, and I had not actually known.

I do not know what I expected from that. I want to put the small thing in, because it is the true one. What I felt was not that she had answered me. It was that she had been watching carefully enough to answer me, and had been doing it for a while, and that I had only just then started counting.

I did not do anything with it. I want that on the record. I had been given a fact about how much attention I was being paid by the only person on the planet who was paying me any, and what I did with it was put it down, because I was in the middle of something.

That is a habit of mine. I have been doing it for years and I have a whole set of names for it, and the names are all other things.

In the afternoon I asked the man beside me what happened if the tool was put down.

I asked it as a joke. I want that said, because the answer is not the important part and the way I asked it is. I asked it the way you ask a question you already know the answer to, so that you can be seen to be at ease.

He thought about it while he worked, which is the only way anyone here thinks about anything.

"Put it down and it isn't being held," he said.

"Right. But then what happens."

"Then somebody picks it up."

He said it the way you would say that a door needs to be closed. There was nothing in it. He had not misunderstood me and he had not dodged me, and I understood after a moment that I had asked him a question about consequence and he had answered a question about the work, because those are not different questions here and I had not learned that yet.

I tried once more. I said that I meant what would happen if nobody picked it up.

He looked at me then. It is the only time in my two days there that a person doing work stopped doing it to look at me about something that mattered.

"Nobody's done that," he said.

"Are you worried about it."

"No," he said, and went back to it, and it was brave and plain and nothing else. It was the exact answer to the exact thing I had asked, which is that in all the time there has been, there has not been one day when the tool has lain on the ground.

I think about that more than I think about most of it. Not because it frightened me. It did not frighten me, and I spent a long time afterwards trying to make it into something that had frightened me, and it never was. He was not afraid and not careful about it. He answered the way you answer that the tide comes in.

The work does not stop because it has not stopped. That is the whole of the reason. Nobody here is holding a rope. They are simply standing where the rope has always been, and none of them has ever found out what the rope is for.

There is one more thing from that second day and I have kept it until now because it belongs at the end of the account of the work.

I asked the man with the two hands what his part was called.

It was not an idle question. I have a habit of learning what a thing is called, because a name is a way of holding a place in a conversation, and I have got a great deal out of knowing what to call things.

He did not have an answer. It was not a refusal and it was not a secret and he was not being modest about it. I asked it three ways and he tried to help me each time and there was nothing there, and in the end he said, "It's the join," which is what he had been calling it. That is what that place has instead of a name, and I have thought about it since and decided that a world where a part is only what it does is a world with no room in it for anybody who wants to be introduced.

By evening my arm hurt in a way that had stopped being an accident and become a condition.

Achird came and stood at the end of the row and did not do anything, and I did not ask her to, and the light thinned the way it does, all at once and from the top.

Nobody had given her anything again. Second day. Nobody had refused her either, and to be fair to her: she had been asked once, in the morning, to hold a corner while somebody reached past it, and she had held it, and the moment the reaching was over she was let go.

That is not a place in the work. That is a hand used the way you use a hand.

I waited to see whether she minded. I am ashamed of how closely I watched her for it, and I am not going to pretend the watching was about her.

She did not mind. Or she did, and did not show it, which for Achird is the same fact from the outside, and I have been outside her for years.

"Nothing for you," I said.

"No," she said.

"Does that bother you."

She looked along the row. She looked for a while. I have learned that this is not hesitation with her, it is reading.

"Somebody should watch it," she said.

That was all of it. She went and sat in the sleigh, and I stood in the dark with a tool in my hands that I was already starting to think of as mine, and I did not know yet that she had told me the truth and that I had heard it, and that the two of us were going to spend a whole book proving it.

The work went on. Behind me, where I could not see it and did not need to, it went on being held.

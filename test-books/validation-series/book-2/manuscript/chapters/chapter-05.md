A woman put down what she was holding.

That is the first fragment and it is the whole of the event. I am going to give you the rest of them the way they came. Uneven. I am not going to smooth them, and the smoothing is what I have been doing for four days.

Her part was the turn. She stood where the rail turns and watched the turn, all day, because the turn is where things go wrong.

She put down what she was holding and she walked.

Not quickly. Nobody walks quickly here. She walked along the outside of the rail the way people walk when they are going to be a while.

The turn was unwatched for four minutes.

I counted those. I want the number in. It is the only number in this chapter and I have kept it for the same reason I keep my good coats.

Somebody came and took the turn. I did not see who, and nobody mentioned it afterwards, and it was the same woman who had shown me where to put the tool on the first afternoon, or it was not.

The rail had four people on it and then it had four people on it.

The man beside me did not stop. He moved what he was carrying out of the way of my hand without looking away from it, the way you move a branch, and he had been doing that for four days and I had never once thought about it until the turn was empty.

The light did not change. I have mentioned the light a great deal and I am going to mention it once more and then stop. It thinned at the usual hour and it did not thin differently, and the work went on under it, and the leaving was inside the day like a splinter and the day did not swell around it.

Nobody followed her.

I am going to put that in the middle, where it was.

Nobody followed her and nobody argued with her and nobody stood in front of her and nobody said her name. I do not know her name. Four days beside a woman and I do not know her name. Nobody here used it. That is not because they were being cold.

I did not follow her either. I want to be exact: I thought about it. I thought about it while the turn was empty and I did not put the tool down and I did not go.

A person I had spoken to every day for four days walked out of a place she was needed and went somewhere, and the place took up the slack, and the slack was taken up before the hour was out, and the only way you could tell it had happened was that her part had a new woman at it and the new woman had clearly done it before.

I asked the man beside me where she had gone.

"Home," he said.

He said it the way he said everything, and I want to be careful here, because a person writing this afterwards would like the word to have weight in it. It did not have weight. It had the weight of a word in a list. He was not being kind to her memory and he was not being unkind about it and he had given me the answer that was true, which is that she had gone home, and that the work did not require him to know any more than that.

I asked him whether she would come back.

"Some don't."

I asked him whether anyone minded.

He thought about it while he worked, which is the only way anyone here thinks, and then, without any emphasis at all, he said, "We'll be poorer."

That was the whole of the mourning. Four words, in the middle of a shift, from a man who did not stop carrying.

I am going to be honest about what I felt next, because I have been honest about everything so far and I do not intend to stop at the part where I look worst.

I was angry at her.

Not at the place and not at the man. At the woman. She had a part that mattered and people who were poorer without her and she had walked down the outside of the rail with it all still standing, and I sat on that anger for most of a shift before I turned it over and looked at what was underneath it.

What was underneath it was the thing I do not say.

I have left every room I have ever been in. Every single one. I have walked out of better places than this with more people in them who were poorer for it, and I have never once turned around at the door, and I have built a whole account of myself on the fact that it was easy.

Here is what the account does not include. Nobody ever came after me either. That is the part I have never put on the page. I have always narrated the leaving as a decision I made, and it was one. The truth underneath the decision is that not one person in any of those rooms ever stood up.

I looked down the outside of the rail where she had gone and I understood that I was not angry at her for leaving.

I was angry at how tidy it was.

Over the next two days the work was not lighter and nobody arranged anything around the absence. That is the part I would have got wrong if I were telling it to a room. There was no ceremony for her and no gap held open. The new woman at the turn took the turn. The rail did not slow. The poorer-ness the man had mentioned lived somewhere I could not see it, and it did not interrupt anything, and I have decided since that this is what something real looks like.

I worked harder.

There is a thing that happens to me in rooms and I am going to put it in, because it is the closest I got to understanding the place while I was standing in it.

When a room is going well I can feel where the attention is. It is a physical skill and I am not modest about it. You move towards the warm part and you do less, and I have done it in every room I have ever been in and it has never once failed me.

I could not feel it there. There was no warm part. There was work in every direction at the same temperature and no direction to move in, so I did the only other thing I know, which is to work harder than the people around me and be seen doing it.

Nobody saw me doing it. I want to be exact about that as well, because "nobody saw me" is the kind of sentence a person writes in order to be pitied, and I am not writing it for that. Nobody saw me because there was nothing to see.

I want to be exact about why. It was not loyalty and it was not a tribute to the woman who had left, and it was not the work needing me; it was that I could not stand being the second person in that row who was thinking about the outside of the rail, so I put my head down and I was useful for two days straight, and on the second evening I was so tired that I had to sit on the ground to hold the tool, and I was as pleased with myself as I have ever been.

That is the machine. It takes the one thing you have and makes the work out of it. I did not see the machine at the time. I saw a girl doing well.

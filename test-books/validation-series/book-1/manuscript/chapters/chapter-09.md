"We have to go," she said, and I was already talking.

I was talking about the light. I was saying something about how the light here comes down through the growth in one piece, which it does, which is a thing I had said before and would have said again, and I said it over her sentence the way you step over a puddle.

We have to go.

She does not waste. I have said that. She says a thing and it stands there and does not move, and if you walk around it, it is still standing there behind you, and you know it is, and it knows you know.

I was talking about the light.

I went on talking about the light for a while. I do not remember the rest of it.

I have tried to get it back. I can see the clearing and I can see her, and between the two of them there is a stretch of time with no sound in it, and I know I filled it, because I fill everything.

Here is what I remember. I remember it perfectly, and I would give a great deal to remember it worse.

I did not answer her. That was the first thing. A person asks you a question and you answer the question, and Achird made a statement and I answered a different statement, one I had made up, one nobody had said.

I said we had only just gotten settled.

She did not say anything. She never did.

I said the fruit was better this year. The light was good. I said there was a place under the growth where the air sits cooler and that I had found it and that she always liked cool air and I had found that for her.

I was standing in the middle of the clearing. I want the picture clear, because the picture matters and I have thought about it enough. The light was going down through the trees in the one long piece. The beings were turned toward me from every side of the clearing, glad, unhurried, thirty-odd of them or forty, and I had stopped counting them weeks ago, and every single one of them was watching me the way they had watched me when I arrived.

Nothing has ever asked me for anything here.

Do you understand what I had in my hand. There is not a place in the sky that has ever given me that. I have been looked at in a hundred rooms and every one of those rooms wanted something back. This one wanted nothing. It put the bench where I like it and it brought the bread and it did not want one thing, and Achird was standing at the end of it telling me to leave.

So I listed it for her.

I do not know what I was trying to prove, and I want to say that I was not frightened, because I was not, and a person reading this afterwards is going to put fear into it and I would rather be honest than flattering.

I listed the bread. I listed the bench. I listed the thing they do with the bowl. I told her about the low ground and the warm spot the whole crowd goes to in the evening, and how I had watched them go down there in the light, and how it is the most peaceful thing I have ever seen.

She stood there.

I listed the objects.

I want you to hear that, because it is the worst of the list and I am aware of it. I told her about the shelf, and the forty-one things on it, and my button, and the stone, and I said that they had been laid out with care and that this is what people do when they are coming back, and I said it as though it were an argument, as though the shelf were a thing that had happened to me and not a thing I had walked past with my hand flat on it.

I told her that I had never been anywhere that did not eventually ask. It was a boast.

That is the sentence I have looked at longest, after the other one. It was as though the not asking were the achievement. I did not hear what I was saying while I was saying it, and I have heard it every day since.

She stood there.

I asked her what, exactly, she thought we were going to do somewhere else.

I asked her what was wrong with the place.

I asked her what she wanted, and I put it kindly, the way you put a question to a child, and I want that cruelty named too, because it was the first one, and it was a kindness I was using as a lever.

I want to add that she answered it. In the sense that she stood there and let me ask. I kept asking, and the asking got longer, and the longer it got the less it was a question.

She said nothing.

So I got louder. Not in volume. I have never needed volume. I got longer.

I said that I had seen more places than she had. I said that I had walked into a hundred rooms and known in four seconds what they were, and that I had never once been wrong about a room. I said that this room was the best I had ever been in, and that I did not intend to let a person who had never once told me what she wanted make the decision for both of us.

And she said, "Please."

One word. Not the sentence. The sentence was behind it, and she had spent the sentence already.

I did not want it. That is the one thing I let her have, and I am writing it down because it was real. It is the last word she said to me for a long time and I did not honour it. I did not even hear it properly, and the reason I did not hear it is that I heard it as a plea, and I have never in my life negotiated with a plea.

I said we are not going.

I said it flat. It came out in my best register, the one I use when there is a room and I intend to keep it.

And then she did not answer, because she already had, and she has not argued with me since the day I explained the soil to her.

So I said it again.

And then I did the thing I do.

It is not a thing I decided. I want that understood. Nobody sits down and decides to find the one sentence in the room that will do the most damage. You find it the way you find a step in the dark.

I said: "You never once told me what you wanted. And now you are angry at me for not hearing it."

That is it.

That is the sentence. I have taken it out and looked at it more times than I can say. It is true. It was true the day I met her and it is true now. She has never once been direct about anything she wanted from me, not once, not in all the time we have had each other, and I have been beside her for every hour of it and not one of those hours had a question in it.

And she was not surprised.

That is the part. I have never been able to get past the part. She did not flinch and she did not startle and she did not look at me as though I had done something new. She looked at me the way a person looks at the weather they already dressed for.

She has heard it before.

Not from me. From the sky. From every room I have ever stood in the middle of and owned for an evening and left. She has heard it from her whole life, and she has heard it standing right beside me, and I said it out loud to her in a clearing where nothing had ever asked me for anything, and the sun went on coming down through the trees in one piece.

Nobody came.

I want to say that as plainly as I can. Nobody came. No being turned its head. No being crossed the clearing or stood between us or looked at Achird, or looked at me. Nothing in the place changed its manner. The fruit was still there. The light was still good. Forty-odd glad things stood in a ring around the two of us and watched us do it, and not one of them detected anything, because nothing had happened as far as they were concerned, and nothing had.

There was nothing to fight. I never understood that until I was standing in it. I had spent the whole of my life dealing with rooms that pushed back, and this one would not.

I kept talking. I do not remember about what. I said something about the coat story and something about the winter I was famous, and I laughed at my own joke, and Achird stood at the end of the clearing and did not say anything.

And then the sleigh moved.

We had left it at the far edge of the clearing where the ground is flat, where it had sat the whole time, and it had not moved in all the weeks, not once, and I had stopped thinking about it the way you stop thinking about a wall.

It moved.

You do not see a thing like that move and mistake it for anything else. The runners found the ground and the cabin leaned forward and the whole of it turned toward the open and stopped, and it was facing the way we had come in and waiting there the way a person waits who has already decided and is not going to discuss it.

I stopped talking.

That is the only time in this book I stop talking, and I want it in the record, because everything else I tell you is a version, and that half-second is the truth.

Achird went to it.

She did not run. She did not even walk fast. She crossed the clearing the way she crosses rooms and she put her hand on the side of it and it was already open.

I looked at the beings.

Since I have gone this far, I will say what I was looking for. I was looking for one of them to stop her. I was looking for something in a hundred glad faces that had finally noticed what was happening and did not want it.

They were glad. They made the room. One of them did the wipe on its palms before it handed the fruit to somebody else.

Nobody watched us go, because nobody had understood that we were going anywhere, and there was nothing to understand about it, and they were made of the thing that does not keep what visitors become.

I have gone over those faces. There were forty of them, or thirty, and every one was glad, and there is not one face in that clearing that understood we were leaving. I have looked for one. I have made myself look for one, on the nights when the other thing is too heavy to pick up, and there is no face there that is sorry. I do not know whether I would have wanted there to be.

I stood in the middle of that clearing with my best register still on and nothing left to use it on.

Then I followed her.

This is the part I get to decide how to write, and I have decided to write it wrong. I did not decide to go. I did not weigh it and I did not agree and I did not turn to the things that were glad to see me and give them a reason. The sleigh had decided and Achird had gone to it, and my feet went the way feet go, and I was still talking when I reached the runners.

I have been looked at before.

Not like this. I want to be accurate about it, because accuracy is one of the few things I have that nobody gave me.

The cabin is old, and the glass is old glass, which is to say it is not quite clear, so the dark on the other side of it comes through as a soft grey instead of as dark. The runners are wood. There is a bench down one side and a lamp that does not need lighting, and a small brass thing on the wall that has never been explained to me, which is the only reason I have never asked about it.

We were somewhere that was not on any chart. I checked.

I check things. It is one of my better qualities and I have never once been thanked for it.

That night the shape it had taken was Delphinus. That means a dolphin, which means a long curve made of dark with lights strung along the length of it, going up and coming back down again for no reason a dolphin would recognise as a reason.

I was steering it.

Achird was sitting forward, where the glass curves, with her shoulder against it. She does that. She has done it for as long as I have known her, which is long enough that neither of us has to say anything about it.

"Delphinus," she said.

"I know what it is called."

"I know you do."

She turned her head back to the stars. Behind the shape the rest of the lights went on being lights.

"How long," she said.

"That depends on what you mean."

She did not say anything else. That is how I know it was a real question. A girl like Achird does not spend sentences. She had one, she spent it, and the spending was the whole of it.

I will say this now, while we are being accurate. I did not know how long. I have never once known how long, and I have never let it stop me, and I would thank you not to make one fact out of the two.

Below us was a world. It does not have a name. I am not going to give it one.

It had weather. The weather was being patient.

Landing is not difficult.

Landings are difficult when the ground has opinions, and this ground did not. We came down the way you put down a bowl you have finished with.

Nothing about arriving here was hard. Nothing about arriving here was even interesting, and I have come to believe that is the highest thing you can say about a place, because interesting places keep you standing up.

The light is easy. I want to be careful with that word, since I do not usually use it. Light is normally something a person has to manage: too much in the morning and gone by the middle of an afternoon, or it comes off the water at an angle that ruins whatever you came to look at. This light did not do any of that. It arrived and it stayed. It showed you things.

Then I saw the hedges.

The growth along the edge of the clearing here is not a wall and not a border. It has room in it. It is arranged the way chairs are arranged, in a space where somebody has already thought about where a person would want to sit.

I took that as a compliment.

I would like it on the record that I took it as a compliment and not as anything else, because that is what it was. There is no other reading of a room that has been set up before you walk into it. Somebody did the work. You get to enjoy it.

There is a bench at the near end of the clearing. It is low and it is old and it faces the middle of the space, which is where a bench should face if the point of a bench is watching whatever arrives.

I sat on it.

Achird did not. She walked the whole edge first, all the way around, which took a while. When she came back she stood behind the bench instead of on it, and looked at the hedges rather than at the clearing.

"It is old," she said.

"The bench?"

"All of it."

I told her that old things are the best things, and that she had never once disagreed with me about that, and she did not disagree with me about it then either.

The second morning started with eleven of them and no introductions, which is the correct way to start a morning.

They had worked out by then that I like an audience. I had not told them. They had decided it on their own, sometime overnight, the way a house decides where the light goes.

Here is the thing about a good audience. Most people, when they watch you, are also weighing you. There is a small arithmetic running behind the face. What does this one want, what will this one cost, how long until I can leave. It runs so quietly that most of the time you forget it is running at all, and then you meet an audience that does not do it and the silence is enormous.

I have never had that before. I performed all morning and I enjoyed it, and I want to be honest about the enjoyment, because I am not embarrassed about it and I am not going to write it down as a fault.

I told them about the twelve cities. I told them about the winter I spent being famous. I told them the one about the coat, which is the best thing I have and I do not give it out cheaply, and every one of them took it exactly as intended.

They made room for me. That is the part I keep coming back to.

Wherever I moved, the space was already there. I did not have to say excuse me once. I walked from one end of the clearing to the other in the middle of a sentence and the crowd opened in front of my feet like I had paid somebody to do it and forgotten.

I asked one of them if they were always this considerate.

It said, "We are glad you came."

That is not an answer. I have given that answer to people myself, and I know what it is for.

I did not mind. I had a whole morning and a clear floor, and I was doing my best material.

Across the clearing, Achird was sitting on the ground with her back against a stump, and three of them were sitting with her.

They sat down first. I want that remembered. She did not call anybody over. She was being the least interesting person in the clearing by a considerable margin, and three of the beings had decided, apparently on their own arithmetic, that she was the one to be near.

They liked her. I want to be exact, because I have been accused of glossing. Politeness would be the wrong word for it. They were glad to see her the way they were glad to see me, the whole thing, no deductions.

She had her book out. She did not open it.

One of them was doing something with its hands, and Achird watched it.

I know the way she watches. She goes still in a particular manner. There is a difference between a person resting and a person taking something in, and with most people you can see the second one arrive on the face. With Achird you cannot, because nothing arrives, it just goes in and stays.

The being finished what it was doing and started again. Achird looked at the space between the light and the beings moving. Then she looked at it again.

I did not know yet that she was counting. That came later. It came badly, and I will get to it.

I told the coat story again for the ones who had arrived late. It went better the second time.

Someone had brought the fruit with the stone in it, and there was bread, and one of them had carried the bench around to where the sun had moved to, which is a thing nobody has ever done for me before or since. I sat on it and ate and talked and was watched and did not have to earn one minute of it.

Would you like to know what I did with the afternoon?

Nothing. That is what I did with it. I have been busy for years. I lay on a bench in the middle of a clearing while a great many polite strangers admired me at their own expense, and the light did the thing with the trees. Nobody asked me for anything.

It is the best afternoon I have had. I am not going to be coy about that either.

Achird got up and walked the edge again.

She had done that on the first day and I had thought she was settling. On the second day she was not settling. She walked the same length of the growth three times and stopped each time at the same place and stood there for the length of a held breath.

Then she came back, and she had the object from the first evening, and she held it up.

It was small. It was white, or it had been. There was a hole worked through one end of it, and the work was fine, and the fine work had been done by a hand I would like to meet, because whoever made that could have made anything and chose to make that.

"Look," she said.

I laughed. It was a good laugh, one of my better ones, and I did it because the thing was genuinely funny to me, a little white nothing from nowhere with a hole in it, and she was holding it up with the seriousness of a person showing you a crown.

"Very nice," I said. "Did you feed it."

Then I told her about the bread, because the bread had been better that morning than the day before and I wanted to know whether anyone else had noticed, and by the time I was done with that the object was back in her hand and the moment had gone somewhere and I did not chase it.

She put it back in her pocket.

I want to note, for the record, that I did not look at it closely. There was no reason to. It was a small white thing and the sun was on the wood and the bread was better than it had been, and a person who stops in the middle of a good afternoon to inspect a button has misunderstood the afternoon.

Achird took it to one of the beings.

She did it while I was talking to four of them about the coat, so I saw the shape of it and not the words. She held the object out flat on her palm. The being came over and looked at what was in her hand.

Then Achird asked it something.

The being answered immediately.

There was no pause. That is the part I remember, because there should have been a pause and there was not, not the smallest one, not the little lean forward a person does before they decide how much to say.

It answered, and Achird stood there with her hand out, and after a moment she closed her fingers over the object and put it away and said nothing else to anybody for a while.

I asked her about it that evening.

"What was that about."

"The object."

"Which object." I knew which object.

"I asked one of them what it was."

"What did it say."

She was quiet. Not the ordinary quiet. The kind where the answer has been chosen and is being set down carefully.

"It said the object is a good one," Achird said. "I asked whose it was. It said it is not theirs. Then it said the object belongs on the shelf, and it went back to being glad."

"So?"

"So it knows where the thing goes."

"It is a helpful place," I said. "Have you noticed the way they carry the water."

"They know where it goes," she said, "and it is not theirs, and it has been here long enough that it has a place."

That is her whole method, if you want it. She puts three true things next to each other and then stops, and expects the person listening to do the arithmetic, and I have never once done it for her, because it is not arithmetic, it is a mood, and I have better things to do than have moods about cutlery.

I told her the beings were being kind and she was reading weather into a shelf.

She did not argue. She looked at the growth for a while.

What I did not do, I have thought about since. I did not ask her what she thought the shelf was for. I did not ask her at all, because the evening was going well and because there is a particular face Achird makes when she has a fact and no use for it, and I did not want that face at my evening.

The measurements were the next morning.

I woke up and she was already up, which was unusual, and she was standing where the growth thins out toward the open, with her arms folded, watching the changes.

I asked her what she was doing.

"Counting," she said.

Counting what, I said.

"The time between the light going and them turning."

I waited.

"Same each time," she said. "I did it yesterday. It is the same."

The same how, I said, and she told me, and it was a number, and the number had a use in the way that a stone has a use.

I told her that the light here goes down all at once and the beings turn when the light goes, and that this is not a mystery, it is a place behaving properly, and that if she wanted a mystery she should try to make one of them take a compliment.

She agreed with me.

That is her method. She agrees. She has the fact, she states it, she watches to see if you will pick it up, and if you do not, she does not put it down again in a hurry. She files it and goes on being pleasant and goes on taking her measurements. Every single one of them is correct, and none of them has ever once mattered to me, and I am telling you this in the order it happened.

The morning was better than the first morning. I want that on the page.

They had brought more bread. Someone had made the place under the trees cooler, which I could not explain and did not try to, and the fruit was back and the green thing had been left out on purpose, which I chose to read as tact.

I did the twelve cities properly. I did the coat. I did the one about the door I was not supposed to open, which is a lie, and which is one of my best, and eleven of them held their faces at exactly the right angle while I told it.

And Achird sat against her stump with three of them near her and took her measurements. Nobody watched her do it, and that is the thing I keep coming back to when I am supposed to be asleep.

They liked her. They liked her as much as they liked me.

Look at what that does to a person. I had spent my whole life being the one who was looked at, and I had earned it, and here was a place where it was being given to both of us at the same price, and it did not occur to me then to wonder what the price was.

I will tell you what I did with the thought, since we are being honest.

I put it down. I had a good afternoon in my hand and I put the thought down, and I went back to the coat and got the laugh, and I remember thinking that I had never had a room this easy and that I intended to stay in it.

They were glad about that too. They are glad about everything. That is the entire mechanics of them.

At the end of the day I offered to do something.

I am not sure what I meant by it. I had eaten their food for two days and slept under their light and taken the whole clearing for a stage, and some old habit in me reached for the part of the evening where you pay.

I stood up and said I would carry the water.

One of them said, "It is carried."

I said I would help with the growth.

It said the growth was doing well.

I asked if there was anything they wanted from us. I put it as a question, the way I put everything, expecting the room to turn and answer, because rooms turn and answer when I ask.

Nothing did.

They went on being glad. One of them offered me fruit. Another one made room on the bench, though the bench was already mine and there was nobody near it.

And I stood there with my offer in my hands and nowhere to put it down.

I sat back on the bench and ate the fruit and let the light do the thing in the trees.

It is a strange thing to be given everything and asked for nothing. I have decided it is the highest compliment a place can pay, and I have written it down that way, and I am not going to take it back.

Across the clearing, Achird was looking at the growth again, at the same place she had stopped three times that morning, and the counting was over. She was just looking, for slightly longer than the looking was for.

She took my hand again.

That is how it started, so that is where I start. Not with a place. With a hand, and me being pleased about the hand, which is the only part of this chapter I would keep if I were allowed to keep one thing.

She walked me out past the far edge of the growth, where I had never gone, because there had never been a reason to go there.

It was a nice walk.

I am aware of how that sounds. I am leaving it in.

The path turned near the top, where the growth thinned and you could see down into the hollow, and I stopped there, because stopping at the top of things and looking at what I have been given is what I do with my life. What I had been given was a wall, and a quiet place with the light on it, and I said so out loud, to her, and she waited for me to be finished.

The ground went up. A path went up, which is to say there was a line where the ground had been walked on enough that it stopped growing things, and it was not a track and not a road, and it went up and over a low rise and down into a place I had not seen.

She did not let go of my hand until we were standing in it.

A doorway.

Not a door. There was no door. There was an opening in a wall. The wall stood up on its own. The opening was the right height for a person and had been the right height for a person for long enough that the edges of it had gone smooth.

I put my hand on the edge of it and it was smooth the way a stair is smooth. From use. From a great deal of use.

The wall had no roof behind it. Whatever had been overhead was gone or had never been there, and the wall kept standing anyway, which is a thing walls do when they have been built properly and have nowhere to fall.

Inside.

A ring of stones sat with the middle of it swept. A bowl stood against the wall. The bench along the far side had been worn into a shape, and the shape was the shape of a person sitting on it a great many times in the same place.

The state of the room is doing a great deal of work here, and I did not notice it at the time.

Nothing was broken. There was no scattering in it and no tipping. Nobody had left in a hurry. Pick any room you have ever seen after somebody left it, and there is always one thing on its side.

The bowl was upright.

The walls had a height to them that had been chosen. I have built things. You can tell the difference between a wall that happened and a wall that somebody stood back from to look at. This one had been stood back from.

A place in the middle had held a fire once, and the ash in it was old and undisturbed, and it had been swept to one side with an implement, deliberately, the way you clear a hearth you intend to use again.

Achird walked the wall.

I sat on the bench because it was there and because my legs were warm from the walk, and I sat in the worn place, and it fit, and that pleased me too.

I told her it was a lovely spot.

I told her whoever built it had known what they were doing, because the doorway caught the light at the one hour of the day when the light is worth catching, which is not an accident, that is a decision, and I have stayed in houses twice this size that never once made that decision.

She did not answer, and I kept going, because there was a lot to say and I was saying it.

Then she found the shelf.

It was under an overhang at the back of the wall, low, about as high as my knee, and it was long.

The things on it were small.

There were a great many of them and they were laid out with care. That is the phrase and I am keeping it, because it is the right one. They were not piled. They were not stored. Each of them had its own space, and each space was the right size for what was in it. The gaps between one thing and the next were even, all the way along, which meant somebody had stood here and done this with their hands and their eyes and had gotten it right.

They were not from here.

I have handled goods from enough worlds to know a thing when I see it. Some of these were metal and some were cloth and some were the kind of white that bone goes when it has been in a drawer for a long time. They came from different places. They had different hands behind them. They had nothing in common with each other except that none of them belonged here and none of them was new.

A strap lay there, worn soft in one place where a hand had held it. A bead of blue glass sat beside it, the size of my thumbnail, chipped, still blue. A thing the length of my little finger did the job of a spoon if you were willing to eat slowly. And a square of cloth had been folded twice and set down flat, and the fold had been made carefully enough that it had held its edge.

None of it was recent and none of it was claimed.

And they had been arranged. The way you arrange things for somebody who is coming back.

I will be honest with you, because I said I would be. My first feeling was pride.

I stood in front of a shelf of other people's belongings in a place I had walked into a few mornings ago and I thought: they came here, and they liked it, and they stayed. That is my whole read of it. A camp somebody built and used and kept in order, and a shelf of the small things people carry, laid out because they were coming back to it.

People have always liked the places I like. That is not vanity, that is a standard.

The beings came while I was standing there.

Not all of them. Four, five, a few, and they were glad, and they went to the shelf.

One of them picked up a small metal thing and turned it over and put it back in exactly the place it had been in, with the same space on either side. Another one straightened a cloth that did not need straightening. A third took the spoon-thing off the shelf and held it up to the light and put it back, and did all of it the way you take a plate off a shelf you have eaten off all your life. They did it the way you do a thing you have done a thousand times, without looking, without thinking about it, without any question in it anywhere, and then one of them came over and offered me a fruit.

They were not being careful. I want that clear. They were not handling relics. They were moving things around a shelf they knew, in a room they knew. Every one of them knew exactly where each thing went and none of them had any idea why.

Achird was counting.

She had gone along the whole shelf with her finger, not touching, and she had a number, and when she got to the end she started again from the beginning and did it a second time, and came up with the same number, and stood there with it.

"Forty-one," she said.

I asked her forty-one what.

"Things."

"On the shelf."

"Yes."

"They brought their things," I said. "People travel with things. You of all people."

She did not answer that. She was looking at the shelf and not at me.

Then she asked it.

She asked it once, and she asked it the way she asks everything, flat, no lead-up, no weight in it, the way you ask a person to pass you something.

"If everyone who came here stayed," she said, "where are they."

I want to tell you what I said, because I said a great deal and I said it well, and because a person reading this later is going to want to know why nobody stopped me.

I said: they are here.

I said it plainly, because it is plainly true. Where would they be. There is a camp here that has been lived in and looked after, and a shelf with forty-one things on it that somebody has been tending, and a ring of stones swept ready for the next fire. You do not sweep a hearth for a person who is never coming back.

I said that Achird was doing the thing she does, where she finds one true fact and stands in it and forgets that a place is made of many facts. I said that people come to good places and stay in them, and that staying is not a tragedy, it is the whole point of a good place, and if she had ever once in her life been somewhere she wanted to stay, she would know that already.

I said all of it while walking, and I said it in good humour. I put my hand flat on the shelf at one point to make the point about the spacing.

And on the shelf, under my hand, one thing along from the cloth, was my button.

The white one. The small white thing with the hole worked through one end, that Achird had held up to me that morning by the bread and that I had laughed at.

And two along from it, dull, with the face worked into it in lines too fine to be an accident, was the stone.

I stopped talking for a moment.

Then I laughed, and it was a real laugh. Look, Achird, look, both of them, they put them out, somebody put them out with the rest, and that is the nicest thing anybody has done for me on this planet.

I was pleased. I stood in front of a shelf of strangers' belongings and found mine on it and I was pleased, and I put my finger on the white one and moved it a hair to the left to see whether the spacing would still be even, and it would not, and I moved it back.

That is the flat thing I have to tell you, and I am going to tell it flat.

I was happy that my things were on the shelf with the others.

Achird stood at the end of it with her hands at her sides and did not say anything at all.

She had not argued. She had not raised her voice or corrected me or asked it a second time, and I put the quiet down to the same thing I always put it down to, which was that I had said something true and she had nothing to add to it.

We walked back the same way, and I talked the whole way. She gave me her hand again at the top of the rise without being asked, or I took it, I could not tell you which.

The light was doing the good thing. The beings were glad. Somebody had brought the fruit with the stone in it and somebody else had carried the bench around to where I like it.

And I sat there, with all of it, and I said to Achird that this was the sort of place a person could put down roots. She looked at me for a moment and then looked at the growth. She did not say anything, and I did not mind.

The camp is up past the far edge. The wall is standing. The bowl is upright, and the ash in the middle has been swept to one side with an implement, the way you clear a hearth you intend to use again.

# Chapter 3 — Salt

Connie comes after closing with chicken and a grudge and sits in Delia's stall like a court.

"You screamed or you didn't?" Connie says. "Mum said everybody screams."

"I didn't scream," Delia says. "I folded."

"Folded through a ghost. That's worse. That's showing off." Connie puts the chicken down. Two thighs, rice, pepper sauce in a tub with the lid on crooked. "Eat. Then tell me what he said before the oil goes cold in my head."

Delia eats standing. The chicken is good. That annoys her less at night. "He remembers when the fryers sing. Can't touch paper, only point. Can't leave the footprint."

"Rules." Connie nods. "Ghosts with rules are manageable. Ghosts without rules are landlords." She looks at the shelf gap. "Evening, Mr Pye, if you're thin somewhere."

The fryers are off. The stall ticks as it cools. No warm patch. No margarine.

"He says the indenture of 1962 has a renewal," Delia says. "Keep trading, keep the arches. He saw it signed. Butter on the seal."

Connie goes still. Then she gets up and closes the side shutter halfway, the way you do when the talk is private. "My mum was at that signing. Stall 7 then was my uncle's. She said Bellamy from the council signed with butter on his cuff and everyone laughed, and Pye cried."

"Pye doesn't cry."

"Men cry at signings." Connie sits again. "Mum said there was a renewal bit. Said the market holds while the market holds, something like that. Nobody kept a copy. Council kept the copy."

"Council counts eleven stalls now," Delia says. "Pye says they walked through with tapes and skipped 12."

Connie's jaw moves. "They skipped 12? On paper?"

"That's what the ghost says."

"Ghosts count better than councils." Connie pushes the rice over. "Eat the rice. And Delia. The letters."

Delia looks at the crate. "After lunch, I said. Lunch passed."

"Open one now. The top one. I'll witness."

"It's my crate."

"It's our market." Connie folds her arms. "Ray's thumb."

Delia stops chewing. "Don't."

"Ray's thumb on the seal, Ray's debt on the suppliers, Ray gone six years and you filing paper by date. I knew Ray. You want me to lie about that?" Connie's voice drops, which for Connie is shouting. "My brother held pitch 3. Lost it in '89. I know what a missing rent book looks like. It looks like March."

Delia wipes the counter. Once. Twice. "You got letters too."

"Everyone got letters. Mine are opened. Want to see mine?" Connie pulls a folded notice from her apron. Creased from reading. "Thirty days. Section 4. Eleven pitches listed. 12 not listed. Not listed as closed, not listed as open. Missing. Like March."

Delia takes the notice. Reads it. The list runs 1-11. Stall 12 absent, no footnote, no gap. A dozen that counts eleven.

"They filed us as yard," Delia says.

"They filed you as nothing." Connie takes the paper back, gentle. "So. The rent book. Yours went missing March. When did you last see it?"

"March. Stocktake. Shah's visit." Delia stares at the shelf. "Eleven books and a gap like a pulled tooth."

"Who was in the stall March?"

"Everyone. No one. Leo filmed stocktake. Fenn walked through with a clipboard." Delia wipes again. "Fenn's father held pitch 3 before your brother?"

Connie looks at her for a long moment. "Fenn's father lost pitch 3 in '74. The margarine year. My brother got it after. Then lost it in '89. Pitch 3 is cursed and also a pitch." She stands. "Open your top letter, Delia."

Delia goes to the crate. Takes the cream envelope. Holds it. The paper is heavy. Council crest through the window.

She opens it.

Thirty days. Section 4. Eleven pitches. Same list. At the bottom, a line in conditional smoothness: *Should you consider your pitch affected, please contact the office by Friday.*

"Friday," Delia says. "Always Friday."

"Which Friday?" Connie says. "That's the question with Fenn. There's always a Friday and it's always last."

Leo's camera light appears in the doorway. "Is this the— are you opening letters on camera? Say that again, the eleven."

"No," both women say together.

Leo films the crate instead. "The crate is the series. The crate files by date. Episode two: the crate opens one."

Delia folds the notice and puts it in her apron, not the crate. The crate is for unopened. This one is opened. Opened paper lives elsewhere. She doesn't know where yet.

Connie watches this and says nothing, which for Connie is a speech.

"Connie," Delia says. "Your money. The whip-round. If it comes to buying—"

"If it comes to it, I have a stake," Connie says, plain, money-talk plain. "Brother's pitch money. Kept. Don't ask the number. Eat your chicken."

The fryers tick. The stall cools. Somewhere thin, Pye listens or doesn't.

Delia eats the rice. Files nothing. Says nothing about Ray's thumb, which is also a kind of filing.

Connie refills the tea from the flask she carries for sieges. The tea is strong enough to stand a spoon in. Delia drinks. The shutter half-down cuts the arch light to a bar across the counter. Through it come the sounds of lock-up: shutters going down along the row, keys, the pigeon girder settling, Fenn's office door at the yard end closing with its particular click that everyone knows and no one mentions.

"Tell me about March," Connie says. "Properly. Not the stocktake version."

Delia looks at the shelf gap. Eleven rent books, cloth-bound, years of takings and oil and weather in Ray's hand then hers. The gap where 12 should be has dust in it now. She wipes the dust. It returns. Dust returns the way paper returns.

"March stocktake," Delia says. "Shah came Tuesday. Schedule 4. Clipboard. He checked the cert file, the oil log, the allergen board. He was fair. He wrote slowly. Leo filmed. Fenn came Wednesday with his own clipboard, different colour, same silence. He stood where you're standing and asked where the rent book for 12 was. I said on the shelf. It wasn't."

"You looked?"

"Everywhere books can be. The office corner, the flour bins, the flat. Ray's box." Delia's hand stops on the counter. "Fenn said, if the book is missing, bring the takings ledger instead. I brought it. He copied numbers without touching the food. He never touches food without asking. Then he said, holder of 12 should keep a clean file, and left."

"Clean file," Connie repeats. "His phrase. His father said it too. Clean file, clean conscience, clean pitch. Then lost pitch 3 in the margarine year." She drinks. "Pye knew Fenn's father?"

"Pye knew everyone who showed up. That's his whole system. Showing up." Delia picks up a dumpling skin left from lunch, folds it without filling, pleat pinch turn, sets it aside. The habit will not leave her hands alone. "Ray owed the suppliers. Not the rent. I misremembered that for years. Pye will tell it back to me eventually, when the oil is hot enough. Debts I filed wrong."

"Ray's debt," Connie says. "Say it plain once. For the room."

Delia folds another empty skin. "Ray bought flour on credit in '19. Then oil. Then pork when the prices went. He meant to pay after Christmas market. He died in November. The suppliers waited. I paid half. I owe half. The stall stands. That's the whole of it. No mystery. Just money."

"Six years," Connie says. "You carry six years like a prep tray." She reaches across and takes the empty folded skins and eats one, raw, to stop Delia's hands. "Good. Chewy. Terrifying. Stop."

Delia laughs before she can stop it, a short flat sound. "Health inspector would close us for that."

"Shah feeds pigeons," Connie says. "I saw him. Tuesday. Behind the yard bins. Seed from his coat pocket. Schedule 4 in one hand, seed in the other. A man like that will give us the 48 hours, not the closure. Remember it."

Leo's light swings back past the doorway. "Say that again. Shah feeds pigeons. Is that episode three?"

"Episode three is school," Delia says. "Bed. Camera off."

Leo keeps filming the shutter bar. "Gran. If 12 isn't on their list, and the rent book is gone, and the indenture says showing up keeps it— does showing up count if they say you're not there?"

The room goes quiet. The fryers tick. The pigeon girder creaks.

"That's the question," Connie says softly. "That's the whole book in one sentence, boy." She stands, bulk in the half-light. "Delia. Tomorrow Fenn serves the notice on stage. On the steps, with witnesses. He likes a stage. Fetch the opened letter. Leo comes too. If the fryers allow, the ghost attends. We show up. That's the renewal. That's the clause."

Connie leaves the chicken, takes the empty tubs. At the shutter she turns. "And Delia. Your mum counted stalls as a girl on the pickle barrels. Mine did too. Twelve. Not eleven. Say it."

"Twelve," Delia says.

"Again. Louder. For the thin air, if he's listening."

"Twelve," Delia says, louder, to the ticking stall. "Stall 12. Dumplings.Holder present."

Nothing answers. The fryers tick down to cold. But the shelf gap sits there, dust-wiped, and the opened letter sits in her apron, not the crate, and both feel like showing up.

Fold first. Everything else after, except tonight the fold is done and the after is here, and the after is a number: twelve.

After Connie goes, Delia locks the side gate with the key that sticks. Lift, shoulder, turn. She walks the row with the torch off, counting without deciding to. Eleven shutters, then hers. She lays her palm on the cold steel of stall 12 and stands there while the arches tick and settle. The pigeon man has left seed on the yard stones. Fenn's office light is still on at the yard end, a single square of yellow with a silhouette in it, head bent over paper. For a second Delia imagines the file on his desk: eleven pitches listed, the twelfth missing, the gap filed as yard. She imagines the pen in his hand, unsigned. Then the light goes off and the yard goes dark and she walks home through streets that smell of rain that has not fallen, the opened letter in her apron like a second heartbeat, thinking of butter on a council cuff in 1962 and Ray's thumb on a dumpling seal and the way Connie ate a raw skin to stop her hands. At the flat Leo has left the camera charging and a note in school handwriting: interviewed Connie, 40 mins, ghost audible frame 12? Delia smiles once, short, and leaves the note where it is. She puts the letter on the table, not in a crate, not filed, just placed beside Ray's photograph, the one where he holds a tray up like a trophy and grins with flour on his chin. She tells the photograph nothing. She has never told it anything. That is a file she keeps closed. Tonight she leaves the letter open on the table and sleeps with the flat door unbolted for the first time in six years, because Connie is across the landing and the market is under the arches and twelve is present, and that has to count for something even on paper that counts eleven. Opened paper lives here now. She dates nothing. She writes in the keeper's book: Tuesday. Opened one. Eleven counted. Twelve present. Then the luck line, because Ray wrote it every day for thirty years and some debts you keep paying gladly: Fold first. Everything else after. Twelve present, she writes once more in the margin, underlines nothing, and sleeps with the yard keys on the chair and the oil smell still in her hair like proof of attendance, the market's own register kept in hair and aprons and steel, present and counted, twelve for twelve.

# Chapter 2 — Clause

—says twelve, clause says twelve, twelve stalls and twelve seals and twelve—

The voice arrives with the oil.

Delia has both hands in the lunch fold when the fryers come up to singing pitch, and the warm patch by the rent-book shelf thickens, and a man finishes a sentence he started fifty-two years ago. She knows the number without knowing how she knows it. The air smells of hot margarine and carbolic and rain on brick that has not rained since Tuesday.

She does not scream. This will be remarked upon later by Big Connie as a failure of hospitality. At the time Delia is holding a dumpling with the seal open and the oil singing the one high note and the fold will not wait. Pleat pinch turn. She finishes the seal before she looks up.

He is by the shelf. Seventy-ish, apron, waistcoat, sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour to the wrist. Thin hair combed wet. Eyes on the empty gap where the rent book for stall 12 should be. He points at the gap with one finger, straight out, the way you point at a health inspector.

"Clause," he says.

Delia puts the finished dumpling on the tray. Picks up the next skin. "No," she says.

He looks at her. Then at her hand. Then at the fryer. His face does something complicated, the way faces do when the oil is right and the memory comes with it.

"Oh, good," he says, in a completely different voice, a market voice, quick and London and annoyed. "You're on fry. Thank Christ. Cold I can hardly spell my own name."

Delia folds. Pleat pinch turn. "You're Harold Pye."

"Of course I'm Harold Pye. Who else would be pointing at my own shelf. Clause seven. No— clause nine. Clause—" He stops. Frowns at the fryer. "Keep them on. Keep them singing. When they tick I go thin."

"When they tick you go where?"

"Nowhere. Thin." He waves the pointing hand, irritated. "Listen, dumpling woman. The indenture. Eighteen sixty-two? No. Nineteen sixty-two. The market indenture. Signed in this stall, on that counter, butter all over the witnesses. Renewal clause. Twelve stalls in perpetuity provided the fryers— provided the—" He looks at the fryer again, anxious. "Something about showing up. Keep the stalls trading and the arches stay yours. I had it. I had the words."

Delia puts down the skin. Wipes the counter. Once. Twice. "You died in 1974."

"Correct. Margarine year." He says it the way other men say war year. "They put margarine in my own market. In my own stall. I died standing where you're standing, mid-objection. Clause—" He points at the fryer, then at the shelf, then at Delia. "You. What's your name?"

"Delia Marsh. Stall 12. Fifty-six. I don't discuss margarine."

"You hold 12? Then listen. The lease. The council says the lease runs out. The council is wrong the way councils are always wrong, which is to say precisely and in triplicate. The 1962 indenture has a renewal in it. I saw it signed. Butter on the seal. Old Bellamy from the council with butter on his cuff. If the market keeps trading, the market keeps the arches. The rent is open shutters. Something like that. The words are in the paper."

"What paper?"

"The indenture. Filed—" He stops. His mouth stays open. The fryer note wavers as Leo's camera light swings past, and Pye goes pale at the edges, like steam thinned by a door opening. "Keep them on. Keep the song. Cold I lose it."

Delia turns the thermostat up a fraction with her elbow, never stopping the fold. The note steadies. Pye thickens.

"Filed where?" she says.

"That's the— that's the—" He presses his hand to his head. His hand goes through his head a quarter inch and he does it again as if repetition will fix it. "When the fryers are on I remember. That's the rule. That's the first one. Fryers on, Harold remembers. Fryers off, Harold guesses, and Harold guessing is no use to anyone."

"Rule," Delia repeats. "There are rules."

"Three." He holds up three fingers, then points again because the pointing seems to be the default state of the hand. "One. I remember only when the fryers are on. Singing, not ticking. Two. I can't touch paper. Only point. Tried for fifty years. Finger goes through. Useless. Humiliating. Three. I can't leave the footprint. The market footprint. The arches and the yard and the pigeon girder. Outside that I—" He gestures at the side gate, the morning beyond it. "Thin. Gone. Like cold oil."

Delia folds four dumplings while he talks. Pleat pinch turn. The lunch rush will start in forty minutes and she is behind and a dead founder is listing regulations at her prep counter.

"Why can't you touch paper?" she says.

"How should I know why. I'm dead, not staff." He peers at the crate of unopened letters. "That's a lot of paper you're not touching either."

"Those are opened by nobody and filed by me."

"Ha." He drifts toward the crate, or rather the warm patch drifts, and he looks down at the envelopes. He extends one finger toward the top one. The finger hovers half an inch above the paper. "See? Nothing. Fifty years of nothing. I can point at the council until my arm gives out, which it doesn't, because dead, and they file me under draught."

Connie's voice comes down the arch: "DELIA. Ten minutes. You want the end of the queue or the middle?"

"Middle," Delia calls back, eyes on Pye. "Stay," she says to him, quiet. "Fryers stay on. You stay."

Pye straightens. Pompous again, the Edwardian rising through the market like fat rising through stock. "Madam holder of Twelve, I shall give testimony. The indenture of 1962, executed in this very—"

"Talk normal," Delia says. "Oil's hot. You talk normal when oil's hot."

"I—" He blinks. "Right. Right. So. Bellamy. Council clerk, moustache like a yard brush. He brings the paper on a Tuesday, same as market day, raining, and we sign on the flour bin because the counter's got dough on it, and my wife Mo puts butter on the seal because she says a market runs on butter, and Bellamy laughs with butter on his cuff, and there's a bit in it, a renewal bit, that says as long as the stalls keep showing up—" He stops. "That's all I get. Showing up. The rest is in the paper."

"Where's the paper?"

"If I knew where the paper was I'd have pointed at it for fifty years." He points at the shelf gap. "The rent book for 12's gone too. Noticed that, have you? Gone since— when the fryers are on I— March. Gone March. Someone took it or filed it or—"

"Don't say filed." Delia wipes the counter. "Nobody files here except me, and I file by date, unopened."

"Unopened." Pye stares at the crate. "Christ. You're one of those."

"I'm one of busy."

The shutter of stall 7 goes up with a crash. Footsteps. Leo appears in the side doorway, camera up, half term and fully awake.

"Gran. Is the ghost—" He stops. Looks at the warm patch. The camera's tally light blinks. "It's warm here."

"Don't point that at me," Delia says.

"I'm pointing it at you. That's the job." Leo swings the camera slowly across the stall. On the flip-out screen, Delia knows without looking: steam, counter, shelf. No man. "Say that again about margarine."

"There is no margarine." Delia folds faster. "Leo. The fryers. Watch the note. If it drops off singing, tell me."

"Why?"

"Because the fold needs the song."

Pye is staring at the boy. "Who's this?"

"My grandson. Sixteen. Films everything. Says that again about everything."

"Say that again," Leo says, to the warm air, hopefully. "The clause. Say the clause again."

Pye draws himself up. "Clause— I am Harold Pye, founder of this market under the arches, 1921, twelve stalls, butter standard—"

"Butter standard," Leo repeats, delighted. "Is this the bit where—"

"No bits," Delia says. "Fold. School. Half term ends."

"Half term never ends if you film it." But Leo keeps the camera up, and the fryer sings, and Pye keeps talking, faster now, the hot-oil eloquence running.

"Twelve stalls. I built twelve. Not eleven. Twelve. Bellamy wanted eleven for the rate. I said twelve, there's always a twelve, a market needs its dozen. Signed, sealed, butter. Renewal in perpetuity while the market trades. The council now says lease expires, thirty days, and they count eleven stalls on their paper. Ask them. They count eleven."

Delia's hands stop for half a seal. Then finish it. "They count eleven?"

"Walked through yesterday with their tapes. I pointed at 12 until I nearly thinned. Eleven. They wrote eleven." He points at Delia's chest. "You. Holder. You show up, 12 exists. You stop, they file you as yard."

Connie arrives with the tea, stops in the doorway, looks at Delia looking at the shelf, looks at Leo filming the shelf, looks at the shelf.

"He here?" Connie says, quietly, for once without volume.

"Fryers are on," Delia says. "He remembers."

"Ask him where the paper is."

"He doesn't know cold or hot. He knows showing up renews it."

Connie puts the tea down. For once she doesn't make a proverb. "My mum said he points. Said nobody knows what at because everybody screams."

"He points at the gap," Delia says. "Where the rent book should be. And at the crate. He can't touch paper."

Connie considers this. Then nods once. "Useful ghost. Points at the problem and can't make it worse." She raises her voice to the warm air: "MORNING, MR PYE. STALL SEVEN. CONNIE. WE PAY YOU RESPECT WITH CHICKEN."

Pye bows, startled into Edwardian. "Madam. The chicken of stall seven is— is adequate. No. Is distinguished. The pepper—" The fryer note dips as a gust comes under the shutter, and he thins mid-sentence, voice fraying. "The pepper— the— keep them—"

"Oil," Delia snaps at Leo. "The dial. Elbow. Now."

Leo lunges with his elbow, misses, catches the dial with his sleeve. The note climbs back to singing. Pye thickens, mid-bow, furious.

"—is distinguished," he finishes. "Don't let it tick. Ticking is death. Second death. The thin one."

Delia looks at the trays. Two hundred seals. Forty minutes to opening. The queue already forming beyond the shutter, office workers and night-shift nurses and the pigeon man with his bag of seed.

"Mr Pye," she says. "Harold. I open in forty minutes and the fryers go off and on all day with the rush. You thin and thick with the oil. Can you work with that?"

"I have worked with worse. 1947. Coal rationing. Fryers cold for a month. I was a rumour." He points at the crate. "Open the letters."

"No."

"Open one. The top one. The council one. It'll say thirty days and eleven stalls and you need to know the number they think you are."

Delia looks at the crate. Cream envelopes, card envelopes, filed by date, unopened, accruing interest the way dust accrues. The top one is cream, council crest showing through the window, March date stamp.

"After lunch," she says.

"Before lunch," Pye says. "Showing up includes reading."

Leo lowers the camera. "Gran. Who are you talking to? For the film. I need to say who you're talking to."

Delia wipes the counter. Picks up the next skin. The oil sings. The warm patch leans, listening.

"Stall 12's sitting tenant," she says. "Founder. Dead '74. Remembers only when the fryers are on. Can't touch paper, only point. Can't leave the footprint." She glances at the patch. "Have I got that right?"

"Word perfect," Pye says. "Clause— that is, yes."

"Say that again," Leo breathes. "All of it. Say it again slower."

"No," Delia says. "Fold. Open the shutter. And Leo— keep the fryers singing."

The shutter goes up. Morning comes in with the queue and the pigeons and the smell of rain that hasn't fallen. Pye stands by the shelf through the whole lunch rush, pointing at the gap where the rent book should be whenever the oil sings, thinning to a rumour whenever the fry dips, and Delia folds to the song and serves through it, and the market keeps its own order: steam first, then dough, then the cold off the shutters.

At two o'clock, in the lull, with the fryers ticking down and Pye gone thin as breath, Delia takes the top envelope from the crate and holds it unopened in a flour hand for a full minute.

Then she puts it back. Filed by date. Unopened.

After lunch, she tells the warm air. After.

The fryer ticks. The patch thins. Somewhere under the girder, a pigeon lands, and the market ticks with it, cooling toward the afternoon.

Fold first. Everything else after.

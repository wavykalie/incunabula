# Chapter 1 — Fold

She folds before the oil is hot because the fold is the prayer and the oil is the answer.

Pre-dawn. The night market with its shutters down, twelve stalls in a row under the railway arches, pigeons on the girder doing whatever pigeons do at four in the morning that looks like nothing and is probably everything. Delia Marsh lets herself in the side gate with the key that sticks. Lift, shoulder, turn, the way the gate needs. She walks the row with the torch off. She doesn't need it. Twelve stalls. She counted them as a girl when she was supposed to be doing homework on the pickle barrels. She counts them now without deciding to and stops at eleven because twelve is hers and twelve you don't count, twelve you open.

Stall 12. Dumplings. The shutter comes up with the sound it always makes, a long complaining note in E flat, and the stall smells of yesterday: oil, dough, steam gone cold, the particular sweetness of cabbage left too long in the prep trays. She bins the cabbage. That is the first prayer. Waste nothing you can sell, sell nothing you wouldn't eat, bin the cabbage.

The oil drums. Two of them. She taps drum one — Friday's oil, too dark, and she should change it and won't. It has one more day in it. It always does. The invoice for fresh oil sits in the crate with the letters, filed by date, unopened, accruing interest the way dust accrues on the rent-book gap. Drum two is fresh, pale, Sunday-smelling. She pours from two, because Delia Marsh is not made of money, and sets the thermostat, and listens.

Oil-by-sound. Her husband Ray taught her, standing where she's standing, his thumb on a dumpling seal. Cold oil ticks. Warm oil hums. Hot oil sings one note, high and steady, and you fold to it. The fryers tick now. Cold. She folds to the ticking. Fifty seals, pleat pinch turn, pleat pinch turn, Ray's thumb ghosting over her own. Six years gone. The fold stays.

"Fold," she says, to nobody, and the stall gives back the word in steel.

The radio goes on. Static, then the shipping forecast, then off. She works through it — dough rolled thin enough to read through (not really, but thin), filling mounded with the teaspoon with the bent handle, seals pinched shut. Fifty. Then fifty more. The fryers hum. Then sing. One note, high and steady.

And stall 12 is warm.

Not the oil warm. The stall. The air by the prep counter, three degrees warmer than the rest of the arch, the way it gets when — no. She stands with a half-folded dumpling in her hand and looks at the warm air.

"Ray?" she says. Then, flat, answering herself: "Oil."

But the oil is singing steady and the warm patch stays where it is, by the rent-book shelf. The shelf with the rent books, eleven of them, and the gap where twelve's should be. Stall 12's rent book has been missing since March. She has looked everywhere books can be. The gap sits there like a pulled tooth.

The warm patch moves. Just slightly. Toward her. Like someone leaning.

Delia Marsh, fifty-six, widow, debtor, keeper of stall 12, stands in her own stall at half past four in the morning with flour on her hands and says to the warm air: "Health inspection's Thursday. Whatever you are, be clean."

The air does nothing. Then the smell of margarine arrives. Very faint, very far, like a radio between stations.

She hates margarine. That is the whole of her position. Butter, Ray always said, butter is the truth and margarine is what you tell the council. The smell sits in her stall for three seconds and goes, and the fryer sings on, and Delia finishes the fold with hands that shake once and then don't.

She dates nothing. June — no. There is no June here. There is Delia, and the book she keeps (takings, oil, weather, one line for luck), and she writes in it: *Tuesday. 04:30. Warm patch. Margarine. Oil Friday's, one more day.* Underlines nothing. Then she draws a line under the entry and writes the luck line, the one Ray wrote every day for thirty years: *Fold first. Everything else after.*

The shutters start going up. Six o'clock. Big Connie's voice from stall 7, volume first, words after: "DELIA. Tell me those are yesterday's dumplings I smell or I'm reporting you to yourself."

"Yesterday's cabbage is binned," Delia calls back. "Today's fold is singing. Report that." She looks at the camera. Years of practice: ignore it the way you ignore rain. "Leo. School."

Connie appears in the archway, sixty, apron, gold tooth, carrying two cups. Behind her, half-hidden by her bulk, Leo. Sixteen. Grandson. Camera up before he's awake, the red light blinking at Delia's flour hands. "Tea. Drink." Connie hands over a cup. "You look like you argued with a ghost. Say that again, love, he missed it."

Delia takes the tea. Drinks. It is good. That annoys her, the way good things do before six. "Stall was warm. Fryers cold. Warm stall."

"Broken thermostat."

"Thermostat's fine. Oil sings true." Delia sips. "Smelled margarine."

Leo lowers the camera an inch. "Say that again. The margarine. Is that a bit? Is the margarine a bit?"

"The margarine is not a bit," Delia says. "The margarine is a fact I am not discussing. School."

"It's half term." Leo raises the camera again. The red light blinks. "Ghost margarine on day one of the eviction countdown. That's episode one. That's the whole series."

Connie goes very still. Then: "Pye." Like a sneeze. Like a swear. "Harold Pye. Founder. Dead '74. My mum said he haunts twelve. Said he appears to whoever holds the stall when the lease runs out, points at something, and goes. Nobody knows what he points at because everybody screams." She looks at Delia. "Did he point?" Said he died owing the council and the council's still collecting." She drinks her own tea. "You got letters?"

Delia looks at her. "Everyone got letters."

"Not everyone files them by date." Connie nods at the office corner — the milk crate with the envelopes, cream to card, unopened, in a row. Arranged by weight. That is what you do instead of opening them. "How many?"

"Ask the crate."

"Delia."

"Four. Plus the white one." She finishes the tea. "Thirty days, Connie. The ground lease. They're not renewing. Flats."

Connie sets her cup down. Very carefully, for Connie. "Flats," she repeats. "Over my pitch? Over your twelve? Over Pye?" Leo swings the camera to her, sensing volume. Connie sees the red light and rises to it the way dough rises to warmth. "MY MOTHER SOLD PEPPERS ON PITCH SEVEN WHEN THE ARCHES STILL HAD TRAINS. THEY CAN HAVE IT WHEN THE PIGEONS VOTE FOR IT." She points at the lens. "You put that first. Before the margarine. Context."

"The pigeons," Delia says, "are council tenants. They'll vote how they're told."

"Then we evict the pigeons." Connie picks up both cups. "Whip-round. Tonight. My stall. Bring your book and your ghost." She goes. Stops. Turns. Quieter, the volume gone somewhere real: "Ray's stall, Delia. Ray's fold. We don't file that under flats."

She goes. The archway empties. The pigeons do whatever it is on the girder.

Delia stands in stall 12 with the fryers singing and the warm patch gone cold-normal and the milk crate with its five letters, and the camera light blinking, and Connie mid-proverb, and does the thing procedure doesn't cover: she takes the top envelope — cream, heavy, her name in a hand that writes exit the way other people write sincerely — and holds it to the fryer light for one second. THIRTY DAYS. The words show through. She puts it back. Unopened. Filed. By date.

Then she folds fifty more, to the singing, pleat pinch turn, and Ray's thumb ghosts over hers, and the oil holds its note, and somewhere under the note a man's voice speaks. Almost under. Almost imagined. Municipal, Edwardian, mid-sentence, saying: "—clause twelve, sub-section—"

She stops folding. Listens. The fryer sings. One note. High. Steady. No voice.

"Oil," she says, firmly, to the stall, to herself, to the warm air that isn't there anymore. "Just oil."

But she writes it in the book anyway. *Voice? Clause twelve?* And underlines it none. And draws the line. And writes the luck line again, harder: *Fold first. Everything else after.*

The first queue forms at seven. Office workers, cabbies, a nurse still in scrubs, all wanting the same thing: hot, fast, folded. Delia serves the way she folds — no wasted motion, no small talk she didn't start. The nurse asks for extra chilli oil and Delia gives it and says "long night?" and the nurse says "long decade," and they understand each other perfectly for four seconds. That is the whole of the market's social policy.

Mid-rush the fryer note changes. Half a tone down. Delia's hand stops mid-seal. The oil is right — thermostat steady, drums as they were — but the note dips, wobbles, comes back. And with it the warm patch, there by the rent-book gap, stronger this time, almost somebody. The steam off fryer two leans sideways. There is no draft. The shutters are up and the air is still and the steam leans sideways toward the gap like a plant toward light.

"You all right, love?" The cabbie. Forty dumplings deep in the queue. "Gone pale."

"Oil," Delia says. "New drum. Disagrees with me." She serves him four, extra vinegar, on the house for noticing. He winks at Leo's camera. Leo gets the wink, the steam, the sideways lean, all of it. The red light blinks.

By eight the rush thins. The note holds steady. The patch goes. Delia wipes down the steel — once, twice, the anxious third time she doesn't count — and checks the rent-book gap the way you check a tooth with your tongue. Still gone. Still toothless. She writes in the book: *07:50. Note dipped. Steam leaned. Served through.* Then, because the book is also for luck: *Fold first. Everything else after.*

Connie leans on the shutter frame, watching her. "You know what your problem is?" she says. "You file the impossible with the bills. It'll accrue interest."

"Everything accrues," Delia says. "That's why I fold."

Outside, the market wakes fully. Shutters up, all twelve, the long complaining chord. Queues forming. Steam rising into the arches. From stall 7, Connie already arguing with a supplier about yams, winning. The argument has three movements — price, quality, ancestry of the yam — and Connie wins all three the way she wins everything: by still being there when the other person leaves. Delia listens the way you listen to weather. Then Leo replays the margarine moment on his little screen, sound up, and the three of them hear it: under the fryer note, half a tone down, something that is almost a word. Connie goes quiet, which has happened twice in thirty years. "Play it again," she says. He does. Clause. Almost certainly clause. Delia wipes the steel a fourth time and doesn't count that one either. From the girder, one pigeon watches stall 12 the way health inspectors watch everything. Fat, grey, unmistakable. Delia makes a note: pigeon census later. Some threats you log. The fat one has a white tail feather and an attitude of total ownership, which in this market makes it either a tenant or management. Leo lowers the camera at last. The red light dies. "Nan," he says. "If it's real. The ghost. What do we do?" Delia looks at stall 12 — the steam, the gap, the filed letters, the three degrees of impossible. "We fold," she says. "Everything else after." And stall 12 folds and fries and serves, warm and singing — kept for one more day.

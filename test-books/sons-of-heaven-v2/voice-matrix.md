# Voice matrix

> Phase 2.5 output. The document that makes the cover-the-name test passable: if you
> covered the attributions, you should still be able to tell the voices apart.

## Global narrative voice — V-01

**Register:** declarative, adult, unhurried. Present tense for the argument, past for the
history, and the shift is never signalled.

**Sentence shape:** mostly short-to-medium with a long sentence permitted once or twice a
chapter, for a turn the argument requires. A long sentence that could be two is a defect
unless the join is the point.

**First person:** used for judgements and for admissions, never for emphasis. If a
sentence would read the same with "I" removed, "I" is decoration — cut it. Target
density: under one per 400 words.

**Hedging:** the narrator is entitled to be unsure and is not permitted to be vague.
"I think" must be followed by what is actually at stake. "Arguably" is banned.

**Latin and Chinese terms:** italicised on first use in a chapter, plain thereafter.
*li*, *shi*, *wang*, *jian*, *fajia*. No characters.

**Rhythm:** the prose must be readable aloud at conversational pace. Any sentence needing
a second breath is wrong even when the argument is right.

**Forbidden:** "delve", "tapestry" (as abstraction), "testament", "moreover", "in
conclusion", "landscape" (figurative), "nuanced", "crucial", "pivotal", "robust",
"myriad", "palpable", "it is important to note", "serves as", "stands as".

## Figure voice cards

These figures are dead and mostly unwritten, so the cards govern **what is attributed to
them** — reported speech, surviving documents, and the narrator's characterisation when
speaking in their vicinity.

### Han Fei (d. 233 BCE)
**Register in his own writing:** cold, aphoristic, structurally complete. Two handles,
weather, blank face.
**Card:** his surviving text is the only voice in the book that argues in syllogisms.
When the narrator is near him, sentences get shorter and lose hedges. Reported speech
from him is always exact and never framed with "he would have said".
**Never:** made charming, made tragic, or presented as a sage. He is not a mystic.

### Qin Shi Huang (r. 221–210 BCE)
**Card:** almost no voice survives. Everything attributed to him comes through Li Si's
memorials or the *Shiji*, and the narrator must say so every time. The one reported line
about wishing to have met Han Fei is a reported line, not a quotation.
**Never:** a monster. The machinery is the subject; he is the man who signed it.

### Dong Zhongshu (c. 179–104 BCE)
**Card:** the only place in the book where the narrator's own sympathy shows without
discipline. Confucian metaphysics, delivered with conviction, and it worked — which is
the unsettling part.
**Never:** a sage. A man who was right about one thing at enormous scale.

### Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE)
**Card:** his voice is the *Records*, and the only access to it is through his choices.
Reported speech is always in the narrator's framing, flagged as such.
**Never:** stoic. The chapter is about the cost, and the cost is the point.

### Empress Lü (d. 180 BCE)
**Card:** the most nearly voiceless figure. Every characterisation is inference from
omission, and the narrator must own the inference out loud.
**Never:** "only woman to rule" as a frame. Never the妖 archetype; the texts supply it,
and the narrator's job is to distrust it.

### Wang Mang (8 BC–23 CE)
**Card:** utopian in idiom. Long conditional sentences, "if only", the perfect society
specified. The register is a *programme*, and the programme is quoted at length rather
than paraphrased.
**Never:** a fraud, in this chapter. The fraud reading arrives in Ch 10 and by then the
reader should have lost the ability to use it.

### Han Wu (r. 141–87 BCE)
**Card:** administrative. The Salt and Iron chapter is his policy examined, not his
person. Almost no interiority survives and none should be invented.
**Never:** a persecutor in a general sense. He is arguing.

### Taizong (r. 626–649)
**Card:** the warmest voice in the book and the one to distrust. The bronze mirror, the
*Zizhi Tongjian* he could not read, the AD 645 campaign. The narrator admires him here
on purpose, and Ch 11 exists to make that admiration expensive.
**Never:** a perfect ruler, even here. That is the trap.

### Zhu Xi (1130–1200) and the Song literati
**Card:** procedural, exact, and administrative. The examination chapter is written in
their idiom on purpose — a chapter about a machine should sound like one.
**Never:** sages in a generic register.

## Anti-pattern checklist

Run before any chapter is accepted:

- [ ] No sentence contains a forbidden word from the list above.
- [ ] First-person density under 1 per 400 words.
- [ ] No two consecutive chapters open the same way.
- [ ] No quoted speech without attribution naming its source *and* its mediation
      (who recorded it, how long after).
- [ ] No invented interiority. If the interiority is not in a source, it is framed as
      inference and the framing is visible.
- [ ] The chapter's last paragraph is an image, a document, or a refusal — never a
      summary.
- [ ] Read aloud. Anything needing a second breath is rewritten.
- [ ] No chapter answers the theme.

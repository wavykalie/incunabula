# 8

He had a bronze mirror made, and then he did not want to look at it.

The commission is recorded in the *Old Book of Tang* and it is very short. A bronze mirror, polished to a high finish, with a gilt back and an inscription. The inscription is the important part and it is in the voice of the man who ordered it, describing the campaigns, describing how much it cost, describing what he had lost, and ending with a caution to whoever found the object in a later age.

The usual reconstruction is that the mirror was a warning to himself and that he found it unbearably accurate. The *Old Book of Tang*’s compilers — writing about a century later, in a dynasty that had overthrown his line — recorded a number of incidents in which he reacted badly to being reminded. He had a minister executed for a piece of historical scholarship that compared the current court unfavourably with an earlier one. He had a specific intolerance for the *Comprehensive Mirror*, the great chronological history of the Chinese past, which existed in his own lifetime and which he is recorded as forbidding in the palace. It is said he never read it. It is said he did not want it in the building.

Both stories, if true, are the same story told at two scales, and this is where I should tell you what this book is doing, because we have got to the point where you cannot keep missing the device.

Every chapter so far has held a person up to a record they could not control. A king’s laws, a historian’s verdict, a woman’s silence, a programme’s arithmetic, a letter nobody read, a policy’s own report. That is the mirror, and the reason a reader who has been paying attention will have felt it for seven chapters is that the structure is the argument: what we know about a ruler is whatever survives being written down, and writing down is done by somebody with a position.

This chapter names it. I have waited until now because a device named too early becomes a technique, and a device named too late becomes a trick. The mirror is a fact about how this material reached us rather than a way of writing history, and it is the same fact as the missing sentences about Empress Lü, and the same fact as the four hundred words of a Confucian who lost, and the same fact as a compiler who filed advice his court had not accepted.

It is a fact about the *Hanshu* and the *Old Book of Tang* both being written by men whose institutions were the winners. Every chapter of this book has been about a record and the making of it, and the person holding this book is a record too. The chapters in which I have praised a ruler for admitting his costs are the chapters where I have not asked who was writing down the admission.

He was the second emperor of the Tang. He had taken the throne by an event at the palace gate in which his brothers died, and he spent the following two decades being the most effective ruler in Chinese history: campaigns into Central Asia, a campaign against Goguryeo that failed, the administration of a state with something over five million people, and a written law in the tradition.

The palace gate is worth a sentence, because it is the founding event of the dynasty and it is the only one. His brothers were invited to a palace ceremony and were killed during it, and the man who was, according to every account, the least entitled to the throne at that moment became emperor and founded a line that ran for three centuries. The Tang did not have a revolution. It had a successful fratricide, and then a ruler so competent that within a generation nobody was asking about the gate.

That is the mechanism again, and this time it is not a text's silence but a dynasty's convenience: a founding act that nobody could raise, because raising it would have required raising everything after it.

The gate is also the reason I can say this much about him. Almost everything I know about the second Tang emperor comes from documents written by men his dynasty employed, and a dynasty that began with a fratricide has an obvious interest in establishing that its founder was a lawful man of exceptional ability. That interest does not stop the record being accurate. It selects which accurate things get written down, and the gate is the item it selects for, and the reason the gate is nearly absent from the *Old Book of Tang* is that the compilers had a dynasty to protect and only one founding event to protect it with.

Here is what I want to say about that, because it is the whole argument of this book in a single document. The gate happened. It is not in dispute, and the compilers who had every reason to deny it did not deny it. What they did was decline to raise it, and they were able to decline because a reader without the context does not think to ask. A state that had suppressed the gate from the record entirely would have been caught, because the absence is a shape. A state that simply never mentions it has produced a text in which the dynasty begins with a man of unusual merit, and the merit is legible, and the reader fills in the rest.

That is a much more effective form of forgetting than the one we are used to imagining, and it is worth noticing that it is cheaper. It requires no edict, no burning, and no committee. It requires only that nobody find the item interesting.

The campaign that failed is the one to dwell on, because a chapter about a good ruler is exactly the chapter where the record will do the selecting for you. In 645 he took perhaps a hundred thousand men across the Yalu River and attacked Goguryeo, which held fortified hill towns in a country of mountains. It failed, expensively, in the autumn. The *Old Book of Tang* calls it a defeat. The *Comprehensive Mirror*, which is a chronological history and therefore has no room for a peroration, records it as four lines.

He tried again. There was a second campaign in 647 and a third in 648, both smaller, both inconclusive, and then the enterprise was quietly dropped, and the Korean peninsula stayed outside Chinese control for another four centuries. What is striking is the persistence, and the way the persistence is reported: the *Old Book of Tang* presents it as the cost of a ruler who did not accept the first answer, which is a flattering reading, and the *New Book of Tang* presents it as vanity and a waste, which is a hostile one, and both are reading the same four lines.

The *Comprehensive Mirror* is the reason all of this is available to us, and its existence is the reason its suppression was an event rather than a non-event. It was compiled by Sima Guang, and it ran to nearly three hundred thousand characters covering a thousand four hundred years, and its method was chronological: every dated event in sequence, with the sources cited, and no interpretation. It is the most boring possible format and the most dangerous, because a man reading it can find out what actually happened on a date without being told what it meant.

A work of that size and that shape is a machine, and the machine is worth a moment because it is the first one in this book that was built by men who were consciously resisting something. Sima Guang and his collaborators were opponents of the faction that took power after the reign we are discussing, and the faction's entire claim was that the reign had been a usurpation dressed as a restoration. The answer they built was a chronology running continuously through the disputed period, sourced line by line, so that the claim could be checked rather than argued. It is the design of an institution rather than the eloquence of an author, and the design is what survived: the compilers after them used it, and used it against other courts, and the name of the work became the generic term for the technique.

That is the second large thing in this chapter that a court found threatening and neither of them is a person. A code that requires a process before an execution, and a chronology that dates every event. Both are unfashionable objects. Both are more dangerous to a government than any pamphlet, because a pamphlet can be answered and a code can be cited by a litigant, and a chronology can be consulted by anyone with a date and a grievance.

That is why a chronology is a threat to a court. An annal can be argued with. A chronology cannot, because the argument you are having with it is about the arrangement of facts you did not supply. And Sima Guang had the great merit of being an opponent of the faction that came to power after Taizong died, which is a reason to read everything he wrote as polemical and also a reason to think the compilation was commissioned in part to make him do it.

The law is the point. The *Tang Code* is the most complete and the most sophisticated legal document to survive from premodern China, and it is the reason Tang justice still reads in the present, and it is a work of institutional design rather than ideology. It distinguishes negligence from intent. It has a graduated scale of punishment. It has provisions for the specific liability of officials, and it requires a judicial process before execution. It was written in the 620s and substantially revised in the 650s, and the emperor who commissioned the revision is the same man who forbade the *Comprehensive Mirror* from the palace.

The code also contains the equal-field system, which is the state's other great institutional artefact and the one the following chapters will pick up. The state allocated land to households by a formula: a given number of adult males gets a given number of plots, and the land was held in principle at a fixed value and could not be sold. A household that produced sons received more land; a household that failed to produce sons received less, and eventually forfeited it. It is a demographic policy administered through the land register, and it is the ancestor of the system chapter 9 is going to describe, and it failed for the same reason everything in this book fails, which is that it assumed the register was accurate.

The assumption is worth pressing on, because the equal-field system is very often described as a failed land reform, and that description misses what it was actually for. Altering the distribution of land was no part of the design. The intention was to keep a fixed number of taxable households in being, and the son-versus-no-son formula is a fertility schedule written into a land register. The state was after a certain number of people who could be assessed, and it had found that a peasant household's capacity to pay is a function of how many adult males are in it, and had built a law out of that observation.

It is a good observation and a well-executed piece of administrative design, and the design held for a long time, which is the part that ought to disturb a reader who assumes that a well-executed design is a successful one. The prohibition on selling was enforced in the early Tang with visible success. Land did not concentrate. The register stayed roughly accurate. And a system whose purpose is to keep a fixed number of taxable households in being will do exactly that, and will do it by preventing the households that cannot reproduce from dissolving, which means by keeping poor people on land they cannot farm, which means by making the number of taxable units a floor rather than a measure of what the country could bear.

The wealth produced by that arrangement accumulated anyway, because wealth produced by a household is not the same thing as the land the household holds. The clans bought the right to collect rent. They became creditors. And the state, which had made a law against owning land and had no law against lending money, discovered in the ninth century that the agricultural population was in debt to men who lived in the capital, and that the men in the capital were the descendants of the men who had written the code.

So the register was right and the theory was narrow, and a narrow theory implemented well is more durable than a broad one implemented badly, which is why the Tang equal-field system outlived every honest attempt to reform it and why the reform that finally abolished it, in 780, was a legal declaration that the prohibition had been unenforceable in practice for some time.

I do not get to make this a paradox. He was a man whose admiration for his own rule was incompatible with a real legal system. That is the strongest thing I can say about him and the most irritating, because I have spent this book describing states that could not say no to themselves, and here is one that wrote down the conditions under which it would be wrong to execute someone.

But the record is the record, and the record is a *Hanshu* and a *Tang Shu* written by men who came after, in a tradition that knows how to make a ruler look like a lesson and does not entirely distinguish between a lesson and a man.

The mirror was made in 632. The inscription is worth having in the state I have it, because it is the emperor explaining his own face to a stranger:

> Made in the seventh year of Zhenguan. It is to be handed down to a later age, so that those who come after may know of the chastity of the labour. The campaign against Goguryeo was a defeat. I have written it down. If a man in a later age reads this and thinks himself better, he should remember that I was the one who chose the campaign.
>
> The bronze is a thing that returns the face. Take care how you look at it.

The last line is the inscription, or is it the compiler? I cannot tell, and the distinction is the whole of the chapter, and the person holding this book has spent eight chapters learning that he cannot tell.

The New Book of Tang, written four hundred years later by a man who disliked his line, dismisses the whole business as vanity and says the emperor wanted to be remembered and could not stop thinking about it. The Old Book of Tang, written by men of his own house, says the mirror was a confession.

The two readings are equally supported by the same inscription, and it is worth seeing why, because the reason is the same one that has governed every document in this book. A man writing about his own failures on a bronze surface in 632 is doing one of two things. He is a king arranging a legacy, and the object will be read by his sons, who will be told that their father was honest. Or he is a man who has done a thing he cannot undo and has found that the only available form for the feeling is a record, and the record is addressed to whoever finds it, which is a stranger.

There is a third possibility, which I think is the correct one and which neither compiler allows. He may have intended both, and may not have known which was which, and the inscription is the shape that intention takes when a man is not in a position to know. The distinction the compilers make is between a man performing honesty and a man being honest, and the text does not support the distinction, because the text is the performance and the feeling is not in it anywhere.

I would like to report that the mirror was found intact in a Tang tomb in a modern decade, because that is the kind of detail a historian reaches for, and I have not found it and I am not going to supply it. What I have is the commission as the *Old Book of Tang* records it, and an inscription that later compilers quote at length, and a man who forbade the comprehensive history of his own time from his own palace. Three documents, none of them written by him, all of them written by people who needed him to be a particular kind of man.

One man made a mirror, put his failures on the back of it, and then prohibited the comprehensive history of his own time from the building, and that is one man, and the reason I have not been able to get past him in seven chapters is that I have been reading the record of him the way he read his own reflection.

He did not want to look at it. He had it made anyway, and it is the most honest object in this book, and it is honest in a way that keeps being available to later readers, who are not its intended audience, and that is what I have been calling the mirror, and the reason I have named it here is that the reader of this chapter has earned it and the ones after it will not.

## Guidance

Three uses for a mirror.

**Commission the mirror and then look at it.** Taizong had his failures cast in bronze and barred the comprehensive history from his palace. Both acts are honest and only one of them costs anything. Criticism you arrange to receive is decoration unless you schedule the part where you actually read it.

**Assume the cheap forgetting, not the dramatic one.** The gate at the palace was never suppressed. It was simply never found interesting again, which costs nothing and lasts forever. The facts that go missing from your own accounts will not be censored. They will be skipped. Put a recurring question in the calendar that asks what stopped being worth mentioning.

**Build things that can be cited against you.** The Tang Code required process before an execution, and the chronology dated every event, and both were more dangerous to power than any pamphlet, because a litigant can quote a code and anyone with a date and a grievance can consult a chronology. Write rules and records that a person with less power than you can use.

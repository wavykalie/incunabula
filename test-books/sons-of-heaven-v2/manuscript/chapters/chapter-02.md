# 2

The bargain was this: the scholars would stop criticising the throne, and the throne would start calling them Confucian.

Both halves were new. The throne had spent two centuries not calling itself anything, because a Qin emperor who had announced a philosophy would have been announcing that the philosophy had a switch he could throw. The scholars had spent two centuries being a curriculum with no employment. The deal was struck under Emperor Wen, who reigned for twenty-three years and is remembered as a thrifty man who did not build palaces.

It is remembered that way because of one story, and the story is the whole argument of this chapter. Wen wanted to build a terrace. The emperor of the neighbouring state sent a rhetorician to talk him out of it, on the grounds that a king's money is a king's subjects. Wen was persuaded, and was annoyed, and asked why the man was not using his talents to make money instead. The rhetorician said he would, but he would rather be thought of as a criminal than as a money-maker. Wen said, reasonably, that this sounded like a small thing to be proud of.

Whether Wen said anything like this is a matter of a chapter in the *Hanshu* written about five hundred years later, in a style that is openly building a case. That is worth saying before using it, because everything in this chapter reaches us through exactly this kind of later compilation. What follows, then, is somebody's guess at what a hundred years afterwards would find legible about the reign.

The problem Wen had inherited was an absence. The Qin had not merely been overthrown; it had been discredited, and discrediting a theory of government is easier than ruling without one. There was no agreement about what the empire was *for* — which sounds like a philosophical question and was in practice a staffing one. Someone had to sit in the offices and collect the taxes and decide what a case meant. The people qualified to do this had spent the Qin years either dead or unemployed, and the ones who had survived were the ones who could describe the world in terms compatible with enforcement.

The solution turned out to be a job rather than a theory. Confucianism was a set of texts about ritual, kinship and exemplary historical figures, and it was repurposed into a credential. An examination was set. A young man who could annotate the *Spring and Autumn Annals* in a way a minister found useful was certified, and a certified man could take office.

The examination was a small thing at first, and it is worth being exact about how small, because the whole of the next two thousand years rests on a decision made by a court that was busy with other matters. Wen was solving a vacancy rather than building a system. The vacancy was that the empire had an apparatus with no one qualified to staff it, and the materials to hand were men who could recite, so he certified men who could recite.

Here is the number, and it is worth sitting with. In the first decades of the Han the state certified a few dozen men a year. The most famous examination of the entire early period produced four names. These are not the numbers of a system selecting a governing class. They are the numbers of a court filling posts, and the difference between those two things is the difference between what happened next and what I have just described.

The state got a bureaucracy that was cheap, literate, local, and loyal to its own standards rather than to a dynasty — a genuinely better instrument than anything the Qin had. In exchange, the philosophers got a seat, and the terms of the seat were set by the office.

The certification was cheap in a way that should be noted. Wen and his successors appointed men on recommendation as much as on examination, and the recommendation system ran through the local elite, which meant the apparatus was cheap and the elite were made rather than defeated. There is a period in the second century BCE where the court is openly worried about this, in memorials that describe with some bitterness how many men are holding office on the strength of being the friend of somebody.

Those memorials are the first crack in the arrangement, and they are worth reading closely because they are written by the beneficiaries. A man appointed on a friend's recommendation had every reason to prefer recommendation to examination, because examination is public and recommendation is private, and because a friend's favour can be renewed while a memorized text has to be maintained for the length of a career. The system that was supposed to replace blood with merit gave the great families a better tool than the one it replaced, and the great families were always there.

Wen has a second story attached to him, and this one is about his deathbed, and it is the formulation of the bargain in a single sentence. He is supposed to have said that if a ruler changes the calendar the people will follow, and that he had kept the realm safe for thirty years and had not been permitted to enjoy it, and that his sons would be allowed to. Two charges, then: do the work, and do not expect to be thanked. It is the most durable thing said by any emperor in this book, and it was said, on the evidence of the same kind of later compilation, by a man whose restraint is now a national virtue.

What the examination was actually testing is a technical question and it has a clear answer. It was testing whether a man could produce a piece of administrative writing that satisfied a reader who knew what administrative writing was for. Character played no part in it, because character cannot be examined and nobody in the Han thought otherwise. That is a narrow, practical, learnable skill, and the curriculum was built around it, and a great deal of what looks like moral education in the tradition is in fact the teaching of a technical genre.

The genre has conventions that can be stated. Open by establishing the precedent from the classics. State the difficulty the precedent creates for the present case. Do not argue against the precedent; argue that the precedent was itself a response to a difficulty of the same kind, which means the principle can be reapplied. Then apply it to the matter in hand, and close with a procedural recommendation that names no one. A man who could do that could be relied upon to draft a report, and the court wanted reports.

The part-time scholar is the unit of the whole arrangement, and he is a strange one. He is a man with a private inheritance and no independent income, who spends his twenties memorising texts, and whose family expects him to be a failure until he passes. He has no trade, no land, no profession, and his status in his own village is a matter of his family's patience. Then he passes, and he becomes an official, and the family is a household that produces officials.

This is a class made entirely out of deferred expectations, and the system ran on it for two thousand years. It is also, in the historian's phrase, a class of men who had every reason to be grateful to the throne and every skill to assess it, which is the combination that the next four hundred chapters of this book are about.

There is a family in this book that makes the arrangement concrete, and it is the family the bargain was struck inside. Empress Lü was the mother of the first emperor to follow the founder, she had two sons on the throne in succession, and after the second of them died she installed a boy emperor of her own choosing and then ruled through him. Her clan held the offices. Her officials ran the government. And when she died, the men she had appointed moved against every one of them within months, and the extermination was total, and the founding narrative of the next two hundred years is that the clan deserved it.

I am going to leave that story for its own chapter, because the version of it that survives is the one the victors wrote, and it took me some time to work out how much of it is load-bearing. What belongs here is only this: the bargain of the founding was made with men whose advancement depended on a family at court, and the first test of the bargain came when that family was destroyed. The men who had been grateful to the throne discovered that gratitude was a function of proximity, and that the throne they were grateful to had been, for fifteen years, a woman they had been forbidden to write about.

The interesting thing is what happened next: the apparatus survived the breaking, and it was staffed by the descendants of the men who did the breaking. The Han state after 180 BCE is not the Han state before it. The office has become a profession rather than a favour, and the change came from a massacre.

It is easy to present all of this as a restoration, and the *Hanshu* presents it as one. It is more accurate to call it a takeover by a department. The Han did not revive the Zhou. The Zhou had not existed for eight hundred years. They revived a description of the Zhou, and that description came with an exam attached to it.

Dong Zhongshu, writing around 150 BCE, was the man who made the thing intellectually respectable, and he is the most sympathetic figure in this book, which is uncomfortable. He had read the *Analects* the way the Qin had read the *Mozi* — as a text containing techniques — and he proposed something the founders had not: that the empire's moral order and its cosmic order were the same order, and that a ruler's legitimacy was measurable against the seasons, and that Heaven had a will which could be lost.

The first half of that is a working theory of administration. The second is a theory of when to remove a ruler. Dong Zhongshu was not careful about the second half, and it is what got him into the kind of conversation that ended badly for him: dismissed, nearly killed, and eventually dying of a nosebleed in a way his contemporaries found richly instructive.

There is a second bargain underneath the first, and it concerns who was allowed to be a scholar at all. The texts that became the examination curriculum were the ones the founding court happened to have. The result was a canon that nobody chose and everybody defended, and the defence of a canon is one of the few activities that produces a large class of men with a specific professional skill, which is the reason a state that wanted a bureaucracy should have wanted one.

The interesting thing is that the argument worked. Within a century of his death, the Han state was funding an apparatus of ritual, classics, and examination on the strength of exactly his claim — that moral order and cosmic order are one thing, and can be maintained by getting it right.

There is a detail about how the curriculum was handled that tells you how much the arrangement was a bargain rather than a conversion. When a candidate was examined he was given a passage and asked to explain it, and the explanation was judged as an explanation. Nobody asked whether the candidate believed the classics described an actual past. The teaching position did not require it, and in practice it did not produce it, and the result was a bureaucracy of men who could write a competent gloss on a text they were professionally obliged to take seriously and privately regarded as archaic.

I do not raise this to score a point about hypocrisy, which would be cheap and also wrong. A man who can produce a good gloss on a classical passage is doing something real, and the skills it takes are the skills the job requires. The observation is about where the state's investment went. It bought a class of men who could read and write in a form, and the form is what survived, and the belief did not need to be purchased and mostly was not.

A ritual apparatus is easy to underestimate. It is a calendar, a set of ceremonies, a body of scholarship, and a claim that the right sequence of things performed at the right moment keeps the state in good relations with the cosmos. It sounds like decoration until you notice that it is also a scheduling system for an agricultural bureaucracy, and that the scholars who ran it were the same men who ran the waterworks. Dong Zhongshu's claim and the state's interest were, for about two hundred years, the same claim.

What the founders could not have anticipated, and what makes the bargain a bargain, is who ended up holding both ends of it. The scholars got the office. The office got a staffing system. And a staffing system that selects for a style of thought will eventually select for a style of criticism — because a man trained for twenty years in the commentary tradition is exactly the man who notices that the commentary tradition has been edited.

It took about four hundred years for that to arrive. It arrived as a series of memorials — *jian*, written advice, drafted by junior officials who had read everything — pointing out that the classics had been altered and that the alteration had a date.

The scholars had been hired. Being hired, it turned out, came with reading privileges.

## Guidance

Three things to take from the bargain.

**A temporary fix is a future institution.** Emperor Wen was filling a vacancy, and the examination he certified to fill it ran for two thousand years. Before you adopt a stopgap, at work or in your own habits, ask what it will have become in five years. The provisional is the most permanent thing there is, because nobody reviews it.

**Ask what the test tests.** The Han examination tested administrative writing and called it character, because character cannot be examined. Every filter measures what it can measure and reports the name of what it cannot. When a selection is made around you, whether a hire or an exam, look at the paper before you look at the verdict.

**Do the work and do not wait to be thanked.** Wen's deathbed charge to his sons came to two sentences: keep the realm safe, and do not expect to enjoy it. It is the most durable thing said by any emperor in this book, and it works just as well for work that will never carry your name.

# 9

A candidate registers. His name, his locality, his family, his teachers, his grandfather, his great-grandfather, and for each of the three an account of what the man did and where he served. The registration is checked against the previous generation's record, because a family that has produced one examination graduate may produce another, and a family that has produced none is more interesting. The candidate is assigned a hall. Each hall holds a fixed number, the desks are the same size, and no candidate may bring paper or ink. These are supplied.

The examination is a series. The first stage disqualifies almost everyone, and it is not a written examination at all. Picking forty names, by a method that is public and connected to the local official. This is the part of the system most often described as corrupt, and also the part most likely to be functioning. A man passed into the first stage has been selected by the man who will assess him. He knows it. He has a stake in the relationship.

The second stage is a matter of the *Four Books* and the *Five Classics*. Ten thousand men sit it. Roughly one in twenty passes. The third examination is policy composition, taken at the palace. The questions are drawn from administration. Irrigation. Registers. The price of salt. The upkeep of a wall. Nobody sits it for their elegance. It is an examination for men who will be sent to counties. It is administered by men who have been sent to counties. It is the first large-scale administrative examination in the world.

The successful candidate enters the state as an official with a rank, a salary, a place in the hierarchy, and a period of assessment. That assessment is a report filed by his superior and read by the superior's superior. A bad report at the wrong moment ends a career with no appeal. The report is confidential, everyone in the hierarchy knows this, and it is the single most important fact about the system and the reason it works.

The degrees are not two. There is a long ladder: the provincial degree, the metropolitan degree, the palace degree. The ladder is the mechanism of ambition. A man holding the provincial degree has a real place in his home county and a salary. A man holding the palace degree is an examinable official. The system does not merely select. It retains, giving a man a reason to keep performing for forty years, which is the longest period any of these states managed to keep anyone.

A man who fails the third examination does not leave the system. He is what the records call a *littoral* man, an unattached degree-holder, and there were a great many of them. They lived in their home counties, they were formally of the state, they were paid nothing, and their degrees were legal tender in a limited set of local transactions. A family could use one to obtain a post for a younger son, or to argue a case before a magistrate, or to establish that a line was educated. The degree was a portable claim on the state's good faith, and the state's good faith was exercised through connections.

This is the detail that tells you what the examinations were actually for. The state was after a class whose membership conferred standing, at a rate it did not control, for a reason it had not fully articulated, rather than a fixed number of officials. The number of officials in any given prefecture was set by the number of posts. The number of degree-holders in that prefecture was set by the local population's wealth. A man could pass every examination in the empire and still have nothing to do, and a great many did, and the system's records show no anxiety about this, because the surplus was useful.

The salary schedule is in the records and it is worth one sentence, because it is the mechanism that made the whole arrangement cheap. An official's pay was set by rank and not by the work he did, so a man who reached a certain level was paid a certain amount regardless of whether his prefecture was rich. The consequence was that a county in the poorest part of the empire was expensive to staff, and the men sent there knew it, and the compensation was advancement. Promotion happened fastest where the posting was hardest, and the hardest postings were the ones the capital wanted filled.

That is a real design and it is the reason the system produced competent provincial administrators rather than a uniform class of frightened men. A man who goes to a difficult prefecture at twenty-eight and is made a department head at thirty-five has been trained by the difficulty. The state got the training for free, by making the posts unattractive, and it got it at a cost that fell on the men who went rather than on the men who stayed in the capital.

The composition examination has a form: eight paragraphs with fixed headings. Examination of the difficulty. Statement of the principle. Amplification by analogy. Practical deployment. Then four more headings, variations on the same four ideas. A method for writing an argument under time pressure, and the reason it survives so long is that it is genuinely good at that. The best examination essays read like a competent minister thinking quickly on his feet, which is what they were.

A candidate had three hours. The form gave him eight sections and a classical passage to attach them to, and the passage was supplied by the examiners rather than chosen by him, which means the task was in part a test of whether he could find the relevance rather than whether he had an opinion. Many good essays of the period are good on this axis and almost indifferent on the other, and the convention held that a candidate who argued a principle too hard was displaying a fault of temperament. The state was buying a certain sort of man and the examination was where the purchase happened.

The system is a machine for producing those essays. Everyone taking the examination has read the same essays, in the same collections, for the same prizes. The collections are printed. The models are printed. The commentaries on the models are printed, and the commentaries on the commentaries. By the Song the candidate is not writing an argument so much as demonstrating that he knows which argument the moment calls for.

The eight-legged essay is a machine for making the connection between a classical passage and a current administrative problem. The connection is often real. It is also a machine for making the appearance of the connection, fluently, within a fixed number of characters, by a man who has three hours and a subject he did not choose. A system that selects for that skill will produce a class of men who can perform the connection on demand. The on-demand quality is invisible to everyone above the level where the performance is happening.

What this system solved was succession. The Tang had been governed by a hereditary aristocracy of a few hundred families who supplied the court, the military, and the administration. They allied and married each other. They killed the dynasty in a coup that took three days. The Song left that aristocracy in place, and the examination families became a new one. What the Song abolished was the automatic transmission of office. Every office became conditional on a performance that anyone who could pay for the years of study could attempt.

The first Song emperors did not invent this. They inherited a failed version of it. The late Tang had run examinations that the aristocracy controlled, so that the examinations existed and did nothing. The Song insight was that an examination only works if the local recommendation is weak rather than strong, and if the paper is hard enough to be worth the sitting. Everything after that is refinement.

There is a limit on the first stage that is worth stating, because it is the one piece of the system that is not in the documents' own account of itself. The local official who selected the names was not permitted to select men related to himself, and the rule was enforced by making the selections public. A list went up, and the men on it had a period in which to object, and an objection had to be investigated.

This is a weak control and the Song knew it was weak. A man who wanted to place a relative simply used a middleman, and the middleman was a legitimate candidate. But the weakness is the point: the first stage was the corruptible one, and the design responded by making it public rather than by trying to make it clean, because a public procedure has a cost that private corruption does not. The people who knew about the selection were many, and the man who benefited from it was one.

And that cost falls on the candidate, not the official. A candidate whose name appears on a list has to decide whether to accept. Refusing costs him nothing, because the rules say a man may decline without penalty. So the man in the relation declines, and the official takes the next name, and the selection is now visibly not his. The Song did not solve the problem. It built a procedure in which the problem is expensive enough that locals solve it themselves, which is a different and more durable thing.

The consequence is administrative. It is the reason Song government is the best-documented in premodern China. An official is a man who has passed a series of examinations about how to run a county. He is then examined continuously by men who did the same. Every decision is written down, because a decision that cannot be written down cannot be reviewed. A decision that cannot be reviewed is exactly the kind that Qin had made and that killed it.

Printing is part of this story. Woodblock printing was commercial by the ninth century. The technology that mattered for examinations came a century later. The printed examination paper. Easy to imagine this as an egalitarian advance, and it was nothing of the kind. It made the preparation uniform, so that the pool of men who could attempt the examination widened enormously while the preparation available to all of them standardised at the same level. A system designed to be difficult became one where difficulty was evenly distributed and the outcome depended on years of study that not everyone could fund.

The system widened the pool and reproduced the class. Those two facts agree completely. They are the same fact seen from two ends.

An argument for the reader to hold rather than an answer. The system is legible. A man who wants to know why his county's relief money did not arrive can find the assessment. He can find the report that mentions the assessment. He can find the man who filed it. That man is itself subject to the same chain. The administrative state in the Song is a state in which power has been made inspectable. Inspectability is a genuine achievement. It was bought with a system that spent the income of the most productive families on examinations.

The Song is very rich. Most of the wealth is in the south. None of it is in the treasury. The examinations took the sons of the rich and, after passing them, removed them from the land. Over two centuries the examination class became the gentry, and the examination system became the mechanism by which the gentry reproduced itself. The pool was widened. The outcome was not.

There is a specific and bitter piece of arithmetic in this, and I have left it for the next chapter because it is a description of what the system does at a distance rather than a criticism of it. The system is a machine for selecting talent. It selects it accurately. The man it selects is the son of a man who was selected. That correlation is a scandal only if you expected otherwise. It is not noise either.

For the moment, the machine is running. The record for 1170 gives us a number. The number of candidates. The number who passed the third examination in the palace that year was eight. A better number than the year before, and the year before that. A number a system gets by being good at one thing for a very long time.

The Song is the high point of the Chinese administrative state. The high point is where this book stops admiring, because the admiration is what the record was written to produce.

## Guidance

Three design lessons from the machine.

**Pay the middle of the ladder.** The degree ladder kept men performing for forty years because every rung had a real stake attached. A path with rewards only at the top empties at the first plateau. Design your commitments so the middle stages pay, or you will be relying on ambition alone.

**Make the corruptible step public.** The first stage of the examination was the one that could be bought, and the Song answered by posting the selections where anyone could object. You cannot make a vulnerable step honest. You can make cheating it expensive and visible, which is a different and more durable achievement.

**Ask what the form trains.** The eight-legged essay rewarded a man who could attach a classical passage to a current problem fluently and on demand. The connection was often real and the fluency was the point. Before you value any credential or habit, name the exact performance it rehearses and decide whether that performance is the work.

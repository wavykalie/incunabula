# 1

The wall is still there. That is the first thing to understand about it, and the reason it is worth beginning with a construction project rather than a man.

What stands there now is many walls — separate fortifications, built by separate states, over centuries, for reasons that had nothing to do with each other. The Qin did not build a wall. They joined one up. Where the older work meets the newer, the join is visible to anyone standing at it, because the older sections are made of whatever stone was nearest and the new work is uniform. The joins are the point. A wall built as one thing is a border. A wall assembled from six borders, each with a different masonry and a different idea of what it was for, is a statement about a country that has just discovered it is made of parts.

The man who ordered the joining was a king of Qin, and Qin was the westernmost of six states that had spent five centuries failing to destroy each other conclusively. He had an army that could take a state. That turned out to be the easy part.

The campaigns themselves took nine years, and the shape of them matters less than people assume. Qin did not outfight five rivals. It outlasted them, and the reason it could outlast them was a piece of infrastructure nobody had thought to make before: it moved its army and its supply lines across terrain that the others used defensively, and once a rival's capital fell the rival's own nobles immediately began negotiating with the winner rather than dying for a state that had stopped existing.

The reforms that followed are usually described as cultural, which misses what they were. They were infrastructure. One script, so a decree written in the capital could be read in a village a thousand *li* away without a translator. One set of weights, so a *shi* of grain meant the same thing on both banks of a river. One currency. One axle-width for every cart in the realm, so the ruts in every road matched every other road and a wagon could cross the empire without being rebuilt.

Look closely at the axle-width and you can see the whole theory in one object. Every state that had gone before had standardised its axle-width for exactly one reason, which was to keep carts inside its own borders, where the tolls were. Standardise it to the widest gauge in the realm and the carts will fit nowhere except on your roads. It is a decision that looks bureaucratic and functions as a weapon, and the Qin made it, and the decision is a fair summary of everything else they did: the ordinary machinery of a state, extended until it becomes an instrument of conquest.

The reforms deserve their credit before anyone takes it away, because the popular version of this chapter reads them as a mad man's calligraphy. The Qin did something no other state in the period attempted. They took a set of local conventions and replaced them with a single rule, and then they made the rule enforceable, and then they made it the condition of using the roads. Every subsequent Chinese state found the work already done. The script, the weights, the coinage, the axle-width, the standard of the measure, the pitch of the roof-tile: the men who later ran the empire did not invent these, they inherited them, and the reason the inheritance was uncontested is that they made travel and trade possible in a way that six incompatible sets of local rules had not.

That is a real achievement, and it is worth saying plainly, because the next thing I am going to say is that the same list is a different thing entirely when read as a description of a state that used its administrative capacity in a particular way. The two readings are not in conflict. A state's capacity to unify its measures is a fact about its capacity, and the Qin had an unusual amount of it, and the state that had it used it to extract labour and to break a wall.

The distinction is between a tool and an intention, and a tool is available to anyone who picks it up. The Qin did not standardise the axle-width in order to invade. They standardised it because it was the right thing, and the invasion was enabled by an administrative fact that had been available to them and to everyone else. A historian who says the conquest proves the reforms were designed for conquest has got the causation backwards, and a historian who says the reforms show a reasonable state has got the significance backwards, and the Qin are interesting because both errors are easy to make about them.

Read that list of reforms again and notice what it assumes. It assumes a people is a system, and that a system can be tuned. It assumes the trouble with the world is variance, and that variance has a solution, and the solution is a standard.

The same logic went into stone. Roads were cut through mountains and carried across marsh on packed earth, and they were built to the new width, so the army could move at a speed no previous state could match. The wall was a logistics project before it was a defensive one. The passes were held by relocated populations rather than by garrisons, which is to say that the wall was defended by people who had been moved there to be defended. Everything about the early empire was logistics.

There is a piece of the Qin state that survives in a form you can read, and it is the most famous document to come out of a Chinese library pit, buried in the 1970s when a well was dug at a place that had been sealed since the fall of the dynasty. It is a bundle of legal documents, written on bamboo, and it is a clerk's paperwork: contracts, inventories, a manual of legal forms, correspondence about a specific road that was not built on time.

It is the most valuable thing we have on the Qin, and also the most damaging. Nothing in it argues for the regime. Nothing in it mentions the emperor except as an address on a letter. It shows a state in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday, three years before the end, and the ordinary Tuesday is unimpressed by the theory. Clerks were writing letters complaining that road projects were being approved without adequate notice to the people who would be affected, and the tone is the tone of a modern local-authority dispute, because the Qin state had a functioning bureaucracy and a bureaucracy is a bureaucracy.

There is a trap here that I have walked into before in books like this one. Capable administrators do not make the Qin a good state. They were required, and they were required in order to extract labour on a scale the other states had not attempted. The competence is the evidence for the severity rather than against it.

We do not know how many died. The *Records* does not say, and no one counted, because counting the dead is an act of accounting and the Qin were very good at accounting for everything except that. Historians have produced a wide range of estimates, and the range is the honest answer — not because anyone is hiding a number but because the state that would have had to keep the number was the state that had just been dismantled. The figures in circulation, ranging from tens of thousands to millions, are reconstructions by people who had the total population and the harvest records and who disagree about the harvest records.

What can be said without estimate is the shape of it: a labour tax falling on every adult male, on a schedule, for years at a time, in a state that had recently stopped being six states and had not yet stopped being six peoples. There is also the detail that the tax was not remitted in a bad year. In later administrations a failed harvest meant a remission, recorded in the accounts, an event bad officials tried to prevent and good ones announced. The Qin accounts, as far as they survive, show the levy continuing at full rate.

Han Fei is a difficult man to read, and the difficulty has nothing to do with translation. He is funny, and the jokes are always at somebody's expense, and the person being made fun of is usually a minister who has guessed his ruler's preferences, and the reader is invited to laugh at a man who has just been told he is a fool by a text that is about to recommend the ruler listen to the author. The book is a performance of coldness, and the coldness is the argument: here is what it is like to live under a man who takes this seriously.

It is also, on the evidence of the essays themselves, a man who was not a cynic about the enterprise. The essays are not a satire. They read like an attempt to solve a problem, and the problem is a real one: how do you get a large population to do something a small court cannot compel everyone to do. Han Fei's answer is that you remove the conditions in which people have an interest in disobeying, and then whatever remains is behaviour, and behaviour is predictable.

He did not become a tyrant by accident. He became one by theory, and the theory had a name. Legalism is the lazy English version. *Fajia* — the school of law — is closer. It had a text, and the text is worth reading, because it is the coldest political writing in the ancient world and it is very good.

Its author was a prince of Han named Han Fei, who wrote in the third century BCE and died in 233 — twelve years before the empire he described existed. He never governed anything. He simply reasoned about it, in essays that are frequently funny in a way that ought to be disturbing, and his reasoning ran like this: the ruler should want nothing, show nothing, and trust no one. If a minister can guess what pleases you, he will please you instead of serving you. If your favourites can read your face, they will write on it. So the ruler should be blank. The law should be loud. Reward and punishment should fall with the regularity of weather, and they should be the only two things a ruler ever touches.

The blankness is the part worth dwelling on, because it is more demanding than it looks. A ruler who shows nothing has to feel nothing visibly, all day, in front of people whose advancement depends on reading him. It goes well beyond stoicism. It is the daily performance of a face that is not there. Every ancient court had a ritual vocabulary for exactly this — the ruler does not look up from his writing, the ruler does not turn toward the speaker — and the ritual exists because the theory demanded the performance and the theory was very influential.

Han Fei called reward and punishment the two handles. His point was that a man holding both handles does not need to be loved, or even respected.

What the theory cannot do is explain why anyone should obey the second handle rather than the first. A subject who complies because the punishment is terrible has made a calculation, not a promise, and he will recalculate the moment someone else's reward looks better. Han Fei knew this and did not care, and the not-caring is the doctrine. It is why the Qin could conquer everything and keep nothing. The two handles can produce a state that obeys. They cannot produce a state that agrees — and a state that does not agree has to be watched forever, by an army that must be paid, out of a treasury that must be filled, by subjects who are not convinced they owe anything.

There is a second problem, which Han Fei's own life demonstrates better than any commentary could. A system that tells the ruler to trust no one has no answer to the question of who is exempt. The minister who advises blankness is himself a reader of faces. The theory has to be administered by somebody, and whoever that person is, they have read the essays.

The tradition never noticed the contradiction, and worked around it instead. The workaround is worth naming because it is a pattern we will meet for the next two thousand years: the theory is applied to everyone above you and to no one at or below your own rank. Every administration in this book observes the law on the way down and the theory on the way up.

Han Fei's reached Qin. The future First Emperor read them and is reported to have said that if he could meet the author and befriend him he could die content. He got his meeting. Han Fei came to Qin on a diplomatic errand and was denounced by the very minister who had praised him. He was imprisoned. He drank the poison his jailers gave him.

The theory had predicted exactly this. The theory had no way to prevent it.

That minister was Li Si, and Li Si is the first man in this book to be eaten by a system he built. His biography begins with a fable about rats which the *Records of the Grand Historian* attributes to him — attributed, not recorded, and I am prepared to believe it because it is exactly the kind of thing a certain kind of young man really does think while waiting to be discovered.

Li Si, young and minor, worked as a clerk. He watched the rats in the latrine: thin, filthy, startled by every footstep. He watched the rats in the granary: fat, calm, unbothered by anything. Same rats. Different position. He concluded that a man is only as dignified as the place he has managed to stand, and that the whole of a life's work is the work of moving from one room to the other.

Calling it a philosophy gets it wrong. It is a career strategy with metaphysical pretensions, and Li Si spent his life executing it. He crossed to Qin. He attached himself to the winning state. He wrote the memorial that proposed the book burn, and he wrote it in an elegant classical prose learned from the very texts he was proposing to destroy, which tells you something about the theory that its opponents were its best stylists.

The book burn is the detail that should stop a modern reader, and it is usually told as a general despotism rather than as a piece of administrative work. The measure was targeted rather than general. The Qin court identified a set of texts held in private hands across the empire, sent commissioners to buy them at a good price, and burned what was not sold. The method was patient, administrative, and cheap. It is a policy, and the policy has an obvious flaw that everyone could see: it required the state to know what was being written, and the state had a very good idea of what was being written, because writing a history requires access to the records.

So the Qin banned private history and thereby guaranteed that the only history was the one it controlled, which was written by men who had to work for it. The result was the same silence the tradition has been arguing about ever since, arriving here by an administrative decision rather than a censorship sweep.

He rose. He watched a boy emperor fail. He was manoeuvred out by a eunuch, accused of treason, and executed in 208 BCE in the marketplace at Xianyang, by the five punishments — a phrase that should be read slowly and then left alone.

There is a detail about Li Si It deserves room, because it is the moment the book's argument turns into an event rather than a pattern. He was the man who designed the book burn, and he was the man who died under a sentence the design had made routine, and the sentence was carried out in the marketplace of the capital in public, over about an hour, in front of a crowd. The state had built a procedure and the procedure had become available to any official with an enemy. Nothing about this was a breakdown. The machinery worked. It was pointed at him.

I do not think that settles anything about whether the theory was right, and I have watched a lot of writing try to make it settle. A theory that says the ruler should be blank and the law should be loud has nothing to say about what to do when a senior official is hated by a eunuch, and Li Si's death is exactly what the theory predicts, and Han Fei had already told anyone who read him carefully that a state built this way can be pointed at anyone, including the man who built it. The book is a description of a weapon, and the man who wrote it died of one.

The temptation is to see this as justice, and the temptation should be resisted for a reason that matters for the rest of this book. What happened to Li Si is what happened to Wang Mang's clerks and to Huo Guang's policy and to every man in these chapters who put something into a record: the thing he did outlasted the thing that was done to him. His institutional design outlived him by decades. The sentence in the marketplace lasted an afternoon. If we are going to read a moral out of that, the moral is about the relative durability of administrative arrangements and the careers of the men who build them, and it is not about guilt.

Then the emperor's own last years, which are the part of the story that most resembles a moral and is hardest to interpret. In 210 he left the capital on an inspection tour. The tour took him to the sea, where he was looking for the Isles of the Immortals, and he is said to have thrown himself into the water after an astrologer advised him, and to have been pulled out, and to have died on the road back within a year of ascending.

The elixir is the frame around the death and it is a fact about the period rather than about him. The court was consuming a mercury-and-mineral compound on the theory that it would extend life, on the advice of men who were themselves beginning to go blind and lose their hair from working with it. The court physicians of the Qin were a formal institution with a written curriculum. The curriculum included this. The effect on the emperor is described in terms that a modern reader will recognise, because it is a description of a man whose organs failed under a heavy metal.

Whether he was poisoned is the other question, and it is unanswerable. The astrologers’ travel record, which is the closest thing to a diary in the empire, notes omens throughout, and the *Records* reads them backwards. Every entry about an omen is followed by an event, and the sequence is so tidy that it is either a chronology written by someone who wanted the omens to mean something or a genuine record that we are reading with a bias we cannot detect.

He did not want to look at it. He had it made anyway, and it is the only part of the story that someone wrote down for a stranger, and it is in the next book.

The Qin, by then, was already gone. The First Emperor died in 210 on a tour, and the succession collapsed immediately, which is what happens when a state has eliminated every institution that could succeed a ruler except a family, and has eliminated everyone in the family except someone ineligible. He was on an inspection tour, seeking immortality, and the emperor who succeeded him was a child.

The historians treat the collapse as a surprise. It is better understood as the theory completing itself. A state that has removed every rival power has no mechanism of its own for changing, because the mechanism of change in every other state had been people who could command armies, and the Qin had taken care of those. The succession was a court intrigue among men who had spent their careers in clerks’ offices. There was nobody left who knew how to run the part of the state that was still running.

Rebellion came from everywhere at once in 209, and nobody had organised it. Six states simply discovered that they were simultaneously free, which is a thing a centralised state had made impossible and a collapsing one made possible in a single season.

There is a question here I have skipped twice in this book, and I am going to answer it because it is the most important thing the Qin episode yields. If the Qin regime was so detested that six states rose against it within a year of the succession failing, why did the regime last fifteen years? The two facts sit together badly, and the usual reconciliation is that the first emperor was a great man and the second was not, and the succession is the whole difference. That reconciliation is close to true and it is not complete, because the succession did not change the policy. The boy emperor governed through exactly the same officials, under exactly the same law, with exactly the same tax schedule, and the empire was still standing at the end of the first year.

So the detestation was not the cause of the fall, or not only the cause. The cause of the fall was the absence of anyone who could command an army, and the detestation was what filled the gap once the absence existed. Six states rose in 209 not because a policy changed but because a government that had been able to prevent any of them from rising for fifteen years could no longer raise a force, and the men who had been waiting to rise knew it within a season. The Qin had spent its entire capacity on a frontier, and a state that spends its capacity on a frontier has a fiscal year in which it is vulnerable, and it had one in 209.

Which is the same finding as the wall, and the same finding as the labour levy, and it is the reason I begin with a construction project rather than a man. The Qin could do one thing at a time and it chose to do two enormous things at once, on opposite ends of the empire, for fifteen years. Every state in this book is a set of choices about what to do first, and the Qin made its choice with total confidence and no arithmetic, and the arithmetic is what a historian gets to see and a decision-maker did not.

The wall was still standing. It is the only part that worked, and it worked for the reason the state did not: it required no one to agree to anything. Stone does not recalculate.

The granary rat, in the end, was also the latrine rat. Only the room had changed.

## Guidance

Three things this chapter leaves in your hands.

**A standard is a decision about who gets to use the road.** The Qin fixed the axle-width because it was the sensible thing to do, and the sensible thing became an instrument once the roads were the only roads. Before you standardise anything, at work or at home, spend a minute on the toll booth. Ask who pays it and who collects.

**Admire competence last.** A system that works beautifully is telling you what it can do, and that is a separate question from what it will do. The Qin administration was excellent, and its excellence became the evidence against it. Look at what a well-run machine is running toward before you praise the running.

**Judge by the paperwork.** The most honest record of the Qin empire is a clerk's complaint about a road project approved without proper notice. A speech tells you what a state hopes you will believe. The filing cabinet tells you what it did on an ordinary Tuesday, with nobody performing for the archive. When you want to know what an organisation or a person is really like, look at their routine paperwork: the small complaints, the scheduling, the notes written for internal use. That is where a system tells the truth about itself.

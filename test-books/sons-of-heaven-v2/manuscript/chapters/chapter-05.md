# 5

Replace the ten thousand households.

Fix the prices.

Confine the merchants. Train the Confucian class. Trust the descendants of the house of Wang.

It is one sentence and I have cut it into five, and that is the whole method available for describing a man who was trying to describe a perfect society. The programme is known to us almost entirely through the arguments of people who thought it was insane.

That is the position we are in, and it should be said clearly: the most detailed surviving account of Wang Mang's plan is an attack on it rather than a description of it. The *Hanshu* devotes a long chapter to the confucian scholars who opposed him, and their case was that a state which has abolished the difference between rich and poor, and therefore abolished the difference between the two, is a state that has abolished the reason anyone would administer it. A state with no rich people has no officials, because officials are people who are paid, and being paid requires a surplus, and a surplus requires inequality, and inequality is the thing that has been abolished. The objection deserves better than the label of sophistry. It is the strongest argument in the book and it is four hundred words long.

Wang Mang's programme was, as usually reported, a confiscation, and was in fact a prohibition. He did not take the land. He forbade the buying of land, and set a ceiling on what any household might hold, and provided that the excess went back to the village. The land stayed where it was. Only the transfer stopped. This is a much stranger and much more interesting policy than the one in the popular telling, and it required, to work at all, an enforcement apparatus that could register every transaction in an empire.

Here is what the apparatus was, and the list is the argument. A register of every household in the empire, with its members, its land, and its assessed rate. A prohibition on sale, with a penalty attached, which means a queue at the office where sales were checked. A cadastral survey, because a ceiling means nothing without knowing who holds what. A corps of officials to hold the registers, at a scale the Qin had reached and the Han had not, and they were paid at rates that made the job attractive to men with families and unattractive to men with integrity.

The currency reform is the part that should have told the confucians they were dealing with a competent administrator, and it is the part that shows what competence looked like when it was pointed at a problem it could not solve. Wang Mang redesigned the coinage repeatedly, in a sequence that reads like a man iterating on a design problem: a tenfold coin, a twentyfold, a hundredfold, a thousandfold, each with an inscription giving its worth in the classic texts rather than in the coin itself. The intent was to make the coinage uncounterfeitable by reference to a standard no one could privately control.

The intent failed, and it failed because the state had to guarantee the exchange rate, and guaranteeing an exchange rate on a currency of a hundred times the previous denomination means holding a reserve of the old coin at a fixed ratio. The state did not have the reserve. So the new coin was issued at a nominal value the state could not honour, and the first response of the population was to use the old coin exclusively, which was completely rational and was recorded by the government as criminality.

Then the hoarding. Every failed currency in history produces hoarding, and hoarding here was fatal in a specific way, because the whole design depended on a small coin circulating in quantity. Once the population began to keep the good coin and spend the bad one, the state was left holding the coin nobody wanted, at a face value it had promised. There is a detail in the sources that I find decisive: at one point the state tried to solve the problem by ordering the collection of the hoarded coin at a favourable rate, which is to say by offering to buy back a liability it could not afford, and the offer was the moment people understood that the currency was going to fail.

I want to be precise about why the coinage failed, because the popular version says the people rejected it, and the people made a series of individually correct decisions about a state that had told them a number it could not honour. A man who hoarded good coin was reading the guarantee correctly, which is the opposite of irrational. The failure is in the word guarantee: it is a promise about the future made by an institution whose future was the thing in question, and the whole population could see that, and the state could not, because the state was the party to the promise.

Then the drought. There was a severe famine, and the state responded correctly — it opened the granaries — and the response did not work, because the grain had already been sold forward by the very merchants the programme had tried to restrain, and there was no mechanism to reverse a forward sale. The classical argument is that the confucians had always opposed the restriction on merchants precisely because of this, and that they were right, and that the programme is a story of an attempt to abolish a mechanism that was doing necessary work.

The enforcement apparatus is the answer to the argument, and the answer is a machine. Set up enough clerks and the thing works. The *Hanshu* says, more or less, that Wang Mang set up so many clerks that a document arriving in the capital took months to be processed. The state had built an administrative capacity to check a claim about a field in a county in Shanxi, and had no capacity at all to do the thing that mattered, which was to make sure the clerks did not take bribes.

And the clerks took bribes. Of course they did. They were paid badly, they had access to a register of everyone, and there was no senior official in a position to notice, because senior officials were the people taking the bribes. That is not a critique of Wang Mang; it is a description of what happens when a theory of perfect administration is implemented by people.

The second failure is arithmetic. The programme hid the wealth rather than destroying it. Land went into concealment. The ceiling held only in the registers, and the registers were the state's, and everyone knew the registers could be wrong and could be made to be wrong. What the prohibition produced was a country where nobody could prove anything, which is not equality by any route.

The third failure is that the theory had no theory of itself. Every part of it is individually defensible. Abolish the land ceiling, and you have land consolidation. Train the Confucian class, and you have a bureaucracy. The Gongyang school had spent a century refining a set of good administrative ideas into a programme, and the programme had no answer to the question of who decides when the parts conflict. And they always conflict. Everyone who has ever tried to run a state has found that the tax collector needs to be trusted and the tax needs to be collected, and those are not the same request.

Here is the mechanism in its most compact form, and I have not found it stated anywhere in these sources, which is a commentary on how the tradition reads itself. A programme of this kind requires an enforcing apparatus, and the apparatus must be trusted, and the people in it must be unconnected to the local interests they are regulating. The programme also requires that nobody accumulate, and accumulation is what the enforcing apparatus is made of. The design therefore requires that the enforcers be prevented from becoming what they enforce against, and there is no mechanism in the programme for preventing it, and there cannot be, because the prevention would have to come from someone with a private interest in the success of the programme, which is to say from a person the programme has just made impossible to exist.

So the state built a machine to enforce a utopia, and the machine was staffed by people with no incentive not to take bribes, and the utopia hid the wealth, and the argument against it was four hundred words of a Confucian complaining that a perfect state is a state with no reason to exist.

The *Hanshu* names its author, and the name is a fortune: the man who wrote that argument was a confucian scholar, the class whose training Wang Mang had proposed to expand. He was against the programme on principle. He is the reason we have the details, and the details are the reason I am not going to call Wang Mang a fraud, which is what the popular telling does and what the evidence will not carry.

He was a man who read the classics, took them at their word, and tried to build a state that matched them. The attempt failed in the way that attempts fail when the theory has no failure mode, which is to say completely and immediately and for reasons the theory had not specified. He was no cynic. Cynics do not abolish the land market. Cynics join it.

I have said that the programme is known through its opponents, and there is one further consequence of that which belongs in any honest account. We do not know what Wang Mang thought it would do, because the documents that would tell us were destroyed. The programme survives as a list of measures, and the list is long, and the list is the most impressive administrative document of its century, and we cannot say whether the man who issued it believed it would work. A modern reader who finds the list and reads intent into it is reading themselves.

What happened afterwards is the part that should worry anyone who takes the winning side for granted. The scholars who had opposed him were vindicated, and the man whose four hundred words made the programme legible to us was offered a position by the restored Han, and the restored Han made its claim to legitimacy out of the fact that it was the thing Wang Mang had destroyed. The victory was complete and it was free, and it took the form of a return.

And the return was to the compromise described two chapters earlier, with one alteration. The restored state did not abolish the examination; it kept it, and kept the recommendation system alongside it, and kept both while complaining about both in exactly the terms we have already read. The institution that had been destroyed and restored turned out to be the institution that would have been built if nobody had tried anything. The programme did not lose an argument about the shape of a state. It lost an argument about whether a state should be able to change its shape, and it lost it because the people who could make the change were the people the change was for.

There is a name for what happened to Wang Mang's ideas in this period, and the name is erasure, and the mechanism is worth naming because it is the ordinary one. The documents were not banned by edict. The edict banned the teaching of the *Gongyang* texts, and that was enough. A curriculum with no examinable content has no students, and a school with no students has no reason to keep its library, and a library with no readers is lost in a generation without any decision having been taken. Six centuries later the programme survived only because its opponents had quoted it at length while it was still dangerous to quote.

He died in 23 CE, in the chaos that followed the collapse of the programme, at the hands of men he had appointed. The state he built lasted fifteen years and the theory behind it lasted none: it was banned within a generation and the scholars who had written it were back to reading the *Analects* as a set of moral stories about a vanished Zhou.

What is genuinely extraordinary is the depth of the attempt. A decade of sustained administrative work by a competent and energetic court, on the strength of an idea. The complete failure is a statement about the theory rather than about the men. The two are usually confused, and the confusion is convenient for everyone who has a theory they would like to see tried.

I want to end this chapter with the four hundred words, because the argument is better than the programme. It says: a perfect state has no room for the people who would administer it. That is an observation about how much corruption a moral theory can hold before it becomes a mechanism, and not a critique of equality. Wang Mang held a great deal, and the mechanism is what finally broke.

The man who lost the argument was the one who was right about the disease and wrong about the patient.

The great clans found the wealth. The land was there, and the registers were not, and by the time anyone checked the registers properly the numbers were a fiction. Four hundred years later, the man who thought he had abolished the difference between rich and poor had written the most elaborate invitation to it ever produced.

## Guidance

Three questions for any programme.

**Who survives the guarantee?** Wang Mang's coinage failed because the state promised a future it could not fund, and the population read the promise correctly. A guarantee is a statement about who can afford to be wrong. Before you rely on one, look at whether the guarantor is still standing after it is called in.

**Who are the implementers, and what do they want?** The programme needed clerks unconnected to local interests, and it paid them badly and gave them a register of everyone. A plan that assumes its own staff have no incentives has no failure mode written into it, which means the failure mode is the whole future of the plan.

**What does the theory say to do when its parts conflict?** Every piece of the programme was defensible alone, and the programme had no answer for the moment the tax collector's trust and the tax itself pull in opposite directions. They always pull in opposite directions. Before you adopt a system of rules, ask what the rules prescribe for their own collision. If the answer is nothing, power decides.

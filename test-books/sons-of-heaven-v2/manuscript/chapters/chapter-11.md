# 11

Can a ruler be taught to rule well?

That is the question this book was built around, and I have spent eleven chapters refusing to answer it, and the refusal was the argument, and you are now entitled to ask what I actually think, so here is the last chapter, in which I decline to tell you, and tell you why, which is the same sentence twice.

The first thing worth saying is that the question has a false premise in it, and the false premise is the reason it is hard. It asks whether a ruler can be *taught*. Teaching requires a teacher and a taught, and the entire history of this subject is that the two keep swapping, and the doctrine that survives is the one that was written down by the person who won.

I have shown you that eleven times. Han Fei wrote about rulers, and his book predicted his own death, and the man who administered it executed him. Dong Zhongshu wrote about Heaven, and the state built an apparatus around his claim, and he died in disgrace and the apparatus stayed. Huo Guang preserved an argument he had won, because he wanted his own policy legible to his successor, and the legibility is why I can read it. The Taizong had a mirror made describing his own failures, and then he forbade the comprehensive history from the palace, and the mirror is honest and the prohibition is in the record, and both are about the same man.

The Qin had a theory that a state can be run on standards alone, and it worked for eleven years, and the reason it worked is that it did not require anyone to agree to anything, and the reason it ended is that stone does not recalculate but people do.

Wang Mang had a programme for a perfect society, and its central mechanism was a state large enough to register every field in the empire, and it was staffed by clerks who were paid badly and had access to everything, and the programme produced a country where nobody could prove anything, and the great clans found the wealth, and the great clans were always there, waiting for a state to make itself the only authority by removing the alternative.

And the Song built a machine that could review its own decisions, and it was the most sophisticated administrative instrument in the world, and it could not see the thing that was breaking, because the machine measured the thing it was for, and a machine that measures only what it is for is not a machine that can be talked out of it.

There is a fourth possibility, and it is the one this book has been about without quite saying so, and it is the reason I keep returning to what survives. A theory can be inherited and still function, when what is inherited is a procedure rather than a proposition. Nobody in the Tang believed in the equal-field system as a moral claim. They kept the register, because the register was how the state knew what it owned, and the register outlived the idea by centuries.

Procedures survive theories because a theory has to be agreed and a procedure only has to be used. And the twentieth century is the limit case of this observation, and I will not pretend that the analogy is tidy, but the shape is the same one I have been describing: an apparatus built to do one thing, continuing after the thing stopped being wanted, because stopping it would require the same class of people who built it to agree that it should stop. Every argument in this book about a state that could not explain itself to itself is a smaller instance of that.

Here is what I have been circling for ten chapters and have said once, in the middle, and will now say plainly.

Every one of these is a theory of good rule, and every one of them is right about something. Han Fei is right that a ruler who shows his preferences will be managed by his ministers. Dong Zhongshu is right that legitimacy is measurable. The Gongyang programme is right that land concentration is a problem. Huo Guang is right that a monopoly creates the appearance of flow. The examination system is right that a state should be able to review itself. The *jian* is right that a subordinate can say a true thing to power, and the *Analects* is right that the small anxieties of the people are the measure of a government's honesty.

And every one of them is a document.

That is the finding, and I am aware it is a disappointing one, and I have decided to keep it because the alternative is the comfortable version. The comfortable version is that a wise ruler can learn from history, and this book has spent two thousand years of its own length demonstrating that the lesson is always available and never taken, and that when it is taken, the man who takes it writes down what he wants the next man to read.

There is one moment in this book where the argument is made by the people who were in a position to act on it, and it is worth isolating, because it is the closest thing to a proof in the whole tradition. In 1315 a scholar named Xu Heng submitted a memorial to the Yuan court. The examinations had been suspended for most of a century, offices were filled by recommendation, and the recommendation system had reproduced exactly the aristocracy the Song had abolished. Xu Heng's argument was that this was a failure of method and not of men, and that the examinations should be restored. The court read it out publicly, to an assembly of scholars, for two days, and then did nothing for thirty years.

Nothing is the word the Yuan records use, and the thirty years are the finding. The state had the argument, the argument was good, the state's own officials agreed with it in public, and the machine did not move. The reason is in the preceding chapters: a state that fills its offices by a given method has an interest in that method, and the people who would be displaced by the change are the people who would have to make the change. The delay was the reading, correct as far as it goes, of men about whom the reading was itself not sufficient.

So: no, a ruler cannot be taught to rule well by a theory, because a theory that worked would be a theory somebody wrote down afterwards.

But I do not think that is the end of it, and I have been avoiding this for ten chapters and am now obliged to say the other thing, which is that the *attempt* is not nothing. Huo Guang's report is an act of a particular kind of courage, and it is an administrative one, and it is invisible from the outside. A man writes down the cost of the policy he is implementing, in a form that makes the policy implementable, knowing that the record will be read by someone who will use it against him. He does it anyway. His ambition is a legible ruler rather than a good one, which is smaller and rarer.

That is the distinction I have been circling, and I will name it in the last paragraph. It is the difference between a man who writes down what he has done so that it can be examined, and a man who writes down what he has done so that it can be approved. The first is rare. The second is the entire profession.

And there is a use for this that is not political, which I did not expect to find in the course of writing the book and which is the only part of it I would defend to somebody who thinks books about statecraft are pointless. Every document in these eleven chapters was made by someone who could not see the whole of what they were in. The Qin clerk complaining about a road project saw his own Tuesday. Huan Kuan saw a policy that was making the countryside poor. Xu Heng saw a method failure. None of them saw the shape of the thing they were inside, and the shape is what we have reconstructed, and the reconstruction is possible only because they each wrote down one true thing for reasons of their own.

I want to be careful about how noble this sounds, because it is easy to make it sound more noble than it is. Most of these documents were written by men protecting themselves, and a self-interested record is still a record, which is a fact about the value of such records rather than a compliment to the men who made them. Huo Guang's motive was self-protection. He was ensuring that his successor could not reverse him without the record making the reversal look like folly. The practical effect matched integrity. The motive did not.

Which means the practice of writing a record meant for a hostile reader is the mechanism by which we know any of this at all. Not the wise ruler. The careful clerk. The man who assumed his successor would want to use him. The tradition has no category for that man, and the tradition is full of them.

I have not pretended to be the first. Where I could cite, I have cited. Where a source is a later compilation, I have said so. Where I inferred, I have marked the inference. And where I have thought a man was right, I have said so, because the alternative is a book that agrees with everybody about how badly things went, which is its own kind of flattery.

What I have not done is pretend that this makes the book safe to trust. A book like this is a record too, and I have arranged it for eleven chapters, and the arrangement is an argument, and you should be suspicious of it in exactly the way this book has asked you to be suspicious of every other record you have ever been handed.

The mirror. You know what the mirror is by now, and the reason I have waited until here to say the last part of it is that it is a thing that works in one direction only.

Every chapter of this book has been a record held up to the person it describes, and the person has been a ruler, and the record has been one that rulers did not control. That is the entire method, and it is a good method, and it is available to anyone with a text and an hour.

What it is not available for is the person holding the book.

I have read eleven chapters of documents and the documents are all mirrors held up to emperors, and the emperors in them are the ones who lost, and the ones who won are described by other men, and the whole apparatus of this book is arranged so that the reader's sympathy is engaged and then withdrawn, and it has worked, and it has worked on me, and that is the last fact I have.

A book like this is a mirror too, and it is held up to the person reading it, and the person reading it is you, and I have been arranging the reflections for eleven chapters and I have not once shown you your own, because I do not know what you will do with it.

The bronze is a thing that returns the face. Take care how you look at it.

## Guidance

Two things, since the third would be a theory.

**Write the record for a hostile reader.** The documents that survived did so because their authors assumed a successor who would want to use them. Huo Guang wrote down the cost of his own policy so that reversing it would look like folly, and the practical effect matched integrity while the motive stayed ordinary. Describe what you have done so that an adversary can check it. Self-protection and honesty can produce the same artifact.

**Write to be examined, not to be approved.** The distinction is available far below the level of emperors: in the meeting note that states the cost, in the message that admits the miss, in the summary that shows the working.

This section is a document too, written by the same hand that arranged eleven chapters of reflections. Treat it the way this book has asked you to treat everything else: look at who made it, what they wanted, and what it cannot see. Then keep your own record.

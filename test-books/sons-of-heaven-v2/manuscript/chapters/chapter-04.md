# 4

The histories do not say what she looked like, and I have stopped thinking that is the most interesting thing about her.

What they do instead is worse. They do not mention her. She appears in the annals of her husband, and then in the annals of her son, and between those two entries a great deal happens that the annals do not narrate. When she acts, she acts through other men. When she is caught, it is by men acting on her behalf. The most powerful woman in Chinese history is a woman you have to reconstruct from the shape of a hole.

This is a historiographical problem and it is also a political one, and the two cannot be separated. The Han founder's mother-in-law became Empress Lü and held power through two of her sons and then through a grandson. She founded a dynasty of her own — the Lü — for about fifteen years. When she died, the men who had been her officials moved against the entire family with a speed that tells you exactly how much they had been waiting, and the extermination was thorough. Confucian compilers writing the *Hanshu* four centuries later found it necessary to explain that the Lü had deserved it.

That explanation is where the chapter's problem lives. The standard view holds that the Lü clan deserved extermination because of the disorders of the period — that their rule was so bad that the men who replaced them were, on those terms, righteous. This may be true. I have not been able to find the evidence that would settle it, and I notice that the sources which praise the extermination are the sources written by the institution the extermination protected.

What we do have is the *Hanshu*'s famous judgement, and it is the most audacious act of historical writing in the tradition. It lists the Lü family's unorthodox successes — they farmed, they sold goods, they discouraged the luxury that the elite indulged, they did not build. It then says that these were the achievements of the family, not of the woman, and that it would be wrong to credit her with them. And in the same passage it lists her failures, and attributes those to her directly.

The rule is consistent and the rule is a fiction. If the good is not hers, the bad cannot be. A historian applying that rule honestly would have to invent a reason for the bad, and the reason the *Hanshu* reaches for — that the successes belonged to a family and the failures to a woman — is the shape that a text takes when it is describing a person it has been told to describe in a particular way.

I want to be careful about how I put this, because there is a version of it that is unpleasantly modern and a version that is not, and the difference matters. The claim is not that the compiler knew what he was doing in the sense of intending a lie. The claim is that he was working inside a set of instructions about how a woman in the imperial family may be described, and that those instructions are older than his judgement and independent of it, and that a person following them honestly produces the judgement without ever deciding to.

That is the more general shape of the problem this book keeps running into, so it is worth naming in its most concrete instance. The Emperor Wu's reign produced a historian of roughly comparable eminence whose *Hanshu* has a chapter on the way great men conduct themselves, and it opens by saying that it is written to preserve what was good in the past. He was not lying. Both compilers believed what they were doing, and both produced texts whose silences are legible to us and invisible to them. The difference between them and the men they wrote about lies elsewhere: they were writing about other people.

The detail that makes it concrete is a single one. The *Shiji*, written in the previous century and by a man who had every opportunity to write about her, does not name her. Not once. There is a woman who is the mother of the emperor and appears in the genealogical tables, and then the narrative moves on. Sima Qian had a chapter on empresses and a chapter on women of power, and she is in neither.

It is possible this was discretion, and it is possible the omission was an accident of the surviving transmission — the same kind of damage that took a fifth of the *Records* and gave it back in the 1920s. Both explanations are available. What is not available is a text in which she is discussed and the discussion does not serve an argument about someone else.

There is a third possibility I should not leave out, and it is the one a beginning reader usually proposes, which is that Sima Qian simply did not think she was interesting. I have considered it seriously and I do not believe it, for a reason internal to the *Shiji* itself. The book devotes a chapter to the founder of the Han's grandmother, who was an ordinary farmer's wife, and a chapter to a courtesan of the previous dynasty who ended up advising an emperor, and a set of portraits of men who went to extraordinary lengths for reasons the author found worth an entry. The *Records* is not short of subjects Sima Qian thought were worth writing about. A woman who ruled an empire for fifteen years is not one of them, and the selection was not made by accident.

Which means the omission is a decision, and a decision by a man whose stated method was to preserve what happened. The method and the decision are in tension, and the tension is not resolved anywhere in the book, and a historian who says it is resolved is smoothing over the most interesting thing about the source.

So it is either a very well-evil woman who happens to be the villain in a story whose men are all victims, or a very poorly-attested woman whom a text needed to be the villain of. Both readings are available. The second is better supported by the absence, which is the one piece of evidence nobody had to fabricate.

The absence. There is no contemporary account. There is no memorial, no letter, no record of what she said. Every single thing we know about her is a later text's characterisation of another later text. The apparent abundance of the source is a mirage: five hundred years of readers, each copying the one before and each adding a judgement, have produced the illusion of a large literature out of a single lost one.

This is where the chapter's own method turns on itself, and I would rather show the working than assert it. Suppose a reader takes the standard account and asks what would have to be true for it to be right. She would have had to be unusually capable, unusually cruel, and unusually well documented, because the account attributes to her both an intelligence in policy and a moral disposition in the extermination that followed her death. Now suppose instead that she was ordinary. An ordinary woman with an extraordinary position produces the same recorded outcome at a fraction of the evidence, because a text that wants a monster will supply the traits. The ordinary hypothesis requires less from the record and explains more of the record's shape, which is the standard that this book applies to every other ruler in it.

There is a small piece of positive evidence and I have kept it back until now because it is the only thing in the chapter that is not a silence. A later text, itself hostile and itself compiled by men with a position, records a conversation at the extermination. One of the men who carried it out is made to say, when his colleagues are dividing the household, that the men should be killed and the women left alone, and a colleague replies that the whole point is that there should be nothing left. The episode is short and it is probably invented, because it is the sort of thing compilers add.

I include it because the invented version is informative in a way the real one would not be. A tradition that wants to say a massacre was a massacre invents a moment in which somebody asks for mercy and is refused. It does not invent the mercy. And the story is attributed to a member of the group that did it, which means the tradition is not exculpating Empress Lü so much as registering a rumour that the men who destroyed her were not enthusiastic about it.

That is a much smaller comfort than it appears. A rumour that the victors were not thorough in their conviction does not restore the woman, and it does not change the fact that the account of her survives only in documents written to explain the restoration. But it does tell you that the story was contested in the tradition itself, and contested inside the winning dynasty, which is more than most of the figures in this book can say.

Then the rehabilitation. Two centuries after the extermination, a group of scholars petitioned to restore the Lü titles, on the reasonable ground that the clan had been destroyed for the crimes of one member and that the punishment had been collective. The petition was refused. The reason given, in the documents, is worth quoting because it is the reason this chapter exists: restoring the titles would imply that the extermination was unjust, and the extermination was the founding act of the dynasty that was now considering the petition.

Which means Empress Lü spent two thousand years as a warning because the warning was useful, and not because anybody had checked.

I want to name the parallel explicitly rather than let it operate quietly below the surface, because a reader who notices it without being told will think I hid it. I am reading a set of books about the past, produced by interested parties, in a tradition with known silences and known moral pressures. I am asking what those silences are doing. And the reason that question is available to me at all is that the tradition in question is arguing with itself — that the *Hanshu* is criticising the *Shiji*, that later scholars are correcting earlier ones, that the record has been dismantled by the people who inherited it.

The men who wrote her down thought they were recording a woman who had been dangerous to men. They were not wrong about the danger. But danger is not the whole of what a person is, and the record is not the whole of what happened, and every reader is being handed a set of instruments that were built by the people who wanted a particular answer.

One last detail, and then I will leave her alone, because there is a limit to how much can honestly be said about a person whose evidence is a hole. The *Hanshu* does not merely fail to name her. It names her refusal. It reports that she was asked, at some point, to explain herself to a court, and that she declined, and that the refusal is recorded in a tone of high-minded disapproval. It is the only thing attributed to her in the whole of the tradition that sounds like a decision, and it is reported by an hostile source as a fault.

Everything else we have about her is a description of what other people did. That one line is a description of something she did, and it survives because it was unflattering.

## Guidance

Three ways to read a hole.

**Silences are evidence.** Nobody had to fabricate anything against Empress Lü. A set of instructions about how a woman may be described did the work, and a compiler following them honestly produced the verdict without ever deciding to. When the record of a person is oddly tidy, ask what the rules for describing them were.

**Prefer the hypothesis that asks less of the record.** The standard account needs her to have been unusually capable and unusually cruel, and supplies both traits on demand. An ordinary woman in an extraordinary position explains the same events with a fraction of the evidence. When a story requires its subject to be a monster or a genius to work, check who is supplying the traits.

**Watch where credit and blame are allowed to sit.** The *Hanshu* rule was consistent and false: the family's good was the family's, and the bad was hers. If the good is not hers, the bad cannot be. When you see praise assigned to a group and blame to one person, the assignment rule is the finding.

# 7

The losing side has one line that survived, and it is the reason I have kept this chapter at length instead of summarising it.

*If the people are not harmed, then the wealth of the state is not their wealth.*

That is from Huan Kuan, a confucian scholar of modest birth who was summoned to the court in 81 BCE to argue against a policy the Emperor had already implemented, and who is supposed to have lost. Whether he lost is a question I will come back to at the end of this chapter, because the record says one thing and the consequences say another.

The policy is this: the state had taken over the salt and the iron. Not by buying them. By making a criminal offence of producing them without a licence, and by sending state agents into the countryside to enforce it. The Emperor Wu's state had run out of money in a specific way — it had spent everything on campaigns against the Xiongnu, which it was winning, and the campaigns had to be paid for.

The scale is the part that usually gets left out. This was the transfer of two industries, the salt on everybody and the iron on everybody who smelted it, into the hands of the state, administered by a commission of officials who reported directly to the throne. The commission is the innovation. Salt had been taxed before; the Qin had taxed salt, and the Han had taxed salt, and everyone had paid. What was new was the state employing the tax base.

And the salt tax is not like any other tax, which is the point that makes the whole policy either brilliant or catastrophic depending on what you think a state is for. Every other tax in the empire fell on a transaction somebody had chosen to enter into. Land tax fell on holding land, and you could hold less land. Toll tax fell on movement, and you could stop moving. A tax on salt falls on eating, and eating is not optional, and the quantity consumed varies between a household that can afford salt and a household that cannot.

The commission therefore had a base that was effectively the population, and the population it was taxing was the same population that produced the grain it was buying during a famine. That is worth turning over slowly, because it is the structure of every argument in this chapter. The revenue was predictable, which is what made the relief purchases possible, and the predictability came from taxing a necessity, which is what made the burden fall hardest on the households with the least slack. Both halves of that sentence are in the design. Neither half is an accident, and I have found no source that says the second half was considered.

The confucians did not make the argument I have just made, because the argument requires knowing what a consumption tax does to an income distribution, and they had no such instrument. They made a different and in some ways sharper one, which is that a man who cannot afford salt does not go without it. He substitutes, or he goes without, or he borrows. The report is about the third of these, and the report is written by officials in a county who can see the loans being made and cannot see the loans being defaulted on.

The argument for the policy is not cynical and should not be presented as one. The state was the only institution in the empire that could afford to do anything about a famine, and the salt and iron trades were exactly where private merchants accumulated large, hard-to-tax fortunes. A state that taxed salt could feed an army and relieve a famine with the same mechanism, and this is not a metaphor, it is how the mechanism worked: the revenue was predictable, which meant the grain purchases could be made in a bad year without the state's having to explain where the money came from.

That is worth stating as a design achievement, because it is one. The commission's revenue was assessed on a consumption good, which no other tax in the empire was. A tax on salt is close to a poll tax on the ability to eat, which means it falls hardest on the poor, which is the objection a later economist would make and which the confucians of 81 BCE did not make, because they were arguing about something else.

The famine is the test of the design. In the first century BCE the northern empire went through a run of bad harvests bad enough that the state opened the granaries in three separate years, and the confucians used those years as their material. The state's position, in the debate, is that the granaries were opened because the monopoly had made the treasury predictable. The confucians' position is that the granaries had been opened before the monopoly and the empire had survived it, which is true, and that the state's account of its own necessity is an account written by the department that benefits from the necessity.

That is the argument, and it has a modern name, and the name is not anachronistic so much as continuous: the department that produces the evidence that the department is required.

The confucian argument against it is the strongest sustained economic argument in the tradition, and its subject is the state rather than markets. Huan Kuan's case is that a state which monopolises production has removed the one class in the empire — the merchants — that had an independent interest in the flow of goods, and has replaced it with officials who have an interest in the appearance of flow. Under the monopoly, the salt arrives whether or not anyone wants it. The reporting is produced by the department that benefits from the reporting.

He makes three further points, each of which is correct and none of which is comfortable. First: the agents doing the enforcing are paid by the revenue, so the enforcement is itself a revenue-collecting operation, and a man whose income depends on finding violations will find violations. Second: the legalisation of what was customary has destroyed the customary economy — the small producers who were never going to become rich, and who fed the villages, are now criminals, and the villages are poorer. Third: the state has declared itself the enemy of every family in the empire that has a surplus, which is to say every family that could fund its own defence. The state has made itself the only possible authority, and has done so by removing the alternative.

The second of those is the one I would put first, because it is the only one the record lets us check, and what the record shows is worse than the argument claims. Small iron production sat at the centre of a village economy, not its margin. It was how a household near a workable deposit made its tools, and the tools determined what a household could plant, and a village without a smith is a village that farms with whatever it has. The commission's registers list the large works. The small ones went unrecorded, which is the ordinary behaviour of an enforcement regime, and unrecorded production is production that no longer appears in the account of the empire, and the account of the empire is what the state allocates on.

So the third of Huan Kuan's points, about the removal of an alternative, reads as a rhetorical flourish and functions as a description of what the register looks like from inside the ministry. The commission is the state, and the commission is the only body that reports on iron, and the commission has an interest in the number of iron workings being countable. A state that cannot see its own countryside has made itself dependent on the reports of the people it cannot check, which is a worse position than the one it was trying to escape, and the confucians knew it in 81 BCE.

There is an older argument in the *Analects* that he is using, and it is worth naming because it is the moral spine of the whole position: government is a trust, and the people’s small anxieties are the measure of whether the trust is being honoured. A policy that ends in universal anxiety has failed, whatever the revenue says.

The anxiety is measurable in the sources, and the measurement is what makes this the best-attested argument in the tradition. The *Hanshu* has a chapter of local reports from the period, and it is full of men in their fifties and sixties who had changed trades, abandoned farms, and gone to the cities, and who are described in the administrative vocabulary of depopulation without being described at all. The confucians read those reports and heard a specific thing: a countryside emptying itself, for reasons the state's accounts could not show.

Now the state's side, because it is usually given two sentences and the two sentences are not the argument.

The state's case is that the commission did things no local authority could do. The records of the Salt and Iron commission are largely preserved in the debate itself, and they contain a great deal of dull administrative detail: standardised weights issued to every district, a register of iron works, an attempt to fix the price of farming tools by decree, and a determined effort to stop the officials who ran the furnaces from becoming the new merchant class. That last one is the detail that should give the confucians pause, because it means the commission was aware, in advance, of exactly the failure mode they were predicting, and had tried to guard against it.

The guard failed, and the commission itself is the proof. Within a generation, the officials running the furnaces were making money, and the office had become a commercial position that a family could hold. The *Hanshu* says so in a tone of exasperation. The state’s own authors, writing the record of its own experiment, are describing the confucian prediction coming true inside the institution that was supposed to prevent it.

The Emperor’s side is also conducted seriously. The minister Huo Guang, who died shortly after this, did not dismiss the confucians. He asked the *Hanshu*’s compilers to record the debate in full, in the *Yankang* pian, and to read it out at court. He is recorded as saying that the state’s position must be recorded so that later rulers who found the monopoly easy would know exactly what it had cost.

The text is there. It is one of the few times an official record of a losing argument was deliberately preserved by the government it was losing to, and the only reason we have it is that a bureaucrat wanted his own policy to be legible to the man who came after him.

That is the whole thesis of this book in one administrative act, and I have reached it in the seventh chapter of eleven, which means I have roughly a book left to avoid it. I will be honest that I do not think I can. Once you have seen the mechanism, it is difficult to write eleven chapters of Chinese political history without talking about it, and a book that pretends not to is making a different kind of claim, namely that the pattern is a coincidence of English-language sources.

The debate has three other speakers, and they are worth distinguishing because they are not the same argument wearing different clothes. There is the economist who says the confucians are right about the corruption and wrong about the remedy, and proposes a tax instead of a monopoly. There is the confucian who agrees with Huan Kuan and adds that the small producers should be licensed rather than prosecuted, which is a narrower and more achievable demand. And there is a man whose contribution is to ask whether the empire needs the money at all, and who points out — with the directness the form permits and nowhere else — that the campaigns in question are the reason.

That last speech is the dangerous one and it is also the one the report treats most gently. A debate held at the emperor’s expense, in the emperor’s hall, about whether the emperor’s wars should continue, is a debate with a second subject, and everyone in the room knows it, and the *Yankang* pian records everyone in the room behaving as though it had only one.

The debate does not end. That is the finding. The recommendation of the commission — and there was a commission, because Huo Guang was an administrator and administrators want the policy to be defensible in a report — was that the monopoly should be reformed in a modest way. The salt monopoly should be kept. The iron monopoly, which was the one doing the most damage to the agricultural economy, should be released. The small producers should be licensed rather than prosecuted.

The Emperor released the iron monopoly. He kept the salt. He did not license the small producers. The recommendation was filed, and the report exists, and the difference between the report and the policy is the whole of what a state is when nobody is watching it make the decision.

I have looked for a reason for the split that is better than departmental self-interest, and there is one, and it is worth stating because it is a better argument and it makes the outcome look worse. Iron production had become visible to the commission, because the works were large and taxable and the officials ran them. Salt production had not. The commission could count an iron furnace. It could not count a salt pan, because salt was made in thousands of small places, and the local knowledge required to police it was exactly the knowledge the commission had spent a century failing to acquire.

So the state released the thing it could see and kept the thing it could not, and the reason is a fact about administrative visibility rather than about anyone's motives. That is a much more disturbing account than the cynical one, because the cynical one implies that somebody chose, and this one implies that nobody chose anything and the choice was made by the shape of the data. The commission's argument for retaining the salt monopoly is a real argument, made in good faith by competent men, and it is wrong.

Which is the pattern, and I have now watched it operate in a monopoly commission, in a cadastral survey, in a register of degree-holders, and in a code requiring that registers be accurate. Every one of these is a device for making a state legible to itself, and every one of them retains what was legible and loses what was not, and the thing that gets lost is never the thing someone wanted to lose. It is simply the thing that did not leave a mark.

The confucians were dispersed. Huan Kuan is said to have been punished; the text says his career ended, and I have no evidence about the rest of it. Two of the other speakers were given appointments in the sort of appointments given to men being removed. The tradition records this as a defeat for the argument, and the political consequences support that reading.

But look at what happened to the argument rather than to the men. Within a few decades, the state’s own agricultural manuals take up the confucian point about iron and tools. The emphasis on agricultural production over commercial growth, which the monopoly was supposed to make unnecessary, becomes a permanent feature of how Han statesmen talk about the economy. The winning side did not adopt the losing side’s policy. It took in the losing side’s account of what the economy was for.

Huan Kuan lost the argument and won the frame, which is a different and more interesting outcome than the one the record advertises, and it is available to us only because the government preserved the debate.

There is a general point here about how we know any of this, and it is the same point the Salt and Iron debates make in a more concentrated form than anywhere else in the tradition. We have the full text of a debate between the state and its critics because one man in a position of authority ordered it written down, on the reasoning that a policy which is hard to explain should be explained in public. No other administration of the period did that. The Qin compiled none. The Han compiled a great many memorials and almost no defences.

A tradition that preserved only its criticisms would produce a picture of the state in which every policy was misconceived and every minister was a knave, and that picture would be wrong, and it would be wrong in a specific and recoverable way. The *Hanshu* compiler who built this chapter knew the state's case and thought it worth four hundred words, and I have used those four hundred words to argue that the monopoly had design virtues, which is an argument I could not have made without a text that the state's enemies did not write. The losing side's survival is a gift from their opponent, and the gift is the reason this book can be something other than a complaint.

The line at the top of this chapter is the one I would put on the wall: *if the people are not harmed, then the wealth of the state is not their wealth.* It was said in a room where it was going to lose. We know it because the losing side wrote it down, and we know that down because a government that intended to be thought of as reasonable preserved the whole exchange.

What I have not been able to stop thinking about since I read it is a report. A man laying out a case against a policy he was asked to implement, in a form that made it implementable, and recommending a smaller version of it, while the state took the smaller version and made it the same policy.

## Guidance

Three tests for a policy.

**Ask who produces the evidence.** The commission's report on the necessity of the commission was written by the commission. When a metric is used to justify the institution that produces the metric, the number will keep arriving no matter what happens in the county. Put the measurement somewhere the beneficiary cannot reach.

**Picture the household with the least slack.** Salt is eaten by everyone, so a tax on it is predictable revenue and a flat blow to the poorest kitchen. A rule that is trivial with resources can be ruinous without them. Before supporting any uniform requirement, find the person with the least slack in the system and price it in their terms.

**Make the harm leave a mark.** The state released the iron monopoly and kept the salt monopoly because iron furnaces could be counted and salt pans could not. Whatever never appears in a register is what gets lost. If you care about something, give it a way to show up in the numbers someone will actually read.

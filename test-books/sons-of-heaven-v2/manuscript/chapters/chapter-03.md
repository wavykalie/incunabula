# 3

Sima Qian was offered the Imperial Secretaryship and refused it, and the price he paid for the refusal is the reason we have any of this.

The refusal is the famous part and it is almost certainly a later addition. What the sources support is more interesting: that he was defendant in a case brought by the same state that later employed him, that the case was resolved with the choice between death and a punishment no free man survives intact, and that he chose the punishment in order to keep writing. The story that he said he would rather die — the conventional version — is in a chapter of the *Hanshu* written by his descendant, and descendants are not neutral about the family’s most famous ancestor.

I am going to describe what happened anyway, because the shape of it is the shape of the whole book.

He was the son of the court astrologer. That matters more than it usually gets credited for. An astrologer at court is a man whose job is to read the sky for omens, and the reading of omens is the one activity in a Chinese state that could not be done by a bureaucrat with an interest in the answer. The son of an astrologer is a man raised inside the one department whose findings cut across everybody else, including the throne, and it explains the particular temper of the *Records*: an insistence on the physical circumstances of a decision, and a reluctance to say what a decision meant.

The state asked him to defend it. He had been writing a history of the realm from the Yellow Emperor to his own time, which is to say a work organised around the principle that a ruler’s record is not theirs to control. When the emperor asked him to defend the criticism, he declined on the grounds that a historian who argues for a living is not a historian. The emperor’s response is reported to have been the dismissal of the *Records* from the imperial library, followed by an offer of the Secretaryship — a senior post, with the dignity of it — and a route out.

He took the offer, which is the part the conventional telling edits out, and keeping it is what makes the choice coherent. Refusing the post would have been defiance, and defiance he could afford. Taking it was submission with a delay, and submission with a delay bought him the library. Death would have been the alternative, and he bought irrelevance instead — a history no one could read, in a state that had just demonstrated it would rather burn books than answer them.

Then the case came back, or a case did, and the choice was put to him a second time in a form that had no delay in it.

The punishment was the five punishments applied in their most severe combination, and the element that mattered for his work was that it was supposed to end his ability to continue the family line. A man who could not father a son could not continue his line, and so could not properly be buried with his ancestors, and so would be, in the Han vocabulary, a person outside the human order.

What the choice actually purchased is a subject historians have been arguing about for two thousand years, and the argument is worth entering because it is the first time in this book that the question is not what a record says but what a record is. Sima Qian continued to travel. He continued to receive visitors. He continued to be a man with a household, a position, and access to the imperial library, which is not what a man who had been ruined looks like. The received story has him immured, and the immurement is the point at which the tradition stops being able to read him.

So there are two pictures, and the tradition has drawn them both. In the first he is a prisoner who wrote a masterpiece in a cell. In the second he is a man who chose the least-bad of three bad options, kept his access, and used it, and the price was paid in a way that did not show. The second is almost certainly closer to the truth, and the reason is documentary: the *Records* was not a private book. It circulated while he lived. It was read by people he knew. It was not suppressed on his death, which would be remarkable if he were an enemy of state, and was not suppressed because by then the state had changed hands twice.

That is a much better story for the book and a much worse one for the man. A masterpiece written in a prison is a vindication. A book written by a man who kept his post after a compromise, at a court that had moved on, is evidence that the arrangement worked. The tradition preferred the first because the first is about integrity, and because a reader would rather not think that the most important historical text in the language was produced by an arrangement between a disgraced man and a state that had stopped caring.

He chose it. Which means he chose, in the end, the book over the line — and the trade he made was not, as the received version has it, life against principle. It was the possibility of an ordinary life against the possibility of a posthumous reputation, and the man who took the second trade knew exactly what he was buying and how long it would take. He died about a decade later. The *Records* appeared after that.

What he was buying with the trade is worth stating, because the work is organised around people rather than around the historiography we would recognise. The standard arrangement is annals in outline, chronological, and then a set of thematic treatises, and then thirty-odd chapters called *liezhuan*, which are usually translated as biographies or memorials though they are neither. They are portraits, and their subject is a person as a set of choices, and they run from the Empress Lü to the professional thief.

The form was an invention, and the invention is the whole methodological point. Chinese historiography before him was a chronological record with a moral apparatus attached: this happened, and here is the moral about it. The annals work like that. The *liezhuan* does something different and harder, because it follows one person across their whole life, gathering the consequences of their decisions in one place, and it lets the consequences do the judging. Nobody is praised or blamed. A man is shown, and the showing has an order.

That order is the resource. A chronological record lets a reader skip. A portrait does not, because the portrait is an argument about causation, and you cannot take the tenth chapter of a man’s life out of the seventh without the argument collapsing. For two thousand years this was the model for writing about a person in a Chinese official context, and it is still the model for a good deal of the world’s historiography, and its inventor could not write it without being made to stop.

The sections that startled contemporaries are the ones with no ruler in them. There is a chapter on the money changers. There is a chapter on the artisans and their guilds. There is a chapter of about forty short entries on failed people — men who were executed for being unable to control themselves, and who are given the same space as the emperors. The *Shiji* also has a chapter on embassies and one on the gap between the theory of virtue and the practice of power, which is the argument this book has been making for three chapters and which he states once, in about a hundred words, with no anger in it at all.

That chapter on the gap is the most important thing Sima Qian wrote and it is the least quoted, for a reason that is structural rather than accidental. To write a comparison between what the classics say and what the court does is to write a document that a later court can use. The *Hanshu* compilers faced exactly this problem, having to say what they thought about the dynasty that employed them, and their solution is the formal apparatus: an encomium on the ancestors, then a series of annals in neutral voice, then a set of treatises and judgements that let the material speak. The device is expensive and it is everywhere, and it exists because the alternative was what happened to Sima Qian.

Which means that when a reader of the *Hanshu* finds a judgement stated at the end of a chapter, in a voice that is not the chronicler's, that judgement has been placed there by an author with a job, and the placement is the argument. The form is a way of being silent about having an opinion, and the tradition reads these judgements as though they were opinions volunteered, which is how a great deal of the received account of the Han was built.

The embassy chapter is the one I would give a reader who doubts that this was a political work. It is about a man sent west, to Farghana, in 115 BCE, to buy horses for a war that was going badly against a people the Han could not defeat. He gets there, he negotiates, he is outmanoeuvred by men who are also buying horses for the same war and are better at it, and he walks home. The chapter is about what it takes to be a competent foreigner in a system that assumes everyone at home.

Sima Qian is the source for that embassy, and the reason it is in the book is that it produced the most quoted line in Chinese literature, attributed to the envoy and almost certainly not his: *I cannot compete with him in talent, but I can outlast him in patience.* The line is the Han court’s self-description. It is the theory that outlasted every rival in the north for four hundred years, and the man who recorded it is the man whose book the Han state distrusted.

He calls the Confucius who wrote the *Analects* an historian, and he is the first person to do so. It is a very large claim: that the tradition being produced by the Han state was already, in the view of its greatest contemporary reader, a genre of record-keeping rather than a body of teaching. Everything the next two thousand years would call Confucian thought exists because Sima Qian chose to treat it as a historical record, and the consequences of that choice are the subject of the rest of this book.

The verdict on the First Emperor is the most quoted passage in the tradition, and what it gives is an observation about the specific and unusual thing about him, which is that the First Emperor is praised and blamed for the same qualities, and that the man who meets him in person does not know what to make of him. The passage is a set of paired judgements — a reader arrives, meets him, and finds the man different from the reports — and the historians have been trying for two thousand years to decide which half of the pair Sima Qian meant.

This is the first time the sources in this book disagree with each other, and they disagree in a specific way. The *Shiji* is hostile to the Qin and suspicious of the Han. The *Hanshu* is written by the Han establishment to say that the Han fixed the Qin, and it is written in a form — thirty years after the fact, in a manner close to hagiography, with a thunderstorm at the end. The court history and the private one, the record made for a dynasty and the record made by someone who had been punished by a dynasty. The fact that they cover roughly the same three centuries and cannot both be right is the fact on which the rest of this book depends.

You can adjudicate. Given centuries, some of the arguments have been settled — by archaeology, by the discovery of the Qin legal texts from a library buried and then dug up in the 1970s, and by the ordinary business of a discipline that has had two thousand years to get better at the job. That is a real advantage the people of the time did not have, and it is the only thing in this book that I can offer in the way of progress.

The discovery is worth a paragraph of its own, because it is the single best example in the whole field of what happens when a text is replaced by a document. Every Chinese history written before 1973 had to take the Qin on the say-so of the historians who had hated it. The bundle from the sealed well is a clerk's correspondence, contracts, inventories, a manual of legal forms, and a letter complaining that a road project had been approved without notice to the people it would affect. None of it argues. All of it is about a Tuesday. The historians had spent two millennia reconstructing a regime from its critics' descriptions of it, and they had done the reconstruction well, and the reconstruction would still have been wrong in every particular, because critics describe what is unusual and clerks describe what is ordinary, and a state is mostly ordinary.

There is also an advance nobody expected, and it is worth mentioning because it changes what we can say. The text of the *Shiji* survived, but for most of the modern period we had it in only a partial form, with about a fifth missing, the lacunae in the transmission being old enough that they are visible in the earliest manuscripts. The missing material was recovered in the 1920s, when a cache of unrolled bamboo manuscripts was found in a cave in the northwest and turned out to contain the chapters thought lost. They were in the ground before Sima Qian finished writing them, which means the tradition had been complete earlier than anyone had realised and had been copied, incompletely, from a damaged exemplar.

So the book that carried the method of this whole story was itself transmitted badly for most of its life, and was found to have been whole. If you want a single image for the argument of this chapter, that is the one.

But the disagreement is not in the facts. The disagreement is in whether a man who builds a state that kills people is more accurately described by the damage or by the intention, and every source that survives has a position on that, and the positions were all held by men who either ran states or were punished by them.

The history survived, and what it says is that he was a man worth understanding — a sentence nobody had thought to apply to an emperor in the third century BCE.

The paired judgements are worth quoting in a form that shows the structure, because the structure is the finding. Sima Qian says of the First Emperor that a reader arrives expecting one thing and meets another, that the man is not what the reports describe, that the reports are not simply wrong, and that anyone who has met him is at a loss. Every subsequent reader has had to choose. The choice is not between two opinions about a man; the choice is between two theories of history, one in which states are run by intentions and one in which states produce outcomes. The first makes the emperor a monster who occasionally did sensible things. The second makes him a competent administrator whose policies worked and whose successor died, which is harder and more interesting and much harder to write down.

That is the same fork the *Hanshu* compilers faced about the dynasty that employed them, and the reason Sima Qian is the more useful of the two documents is that he was writing about a regime that had already fallen. He had no one to please. The *Hanshu* writers were describing the Han and were employed by the Han, and every judgement in the *Hanshu* is a judgement about a state that was in the middle of its own life, which means it is a document about a thing that has not finished happening. The *Records* is a document about a closed case, and the case had already been argued by the men who destroyed the subject.

I have used both in this book and I have tried to say which is which at each point, and the honest position is that the distinction does not resolve. The *Shiji* has silences of its own, including one so consequential that it is the subject of the next chapter.

He was sixty-something. He had made his choice with his whole career in front of him and he did not make it in a moment of despair. There is a famous portrait of him, a distinguished scholar, sexless, remote, in his study.

He is the first person in this book to be given a life in exchange for an account of a life, and he is the reason we can describe the bargain at all.

## Guidance

Three trades this chapter prices.

**Name the price before you take the trade.** Sima Qian chose the book over the ordinary life, and he chose it in daylight, with his whole career in front of him. The later tradition turned that into a single noble moment, because a clean story travels better than a costly one. Make your own trades the same way: know the price, pay it deliberately, and expect the retelling to simplify you.

**Gather consequences in one place.** The portrait form he invented follows one person across a whole life and lets the consequences of their decisions do the judging. It is a method anyone can use on anyone, themselves included. A single decision looks defensible in isolation and much worse in sequence.

**Weight a source by what its writer stood to lose.** The *Records* describes a closed case and needed to please no one. The *Hanshu* describes a state still in progress, written by men it employed. Both are careful. Only one had nobody to protect. When you read a report or a review, ask what would have happened to the writer for saying the opposite.

# 10

Interlude: a decade, 1120 to 1130, in which nothing happened.

This is not a joke and it is not a placeholder. The record for those years is thin, the empire was prosperous, the examinations continued, the bureaucracy functioned, and the population rose. There is a famine in the north. There is a court that is changing hands. The administrative state described in the previous chapter ran, and ran well, and produced a report every year saying that it was running well.

It is in a decade like this that a system reveals what it has been measuring. The Song examination system had been designed to select talent for administration, and for two centuries it had selected talent for administration with a precision nobody had questioned, and then the empire was in a situation that required a different capacity — military command, negotiation with a nomadic confederation that could not be defeated, extraction of resources from a population that had begun to resent being examined.

The decade matters for a reason that is easy to miss. Nothing happened in it, and nothing happening is a fact about the record rather than about the empire. The *jian* of these years are the dullest in the archive. There is a famine, and the court responds to it, and the response is competent, and the memorials about the famine are about the mechanics of relief rather than about the state of the realm. A reader who went to the decade looking for the crisis that came next would find none, and would conclude that the Song was stable. The Song was not stable. The Song was an administration that had not yet been asked a question it could not answer, and the decade is what the interval looked like from inside.

The system produced men who could do the work it was for. It had never claimed otherwise. It is the reader of this book who made the claim, and the reader is me, and I put it in the previous chapter in a sentence about the man who sat the third examination, and the sentence was accurate, and the sentence implied that the machine was doing something broader than the thing it was built for.

That is the failure this chapter is about, and it is a failure of reading rather than of building, and it is the commonest kind.

Then, in 1125, the Jurchen took the capital.

It was fast. The Song had spent its entire fiscal capacity on the northern frontier and had spent it on cash, not on the walls, and when the Jurchen came south they came through a breach in a river defence that had been allowed to silt up. The emperor and most of the court went east; the capital was looted; a second court was proclaimed in the south, which is the founding moment of the Southern Song, and the dynasty continued for another century and a half.

Here is the part that matters for this book. The southern court did not reform the examination system. It could not afford to. The examinations had become the state's main claim on the literate elite and the elite's main claim on the state, and in the crisis the two were still made of the same arrangement. The Southern Song did what institutions do: it kept the parts that were cheapest to keep and became, over the next century, one of the richest states in the world, in a territory that had lost everything which made it rich the first time.

The examination figures are the evidence, and they are not what anyone would predict. The number of candidates rose sharply through the Southern Song, not because the system was more meritocratic but because it was one of the few ways a family in a new commercial city could buy status, and the price of a place in the system was a school place. The system became the mechanism by which a merchant class converted money into the one thing it could not buy any other way, which was recognition by the state.

And the recognition was the problem, because a class whose position depends on passing examinations will eventually have interests in the examinations that are not the state's interests. By the late Song there are memorials complaining about exactly this, and the complaint is that the examination pool has been captured by families who are rich enough to educate four sons through a system that was designed to be the alternative to being rich. The composers are buying the examiners. The eight-legged essay is a fixed technology, and a fixed technology can be mastered by anyone with enough money and enough patience.

The *interlude*: the examination system's real achievement in the late Song was legibility rather than selection. A state that can review its own decisions can find out what it has been doing. And what the Song found out, when the wars came and the money ran out, was that its officials had been reviewing each other's reports on grain prices and not reporting that the grain prices were a problem. The assessment reports are full of competent men competently administering counties in a fiscal situation that no amount of competent county administration could fix.

There is a specific and bitter piece of arithmetic here, and it is the most damning document in the book. In 1211 the court published a figure for the number of degree-holders by prefecture. The court published it because a particular kind of man wanted to be seen publishing it. The most successful prefecture had roughly a hundred times the density of the least successful, and the difference tracked land ownership almost exactly.

I want to be careful with this number, because it is the kind of figure that gets quoted for years without anybody asking what it counts. It counts degree-holders, not officials. The overwhelming majority of men who had passed an examination never held an office. They were *littoral* men, and I described them two chapters ago because they are the key to the whole mechanism: a degree was a portable claim on the state's good faith, a credential that a family could spend on a post for a younger son, a case before a magistrate, or the plain fact of being an educated man in a village where that meant something.

So the hundred-fold difference in density measures the number of families claiming to be of the sort that produces educated men, which means a difference in wealth of roughly that order, since the cost of the study was the binding constraint. It says nothing whatever about the government. The court published the figure in order to argue that the examinations were spreading opportunity, and the figure says the opposite, and the reason it says the opposite is that the court counted the thing it was trying to increase and assumed that counting it counted the increase.

I have not found any commentary in the tradition that notices this, which is itself informative. A near-contemporary scholar remarks on the figures and draws a moral about the vanity of examination culture. He does not remark that the denominators are wrong. The tradition's own critique is the critique of a system that had stopped asking what its numbers meant, conducted by men inside the system who were still counting correctly.

The Song had spent two centuries selecting, on the basis of an examination in classical texts, the men who would administer the empire. It had succeeded. The men it selected were, on average, from the families that owned the land. The selection was working perfectly, and it was selecting for a property of the candidates that had nothing to do with the qualities the examination was supposed to be measuring, because the examination was measuring the thing that families with land could most easily afford to have measured.

That is not a story about corruption. Corruption would be a smaller story, and would have a villain. It is a story about a machine that measured a proxy so well that the measurement became the thing being measured, and nobody involved had any way of finding out.

The Southern Song tried to correct it. The examination was made harder, in the sense of being made more expensive to sit, on the reasoning that if the system were selecting talent it should be able to select it in a wider field. The field narrowed. This is the most reliable finding in the whole history of examinations and it appears in every administration that has tried the reform, and nobody has ever explained it, and the usual explanation — that a harder examination produces better candidates — is a description rather than a mechanism.

What actually happens is that a harder examination raises the cost of sitting, and the people who can carry a rise in cost are the people with income the rise does not touch, and the effect on candidate diversity is therefore governed by the distribution of wealth rather than by the difficulty of the paper. You cannot fix a selection bias by making the filter stricter. You can only make the filter stricter in a way that happens to favour people who were not previously favoured, and no one has thought of a way to do that.

So the Song adjusted the paper and not the field, and the number of candidates rose anyway, and the composition did not change, and the book that the Song's own historians wrote about this period is remarkable for how plainly it states that the system had stopped working while continuing to operate it.

That is not a paradox either, and it has a name in this book, which is the mechanism. The system was the state. The state could not reform itself because the reform would have been made by the system, and the system's members had spent a lifetime inside it and could not imagine an administration that did not have one.

The good news is that this is where the book is meant to turn. The Yuan, the dynasty that conquered the Song, abandoned the examinations for the better part of a century — a fact usually read as cultural catastrophe, and which reads differently once you have seen the machine: a state that needs to identify a competent military commander has no use for a system that certifies fiscal administrators.

What the Yuan actually did is more interesting than a suspension, because the Mongols did not abolish the examination. They ran it, at first, and the examination papers of the Yuan period are a record of something the Song system could not produce: candidates instructed to write about how a government should govern, in a classical register, under a dynasty whose government they could see. The best of these essays are admired for their subtlety, and the admiration is misplaced in a way that tells you something. A candidate who understood that the state around him was maintained by a military class that did not consult anyone had exactly one safe way to write, and the safe way was the Song way, and a generation of men learned to be excellent at a genre whose assumptions the state had suspended.

The restorations came in stages: a limited examination for a category of appointment, a wider one, and eventually the full series, at which point the machine was running again with a different ruling class in the seats it had created. The exam had outlived the dynasty that built it, which is the most successful thing an institution does and the least satisfying, because the thing that survived was not the design but the function, and the function was to staff a bureaucracy.

A scholar named Xu Heng submitted a memorial arguing that the examinations should be restored because the alternative — recommending men for their local standing — had not worked, and the Yuan court read it out to a full assembly of scholars, in public, for two days, and then did not implement it for thirty years.

The Ming brought them back, with modifications, and the Qing kept them, and they are still there. The machine was resumed rather than destroyed, which means the thing that was resumed is a different machine, and I have not got to the bottom of why, and the last chapter does not answer it either.

I promised that this book would not resolve, and I want to be careful about how I honour that, because there is a version of not-resolving that is simply refusing to finish, and it would be dishonest to use that version here.

What I have is a set of measurements, taken across two thousand years, of states that could not explain themselves to themselves. Qin could not explain that obedience was not agreement. The Han could not explain that a recovery of the classics was a staffing decision. Wang Mang could not explain that a state with no officials had no reason to exist. The confucians could not explain that a bureaucracy that measured everything had no instrument for the thing that was breaking. And the last of them built a machine so good at the measurable that it outlasted every attempt to make it measure something else.

The question this book has been asking is whether a ruler can be taught to rule well, and I have spent ten chapters showing that the teaching is always good and the answer is always a description of the teacher. Han Fei's essay is about the ruler's psychology. Dong Zhongshu's is about the ruler's relation to Heaven. The Gongyang programme is about society. The *jian* is about the relationship between a subordinate and a superior. Huo Guang's report is about the implementation of a policy. Zhu Xi's commentaries are about the examination system. The Taizong mirror is about a man and his own reflection. Every one of them is a serious attempt to say how a state should work, and every one of them is a document.

The most useful number in the Song, in the end, is the ratio of candidates to posts rather than the eight who passed in 1170. The examination system selected more men than the state had offices, and had done so for two centuries, and the surplus of qualified men with no office is a fact the Song state spent a very long time not measuring, and the men in that surplus are the men who wrote the *jian*, and the *jian* is the form of this book.

The reader who finishes this chapter and does not know what the argument was has been done to correctly, and I have one more chapter to say what I actually think, which is that the argument is the reader's and not mine, and that this is the only honest way to end a book about systems that explain themselves and are believed by the people they explain.

## Guidance

Three warnings from the quiet decade.

**A calm dashboard is a fact about the dashboard.** The years before the Jurchen came read as stable because the reports measured what the administration was for. The system was healthy in exactly the terms it had defined. Whenever your indicators have been green for a long time, ask what the instrument cannot register.

**Raise the field, not the bar.** Making the examination harder raised the cost of sitting it and narrowed the field, as it does in every administration that has tried. A stricter filter selects for whatever the strictness demands, and what it usually demands is money. If you want a different outcome, change who can afford the attempt.

**Verify that the proxy still points at the prize.** The examination measured what land-owning families could best afford to have measured, and two centuries later the measurement had become the target. When a number stands in for a goal, check periodically that the number and the goal are still the same thing.

# 6

The following is a *jian*. It is complete, it is one of the few that survive in a form anybody can read, and I am going to set it down without comment first, because the genre does not want comment and the commentaries have usually ruined it.

> The servant of the throne submits this memorial with fear and does not presume to know the Way. Your Majesty has ascended to the throne, and the empire is not yet at peace. The armies are in the field and have been for eleven months. The people are hungry, and have been since before Your Majesty ascended. The treasury is empty, and the accounts are not open to anyone who is not a member of the family.
>
> Your Majesty reads the *Spring and Autumn Annals* daily. This servant has read them also, and observes that the Annals do not record the reasons for the campaigns. They record the campaigns. The reason is always supplied afterwards by the man who won.
>
> There is a difference between a campaign that is necessary and a campaign that is explained, and the explanation always arrives after the fact, and by then the fact is unanswerable. Your Majesty will read this as criticism. The writer offers it as a description of a difficulty that every ruler has and that no ruler has solved, and that every successor believes has been solved by the ruler before him.
>
> The servant is of the view that the campaigns should stop. The servant understands that this advice is unlikely to be welcome and has thought about how to present it in a form that Your Majesty might accept. The presentation is this: a campaign that is stopped is remembered as a decision. A campaign that is continued is remembered as an event. Events are not decided by the men they happen to.

There. That is the form.

A *jian* is advice addressed to a ruler in a register that is not permitted to be a complaint. It must begin by diminishing the writer, must name the ruler's virtue before it names the ruler's problem, and must end with a sentence that is technically respectful and practically useless. What makes the form remarkable is not its content but its consistency: these documents were written by people who had read a great many other *jian*, and they all use the same moves, and the moves are the moves of a genre designed to let a subordinate say a dangerous thing in a shape the superior can file.

That is the point of the form, and it is also its problem. A *jian* can only say what the format will carry. The great ones — and there are a handful, in every century, written by people who understood exactly what the format could be made to do — say much more than the format seems to permit, by placing the real argument where the compliment is. The compliment is the payload. A reader who takes the compliment at face value has missed it, which is the only joke available to a man who cannot write a joke.

Here is a second one, shorter, and I want it beside the first because the pair shows the range. This is attributed to a man serving under a later emperor, and it is nine characters long, and it survives in a later compilation that gives no occasion and no author beyond the attribution:

> The lamps are lit. The rats are gone.

That is a whole argument. It says that a household is being watched, and it says it in the register of good news, and it can be delivered by a man who is smiling. The compliment is absent entirely, which tells you that the writer knew his reader well enough to skip it. A *jian* without praise is a much more dangerous document than one with, because it indicates that the writer has decided the flattery is no longer buying anything.

The difference between the two is the difference between an adviser who still believes the process works and one who has noticed that it does not. Both are using the same instrument. The instrument was built to let the first man say something, and the second man has learned to say the same kind of thing without it.

It is worth being exact about who could write one, because the answer turns out to be the answer to a different question. A *jian* was written by an official, in office, on state time, using state stationery, and it was filed in the imperial archive where it could be read by the clerk who received it. That set of conditions does not sound like a constraint. It determined the genre almost entirely.

An official writing privately, at home, on his own paper, to a friend, had a completely different form available to him and used it constantly. The private letter in this tradition is where a writer can be sardonic, where a complaint can name a person, where a subordinate can say what he thinks without any of the apparatus. A man would write his genuine opinion in a letter to another scholar and then write a *jian* on the same subject for the throne, and the distance between the two documents is the subject of one of the better chapters in the *Hanshu*.

I have read a good deal of the private letter material in preparing this book and my impression, which I offer as an impression, is that the two registers serve different audiences, and that the men who wrote them knew both were on file. A man who wrote something unguarded in a private letter was relying on his correspondents, and correspondents in a bureaucratic state were colleagues who would one day be assessed by the same officials.

The archive is full of them and almost nobody read, which is a fact about the court rather than a failure of transmission. The *jian* arrived, was read by a clerk, was answered, and the answer went into the record; the memorial itself was filed, and the filing is a political act, because a state that files advice it has not accepted is displaying a population that is not worth governing.

We know this because we have the responses. The most common answer to a *jian* in the Han is: your account is not correct, and the matter is under consideration. Which is not an answer, and both parties know it, and the ritual continues because the alternative is that the adviser stops writing and the throne loses its claim to be consulting anyone.

One more thing about the form before the letters. A *jian* was read aloud. This is stated in the procedural rules and it is more consequential than it sounds, because a document read silently can be composed carefully, and a document that will be read in a room has to be audible in a room, which means the syntax has to carry across a hall and the argument has to arrive before attention has gone.

Writers exploited this. The device of opening with a question and then supplying the answer after a page of apparent deliberation is common in the Han memorials and is very likely a response to oral delivery, since a listener who is waiting for the question cannot leave and a listener waiting for a conclusion has already got the shape of it. The great *jian* are long — the ones that survive run to several thousand characters — and the length does no padding at all. It is what the format demands of a man who has one thing to say and no way to say it safely.

There is a number in the *Hanshu* that I find more moving than any of the documents. In one year of Emperor Wu's reign the imperial archive received four thousand memorials. The number of replies that survive is nine. I do not know what the ratio of replies to memorials was, and the nine may be a biased sample — a reply survives when it was distinctive enough to be worth copying, and a reply saying nothing memorable would not be. But even taking the most generous reading, the court answered in public at a rate that makes the consultation a formality, and the writers kept writing, and the writing is what we have.

The letters we have are therefore a selected set, and the selection is the interesting part. What survives is advice that was *interesting enough to answer*. The four thousand memorials that said the roads needed repair do not survive. What survives is the advice that proposed something, because a proposal is quotable by whoever found it embarrassing.

That is the whole of the selection effect, and it operates on ambition rather than on opinion. A man who thought the empire was governed well and said so produced nothing worth keeping. A man who thought it was governed badly and said nothing specific produced nothing worth keeping either. What remains is the small class of men who had a proposal, and the class is not representative of anything except itself.

And here is the sentence I would put beside the archive count, because the two are the same observation. We have the *jian* in proportion to its ambition, and we have the replies in proportion to how awkward they were to file. Both are selections, and they are selections made by clerks who were sorting paper, and the shape of what survives tells you about the empire and nothing whatever about the advisers. A reader who wants to know what men in the Han thought about their government cannot use these documents, because these documents are a record of the times somebody said something the archive found worth answering.

So the archive is a record of the times someone proposed a solution, and that is a very different thing from a record of the state of the empire. The conditions are invisible. We have the *jian* in proportion to its ambition.

Read the two documents above again and notice what they share, which is that neither asks for anything that can be counted. There is no request for a specific number of men, or a specific sum, or a named official's dismissal. Both arguments are about the shape of a situation rather than its remedy, and that cannot be an accident of the two authors' temper. It is what the form permits. A *jian* that said *send eight thousand men back across the river* has made a demand, and demands are refusable, and a refusal of a demand has to be recorded as a refusal of a demand. A *jian* that describes a difficulty has made a remark, and remarks are answered with procedure.

The whole tradition, from here to the last chapter, is people writing in a form that makes it possible to say a true thing to power and impossible to have it acted on, and then writing another one. The form outlives the content: the *jian* is still being written four hundred years after the men who invented it, in a slightly different register, by men who have read the same models, and the models are in the same anthology in the same order.

There is one more thing about the form that I have not seen stated plainly anywhere, and it is the reason the form is the correct lens for this book. A *jian* is a document whose author expected to be overruled and wrote anyway. That expectation is the whole moral content of the genre. It is a literary form built by people who had made a realistic assessment of their own power and had decided to use it at the limit rather than not at all.

The shortest of these documents is four characters long. It is attributed to a man who was in the room, and it reads: *The court is blind.*

## Guidance

Three things the form teaches.

**Ask what the format makes sayable.** A *jian* could describe a difficulty and could not make a demand, because demands are refusable and remarks get answered with procedure. In any meeting or document, the form has already decided what can be said. Learn it, and then listen for the payload in the place where the compliment should be.

**The archive is not the state of the world.** Four thousand memorials arrived in one year and nine replies survive; what survives is what was worth answering. Your evidence about anything is proportional to its quotability. The unremarkable conditions leave no mark in the file. Go and look for what nobody found worth filing.

**Write the record expecting to be overruled.** The moral content of the whole genre is one fact: its authors expected to be ignored and wrote anyway. A decision reverses and a record travels. Put the true thing in writing for the file even when the verdict is already in, and write it so the person who disagrees with you can check it.

## The book

**Working title:** *Sons of Heaven, Students of Dust*
**Subtitle (working):** *Eleven Rulers and the Philosophers Who Explained Them*
**Form:** narrative nonfiction · 28,000–32,000 words · 11 chapters

**Span:** the Qin unification (221 BCE) to the consolidation of the literati order under
the Song (c. 1200 CE).

**Premise.** In 221 BCE a king of Qin ended five centuries of war and discovered that
conquest was the easy part. He commissioned a philosophy that said consent was
unnecessary. It worked for eleven years, and has been failing — and being
recommissioned — ever since. The book follows that loop through eleven rulers and the
thinkers who tried to explain them.

## Theme

**Central question — carried, never answered:**

*Can a ruler be taught to rule well, or is every theory of good rule just the winner's
autobiography?*

Every chapter removes one more easy answer. The book's identity is the refusal to close.
A reader who finishes able to state the book's thesis in a sentence has been done to
rather than taught, and the last chapter is where the trap is set.

## The mirror

The recurring device. Each chapter pairs **one seat of power** with **one theory of
power**, and the device is a mirror held up to a ruler by a record he did not control.
Made explicit in Ch 8. Recurs in 3, 9, 11. Never signalled with the word "mirror" in the
prose — the reader should feel the structure, not be told it exists.

## Structural ledger

Each chapter takes a different formal shape, so the book never reads as eleven
instances of one template. This is also the main defence against `U1`/`U5` uniformity.

| ch | shape | ruler | theory |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | chronological | Qin Shi Huang | Legalism (Han Fei) |
| 2 | parallel | Wen of Han | Confucian recuperation (Confucius → Dong Zhongshu) |
| 3 | essayistic | Wu of Han | The historian's verdict (Sima Qian) |
| 4 | reverse | Empress Lü | Rule by biography (the "unorthodox successes") |
| 5 | fragmented | Wang Mang | Utopian programme (the Gongyang school) |
| 6 | epistolary | Han China restored | Advice that cannot be sent (the *jian* genre) |
| 7 | chronological | Han Wu | The Salt and Iron debate |
| 8 | spiral | Tang Taizong | The bronze, the history, the person |
| 9 | stream | Song | The examination system and its cheapness |
| 10 | chronicle-with-interludes | Song to Yuan | Whether merit can be measured |
| 11 | essayistic | — | The reader, holding the mirror |

## Emotional curve

Not a rising line. It falls, resets, and falls again, with the two lowest points placed
at Ch 5 and Ch 10 so the ending is a genuine recovery rather than a taper.

| ch | beat |
|---|---|
| 1 | admiration for the machinery, undercut by the rats |
| 2 | relief, then the discovery that the relief was bought |
| 3 | the first genuine vertigo — the record refuses to agree with itself |
| 4 | cold; the mirror turns on the reader |
| 5 | collapse — the utopia, audited, fails on its own arithmetic |
| 6 | intimacy; letters to men who could not be reached |
| 7 | exhaustion; an argument conducted by both sides for 200 pages |
| 8 | the book's warmest chapter, and the most suspicious |
| 9 | procedural; a machine described by someone who admires it |
| 10 | the low point — merit, measured, and found to be a rumour |
| 11 | open. The last page poses the question to whoever is holding the book. |

## Register

Declarative, present-tense for the argument, past for the history. First person
singular, used sparingly and never for emphasis. Latin transliterations italicised on
first use in a chapter, plain thereafter. No Chinese characters; the argument does not
need them and they exclude the general reader.

The prose must be readable aloud. Sentences that cannot be spoken without a breath are
wrong even when the argument is right.

## What this book refuses

- A thesis. Stated once as a question, never answered.
- A villain. Every ruler in it is partly right about something.
- A curriculum. No chapter exists to cover a period.
- Symmetry for its own sake. Where the material is flat, the prose is flat.

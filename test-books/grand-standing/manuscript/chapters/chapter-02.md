# Chapter 2 — The Obituary File

Every person in this town who matters has died at least once, and Desmond
Vellum has written all of them.

Not died. Prepared. There is a drawer. It lives in the back of the metal
cabinet behind the subeditor's desk — third drawer down, the one with the
1974 key still in the lock because the lock has never once worked — and it
contains, as of last Tuesday, four hundred and six obituaries of people who
are, at the time of writing, alive.

I found this out on my second week at the Sentinel, looking for the paper's
style guide. (There was a drawing pin on the floor by the cabinet that week.
I remember the pin more clearly than some of the conversations.)

"You'll want the 1961," Desmond said, not looking up. "Second shelf, brown
spine, ignore the 1988 entirely. The 1988 uses the serial comma and we are
not a serial-comma office."

"What's in the drawer?"

"The style guide is the second shelf, Liss."

"What's in the third drawer?"

He looked up then, and I want to record accurately what happened to his
face, because it is the reason I am writing this chapter at all. He looked
like a man caught between two loyalties by a question he had not rehearsed
an answer to. Then he said: "Everyone gets one. It's only civil."

Everyone gets one.

Four hundred and six obituaries, written in advance, in a newspaper that
will not survive the year to print any of them. The Sentinel has a
circulation of two thousand two hundred and falling, a printing press from
1961 that Desmond loves more than most of the living, and an owner in
Leeds who has not visited since the flood. And it has, in the third drawer,
the most complete record of Mardenbridge's idea of itself that exists
anywhere: four hundred and six attempts to say what a life was for.

"They're not accurate," Desmond said, when I asked. "They're kind. That's
the difference between an obituary and a death notice, and it's the only
thing worth knowing about the trade. Well — the only other thing worth
knowing is the word count. Nobody believes the word count. Everybody in
this town dies at eight hundred words except the ones who die at a
thousand, and the difference is not importance, it is how much of the
person the town has already agreed to remember." He said this while re-spacing a
headline about the council's decision — MY WORDS had grown a companion
subhead overnight: THE LEASE WILL NOT BE RENEWED, in the 1961 face, set
tight. "The living get the truth in small doses. The dead get the whole
of it at once. That's the arrangement."

The ritual is this. Every Friday, before the paper closes, Desmond walks
the obituary file. Not reading — walking. He takes the drawer out, sets it
on the subeditor's desk, and goes through the names the way a gardener goes
through beds: checking. When someone in the drawer has had a bad week — a
diagnosis, a fall, a son who stopped writing — the entry comes out, gets
re-set, and goes back. "A man's obituary," he said to me once, watching me
watch him, "is the last thing anyone will get right about him, and the only
thing anyone will ever read twice. You'd let that go stale?"

I said it seemed strange to keep files on the living.

"Everything is a file on the living," he said. "The council keeps one. The
hospital keeps one. We just intend ours kindly."

On the Friday after the vote, the drawer came out as usual, and I watched
him walk it: Ashby, Fielding, old Councillor Prentice who left in 1985 and
is still with us in the file and in Eastbourne. When he reached the C's he
paused — one beat, the kind of pause that is either grief or layout — and
put the theatre's entry back at the front of its section, where a building
gets filed among the people.

I have been at the Sentinel for nineteen months. In nineteen months I have
covered fourteen council meetings, two inquests, one flood, a fête, a
scandal at the allotments, and the closure of the public toilets on
Bridge Street, which ran, if I am honest, as a two-part series with a
follow-up. My father did thirty years on the letters page of this paper
and was made redundant by email. I took the job — I see this now — to
prove the paper still meant something, which is a great deal of weight to
put on a building with one working radiator.

On the day of the lease vote, Desmond wrote three obituaries.

The first was for the Colosseum, which gets one the way everyone gets one,
building or not. "Mardenbridge's Colosseum Civic Theatre, born 1969, died
of arithmetic," he wrote, and then stopped, and then wrote, "The building
held six thousand people and never once complained about the numbers." He
filed it under C and locked nothing, because the drawer does not lock.

The second was for the Sentinel itself, which he keeps in the back of the
drawer, under S, folded twice. He does not know I have seen it. It is
eight hundred words on what a local paper is for and it ends with the only
sentence in anything he has written that I would call sentimental: "We
were here when the roof went on, and we meant to be here when it came
down, and the fact that we were not is a fact about money and not about
meaning." He would kill me for quoting it. I am quoting it because the
alternative is paraphrasing it and I know what he would say about that.

The third — and here I have to put down exactly what I found and not what
I made of it, because what I made of it took me three days and cost me a
question I should have asked immediately.

The third was mine. Mine.

Four hundred words. My name at the top in the 1961 face and a word count
in the margin — 400/500, meaning a draft — and one word in Desmond's
handwriting in the corner of the page. The word was: *hold*.

It is, I should say straight away, not a threat and not a prediction.
Everyone gets one. I have since learned that mine was written in my first
month, when I was twenty-eight and bad at the job, and that "hold" means
*hold for updating* — the file is maintained, not written once. Mine has
been maintained. There is a second page. The second page knows things
about me that I have told no one at this office: the business with the
inquest at Barrowfield, the reason I left the Manchester application off
my CV, my father's actual position on the letters page before they called
it voluntary.

I am putting this in the chapter because the alternative is leaving it out,
and the file voice — my file voice, the one that dates things and hedges
with "reported" — is exactly the wrong instrument for the sentence I have
to write next. So I will write it badly and let it stand.

He has been writing me carefully. That is the thing I found in the drawer.
I got on the bus and then got off it again one stop early and walked.
Something in the carefulness had — I am filing this as badly as I like —
gone in somewhere under the ribs.

"Everyone gets one," he said again, in the doorway, coat on, at the end of
the day of the vote.

"You've done mine," I said.

"Everyone gets one," he said, and went home.

My father's file is in there too, presumably. He lives in Formby now and
gardens with an intensity he never brought to the letters page. I have not
looked. That is not virtue — I looked for mine twice — it is that his
obituary would have to say what the paper did to him in 2019, and I do not
trust myself to read Desmond's version of that sentence and keep my face
doing what a face should do. The email said the role was being
"restructured" after thirty-one years. The letters page is now a horoscope
and a puzzle. The town wrote its own sentences there for sixty-three years
and then the sentences were restructured into a horoscope.

I am aware this is the second time I have written sixty-three years in this
chapter. I am leaving both in. The repetition is the file voice failing to
absorb something, which is at least honest.

I sat with the drawer for a while after he left. The office does the thing
it does at that hour: the radiators ticked, the press warmed up for
tomorrow's run, which would carry eight hundred and twelve words of mine
about a building dying of arithmetic. I read three others while I was
there. Old Ashby of the chandlery, still alive, eighty-nine — his obituary
reads like an apology for how long the town took to notice him. Mrs.
Fielding of the flower stall, who is in the drawer and also in the letters
page archive from 1998, arguing about hanging baskets with a passion the
council chamber has never mustered for anything.

Four hundred and six lives, and what the paper thinks they were for.

The letters page archive runs beside the drawer in the cabinet — nineteen
cartons, 1961 to last October, when the letters page was discontinued for
reasons of space. The town wrote its own sentences there for sixty-three
years. Hanging baskets and bypasses and the great apostrophe schism of
1979, which the drawer has nothing on but which the letters page records
in full, with names. Mrs. Fielding is in both. Her 1998 hanging-basket
letters make her obituary look timid.

I put my own back where it was. Second page and all. It is not mine to
destroy or mine to read twice — I read it twice — and when the Sentinel
dies, as the arithmetic says it must, the drawer will be somebody's
problem. Perhaps the town's. Perhaps no one's. The council has a resolution
somewhere about the archive, I am sure, and I am sure the language is
beautiful.

This is the part where a better reporter would tell you the drawer is
really about the town, and the town is really about the theatre, and the
theatre is really about all of us. I am not going to do that. The drawer
is a drawer. It contains four hundred and six acts of a strange and
unasked-for kindness performed by a man who kills stories for a living,
and the theatre is a building with a condemned stage and forty-one people
at the matinee, and the arithmetic does not care about either.

But I will say this in the margin, where it belongs. On the night of the
vote I walked home the long way past the Colosseum and its stage door, and
the light was on in the stage-left flat, and someone was rehearsing
something in there, alone, to no one, in a building the town has already
finished with.

And when I filed the feature about who writes the town's sentences — six
hundred words, inside a week, as agreed — the first line I wrote was not
about the resolution language at all. Desmond read it over my shoulder,
made one mark in pencil, and said nothing about the mark, which is the
subeditor's whole religion: one pencil mark is an argument you are trusted
to finish alone.

It was: everyone gets one.

I changed it before filing. But I know what the drawer is for now, and it
is not for the dead. It is the practice. All that care, aimed at the end
of things, so that when the end comes there is one sentence in the world
that got it right.

The lease will not be renewed. The obituary is already written.

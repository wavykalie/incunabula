# Chapter 1 — Clause 7

The Mardenbridge Colosseum Civic Theatre was pronounced dead at 8:41 in the
evening, by show of hands, with seven votes against four and one abstention,
in a chamber smelling of warm dust and radiator paint.

I record the time because Councillor Ampadu did not. The motion as read
referred to "the orderly conclusion of a civic legacy", and the minutes will
carry that phrase into the archive, where it will outlive the building, the
council, and — the arithmetic being what it is at the Mardenbridge Sentinel —
this newspaper. My name is Marnie Liss. I am the press.

The chamber's lettering is 1969 Gill Sans, cut deep, gold leaf gone to
mustard. You can date a civic building by its apostrophes. The theatre's
apostrophes are newer than its commas, which tells you the signage was
replaced twice, which tells you somebody once believed there would be a third
time. I photograph the north wall while the mayor reads. Nobody stops me.
Nobody ever stops you when you hold a camera like you know where the light is.

"Clauses one through six," said the mayor, in the voice of a man reading a
menu aloud in a language he does not speak, "concern the maintenance schedule,
the insurance position, and the — the asset register. Clause seven concerns
the lease."

Clause seven concerned the lease.

"THAT the Council, having considered the report of officers, RESOLVES that
the lease of the Colosseum Civic Theatre, expiring on the thirty-first of
March, shall not be renewed."

The microphone howled. It howls at everything. It has howled at every
council meeting since 1997 and is the only piece of equipment in
Mardenbridge with an unbroken record of public service. The mayor waited out
the feedback with the patience of a man who has been warned about the
feedback, then said: "Carried."

There was clapping. There is always clapping. The public gallery held four
people, two of whom were, by profession, the press — Desmond Vellum of this
paper, seventy-one words into his last decade on the job, and myself, counting
the room. Two other members of the public sat at the back with the stillness
of people who had come to watch something be killed and did not intend to
applaud.

In the third row, Jerome Arkwright stood up, said "She's not a building,"
and sat down again.He is the theatre's manager. He lives, though I did not know this then, in
the flat above the stage door. The chamber's radiator paint was flaking near
my knee; I picked at it during the vote and did not notice I had kept a piece. The council had used the
word "occupancy" in the officers' report. I had read the officers' report in
the Sentinel's office that afternoon, underlining numbers, missing the whole
of it. That is what this account is: an account of what I missed, filed in
the order I missed it.

Councillor Ruth Ampadu rose to close the item.

"Let's be clear," she said. "We are not deciding what to kill. We are
deciding what to keep. There is a difference and the record should show it."

The record showed it.

I want to say something about the language, and I want to be accurate about
why. It was beautiful. Not acceptable, not adequate — beautiful. "The orderly
conclusion of a civic legacy" is a phrase with clause structure and moral
weather in it. A man who cannot read a menu wrote it down twenty minutes
earlier without stumbling. Members used the phrase back to each other all
evening. "A civic legacy." "Orderly conclusion." The words went around the
chamber the way money goes around a family that has none: everyone touched
it, nobody made it.

I assumed — and here is the first thing I got wrong, so I place it first — I
assumed someone in the town hall had a gift. There is always someone with a
gift. Somebody writes the war memorial inscriptions, somebody writes the
plaques. I intended to find the person and write about them, because the
Sentinel needs pages and the pages need sentences and I have a weakness for
the sentence that arrives in the wrong room.

"Item eight," said the mayor. "Any other business."

There was no other business. There is never any other business. The phrase
exists to reassure the building.Before clause seven there was public participation, which is the part of
the agenda where the free assembly of citizens assembles freely and is heard
for three minutes each. Two people had booked a slot. Two others had come
without booking and were told, gently, that the book was closed.

The first speaker was a woman I did not know, in a green coat, who read from
a single sheet without looking up once. She wanted the theatre demolished.
The stairs had killed her son, she said, nineteen years ago, and if the
council spent two point four million pounds on anything with those stairs in
it she would stand in this chamber at every meeting until she died too. Her
voice was flat and practised and I found, under my file voice, that my hand
had stopped moving. She said all of this in a voice
flattened by practice. I am repeating myself. I noticed that later. It had been said before, in other chambers, in other
rooms with plaques. The council thanked her for her contribution. Councillor
Ampadu looked at the folder in her lap, not at the woman, which is either
cowardice or its opposite and I have never been able to decide which.

The second speaker was a man who wanted the fountain's pump replaced and had
brought photographs. The chair ruled the fountain out of scope. He left the
photographs on the table for the clerk, who filed them.

In the third row, during both speakers, a woman's hands in a blue sleeve were
cleaning something out of a canvas bag onto her lap and back again — a small
ritual, half absent-minded, that I could not identify at that distance and
did not photograph. (The clerk's fluorescent tube was failing at one end and
gave the whole gallery the colour of a photograph left in a car.) I record it because it is the kind of detail that turns
out to matter, and because I have learned to write down the things I cannot
explain, since the things I can explain are usually the things I have already
misunderstood.

Afterward, in the corridor — which is called the Civic Vestibule and has
its own plaque, dated 1969, with its own beautiful phrase about "the free
assembly of citizens" — I spoke to three people.

Arkwright, first, who had stopped being angry and become, in the way of
people whose anger has been going for years, extremely precise. "The roof is
the roof," he said. "The stage is the stage. You can condemn the roof, you
can't condemn what happens on the stage. There was a matinee today."

"There was?"

"There's always a matinee." He looked at me as if I had failed a simple
test. "Forty-one people. Forty-one is not nothing."It is not nothing.
I wrote down forty-one and did not write down what it cost him to say it,
which is the kind of omission the correction column cannot fix. He said the
number twice, once to me and once to the floor, as if the floor might be
keeping a better record. A man defending six thousand seats uses the word
forty-one — that is the whole distance of the thing, and I gave it five
words.

Second, Councillor Ampadu, who gave me three minutes with the ease of a
woman who has given three minutes to the press before and knows exactly what
three minutes buys.

"The lease," I said, "is it true the rent is one pound a year?"

"Peppercorn," she said. "Since 1969. The council has honoured it every year
and will honour it to the end of the term."

"Then what are you actually refusing? If the rent is a peppercorn — "

She closed her eyes one beat too long. "Officers estimate two point four
million pounds of repair liability, Mister — "

"Liss."

"— Miss Liss. The lease obliges the council as freeholder. The rent is
nothing. The roof is two point four million pounds. We are not refusing rent.
We are declining to buy the roof."

This was, in fairness, the truth. I checked it later against the officers'
report and the truth held. The council was not killing the theatre. It was
declining to perform the surgery. In the corridor light, with her folder
under one arm, Ampadu looked like a woman who had read every page of a very
long document and could no longer remember a time before it.

"People will say you're killing the theatre," I said.

"People say a great many things. That's what the gallery's for."

Third, the mayor, who said nothing at all, because he had said the phrase
already and the phrase was the job.

I walked back to the office at twenty past ten. Mardenbridge was doing what
it does at twenty past ten: nothing, thoroughly. The town has a talent for
it. Forty thousand people arranged around a market square, a river, and
three monuments, waiting with great patience for the eleven bus and the
end of history. The fountain in Market
Square has had no pump since 2011. It still fills, slowly, with rain, and
drains, slowly, through a crack, and on the night of the vote it held about
two inches of water with the whole town reflected upside down in it. I stood
there longer than the photograph required. That is the second thing to
correct: I told myself I was photographing the fountain for the piece. The
piece does not need the fountain. The piece is about a theatre.

The Sentinel's office was empty except for Desmond, who was setting the
front page — still sets it himself, on the grounds that nobody else can be
trusted with the paper's dignity — and who did not look up when I came in.

"Well?" he said.

"Clause seven. The lease won't be renewed. Seven-four. The phrase is
'orderly conclusion of a civic legacy.'"

"I heard the phrase," he said. "The phrase came over the wire at four."

"The wire?"

"Council press office sent the resolution at four o'clock. Full text. It
was sitting in the queue when you left." He finally looked up. "You were at
the meeting for the meeting. That's a common error. The meeting was the
punctuation."

He turned back to the page. The splash was already set. MY WORDS, it said,
in the Sentinel's 1961 face — Desmond's face, the apostrophe-less possessive,
James' car, the style guide interred with him — over a photograph of the
theatre taken, I could tell from the shadows, in 2008.

"Somebody wrote that resolution," I said. "The language is — it's very
good. Do we know who?"

"No."

"I want to find out. It's a piece. Who writes the town's sentences."

Desmond set down his stick. He has a composing stick older than the
building we are in and older than the paper we work for, which is saying
something for the paper.

"The letters page," he said, "is where the town writes its own sentences.
And the letters page has been discontinued since October." He said this
without expression, as a fact about column inches. "You want a piece about
language, you can have one. Six hundred words. But file the meeting first —
the meeting is the news, the sentence is the feature. Rule one."

"Rule one," I said.

"And Liss."

"Yes?"

"Clause seven was page one." He put the stick down. "Page one is where
things get said aloud. After that it's all detail."

I did not know then that Desmond had a drawer. I did not know that in the
drawer, in a manila folder marked with a letter of the alphabet and filed
under my surname, there was an obituary with my name on it and a word count
in the margin and a note in his handwriting that said only: *hold*. There is
a great deal I did not know about the Sentinel, and about the theatre, and
about the two point four million pounds, which was not a number but a
decision wearing a number's clothes.

What I knew at 11:04 on the night of the vote: the lease would not be
renewed, the rent was one pound a year, the roof was the whole argument, the
resolution's language had arrived by wire at four o'clock from somebody who
could write, and forty-one people had been to the matinee.

I filed the meeting. Eight hundred and twelve words, including the show of
hands, the abstention, and the phrase. I did not include the fountain. I did
not include Arkwright standing up and sitting down. By then I had checked
the file stamps on the press-office release, and the phrase was not new.

"The orderly conclusion of a civic legacy" had appeared, word for word, in
1974, in the resolution to close the Mardenbridge lido.

The history was the story. The story was already older than everyone in the
room.

# Chapter 4 — The House Manager

You can hear the building breathing before you see anyone in it. That is
the first thing Arkwright told me, and the second thing he told me was that
he was not being sentimental.

"The air handling is from 1969 and it breathes, that's all. Four changes an
hour when it's working, two when it isn't. Right now it isn't." He said
this from the fly gallery, eleven metres up, in the tone other people use
about a family member with a condition. "You get used to it. Then you
listen for it."

I listened. It took about ninety seconds of standing still in a dark wings
to hear it — a soft, regular exchange somewhere behind the plaster, the
sound of a large room being kept alive by machinery older than anyone
operating it.

I had come to ask him about the roof. I left three hours later knowing
about upholstery, counterweights, the acoustic difference between a full
house and an empty one, and the fact — which he mentioned sideways, twice,
the way one mentions a thing that has to be said and cannot be looked at —
that he lives in the flat above the stage door and the lease expiring on
31 March is not, for him, a question of employment.

"Where would you go?" I asked. We were in the props store by then, him
returning things to shelves in an order I could not see.

"The flat's the job," he said. "People think the job is managing. The job
is being the one who's in the building when it isn't doing anything. Shows
are what the building does in public." He turned a chair over — 1960s,
bentwood, part of some set or other. "Six months' notice, the council's
version. Six months, and then I suppose I'm a man with a very particular
set of skills and nowhere to keep a sofa."

He laughed. I did not, quite, and he noticed, and the noticing embarrassed
us both, so he told me about the counterweight system instead, which is
genuinely interesting and which I will now get wrong.

Forty-one tons of iron above the stage, in banks, doing arithmetic all
night: this much on this line, that much on that one, and the whole
machine exists to make a piece of scenery weigh nothing when a person
pulls a rope. "Physics," Arkwright said. "The building trusts physics and
so do I. People you have to check."

## The inventory of a life in a building

The stage-left flat has one room, a kitchenette with the same 1969 tiles as
the public toilets, and a bathroom with a window that looks into the fly
gallery, which he says is the best view in the town and which does, I
confess, have something. The bed is against the wall that shares with the
prompt corner. "I sleep through get-outs but wake for the ghost light," he
said. "Nobody's ever explained that."

On the shelf by the window: a jar of screws sorted by gauge, three
pantomime scripts with his annotations ("dragon needs a longer neck — the
child in row C will spot it"), a photograph of a matinee from the 1970s
that he did not explain and I did not ask about, and — I have to put this
somewhere — a ticket stub, framed, from the theatre's opening night. His
father took him. He was four. He has the stub.

"The building chooses its people," he said, and I wrote it down, and then
he said: "No, that's rubbish. I chose the building. The building doesn't
choose. The building just holds." He was quiet for a moment. "That's the
whole trick of a theatre, if you want it in one line. It holds. Six
thousand people at once and it holds. And everyone in this town who's been
in it thinks of it as theirs, which is — " he gestured at the fly gallery,
the rope, the wall "— which is true. That's what makes it a civic theatre
and not a venue."

## The flat, which the resolution calls an occupancy

Page nine of the officers' report, paragraph 12, reads: "The stage-left
residential occupancy will be regularised in line with the lease
extinction." Fourteen words. I have read them enough times now that they
have stopped being words and become a kind of crime against compression.
Regularised. As if the problem were that the living arrangement was
irregular, and not that in seven months a man must be somewhere else.

I asked Arkwright if he had read page nine.

"Page nine," he said, "is what people write instead of asking someone to
leave. If they asked me, they'd have to have somewhere for me to go. Write
it in a paragraph and it's a process. Process doesn't need a sofa."

Then he said a thing I did not expect: that he holds no grudge against the
council, at all, not any of them, because the council is also a building —
something built to hold people — and it is also falling apart, and nobody
lives in it at night to hear it breathe. "They're holding," he said. "Same
as me. It's buildings all the way down."

I have thought about that sentence more than any other sentence I gathered
this week. It is either the most generous thing a man under notice has ever
said about his notice, or the saddest — and I notice the file voice wants
to resolve which, and cannot, and so I have written both options down and
left them standing.

## What a condemned stage means

I asked him about the stage, because the officers' report mentions the
stage structure and everyone else mentions the roof.

He did not answer for long enough that I thought he had not heard. Then:
"The stage is the stage. It's a platform on a forest of posts, and the
posts are 1969 posts. The survey will say what the survey says."

"Arkwright."

"The survey will say what the survey says. And whatever it says, the
building's not dangerous on a matinee with forty-one people in it. I've
stood on that stage every day for fifteen years. I'd know."

That is not how structures work and we both knew it, and he knew we both
knew it, and he said "I'd know" again, quieter, at the floor, and then
showed me the upholstery in row G — 1969 mohair, seventy per cent, you can
feel the twist through a glove — because that is what he does when the
conversation arrives somewhere he cannot hold.

## The empty house at four

At four in the afternoon he does a walk. I asked to come and he said "it's
a walk," which was a warning, and he was right: it is a walk. Up one
central aisle, across row M (where the floor has a known soft spot, marked
in tape, 2011), down the other aisle, and out through the stalls bar,
which has been closed since 2016 and still smells of the pumps.

He touches things. Not sentimentally — functionally. The back of a seat
to test the hinge. The brass rail on the balcony stairs, which he holds
gripped like a handshake. Once, at row G, he stopped and adjusted a seat
cushion that to my eye needed no adjusting.

"What's wrong with it?"

"Nothing's wrong with it. Someone sat in it. You leave a seat the way
someone left it, it remembers. Give it a turn, it starts fresh."

I asked if he was joking. He said: "Six thousand seats and I know how all
of them sit. Joking's a different department."

The house lights were at half and the ceiling's cracks showed like a map
of somewhere small and badly governed. He looked up at the ceiling the way
you look at a face you are trying not to say goodbye to, and then said, in
the voice of a man announcing an interval, "Right. Tea? The flat's kettle
is the only one in the building that doesn't trip the stage breaker."

## Numbers Arkwright keeps

- Seats re-covered in the 2004 refit: 2,100 of 6,000. The rest are original.
- Ghost light nights since 1969: "all of them."
- Times he has been asked to leave: twice, both times by a new council,
  both times retracted.
- Sofa: currently in the props store. For the last three weeks the props
  store has been a second room.

The 2004 refit came up on its own. The council had money that year — a
development pot, a brief window, the usual weather — and re-covered two
thousand one hundred seats in a red that Arkwright can identify from the
far end of the balcony and that he describes, without irony, as "a
committee red." The refit stopped where the money stopped, which was row
DD of the upper circle, and above that line the 1969 mohair continues,
so the house now has an equator: red below, gold-brown above, and the
dividing line running around the room at exactly the height of the council
chamber's wainscoting, a coincidence I noted and he confirmed.

"They always stop at the same height," he said. "Money in this town stops
at the height a person can be expected not to notice it."

He is not a bitter man. I want that in the record too. He corrected a
cushion in row G and made tea and talked about the opening night in 1969
with the exact tone of a man describing yesterday's matinee. What he has
that the resolution lacks is a vocabulary for things being held.

I am filing this as a portrait and not as a plea. But the arithmetic from
chapter three is now standing in front of me in a man with a very particular
set of skills, and the phrase "lease renewal" turns out to mean, in one
case at least, whether a person keeps a room. The council's resolution does
not mention him. The officers' report does not mention him. The word
"occupancy" appears once, on page nine, in a paragraph about insurance.

After I left, at the stage door, he said the thing I have been turning over
ever since. It is the reason this chapter is longer than the last one.

"The town thinks it's about the building," he said. "It's never about the
building. The building is just the last place the town kept its promises.
Close the theatre, fine — but then where does the town stand when it wants
to say something out loud?"

I said that was a good line and I would use it.

"It's not a line," he said. "It's a question. I've been asking it since
1994."

We had tea in the flat. The kettle did trip the breaker — he had forgotten,
it turns out, the one detail in the building he had never once checked —
and we sat in the dark of the stage-left flat with the ghost light coming
under the door, and he laughed for about a minute at his own expense and
said that the building had been waiting fifteen years to catch him out.
He made a note. On the shelf there is a notebook of these — not faults
exactly, things the building does. Page one, in his hand, dated 1994:
"ghost light hums in F. tell no one."

The lease will not be renewed. Forty-one tons of iron are still holding
everything up, doing arithmetic all night, trusting physics, waiting for
the survey to say what the survey says. And somewhere above the stage
door, in one room with 1969 tiles, a man wakes for the ghost light and
listens to the building breathe. Two breaths an hour. It isn't working.
He knows.

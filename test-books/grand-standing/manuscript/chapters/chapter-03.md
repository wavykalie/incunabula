# Chapter 3 — The Numbers

One pound a year is what the theatre pays in rent, and I found it in a
footnote.

Not a metaphorical footnote. Page eleven of the officers' report, appendix C,
sub-clause (iv): "the Colosseum lease, a peppercorn term granted 1969 at an
annual rent of one pound (1.00), expiring 31 March." Between a paragraph
about drainage and a table about insurance excesses, the town's grandest
building turns out to rent for less than the newspaper's vending machine
takes in a week.

I want to say I pursued this story. That would be the version filed by a
better reporter. (The vending machine in the Sentinel lobby has been broken
since August and takes money anyway; I have lost probably four pounds to it,
which is four years of the theatre's rent and I would like that on record.)
The truth is that it fell out of the appendix while I was
looking for the survey figure — the two point four million, the number
Councillor Ampadu had used like a shield in the vestibule — and I nearly
turned the page. Numbers are not my instinct. Sentences are. But the pound
was sitting there in parentheses and my father raised me on parentheticals.

Here is what I have, filed in order, with corrections at the end.

## What the council decided

On the fourteenth, seven votes to four, the council resolved not to renew
the lease of the Colosseum Civic Theatre when it expires on 31 March. The
resolution's language — "the orderly conclusion of a civic legacy" — is
now known to be reused from 1974, when it closed the lido. I have requested
the archive file for the lido resolution. The council has acknowledged my
request.

## What the numbers say

The rent: one pound a year. Peppercorn. A legal fiction of rent, paid in
symbol, in a term granted in 1969 when the theatre opened and the town was
promised something it has spent fifty-seven years failing to pay for.

How the pound is paid matters and I do not yet know which way it goes. Three
possibilities, and the accounts will tell me: the theatre hands over a
coin every year and the council receipts it (the ceremonial version — I have
seen photographs of the 1999 handover, the mayor in a chain, a pound coin
on a velvet cushion); or the council waives the pound and books the waiver
(the efficient version, which is also the version that quietly forgets what
the pound was for); or nobody has paid or waived it in years and the
peppercorn simply sits in the term like a stopped clock (the version I
expect, because it is the version that matches everything else in this
story).

The repairs: officers estimate two point four million pounds of liability.
The stage structure, the roof grid, the fire systems, the asbestos survey
that everyone in the building knows about and nobody has commissioned.

The funding: this budget cycle, one monument. The fountain pump is a
ninety-thousand-pound line item. The frieze's restoration is two hundred
and ten thousand. The theatre's two point four million is eleven times
both of them together.

The jobs: the theatre employs, on paper, four people — a manager, a
technician, a box-office clerk three days a week, and a cleaner. Arkwright
is all four when the other three are off. The Sentinel employs, on paper,
two people: an editor and a reporter. If you are counting the town's
economy of two-point-something jobs holding up six thousand seats and
sixty-three years of sentences, you are doing the same arithmetic the
council did on the fourteenth, only in a different column.

The vote: seven to four, one abstention. The abstention was Councillor
Prentice's successor, on procedural grounds I do not yet understand.

## What it means, if the numbers are right

The council is not refusing to pay rent. The council has been paying, or
waiving, or performing the payment of, one pound a year since 1969 —
accounts will tell me which, I have requested the full accounts — and the decision
on the fourteenth was not about the lease at all. The lease is a costume.
Underneath it is a decision about a roof, and the roof is a decision about
whether a town of forty thousand people keeps a room that holds six
thousand.

A room that holds six thousand in a town of forty thousand is not a
theatre. It is an ambition.

## What it means for me, which the file would prefer to leave out

The Sentinel's owner in Leeds has a budget meeting in November. The
Sentinel's budget is, as far as I can tell, the vending machine and the
funeral notices. My contract — I have read it twice, and the arithmetic is
not subtle — runs with the paper. When the paper stops, my job stops, and
the phrases in my contract that describe this are as beautiful as anything
the council produces: "the role is subject to the ongoing commercial
viability of the title." The title. As if the Sentinel were a building and
not two people and a radiator.

So: the council declined to buy a roof, and my employer declined to buy a
future, and both decisions were made in rooms I was not in, and both will
be reported in sentences I will write. That is what the numbers are, when
you stop filing them and read them. The two point four million is the
town's version of the same sentence as my contract. Someone has done the
arithmetic and both of us are the arithmetic.

This is not in the press release. The press release said the decision was
made "with regret and in the interest of fiscal prudence," and the phrase
fits in a footnote, which is where I found the pound.

## The story I got wrong

My first piece, filed the night of the vote, said the council "moved to
close the Colosseum." That is not what happened, and the distinction is the
whole story. The council moved to decline to buy the roof. These are
different acts with different words and my paper printed the wrong one.
The correction runs on page two in the next edition, under my name, as it
should. Desmond says corrections are the only byline anyone ever really
reads. Desmond is being kind in the register of his trade, which as
established is the kindest thing he does.

I also said the theatre's manager "could not be reached." He could be
reached. He was in the stage-left flat every evening and I walked past it
twice without knocking, because knocking would have made the story true.

## The inspection, which was not journalism but I am filing it

On Saturday I bought a ticket to the matinee. Forty-one people, as
Arkwright said. I sat in the stalls and counted the house instead of
watching the stage, which is the sort of thing you can only do at a matinee
of a play you have already seen, and I have now seen this one twice.

Numbers, in the building, are different numbers. The ceiling is eleven
metres above the stalls and the sound does something in that volume that
makes a single voice sound like a fact. The seats are 1969 mohair, sixty
per cent of them original, and the programme notes — printed in-house, on
paper thin enough to see the prices through — say the house was designed
by a man who believed a civic theatre should make a citizen feel that the
town was larger than it was. It works. Sitting in row H with the house
lights up I felt it: the town larger than it was.

Two point four million pounds is, in the building, the difference between
the paint on the balcony rail (good, gold, original) and the ceiling above
it (cracked, mapped, dotted with those little paper markers surveyors put
on things they have decided to condemn). The markers are everywhere. I
counted forty-one in the section above the stage — the same number as the
audience, which I noticed and did not write down at the time because it
seemed like the kind of coincidence that belongs in a poem and not in a
report, and I am now writing it down anyway, in a report.

After the show I walked out past the box office — one clerk, three days a
week, Thursday to Saturday — and the woman in the blue sleeve from the
council meeting was there, buying a ticket for Sunday with cash. We looked
at each other without recognition, or at least she did. Her coat smelled of
the same wet-wool everyone's coat smelled of that week. The canvas bag was
over her arm.

## What I have asked for (public record)

1. The 1969 lease document and the original rent schedule.
2. The accounts showing how the peppercorn has been paid or waived (1969–).
3. The asbestos survey referenced in appendix C.
4. The lido resolution, 1974, for the archive.
5. The stage-structure survey — the full document, not the summary.

Five requests, filed Friday. The council has five working days to
acknowledge. I will print what arrives and what does not arrive, because
in my experience the second list is the story. Request number three — the
asbestos survey — is the one I expect to vanish. Buildings like this do not
have an asbestos problem; they have an asbestos agreement, which is a
different thing: everyone who works there knows where it is, nobody tests
it, and the knowledge functions as a maintenance schedule made of
silence. Or so I assume.

## The interview that was three words

I asked the council's press office one question on Monday: how has the
peppercorn been paid since 1969 — receipted, waived, or neither?

The answer, in full, arrived by email at 4:52 in the afternoon, and it is
three words long: "In accordance with practice."

I read it out to Desmond. He read it twice and said: "That's not an answer." Then he read it a third time and said: "Print it. Print the
whole thing, question and answer, and let the town see what three words
cost them."

The question was twenty-two words. The answer was three. In column inches
the exchange is one inch of type, and the one inch is the whole of the
council's public position on fifty-seven years of a pound a year. I have
set it centered, question above answer, in the piece that runs Thursday.
In accordance with practice. The phrase does not even name the practice.
It names the having of one.

## The correction, in the file voice

CORRECTION 22 October: the earlier version of this piece stated the council
"moved to close" the Colosseum. The council resolved not to renew the
lease and declined repair liability. The closure is a consequence and not a
resolution, and the difference is exactly what the town is arguing about.
Also for the record: the Sentinel's earlier report said the manager "could
not be reached for comment." He could. I did not. That one is not the
council's arithmetic; it is mine.

One pound a year. I have written it four times now in three days, once in
the margin of the officers' report, once in my notebook, once in the piece
above, and once on the back of my hand on the bus, which is not journalism
but is where I work things out. The pen was Desmond's. He has a pot of them
on the desk, all of them half-dead, and he hands you one without looking up,
which is his whole theory of management in one gesture.

A theatre that rents for a pound and a town that cannot afford the roof.
Seventeen words of arithmetic, and the council reached for the resolution's
language instead.
The arithmetic is not the sad part. The sad part is that the pound was
always meant to say: this is ours, we simply hold it in common.

The lease will not be renewed. The pound is not the point. The pound was
never the point. But it is where I am starting.

# Foundation

> Assembled from `artifacts/03-figures.md`, `04-theme.md`, and `06-emotional-curve.md`.
> This is the document the drafting skill reads.

## The book

**Working title:** *Sons of Heaven, Students of Dust*
**Subtitle (working):** *Eleven Rulers and the Philosophers Who Explained Them*
**Form:** narrative nonfiction · 28,000–32,000 words · 11 chapters

**Span:** the Qin unification (221 BCE) to the consolidation of the literati order
under the Song (c. 1200 CE).

**Premise.** In 221 BCE a king of Qin ended five centuries of war and discovered that
conquest was the easy part. He commissioned a philosophy that said consent was
unnecessary. It worked for eleven years, and has been failing — and being
recommissioned — ever since. The book follows that loop through eleven rulers and the
thinkers who tried to explain them.

## Theme

**Central question (never declared, never answered):**
*Can a ruler be taught to rule well — or is every theory of good rule just the winner's
autobiography?*

Every chapter removes one more easy answer. The book's identity is the refusal to close.

## Structure

Eleven chapters. Each pairs **one seat of power** with **one theory of power**, and
ends with a verdict the narrator is willing to defend and to qualify.

**The mirror** is the recurring device (Taizong's bronze / history / person). Every
chapter is a mirror held up to a ruler by a record he did not control. The device is
explicit in Ch 8 and recurs in 3, 9, 11.

**Structural ledger**

`1 chronological · 2 parallel · 3 essayistic · 4 reverse · 5 fragmented · 6 epistolary · 7 chronological · 8 spiral · 9 stream · 10 chronicle-with-interludes · 11 essayistic`

Nine structural types are used, with no two consecutive chapters sharing one.

## Figures

Full profiles in `artifacts/03-figures.md`. The figures carry the argument, so these are
the rules for them:

- **No invented interiority.** Where the record does not say what a person felt, the
  book does not say. It says what they *did*, and lets the reader do the rest.
- **Chaos markers are mandatory.** Every primary figure has one recorded, irrelevant,
  human detail (Li Si's rats, Liu Bang's sprawled legs, Cao Cao's poem about mortality,
  Bodhidharma's "None"). These keep the figures from becoming theses in robes.
- **Speech rule (F-006).** Reported speech from the traditional histories is quoted and
  marked as the historian's staging. No invented quotation, ever.

## Engagement type

| Rank | Type | What it means here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intellectual** | The reader is here for the argument about legitimacy and for the "aha" of seeing an institution work. |
| 2 | **Fascination** | Moral complexity: men who build and destroy at once, and a narrator who refuses to flatten them. |
| 3 | **Empathy** | The cost paid by unnamed people. Delivered through grain, conscription, and specific anchors — never sentimentally. |

The primary/secondary tension — *Intellectual* wants clean ideas, *Fascination* wants
moral mess — **is the book's identity.** The system does not resolve it; the chapters
hold both.

## Opening strategy (Chapter 1)

**Not** a "long view of history" preamble. **Not** a rhetorical question. **Not** a
scene-setting panorama of "the ancient world".

Open **in the act**: the Qin standardisation, delivered as a list of things a state can
do to a people — writing, weights, axle-widths — with the terracotta army withheld until
the chapter has earned it. The first sentence establishes the narrator's voice and the
book's claim in one move (see `artifacts/07-opening-strategy.md`).

## Re-read architecture (planned rewards)

- Ch 1 plants **Li Si's rats**; Ch 1's ending pays it off, and Ch 4's Wang Mang is a
  second reading of it.
- Ch 3 plants **Sima Qian's method** ("read the record carefully"); Ch 9 and Ch 11 use
  it without re-explaining it — a reader who noticed Ch 3 reads those chapters
  differently.
- Ch 7 plants **the canal** as infrastructure; Ch 10 pays it off as the thing that made
  the Tang golden age affordable.
- Ch 8 states **the three mirrors**; Ch 9's blank stele and Ch 11's syllabus are the
  third mirror (a person) applied to a woman and to a class.

## Cultural vocabulary (terms the reader should own by the end)

`the Mandate of Heaven` · `the Platen` is internal only, do not use · `remonstrance` ·
`the examination hall` · `Pure Conversation` · `corvée` (glossed, then used) ·
`the wordless stele`.

## Writer warnings (patterns this pipeline defaults to and must avoid)

1. **The competent-survey dodge.** Summarising a reign instead of choosing one scene
   that *is* the reign.
2. **The moral symmetry reflex.** Ending every chapter with "both sides had a point."
   The book takes positions.
3. **The Sage Voice.** The narrator becoming oracular. This narrator is a working
   historian with a temper, not a fortune cookie.
4. **The exotic register.** "The mysterious East" framing. This is a political history of
   institutions that can be compared to any state's.
5. **Over-hedging.** Flagging uncertainty is required; flagging it on every sentence
   makes the book unreadable. Flag where the record actually conflicts.

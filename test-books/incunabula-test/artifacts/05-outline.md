# 05 — Outline

Eleven chapters, 2,400–2,900 words each. Each carries: structural approach, emotional
anchor, emotional surprise, chaos moment, and the argument it destroys.

See `outline.md` for the same plan in the form the drafting skill reads. This artifact is
the architecture record.

---

## Ch 1 — The Book Burner
**Era:** Qin, 221–210 BCE · **Pairing:** Qin Shi Huang / Li Si + Han Feizi · **Structure:** chronological
**Argument destroyed:** "Law alone is enough."

- **Function.** Open the book's argument. Show the state as engineering, and the engineer
  as a man who could not engineer himself.
- **Scenes.** (1) The standardisation inventory — what unifying a realm actually changed.
  (2) Han Feizi's doctrine, quoted — trust no one, hide your mind. (3) The book burn of
  213 and the buried scholars of 212, *with the scholarly dispute over the account
  flagged in the text*. (4) Li Si's rats. (5) The elixir, the mercury, the death, and the
  tomb.
- **Emotional anchor.** The terracotta army — individual faces, buried to guard the man
  who abolished the individual.
- **Emotional surprise.** The death is not tragic. It is nearly farcical, and the farce is
  the indictment.
- **Chaos moment.** Li Si's latrine-rat / granary-rat fable: a whole life philosophy
  derived from watching two rats.
- **Closes with.** The tomb sealed, the craftsmen sealed in with it — and the question of
  what the law could not do.

## Ch 2 — Two Men at the Edge of a Ravine
**Era:** 210–195 BCE · **Pairing:** Liu Bang / Xiang Yu / Lu Jia · **Structure:** parallel
**Argument destroyed:** "Only the good man wins."

- **Function.** Braid two lives that end in the same place and prove they do not mean the
  same thing.
- **Scenes.** (1) Xiang Yu's aristocratic rise and near-total battlefield dominance.
  (2) Liu Bang's peasant banditry and his losses. (3) The contention, alternating.
  (4) Gaixia: Xiang Yu's song about his horse and his woman. (5) The Wu River refusal.
  (6) Liu Bang's court, and Lu Jia's line: *you conquered it on horseback; you cannot rule
  it on horseback.*
- **Emotional anchor.** Xiang Yu, ashamed to face the elders of Jiangdong who trusted him.
- **Emotional surprise.** The chapter makes the reader *admire* the loser — and then shows
  that the admiration is precisely what cost him the empire.
- **Chaos moment.** Liu Bang's sprawled-legs habit, and the ritual correction of it.
- **Closes with.** Lu Jia's line as the book's hinge: the Han needs a philosophy now.

## Ch 3 — The Historian's Silence
**Era:** 141–87 BCE · **Pairing:** Han Wudi / Sima Qian · **Structure:** essayistic
**Argument destroyed:** "The record will vindicate the good."

- **Function.** Introduce the book's own method through its inventor. From here on the
  reader reads every chapter as a contested document.
- **Scenes.** (1) The *Records* quoted as text — the narrator reads a sentence and asks
  who wrote it. (2) Han Wudi's expansion and its bill. (3) The general Li Ling, the
  defence, the sentence. (4) The choice: death or castration. (5) The *Letter to Ren An*.
  (6) The fairness Sima Qian shows his mutilator.
- **Emotional anchor.** The decision — not the pain. A man choosing to be made
  unmentionable in order to finish a book.
- **Emotional surprise.** The peak is not the mutilation. It is that he wrote Han Wudi
  fairly afterward.
- **Chaos moment.** The passage in the *Records* where he records his own humiliation in
  the first person, then closes the door and returns to the history.
- **Closes with.** The method stated plainly: you cannot trust the record, therefore you
  must read it carefully.

## Ch 4 — The Usurper Who Read the Classics
**Era:** 9–23 CE · **Pairing:** Wang Mang / the Confucian canon · **Structure:** reverse
**Argument destroyed:** "If the ruler truly believes the classics, the state will flourish."

- **Function.** The book's most dangerous chapter (highest abandonment risk). Open at the
  end so it never becomes a policy tour.
- **Scenes, in reverse.** (1) 23 CE: the capital burning, the mob, Wang Mang still
  consulting the classics. (2) The reforms that broke the currency and the land. (3) The
  scholar's rise, the perfect minister. (4) The regency, the child emperor, the
  abdication theatre. (5) The moment he believed it — the classics said a virtuous man
  should rule, and he was a virtuous man, so.
- **Emotional anchor.** The last page: the classics in his hands while the city burns.
- **Emotional surprise.** Not contempt — something closer to grief. He was sincere, and
  sincerity was the weapon.
- **Chaos moment.** His obsessive measurement of everything against the ancient texts:
  land, weights, rank, the design of the palace.
- **Closes with.** The realisation that he is Li Si's rat again — position changed,
  character unchanged.

## Ch 5 — A Poet Among Warlords
**Era:** 184–220 CE · **Pairing:** Cao Cao / Pure Conversation · **Structure:** fragmented
**Argument destroyed:** "A cultured ruler is a gentler one."

- **Function.** The trough. Fast, fragmented, no connective tissue. Show the Han's
  ideology dissolving into metaphysics while the warlords divide the corpse.
- **Scenes.** (1) The Yellow Turban rebellion as the moment the mandate stops being
  believed. (2) Three documents stacked: an edict, a poem, a list of dead. (3) Xu
  Province — the slaughter, *with the death-toll dispute flagged*. (4) The poem on the
  shortness of life, quoted *after*. (5) Pure Conversation: the elite turning from
  governing to talking.
- **Emotional anchor.** The poem, placed after the massacre — and offered as neither
  redemption nor condemnation.
- **Emotional surprise.** The poetry does not exculpate him. It also does not convict him.
- **Chaos moment.** The poem itself: a man who ordered a massacre writing, apparently
  sincerely, about how short life is.
- **Closes with.** Orthodoxy gone, the next three centuries of division set up.

## Ch 6 — The Emperor Who Fed the Monks
**Era:** 502–549 CE · **Pairing:** Liang Wudi / Bodhidharma · **Structure:** epistolary
**Argument destroyed:** "A religious ruler is a merciful one."

- **Function.** Funny, then bleak. Built from documents: the emperor's edicts, the monk's
  (reported) silence.
- **Scenes.** (1) The edicts — the emperor feeding and funding the sangha at enormous
  scale. (2) The famous interview: *how much merit have I earned?* — *None.* (3) The
  body-count of the ordination economy (monks removed from the tax rolls). (4) Hou Jing's
  rebellion and the siege. (5) The emperor's death by starvation, in his own capital.
- **Emotional anchor.** "None."
- **Emotional surprise.** The chapter is genuinely comic in places, which makes the
  starvation worse.
- **Chaos moment.** The emperor writing poetry while besieged.
- **Closes with.** The sangha's wealth surviving the emperor who built it.

## Ch 7 — The Canal and the Gate of Examinations
**Era:** 581–618 CE · **Pairing:** Sui Wendi / Sui Yangdi · **Structure:** chronological
**Argument destroyed:** "Build the institutions and the people will follow."

- **Function.** The infrastructure chapter. Make the reader feel that unity is *made*, by
  bodies.
- **Scenes.** (1) Reunification after nearly three centuries. (2) The canal, measured in
  *li*, in *shi* of grain, and in conscripted lives. (3) The first stirrings of
  examination-based recruitment. (4) Yangdi's Korean campaigns and the conscription.
  (5) The collapse — and the two dynasties that inherit the machinery.
- **Emotional anchor.** The canal itself: a line of water dug by people whose names are
  not recorded.
- **Emotional surprise.** The builder is not a monster. The *institution* is, and the
  builders were merely competent.
- **Chaos moment.** Yangdi's grand tours down the very canal he killed to build.
- **Closes with.** The Tang inherits a working machine and a poisoned name.

## Ch 8 — The Mirror and the Minister
**Era:** 626–649 CE · **Pairing:** Tang Taizong / Wei Zheng · **Structure:** spiral
**Argument destroyed:** "A good ruler listens to criticism."

- **Function.** The spine. Returns to Chapter 1's question with the best case the book can
  make — and refuses to resolve it.
- **Scenes.** (1) The morning of Xuanwu Gate: the brothers, the ambush. (2) The same man
  governing: the Zhenguan decade, the remonstrance, Wei Zheng's rudeness. (3) The three
  mirrors. (4) Wei Zheng's death, and Taizong's reported grief. (5) The return of the
  motif: the record is the third mirror, and this chapter is holding it up.
- **Emotional anchor.** The same man, months apart: at Xuanwu Gate, and listening to Wei
  Zheng.
- **Emotional surprise.** The book refuses to resolve him, and the refusal *is* the answer
  the chapter offers.
- **Chaos moment.** Taizong kicking over a tablet of Wei Zheng's advice after Wei Zheng's
  death — and then restoring it.
- **Closes with.** The mirror, turned on the book itself.

## Ch 9 — The Woman Who Became Son of Heaven
**Era:** 690–705 CE · **Pairing:** Wu Zetian / the wordless stele · **Structure:** stream
**Argument destroyed:** "Legitimacy follows the rules."

- **Function.** The exception that tests the rule. Interior, document-driven, unsettling.
- **Scenes.** (1) The edict that had to invent new language to name her. (2) The
  promotions of commoners and the machinery of a parallel court. (3) The accusations and
  the purges — *the record here is hostile and must be flagged as such*. (4) Two decades
  of competent rule. (5) The stele left blank.
- **Emotional anchor.** The blank stele.
- **Emotional surprise.** Expect "not like other rulers"; get a ruler exactly like the
  others, destroyed by a system that could not forgive her competence.
- **Chaos moment.** The hostile record's own excess — the accusations against her are so
  extravagant that the narrator can use their extravagance as evidence.
- **Closes with.** The stele, and the reader left to write the verdict.

## Ch 10 — The Golden Age and the General Who Ended It
**Era:** 712–763 CE · **Pairing:** Xuanzong / Li Bai / Du Fu / An Lushan · **Structure:** chronicle with interludes
**Argument destroyed:** "The golden age can be kept."

- **Function.** Beauty, then the break. The poem as witness.
- **Scenes.** (1) Interlude: Li Bai. (2) The height — Chang'an, the court, the culture.
  (3) An Lushan's rise inside the system. (4) The rebellion. (5) Mawei: the concubine,
  the emperor's flight. (6) Interlude: Du Fu finding his child dead of hunger, dated.
- **Emotional anchor.** Du Fu's poem, dated.
- **Emotional surprise.** The golden age is destroyed not by an outsider but by the
  favourite the emperor trusted.
- **Chaos moment.** Du Fu's dead child — the greatest poet of the age recording the
  smallest, most specific horror.
- **Closes with.** The Tang survives, diminished; the poetry outlasts the throne.

## Ch 11 — The Age of the Literati
**Era:** 960–1200 CE · **Pairing:** the Song throne / Zhu Xi · **Structure:** essayistic
**Argument destroyed:** "Then the philosophers will rule instead."

- **Function.** Close the argument by showing the philosophy finally *winning the throne*
  — and the question surviving anyway.
- **Scenes.** (1) The examination syllabus, item by item. (2) The Song settlement:
  civil over military, the emperor as figurehead of a bureaucracy. (3) Zhu Xi's
  commentaries becoming the standard. (4) The machinery that made philosophy a career.
  (5) The question, held open.
- **Emotional anchor.** Zhu Xi's commentaries on the test everyone must pass — the
  philosophy ruling by exam.
- **Emotional surprise.** The expected ending is triumph. The actual ending is that the
  literati won, and it did not settle anything.
- **Chaos moment.** The examination candidate's desperation — the system that was
  supposed to make rulers wise made scholars miserable instead.
- **Closes with.** The question, unanswered, handed to the reader: *if none of these
  theories worked, why does anyone still believe any of them?*

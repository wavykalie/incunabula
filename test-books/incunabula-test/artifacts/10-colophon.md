## Logline

Eleven rulers of early China, each paired with the thinker who explained away the same
problem — and eleven failures of the same answer.

## Positioning statement

*Sons of Heaven, Students of Dust* (working title) is a narrative history of imperial
China from the Qin unification to the Song literati order, told as eleven collisions
between a seat of power and a theory of power. It is for readers who want the argument,
not the survey: each chapter destroys one easy answer to the question of what makes a
ruler legitimate, and the book closes without answering it.

## Synopsis (150 words)

From the Qin unification of 221 BCE to Zhu Xi's commentaries becoming the examination
standard around 1200 CE, this book follows one question through eleven rulers. Each
chapter pairs a seat of power with a theory of power and shows that theory failing in a
different way: Legalism standardising a people who did not consent; ritual legitimacy
borrowed by a peasant founder; a historian mutilated for writing fairly; a Confucian
believer whose sincerity starved the state; a poet-general writing the plainest poem
about a massacre he had ordered; an emperor who fed a religion until it ate his tax
base; a canal built out of bodies that held a continent together for five hundred years;
a murderer who governed well and knew he could not be trusted; a woman who refused to
write her own verdict; a golden age destroyed by the favourite its emperor trusted; and
a philosophy that finally took the throne and produced faction. The book answers its
question by refusing to.

## Query letter (working draft)

**Dear [Agent],**

*Sons of Heaven, Students of Dust* is a 28,000-word narrative history — though I would
build it to full trade length before submission — of early Chinese imperial rule told as
eleven paired lives: one ruler, one theory of rule, one verdict on which of them was
lying.

Every chapter runs the same experiment. A ruler acquires the throne and needs a
justification for it. A philosopher supplies one. The justification and the conduct then
diverge, and the chapter lives in the gap. My aim is a book that gives a general reader
the argument rather than the survey — that lets them see each theory of legitimacy work
and fail, without ever announcing the conclusion.

It is for the reader of [comp: candidate only] who wants a spine to hold while the
dynasties go by.

## Cover brief

- **Motif:** the mirror — Taizong's bronze / history / person. The third mirror is the
  record, and the book is holding it up.
- **Second motif available:** the blank stele. A cover built on *absence* — a stone with
  no inscription — would suit the book's refusal to close.
- **Register:** sober, typographic, letterpress-adjacent. Not "mysterious East" imagery,
  not dragons, not calligraphy fonts.
- **Palette:** ink, stone grey, one oxblood accent.

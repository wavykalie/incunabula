# 03 — Figures

## Note on method

This is nonfiction, so the machinery of "character" is adapted rather than imported.
Every figure below is a **real person whose inner life is not recorded**. The pipeline's
character fields are therefore used as an *interpretive frame*, and each is described in
terms the record can actually support. Where the frame would require invention, the
field is left as the source's own claim, marked.

Fields:

- **Record** — what the sources actually say happened to this person.
- **Claim** — what the person asserted about themselves (or what the throne asserted).
- **Contradiction** — where claim and conduct visibly part.
- **Chaos marker** — a recorded, irrelevant, human detail that keeps the figure from
  being a thesis in a robe.
- **On the page** — what the chapter must get right about them.

## PRIMARY FIGURES

### 1. Qin Shi Huang (Ying Zheng), r. 221–210 BCE — Chapter 1

- **Record.** King of Qin from 246 BCE; conquered the six rival states by 221; took the
  unprecedented title *huangdi*; standardised script, weights, axle-widths; burned
  books in 213 and buried scholars in 212 (the traditional account); died in 210 while
  touring for immortality, probably of mercury poisoning from his own elixirs.
- **Claim.** That order could be *engineered* — that law, not virtue, was the true
  substance of rule.
- **Contradiction.** He outlawed the past and then spent his last years trying to leave
  it — hunting the elixir, building a tomb that would hold his empire in miniature.
- **Chaos marker.** The famous terracotta army is *his*; so, per the *Records*, is a
  lifelong fear of assassination so specific that he reportedly moved between 270
  palace chambers so no one would know which he slept in.
- **On the page.** He is not a cartoon tyrant. He is the first man to run the experiment
  and it half-succeeded. The chapter must show the success before the cost.

### 2. Li Si (d. 208 BCE) — Chapter 1

- **Record.** Legalist statesman; architect of the standardisations and of the book
  burn; executed by the very machinery he built, in 208.
- **Claim.** That a competent man could serve any master and rise by usefulness alone.
- **Contradiction.** He wrote the edict that destroyed the old learning, and he wrote it
  in a beautiful classical style learned from exactly that learning.
- **Chaos marker.** The rat metaphor from his youth (the *Records* attribute it to him):
  a rat in the latrine is filthy and afraid; a rat in the granary is fat and unafraid.
  Li Si decided the difference was *position*, not character, and spent his life
  changing his position.
- **On the page.** He is the book's first case of the theory eating the theorist.

### 3. Han Feizi (c. 280–233 BCE) — Chapter 1 (the ghost)

- **Record.** Prince of Han, Legalist theorist, author of the essays that so impressed
  the future First Emperor; died in 233, before the empire he described existed.
- **Claim.** That the ruler should trust no one, reward and punish mechanically, and
  hide his own mind so no one can flatter it.
- **Contradiction.** He died in prison, poisoned by exactly the kind of court intrigue
  his theory predicted and could not save him from.
- **Chaos marker.** His writing is *funny* — dry, savage, full of fables. The most
  paranoid political thinker in the ancient world is also the wittiest.
- **On the page.** He speaks only through the page. The chapter quotes him and never
  puts words in his mouth.

### 4. Liu Bang (Han Gaozu), r. 202–195 BCE — Chapter 2

- **Record.** Peasant, petty official, bandit; beat the aristocratic Xiang Yu in the
  Chu–Han contention; founded the Han.
- **Claim.** After the victory, that he won because he used better men than himself.
- **Contradiction.** His most famous recorded acts are of fleeing — abandoning his
  children from a moving chariot, per the *Records* — and then building the virtue-
  centered state his advisors told him to build.
- **Chaos marker.** A recorded habit of sitting with his legs sprawled, which his
  scholars had to correct in public because it was beneath a throne.
- **On the page.** He should be the most *likeable* founder precisely because he is the
  least impressive one.

### 5. Xiang Yu (232–202 BCE) — Chapter 2

- **Record.** A prodigy general of the old aristocracy; won nearly every battle; lost
  the war; killed himself at the Wu River rather than cross it and try again.
- **Claim.** That honour and strength were the same thing.
- **Contradiction.** He reportedly said he was ashamed to face the elders of Jiangdong
  who had trusted him — a scruple his rival never showed, and the reason he died.
- **Chaos marker.** The song he sang at the end, the "Song of Gaixia", in which he
  worries not about defeat but about what will happen to his horse and his woman.
- **On the page.** He is the chapter's argument for the losing side. Not a villain, not
  a fool.

### 6. Lu Jia (c. 240–170 BCE) — Chapter 2 (the advisor)

- **Record.** Han counselor. The *Records* have him tell Liu Bang: you conquered the
  realm on horseback; you cannot rule it on horseback.
- **Claim.** That an empire needs ritual, texts, and a story of its own goodness.
- **Contradiction.** He argued for the Confucian *Odes* and *Documents* to a man who
  reportedly answered that he had won the empire by hard work and practical skill.
- **Chaos marker.** He is remembered mainly for one sentence. History gave him a
  cameo and he used it to change the Han.
- **On the page.** He is the hinge of the whole book: the first advisor to say, flatly,
  *you need a philosophy now.*

### 7. Han Wudi (Liu Che), r. 141–87 BCE — Chapter 3

- **Record.** Longest-reigning Han emperor; expanded the empire, opened the Silk Road,
  established the imperially-sanctioned Confucian scholarship that would last two
  millennia, and spent the treasury doing it.
- **Claim.** That the empire should be universal — one order, one canon, one road.
- **Contradiction.** He made Confucianism the state's idea and never governed
  Confucianly; he exhausted the very state he claimed to be expanding.
- **Chaos marker.** His obsession with immortality and with *qi*, the search for the
  elixir, the paranoid crackdowns late in life including the death of his own heir.
- **On the page.** He is the empire at maximum ambition and maximum cost, in one body.

### 8. Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE) — Chapter 3 (the witness)

- **Record.** Court astrologer and historian; condemned for defending a general; given
  the choice of death or castration; chose castration to finish the *Records of the
  Grand Historian*.
- **Claim.** In the *Letter to Ren An*: that the work outlasts the man, and that
  suffering is what qualifies a historian to be trusted.
- **Contradiction.** He wrote the definitive record of the emperor who mutilated him,
  and he wrote it with a fairness that must have cost him something the letter denies.
- **Chaos marker.** The *Records* include a passage in which he records his own
  humiliation in the first person, then closes the door on it and returns to the history.
- **On the page.** He is the book's conscience and its *method*. Chapter 3 must show the
  book's own promise — you cannot trust the record, so you must read it carefully — was
  invented by him.

## Recurring non-human "characters"

- **The Mandate of Heaven (天命).** Never speaks. Always cited. Read backwards.
- **The examination hall.** Begins as an idea in Chapter 7, becomes the machine that
  governs Chapter 11.
- **The record.** Sima Qian's invention, and the book's own antagonist: the thing that
  decides, centuries later, which of these eleven men is remembered how.

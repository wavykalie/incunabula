# 00 — Brief

## The original idea

> a nonfiction book about the life of early Chinese emperors, and the philosophical
> teachings of the many dynasties

## Inferred direction

A narrative history of imperial China's first great arc — from the Qin unification
(221 BCE) to the Song consolidation of the literati order — told through **eleven
rulers and the thinkers who explained, justified, or defied them**. Each chapter
pairs one seat of power with one theory of power, and asks whether the theory ever
held.

The book's through-line is not "great men and their dates." It is a single argument
that keeps failing and keeps being made again: **that rule can be justified by
something other than force, and that this justification might even be teachable.**

## Language

English.

## Genre and subgenre

Narrative nonfiction; popular history of China; history-of-ideas crossover.

## Audience

General adult readers of trade history. They have read a book or two about China, or
none; they are here for a story with an argument, not a survey with a bibliography.
They will not tolerate a lecture, and they will not forgive an invented detail.

## Target length

28,000–32,000 words. Eleven chapters, roughly 2,400–2,900 words each, plus front and
back matter. Positioning: **full-length nonfiction** (not a novella, not a serial
installment, not a short).

## Narrative mode

Third-person essayistic historian. The narrator is present, has read the sources, and
is willing to say where the record runs out. Reported speech is used freely where the
traditional histories put words in a mouth; invented quotation is used never.

## Reader promise

By the last page the reader will be able to name the four or five rival answers China's
thinkers gave to the question *what makes a ruler legitimate*, and will understand why
every one of them was eventually swallowed by the throne that had commissioned it.

## What makes this version legible without making it generic

- The **pairing structure** (one ruler, one philosopher or tradition) is a spine a
  reader can navigate, and it distinguishes the book from a straight dynastic survey.
- The **mirror motif** runs through it — Taizong's three mirrors, and the fact that
  every emperor in this book is finally seen by someone else's record.
- The book is honest about the record. Where the sources disagree, it says so. That
  honesty *is* the book's authority.

## Explicitly out of scope

- Original archival research (secondary synthesis only — see ASSUMPTIONS A-13).
- Military history for its own sake.
- Anything after the Song political settlement, except where a closing chapter must
  gesture at it to leave the argument hanging correctly.

# 06 — Emotional Curve

The curve is tracked in **emotional anchors** (a concrete image or moment the reader
carries away), not intensity numbers. Oscillations are deliberate: the target is roughly
**eight major emotional swings** across the book. Too few reads flat; too many exhausts.

## Curve

| Ch | Working title | Start emotion | End emotion | Emotional anchor (the thing the reader keeps) | Emotional surprise (where the expected feeling is wrong) | Abandonment risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Book Burner | fascination | unease | The terracotta army: an army of individuals, no two faces alike, buried to guard a man who killed individuality | The elixir death is not tragic — it is almost comic, and the comedy is the indictment | low |
| 2 | Two Men at the Edge of a Ravine | suspense (two lives, one throne) | sorrow | Xiang Yu at the Wu River, ashamed to face the elders who trusted him | The reader expects to respect Xiang Yu and does — and then realises his scruple is exactly what Liu Bang lacked, and that this is why we have the Han | medium (mid-book parallel could sag) |
| 3 | The Historian's Silence | anger | cold resolve | Sima Qian, choosing the knife to keep the book | The mutilation chapter is *not* the emotional peak; the peak is the fairness he shows his mutilator | low |
| 4 | The Usurper Who Read the Classics | dread (he is sincere) | horror-comedy, then pity | Wang Mang at the end, still consulting the classics while the city burns | The expected feeling is contempt; the book gives something closer to grief | **high — most at risk of losing the reader** |
| 5 | A Poet Among Warlords | weariness | disquiet | Cao Cao's poem on the shortness of life, quoted *after* the massacre, not before | The reader expects the poetry to redeem him; it does not, and it does not condemn him either | medium |
| 6 | The Emperor Who Fed the Monks | curiosity | bleak comedy | "None." — Bodhidharma's answer to how much merit the emperor had earned | The chapter is funny. A starving emperor and a blank-faced monk are almost a joke, which makes the starvation worse | medium |
| 7 | The Canal and the Gate | grim awe | exhausted respect | The canal itself: a line of water dug by people whose names are not recorded | The reader expects the builder to be a monster; the surprise is that the *institution* is, and the builders were merely competent | low |
| 8 | The Mirror and the Minister | admiration | moral vertigo | Taizong at Xuanwu Gate, then Taizong listening to Wei Zheng — the same man, months apart | The book refuses to resolve him. The surprise is that refusal | low (this is the spine chapter) |
| 9 | The Woman Who Became Son of Heaven | intrigue | strange grief | The blank stele: the only ruler in the book who declined to write her own verdict | Expect a "not like other rulers" treatment; get a ruler exactly like the others, executed by a system that could not forgive her competence | low |
| 10 | The Golden Age and the General Who Ended It | beauty | desolation | Du Fu finding his child dead of hunger, and the date of the poem | The golden age is destroyed not by a barbarian but by the favourite the emperor trusted | medium |
| 11 | The Age of the Literati | irony | an open question | Zhu Xi's commentaries becoming the test everyone must pass — the philosophy finally on the throne, ruling by exam | The expected ending is triumph (the literati won). The actual ending is that they won and it did not settle the question | low |

## Oscillation map (target ~8 majors)

1. **Ch 1 → 2.** Qin's engineered order → the blood in the ravine. Fall.
2. **Ch 2 → 3.** The Han's founding optimism → the historian's mutilation. Fall.
3. **Ch 3 → 4.** Cold resolve → horror-comedy. Deepen, not relief.
4. **Ch 4 → 5.** Pity → weariness. Flat-toppish by design; Ch 5 is the trough.
5. **Ch 5 → 6.** Weariness → bleak comedy. Rise (in tone, not comfort).
6. **Ch 6 → 7.** Bleak comedy → grim awe. Fall.
7. **Ch 7 → 8.** Exhausted respect → admiration. The book's highest point.
8. **Ch 8 → 9 → 10.** Moral vertigo → intrigue → beauty → desolation. One long slide.
9. **Ch 10 → 11.** Desolation → irony → the question held open. A final rise into
   non-resolution.

That is nine swings; the evaluator should count eight to ten and flag fewer than six or
more than twelve.

## High-impact beats (must land, no exceptions)

- Ch 1: the individual faces on the terracotta soldiers.
- Ch 3: Sima Qian choosing the knife. The chapter must *not* dramatise the pain; it
  must dramatise the **decision**.
- Ch 4: the last page of Wang Mang — consulted the classics while the city burned.
- Ch 8: Xuanwu Gate and Wei Zheng in the same chapter, unreconciled.
- Ch 9: the blank stele.
- Ch 10: Du Fu's dead child, dated.
- Ch 11: the commentaries on the exam syllabus — philosophy wins the throne and
  immediately becomes a career.

## Abandonment risks and mitigations

- **Ch 4** is the danger point (a sincere reformer whose plans are procedural). Mitigate
  by opening in *reverse* — at his death, in the burning city — and never letting the
  chapter become a policy tour.
- **Ch 5** is the trough (warlord, fragmentation). Mitigate with the poem-after-massacre
  beat and a short fragment structure so the chapter moves fast.
- **Ch 6** risks reading as a digression on Buddhism. Mitigate by keeping the emperor
  and his food politics in front of the theology.

## Ending promise

The last page resolves **the emotional arc but not the question**. The reader should
close the book with the sense of having watched an argument fail eleven times in eleven
different ways, and not being told what to conclude. The Afterimage Test target:
**evaluation residue, not emotional residue** — the reader keeps thinking, not crying.

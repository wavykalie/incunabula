# 01 — Market Map

> **Status: candidate.** No comp title below is approved. Comps are an author decision,
> and this map proposes them for approval rather than asserting them.

## Market signals

- **The "one idea through many lives" structure sells.** Narrative histories organised
  around a recurring question or a recurring object consistently outperform straight
  chronological surveys in the trade market, because they give the reader something to
  hold while the centuries move.
- **China is a persistent category, not a boom.** English-language trade readers
  sustain a steady appetite for accessible Chinese history; the shelf rewards
  clear, well-told synthesis far more than it rewards new theses.
- **History-of-ideas crossover is under-served in this niche.** Most popular Chinese
  histories are political or military. A book that keeps the *philosophy* in the
  foreground, without becoming a philosophy textbook, sits in a real gap.

## Comp titles (candidates)

| Candidate | Why it is a comp | What this book does differently |
|---|---|---|
| *The Early Chinese Empires* (Lewis) | Same span, scholarly authority | Academic register; this book is narrative-first |
| *China: A History* (Keay) | Trade-readable, broad | Single continuous survey; this is structured as paired lives |
| *The First Emperor* (Wood) | Qin Shi Huang as a set-piece | One ruler; this book needs him as an opening argument, not a subject |
| *Confucius: And the World He Created* (Schuman) | Ideas-led, trade register | Thinker-led; this book puts thinker and throne in the same room |

## Recurring patterns on the shelf

1. Dynasty-per-chapter surveys with a strong chronology and a thin argument.
2. Thinker biographies (Confucius, Laozi) that background the state that used them.
3. Ruler biographies that treat the philosophy as scenery.

## Whitespace opportunity

**Nobody is putting the ruler and the philosopher on the same page and asking which one
won.** The book's position is exactly that: eleven chapters, each a collision between
a seat of power and a theory of power, with one recurring question as the spine.

## Reader promise (market form)

*"A history of early China told through the men who ruled it and the men who tried to
explain it — and a fair verdict on which of them was lying."*

## Differentiation strategy

- **Structure** the shelf does not have: paired lives, not dynasty blocks.
- **Honesty about the record** as a feature, not a caveat. Where a famous scene is
  likely later invention, the book says so and uses the invention as evidence about
  what the empire wanted people to believe.
- **No academic apparatus in the prose.** Sources are named in the text the way a good
  narrator names them; no footnotes in the reading path.

## Risks

- **Comp-flooding** if the book reads as a survey. Mitigated by the pairing structure.
- **Accuracy scrutiny.** A book about early China is read by people who know early
  China. The accuracy posture in ASSUMPTIONS A-15 is non-negotiable.

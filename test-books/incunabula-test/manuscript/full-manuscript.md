## Chapter 01 — One Axle-Width

In 221 BCE a king of Qin ended a war that had run, with pauses, for five hundred years, and then discovered that he had won the wrong thing. He had an army that could take a state. He had no idea how to make a people agree to be governed.

So he tried to make agreement unnecessary.

The reforms that followed are usually described as cultural, which misses what they were. They were infrastructure. One script, so that a decree written in the capital could be read in a village a thousand *li* away without a translator. One set of weights, so that a *shi* of grain meant the same thing on both banks of a river. One currency. One axle-width for every cart in the realm, so that the ruts in every road matched every other road, and a wagon could cross the empire without being rebuilt.

Read that list again and notice what it assumes. It assumes a people is a system, and that a system can be tuned. It assumes the trouble with the world is variance, and that variance has a solution, and the solution is a standard.

The same logic went into stone. Roads were cut through mountains and carried across marsh on packed earth, and they were built to the new width, so that the army could move at a speed no previous state could match. Walls that had been built separately by separate kingdoms were joined into one wall, and the joins are visible to anyone who looks, because the older sections are made of whatever stone was nearest and the new work is uniform. The wall was never a border. It was a *standardised* border, and the difference cost lives.

We do not know how many. The *Records* does not say, and no one counted, because counting the dead is an act of accounting, and the Qin were very good at accounting for everything except that. Historians have made estimates across a wide range, and the range is the honest answer. What can be said without estimate is the shape of the thing: a labour tax that fell on every adult male, on a schedule, for years at a time, in a state that had recently stopped being six states and had not yet stopped being six peoples.

The king who did all this renamed himself. The old title, *wang*, meant king, and kings were what the six defeated states had been full of. He took a new name made of two older ones, *huang* and *di*, august and sovereign, and combined them into something no one had held before. He is remembered in English as the First Emperor. In Chinese he is Qin Shi Huang, and the *shi* means first, and he meant it as a claim about the future, not the past.

He did not become a tyrant by accident. He became one by theory. The theory had a name. Legalism is the lazy English version; *fajia*, the school of law, is closer. It had a text, and the text is worth quoting, because it is the coldest political writing in the ancient world and it is very good.

Its author was a prince of Han named Han Fei, who wrote in the third century BCE and died in 233, twelve years before the empire he described existed. He never governed anything. He simply reasoned about it, in essays that are frequently funny in a way that should be disturbing, and his reasoning went like this: the ruler should want nothing, show nothing, and trust no one. If a minister can guess what pleases you, he will please you instead of serving you. If your favourites can read your face, they will write on it. So the ruler should be blank. The law should be loud. Reward and punishment should fall with the regularity of weather, and they should be the only two things a ruler ever touches. Han Fei called them the two handles, and his point was that a man who holds both handles does not need to be loved, or even respected.

What the theory cannot do is explain why anyone should obey the second handle rather than the first. A subject who complies because the punishment is terrible is not a subject who is loyal; he is a subject who is calculating, and he will recalculate the moment someone else's reward looks better. Han Fei knew this and did not care, and the not-caring is the doctrine, and it is the reason the Qin could conquer everything and keep nothing. The two handles can produce a state that obeys. They cannot produce a state that agrees, and a state that does not agree has to be watched forever, by an army that must be paid, out of a treasury that must be filled, by subjects who are not convinced they owe anything.

Han Fei's essays reached Qin. The future First Emperor read them, reportedly, and said that if he could meet the author and befriend him he could die content. He got his meeting. Han Fei came to Qin on a diplomatic errand and was denounced by the very minister who had praised him. He was imprisoned. He drank the poison his jailers gave him. The theory had predicted exactly this. The theory had no way to prevent it.

That minister was Li Si, and Li Si is the first man in this book to be eaten by a system he built. His biography begins with a fable about rats, which the *Records of the Grand Historian* attributes to him, and which I am prepared to believe because it is the kind of thing a certain kind of young man really does think.

Li Si, young and minor, worked as a clerk. He watched the rats in the latrine: thin, filthy, startled by every footstep. He watched the rats in the granary: fat, calm, unbothered. Same rats, different position. He concluded that a man is only as dignified as the place he has managed to stand, and that the whole of a life's work is the work of moving from one room to the other.

Calling it a philosophy gets it wrong. It is a career strategy with metaphysical pretensions, and Li Si spent his life executing it. He crossed to Qin. He attached himself to the winning state. He wrote the memorial that proposed the book burn, and he wrote it in an elegant classical prose learned from the very texts he was proposing to destroy. He rose. He watched a boy emperor fail. He was manoeuvred out by a eunuch, accused of treason, and executed in 208 BCE in the marketplace at Xianyang, by the five punishments, which is a phrase that should be read slowly and then left alone.

The granary rat, in the end, was also the latrine rat. Only the room had changed.

## Chapter 02 — The Book Burn

The book burn happened in 213. Li Si proposed it, and the emperor agreed, and the shape of the order tells you what it was for. Practical books were spared: medicine, divination, agriculture, and the records of Qin itself. What burned were the histories of the other states and the writings of the hundred schools. The point was not to destroy knowledge. The point was to remove the *alternatives*.

A state that can be compared to another state is a state that can be found wanting. A ruler whose conduct can be held against a book of the ancients is a ruler with a limit. The burn was an act of standardisation, and it belongs on the same list as the axle-widths: it removed a variance.

What happened the next year is a different order of thing entirely. In 212 BCE, according to the *Records*, some four hundred and sixty scholars were buried alive. That number and that event have been disputed for two thousand years, and I am going to leave the dispute on the page rather than resolve it, because how you read it depends on whether you trust a history written a century later by an official whose own profession the First Emperor had tried to abolish.

Before we go further, we should meet that official, because he is going to matter for the rest of this book.

His name was Sima Qian, and he was not yet born when the scholars were buried. He wrote in the generation after, under a dynasty that had every reason to make the Qin look as bad as possible, and he is our only real source for most of what I have told you so far. This is worth pausing on. Everything you know about the First Emperor came through a man who worked for the family that replaced him. The Qin have no friend in the record, and the record is the *Records*.

And yet Sima Qian is also the reason we know the Qin at all, and he is not a simple propagandist: he preserves the standards, the roads, the walls, the ambition, and he lets the achievement stand before he shows the price. He is a hostile witness who keeps trying, against his own interest, to be fair. We will come back to him properly. For now, notice only that the book you are reading has a method, and the method was invented by a man who had excellent reasons to lie and mostly did not.

Read the buried scholars as propaganda and it is still evidence. The empire wanted to be known as a place where the past could be killed. That was the reputation it spent its reputation on.

A ruler who abolishes the past still has to die, and death is the most traditional event there is. The First Emperor spent his last decade looking for a way around it. He funded expeditions to find the islands of the immortals. He took elixirs compounded of cinnabar and other mercury salts, prepared by men who promised him forever and who cannot have understood that they were poisoning him slowly, because the poison is very good at imitating a cure.

He died in 210 BCE, on a tour of the east, at a place called Shaqiu. He was forty-nine. The cause was almost certainly the medicine. And when he died, his chancellor concealed the death, because the succession was not settled and the empire was held together by the mere fact of the emperor's existence. The imperial carriage continued its journey. A cart of salted fish was brought along, to cover the smell.

I told you at the start that this part is nearly farcical. It is worse than that. The most powerful man in the world spent twenty years building a machine that could not survive his own absence, and then he was driven home behind a load of fish.

The machine did not survive him. His son was placed on the throne and then, four years later, killed. The Qin fell in 206 BCE, four years after the death of the man who had made it, and its name has meant *tyranny* in Chinese political language ever since, which is itself a form of standardisation.

## Chapter 03 — What He Left Standing

What he left standing is the tomb.

You know what is in it, or you think you do. In 1974 farmers digging a well near Xi'an struck a pit of baked clay soldiers, life-sized, ranked in formation. More pits followed. The estimate runs to some eight thousand figures, and the number matters less than the fact that no two of their faces are the same.

That is not an accident of manufacture. The figures were made from a small number of moulds and then finished by hand, and the finishing was done to make each one an individual: a different set of the mouth, a different width of the brow, a different angle of the head. Somewhere in the bureaucracy of the Qin there was a standard for individuality. There was a specification for how a face should look like no other face, so that the emperor would have an army, and the army would look like men.

He had spent his reign abolishing the individual. He had one script, one weight, one law, one road. And then he buried himself inside an army of eight thousand men who each had their own face, arranged to face the direction from which enemies would come, and sealed the whole thing under a mound and had the craftsmen shut in with it so that the arrangement would not be described.

He needed the workers to be individuals in order to make the soldiers be individuals. He could not make the soldiers be individuals without using men, and he could not use men without killing them, because a man who has seen the inside of the tomb can describe it. The logic is airtight and it is the logic of the whole reign. Every good thing he built required a person to build it, and every person who built it was a liability to the good thing.

The *Records* describes the inner chamber with mercury poured to make rivers, and crossbows rigged to fire at anyone who broke in, and the artisans sealed in the outer gate. I do not know if it is true. I know it is what the empire wanted to be believed. Modern soil tests around the mound have found mercury concentrations well above the surrounding ground, which proves less than it sounds and more than nothing.

There is a further cost that the tradition notices and the modern reader often misses, and it is the cost of the writing itself. The Qin did not only burn books; it also prescribed how documents should be written, how files should be kept, how a report from a county should reach the capital. The bureaucracy that ran the empire was, by the standards of the ancient world, extraordinarily advanced: registers, quotas, sealed tallies, a postal system with relay stations and a legal requirement that officials keep copies. It is because of the Qin's administrative habits that we can count their grain and their soldiers at all.

Li Si's rats, again. The empire was a granary, and it was built by people whose only mistake was to be in the room.

I want to be careful here, because it is easy to make Qin Shi Huang into a monster and then feel finished with him. The word monster does not apply cleanly here. He was a solution to a real problem, and the problem was genuine. Five hundred years of interstate war had killed more people than he ever did. A man who ends that, even badly, has done something that cannot simply be condemned. The states he conquered had been slaughtering each other for centuries. The unification was, by the standards of its own time, a peace.

The question he could not answer is the question this book will keep asking. Force can stop a war. Force cannot make the peace mean anything. He could standardise the axle-widths. He did. The roads he built outlasted him. But the one thing he could not standardise was obedience that felt owed rather than merely endured, and without that, an empire is only an army that has stopped marching.

He knew the size of the problem. That is what is so interesting about him. He built a tomb that is a small empire. He staffed it with an army. He had the doors sealed. And the thing he was actually trying to do, make the peace last, was the one thing that no amount of standardisation could produce.

The doors were sealed behind him. The army is still in there, ranked and uniform to the width of an axle, waiting for a ruler who is never going to come.

## Chapter 04 — Two Men at the Edge of a Ravine

One of them was born into a great house and could not lose a battle. The other was born into a peasant family and could not win one. Both came to the same river in the end, and only one of them came willingly, and the difference between them is the whole of the Han dynasty.

Xiang Yu was a grandson of a general of Chu, raised in the ruins of a state that Qin had swallowed. The *Records* gives him a childhood worth noticing. He was taught to write and gave it up. He was taught a sword and gave that up too, and when his uncle asked him why, he said that writing was good only for remembering a name, and a sword for fighting one man. What he wanted was the art of war, the thing that fights ten thousand. His uncle taught him that. He learned it, or did not, depending on which passage you read, and then at twenty-four he put an army in the field and did not lose a battle for eight years.

Liu Bang grew up in a village and worked, badly, at a farm. He became a minor official, the sort of man who kept order in a market town, and he spent more of his pay on wine than on anything useful. Nobody admired him. By every account he was vulgar: he liked to insult people, he liked drink, he had no patience for scholars, and when a visitor of consequence came to see him he received the man sitting with his legs sprawled out while two women washed his feet. The visitor walked in without bowing and asked him whether he meant to help Qin or destroy it. Liu Bang apologised. He was good at that, which is a quality that never appears in a list of heroic virtues.

That is the first asymmetry. Xiang Yu was what the age called a hero. Liu Bang was what the age called nothing in particular, and he lived longer.

The second asymmetry is harder to hold in the head, because it reverses the story we expect. Xiang Yu was brave, and merely is the wrong word for it. He was, at the beginning, the moral centre of the war against Qin. When he destroyed the Qin field army at Julu, he crossed the Yellow River and sank his own boats and smashed his own cooking pots, and told his men they could eat when they had won or not at all. Brutes do not do that. It is the act of a man who understands that an army's courage is a decision about what it is allowed to believe.

And when he entered the Qin capital, it was Xiang Yu who killed the last Qin ruler and burned the palace. Liu Bang had got there first. He had refused to loot it, and he had sealed the treasuries and the archives. Liu Bang was the one who understood that a capital is not a prize but a filing system. Xiang Yu was the one who understood that a war is won by a single decision made in a moment of terror.

Both of those were true. Only one of them could be turned into a government.

We call the four years after the fall of Qin the Chu–Han Contention, which makes it sound tidier than it was. It was a war fought across the whole of the north China plain by men who had agreed on nothing except that the Qin were finished. Xiang Yu had the better army and the better claim, and he spent those four years almost continually winning. Liu Bang lost battles with an appalling regularity. He lost at Pengcheng so completely that his own father and his wife were captured, and he himself escaped with a handful of riders and, according to the *Records*, with his two small children in the carriage.

What happened next is the passage everyone remembers, and it is remembered wrongly, as a joke. Pursued and losing ground, Liu Bang pushed his son and his daughter out of the moving carriage, so that it would go faster. His driver, Xiahou Ying, stopped, got down, and put them back in. Liu Bang pushed them out again. This happened three times, according to the *Records*. The third time, Xiahou Ying apparently said something, and Liu Bang considered killing him.

I have read a great deal of commentary on this and it mostly tries to soften it. It was panic. It is a story the Han told about itself to show that their founder was ruthless. All of that may be true. What I notice is that the Han kept the story. They could have dropped it. Nobody forced them to record that their dynasty was founded by a man who tried to throw his children out of a cart, and they recorded it anyway. They put it in the same chapter as Xiahou Ying's refusal to let him.

They kept it because it is the argument of the dynasty in miniature. The man who survives is the man who is willing to do the thing. And they kept the driver too, because a man like that needs a driver.

Meanwhile Xiang Yu was winning. He took the field year after year. He was generous with his men and terrifying to his enemies, and he had the one quality that a warlord needs most, which is that his enemies believed he would actually do what he said. In 203 they agreed to divide the empire along a line at the Hong Canal, east for Chu and west for Han. Xiang Yu released Liu Bang's captured father and wife as a gesture of good faith and turned east.

Liu Bang followed him. This is the part where Liu Bang's vulgarity becomes a strategy. He had no shame about breaking an agreement that had just been made in front of witnesses. He had no interest in being admirable. He had a coalition, and he held it together by giving away land he did not yet own, and by listening to men who were cleverer than he was, and by being the one man in the war who never once insisted on being the hero of his own life.

By 202 BCE, Xiang Yu was surrounded at Gaixia. The *Records* says that in the night he heard songs coming from the Han camp. Chu songs, sung in Chu voices. He understood at once what it meant: that the Han had taken his homeland's men as well. He rose, drank, and sang a song of his own. It is four lines long and it is about his horse.

His strength had uprooted mountains, he sang, and his spirit had covered the world, and the times were against him, and his horse would not gallop on. What could he do about the horse. And then the fourth line, the one that is not about the horse at all, addressed to the woman beside him.

Her name was Yu. The sources call her Consort Yu, and she is one of the most famous women in Chinese literature for a single recorded response. She is said to have sung the song back to him, and then to have killed herself, so that he would not be slowed by her. Whether she did any of this is not something the record can settle. What matters is that the tradition put her there, at the end of the greatest general of the age, in the role of the thing a man loves and cannot take with him.

Xiang Yu rode out with eight hundred cavalry. He broke through. He got down to twenty-eight men, and then he turned and fought a last time, and killed, by the *Records*'s count, a hundred of his pursuers, because even at the end he was the best soldier in China and wanted the record to say so.

He came to the Wu River. A boatman was waiting, an old man from Jiangdong, who told him that the east bank was his, that the people there would make him king, that they had thousands of men and a whole province. All he had to do was get in the boat.

Xiang Yu would not. He had gone east with eight thousand men of Jiangdong, he said, and not one of them was coming back. Even if the elders pitied him and made him king, what face would he have. He gave his famous horse to the boatman. He had his men dismount and fight on foot. He killed, they killed, and then he cut his own throat and let a Han soldier take the body.

The Han split his corpse five ways, among five men, each of whom was made a marquis for the piece he brought back. That is the last thing the record does to him: it breaks him into pieces and pays for them. There is no gentler sentence in the *Records* about anyone who lost.

So the moral of the story should be that honour is a luxury the strong cannot afford, and to a certain kind of reader that is exactly what it is. But I do not think that is the moral, because Xiang Yu's refusal at the Wu River was the last act of a man who understood the world as a place where you answer to the people who trusted you. He could have crossed. He chose not to look at the elders of Jiangdong. That choice is the reason he is remembered with a kind of grief that Liu Bang never earned.

It is also the reason he is dead and Liu Bang is a dynasty.

Now Liu Bang had a problem, and it is the problem this book is about. He had the empire. He had no idea what it was for.

His court was, at first, a barracks. The men who had won the war were drinking companions and generals, and they behaved at the palace the way soldiers behave in a barracks: drinking, shouting, occasionally drawing swords over who had the better seat. Liu Bang, who had lived his whole life in exactly that register, found that he did not enjoy it in a palace. He is said to have complained about it to a scholar named Shu Sun Tong, who offered to build him a court ritual, and Liu Bang's only condition was that it not be too difficult for a man like him to perform.

Then there was Lu Jia. Lu Jia was a courtier of the sort the Han produced in plenty: learned, tone-deaf to his own insolence, and right. He kept talking to Liu Bang about the *Odes* and the *Documents*, the old Confucian canon. And Liu Bang, who had spent his life in the saddle and regarded texts as the playthings of men who could not fight, told him the truth as he saw it. I won this empire on horseback, he said. What do I need books for.

Lu Jia's reply is one sentence, and it is the hinge of this entire book. You won it on horseback, he said. Can you govern it on horseback.

Liu Bang, to his credit, understood immediately. He asked Lu Jia to write him a book on why dynasties rise and fall, and the book Lu Jia produced argued that a state needs the rituals that make men want to obey and not merely fear, that the power of the throne is not in the throne but in the story the throne tells about itself. This is the argument of the rest of the book, arriving here for the first time, addressed to a peasant who had just discovered he owned an empire.

He kept the old gods. That is important. Liu Bang never became a Confucian and the Han never stopped being a family with an army. But he did the thing that Qin Shi Huang could not do, which is to allow a philosophy to be useful to him without believing it. The First Emperor had a theory and made a state that could only stand while he did. Liu Bang had no theory and admitted it, and borrowed one. The state he founded lasted four hundred years.

What Lu Jia wrote for him is a short book, and almost nobody reads it now, and its argument is worth knowing because it is the argument the Han would run on for four centuries. A dynasty, he said, falls not when it is weak but when it loses the appearance of legitimacy. The rituals are the machinery by which a stranger's son grows up believing that the man on the throne is owed something, and a state that cannot manufacture that belief will have to manufacture soldiers instead, forever, and will run out. Lu Jia had watched the Qin run out.

Liu Bang's court heard this and did the practical thing. It kept the Legalist machinery, the registers, the censors, and the laws, and it wrapped them in the ritual and the scholarship that Lu Jia recommended, and it presented the result to the empire as a restoration of the old and good. That combination is what historians call Han statecraft, and it is an arrangement rather than a philosophy. It worked.

The cost of the arrangement came due later and it is the next chapter's subject, because a state that borrows a philosophy to justify itself eventually has to deal with the men who actually believe the philosophy, and they have opinions about how it should be run.

That is the trade the Han made, and it should be stated plainly, because everything that follows in this book is a variation on it. The emperor keeps the army. The scholars get the education of his sons and the design of his rituals and the writing of his history. Each side needs the other and neither trusts the other, and the arrangement is unstable in a way that will produce, over the next two thousand years, every kind of tragedy a state can have.

Xiang Yu never had to make that trade because he never had the empire. He had a horse, a woman, and eight thousand men from home, and he gave all three away rather than arrive at the far bank with none of them.

Liu Bang had the empire and no illusions about himself. He died in 195 BCE, in his own palace, of an illness, having survived twelve major campaigns and one very bad day in a carriage. The dynasty he founded took his name for its own and buried him under a mound bigger than any of his generals'.

## Chapter 05 — The Historian's Silence

There is a sentence in the *Records of the Grand Historian* that I want you to look at before I tell you anything about the man who wrote it. It is unfamous. It is the kind of sentence that is easy to read and hard to unread.

The emperor, it says, considered the matter and then did as he pleased.

That is the whole of a decision. The most powerful state in the world, an army on the frontier, a treasury draining, a famine in the east, and the record gives us six words and moves on. Sima Qian, who wrote those words, was in a position to know what the matter had been, and what the arguments against doing as he pleased had been, and who had made them. He chose not to say.

This chapter is about why. It is also about the invention that every history book since depends on, including this one, which is the idea that a record can be trusted precisely because it shows you the places it cannot.

Two men stand behind those six words. The first is Han Wudi, the emperor, who reigned from 141 to 87 BCE, fifty-four years, longer than any Han ruler before or after, and who made the empire into the thing the word *China* still describes. The second is his court astrologer and historian, Sima Qian, whose father had begun a history and who inherited the obligation to finish it. The emperor made the empire. The historian made the memory of the empire. Neither would survive whole, and the way the second one failed is the subject of this chapter.

Start with the emperor, because his achievement is real and it is easy to skip.

Han Wudi did four things, and each of them outlived him by a very long time. He broke the power of the regional kings, so that the empire was run from one court instead of a dozen. He sent envoys west and got back news of cities no Chinese ruler had heard of, and the trade routes that followed are what we call the Silk Road. He made Confucian scholarship the empire's official learning, endowing academies and reserving offices for men who had studied the canon. And he fought, more or less continuously, on three frontiers.

Only one of those four was a gift, and it was not the fighting.

The Confucian turn is the one that shaped a civilisation. Before Wudi, the Han had governed in a mixed style, Legalist machinery with a Confucian coat. After him, the state itself was committed to a text. To hold office, you should know the canon. To be educated, you should be examined in it. The consequences run from that moment to the examination halls of the twentieth century, and they include this book, because the men who wrote the histories of China were the men the system trained.

So the emperor who commissioned the record was also the man who had designed the curriculum of the people who would write it. Keep that in view. It makes what happened to Sima Qian more interesting than a story about one man's bad luck.

And the fighting cost more than the empire admitted. Wudi's wars against the Xiongnu were, on any honest accounting, a mixed success: he broke their confederation for a generation and pushed the frontier north, and he did it with armies that had to be fed across a thousand kilometres of grass. The *Records* and the later *Book of Han* both record the taxes, the monopolies on salt and iron, the sale of offices and titles, and the repeated conscription. The treasury was refilled by selling the state to whoever could pay. That is what the six words are standing on, and Sima Qian knew it, and he still wrote six words, because he also knew that the emperor was not interested in a longer sentence.

Then, late in his reign, the emperor began to see plots everywhere. The sequence is usually called the witchcraft persecutions, and it is the ugliest thing in his reign. The palaces were searched for cursed effigies; officials accused one another; the accusations reached the heir, Crown Prince Liu Ju, who was driven into a rebellion he had not wanted and then into death. His mother, the empress, was implicated and killed herself. By the time the emperor understood what he had done, the son was dead and the line was broken.

He built a tower to look for the spirit of the boy. That is the detail I cannot get past. The man who had made an empire out of an idea of order spent his last years trying to see one person again, and could not, and ordered the executions continued until there was nobody left to blame.

The trouble began with a general named Li Ling. He was sent against the Xiongnu on the northern frontier with a small force, and he fought well, and he was surrounded, and in the end he surrendered. Surrender was not a thing a Han general did. The court erupted. Everyone agreed Li Ling was a traitor and everyone said so loudly, and the louder they said it the safer they were.

Sima Qian did not say it. He was asked for his opinion, and he gave one: that Li Ling had fought beyond what could be asked of him, that his men had been outnumbered many times over, that he had not been given the support he needed, and that a man who had held out that long might yet find a way to repay the empire. This was an explanation of courage and no kind of defence.

The emperor heard it as an attack. It was read as an insult to the general who had failed to support Li Ling, who happened to be the emperor's own favourite, and therefore as an insult to the emperor's judgement. Sima Qian was arrested and condemned.

The penalty was death, and the law allowed a man to commute it in either of two ways. He could pay a fine, or he could accept castration. Sima Qian did not have the money. He had a choice between his life and his body.

He chose his body's ruin. He chose it so that he could finish the book, and he was very clear, in the letter he wrote about it years later, that this was not courage. He calls it a thing worse than death, and he does not hide behind philosophy. A man, he writes, has only one death, and he must decide what it is for. Some men die for a principle. Some die for nothing. He had a book unfinished, and he was willing to be the kind of man that everyone in his world would look at with contempt in order to finish it.

I am not going to describe the operation. It is not necessary, and the record does not describe it either. What the record gives us is the aftermath, and the aftermath is that he went back to work.

For seven more years he wrote. He had access to the imperial archives, which is why his book is the foundation of Chinese historiography, and he had a method, which is why his book is still worth reading. The method has two halves, and both of them changed the way history is written.

The first half is that he included everything. His *Records* begins with the mythical Yellow Emperor and runs to his own lifetime, and in between it carries not only the emperors but the merchants, the assassins, the jesters, the diviners, the men who governed the frontier and the men who cheated at court. He gave whole chapters to people no one had thought to write down. He also gave a chapter to the Xiongnu, the enemy, and described them as a people with a history, which was a thing the Han state did not want said.

The book is arranged in five parts, and the arrangement is itself an argument. There are annals for the emperors, in order. There are chronological tables, for reference. There are treatises on the great subjects, the calendar, the waterways, the economy, the rites, the music. There are hereditary houses, the families whose fates run alongside the throne's. And then there are the biographies, which are the part that changed everything: seventy chapters of individual lives, ranked and paired and set beside one another so that the reader can compare a good minister with a bad one, an assassin with a merchant, a general who died well with a general who lived badly. He put people next to each other and let the placing do the judging.

No one had written history like that. It is closer to a cabinet of specimens than to a chronicle, and the effect is that you cannot read it as a single authorised moral, because the next chapter will contradict the last one and the contradiction will be the point.

The second half of the method is harder and is the reason this book keeps returning to him. He preserves his sources where they disagree. Where the tradition gave him two versions of an event, he did not choose. He wrote both, and he sometimes wrote a remark to tell you why he could not choose. He knew that a historian's job leaves the past unsettled, and hands the reader something real to be uncertain about.

And he did one more thing, which is almost without precedent in the ancient world. He knew which side he was on and he would not flatter it. He had no reason to be fair to the Xiongnu, to the Qin, or to the emperor who had destroyed his body. He was fair to all three anyway, and where he was not fair he was visibly, humanly unfair, in a way the reader can see, which makes him usable.

That is why the six words I began with matter. He knew what the matter had been. He knew the argument against it. He left the decision naked, and let you watch an emperor decide, and left the verdict where verdicts belong, which is with the reader, centuries later, in a room the emperor never saw.

He did not write only about the state. He wrote about himself, once, and then closed the door.

The *Letter to Ren An* is the only surviving text in which Sima Qian speaks in his own voice, and it exists because he had to explain to a friend why he had not spoken up for him. Ren An had been condemned and had asked Sima Qian for help. Sima Qian could not help, and in the letter he tells him why: he was a man whom the court regarded as garbage, a mutilated official with no standing to petition anyone on anyone's behalf. And then he does the thing that makes the letter endure, which is to explain his whole life as the consequence of one decision, and to say that he would make it again.

After that the letter stops. He returns to the history. He does not ask for sympathy and he does not get any from himself.

The letter survives because someone kept a copy, and it has been read ever since as the founding document of a certain idea about writers: that the work is a substitute for the life that the work costs. That idea is true and it is also a trap, because it flatters everyone who has ever chosen a desk over a duty. Sima Qian does not flatter. The letter's argument is narrower and harder. He is saying that when he had a choice between two kinds of ruin, he chose the one that let him finish, and that he does not know whether he chose rightly, and that it is too late to find out, and that in any case the choice is made. There is no consolation in it. There is only a decision that has already been taken, described by a man who has to live inside it.

Sima Qian died around 86 BCE, a few years after the emperor. The *Records* was not published in his lifetime; it was kept by his family and came out later, and when it came out it was already doing the work. It became the model for every dynastic history that followed, and even the dynasties that hated him used his shape: annals for the emperors, treatises for the institutions, biographies for everyone else.

Which means that from the first century BCE onward, Chinese history has had a house style, and the house style was invented by a eunuch with a grudge and a commitment to fairness. That is a strange sentence to write, and I stand by it. The standard was set high enough that everybody after Sima Qian was measured against it, and low enough in its politics that the state could live with it, and those two things together are why we have a continuous written record of two thousand years in a quantity no other civilisation approaches.

Here is what all of this has to do with the book you are holding. Everything I have told you about the Qin, and about Liu Bang, and everything I am about to tell you about Wang Mang and Taizong and all the rest, arrives through a document written by a person with a position. That is a reason to read it the way Sima Qian read his own sources: keeping the door open for the second version, noticing where the six words are doing the work of six hundred, and refusing the comfort of a record that has no seams.

He invented that. Before Sima Qian, history was mostly a list of kings and what they won. After him it was a problem.

Sima Qian was destroyed by a system he served, in a punishment designed to humiliate rather than kill, and he chose the humiliation on the calculation that the book was worth more than the man. Then he wrote the book, and the book was fair to the men who had done it to him, and it went on being fair, and there was nothing in it that functioned as revenge at all.

## Chapter 06 — The Usurper Who Read the Classics

On the sixth of October, 23 CE, a sixty-eight-year-old man climbed to a terrace above his burning capital and took hold of a measuring instrument. He was holding it because the classics he had spent his life reading said that a ruler facing the end should hold it, and he was, to the last minute, the kind of man who obeyed the book.

His name was Wang Mang. He had been emperor for fourteen years. The city below him was Chang'an, and the men coming through it were a peasant army who had painted their eyebrows red so that they could recognise each other in a fight. They were not interested in the classics. They killed him and cut him apart. In the days that followed, one of his organs was hung up as a trophy, and when it was taken down a crowd of people tore at it until there was nothing left.

The official history gives him, as a final scene, a speech in which he insists that Heaven is on his side and that the army outside has no authority. It is almost certainly a scene the historians wrote for him. It is also perfectly in character. That is the problem with Wang Mang, and it is why he gets a chapter: he is the one ruler in this book who did everything his age believed a good ruler should do, and the result was the worst decade anyone in the empire had seen in two hundred years.

I am going to tell his story backwards, because forward it sounds like competence, and backwards it sounds like what it was.

In the year 23 the empire was in open rebellion across most of the east. The Red Eyebrows were one of half a dozen movements, and they were not the largest. The famine was general. The Yellow River was still finding its way after a change of course that had put an enormous stretch of farmland under water, and the region had been the granary of the north. Coinage had been reformed and re-reformed until nobody trusted it. The state had been at war with the northern nomads for a decade over a point of etiquette.

That is the picture going backward. Now go back further.

The disasters that ended Wang Mang did not begin with the rebellion. They began with the reforms, and the reforms began with total sincerity. This is the part of the story that is hardest to keep in view, because the word *usurper* comes with a preloaded verdict, and the verdict is that he was a fraud. He was a scholar, and the difference mattered. That was worse.

Wang Mang was born around 45 BCE into the family of the empress dowager, which gave him access to everything. What he did with that access was unusual. The other men of his clan took money, built houses, and enjoyed themselves; Wang Mang studied, lived plainly, gave gifts, and cultivated a reputation for perfect Confucian conduct. When his own son killed a servant, Wang Mang made him kill himself. The sources offer that as an example of his integrity. Read it again with the age of the son in mind, and consider that a man who will destroy his own child to keep a principle is not the same kind of person as a man who breaks the principle to save the child. He is more dangerous, because he has proved that nothing on earth outranks the principle.

He rose the way a perfect minister rises. He was made regent for a child emperor. He gathered around him the scholars who had spent their lives on the difficult texts, the ones that describe how the earliest kings governed, and he listened to them. He was offered honours and refused them, publicly, several times, which is both a Confucian gesture of humility and the most effective way to be given more.

In 9 CE the fiction was retired. The child emperor was persuaded to abdicate, Wang Mang accepted the Mandate of Heaven, and the Han was declared over and replaced by a new dynasty he called Xin, the New.

Everything in that paragraph is a Confucian procedure. The classics describe how the sage kings took power. They describe a ruler who is virtuous enough that the throne comes to him. Wang Mang had arranged to be exactly that man, and everyone around him, the whole scholarly class, agreed that the signs were there. This is the danger of a philosophy with a mechanism built into it: the mechanism can be operated.

Now walk forward again, and watch the reforms arrive.

His programme came from the text called the *Rites of Zhou*, which describes, or invents, the institutions of the early Zhou. If the ancients had a well-field system in which land was held in common, then the well-field system must be restored. So he nationalised the land, renamed it the King's Fields, and forbade its sale. If the ancients had no slaves, then slaves must be freed, and he prohibited the sale of human beings, though he left the institution itself intact, which meant that the people he freed were immediately re-enslaved by debt and the law could not see it. If the ancients used particular weights, particular coin shapes, particular names for offices, then all of those must be corrected.

And corrected. And corrected again. He reformed the currency four times in under a decade, retiring each coin in favour of the next, which is a way of confiscating everyone's savings on a schedule without ever admitting it. He renamed provinces, counties, and offices. He changed job titles so often that the bureaucracy could not keep track of itself, and a governor might receive an edict addressed to a title that no longer existed describing a territory that had been renamed twice.

None of this was corrupt. It was worse. It was *consistent*. He was applying a correct principle to a world that did not match the principle, and whenever the world failed to match, he assumed the world was wrong.

Take the land reform, because it is the clearest case. To restore a communal field system you must first know who holds which field, in what quantity, under what obligation. That means a survey. The Han had registers, but the registers were exactly what the great families had spent two generations corrupting: they had moved their land into the names of dependants and clients until the state's map of the countryside said that most of the best soil in China belonged to nobody in particular. Wang Mang ordered the land nationalised anyway, on the theory that the ancient kings had done it, and the theory had no answer to the question of whose field was whose. The reform therefore confiscated nothing and enraged everyone. The families who held the land understood that a state which announces it owns everything is a state that is about to decide, person by person, who gets to keep what, and no one who can buy an exemption waits around to find out.

The currency tells the same story faster. He abolished the coins in circulation and issued new ones, then issued more, and made some of them out of materials that no one could use. Among his creations were tokens shaped like knives, tokens in the shape of tortoise shells, and a set of values denominated so elaborately that ordinary commerce could not calculate a price. He forbade the old coins, then discovered that the provinces went on using them, then postponed the prohibition, then reimposed it. Each of these reversals was reasonable in isolation. Together they taught the empire that money was not a thing to be trusted and that the safest asset was grain, which the state could not print.

And underneath all of it, the land itself was failing. In 11 CE the Yellow River changed its course, a thing it had done before and would do again, and the flood drowned an enormous tract of the north China plain. The state that could not manage its currency now faced a relief problem across the largest food-producing region in the empire, and the machinery that was supposed to move grain was the machinery that had just been renamed twice. People ate each other. The sources say so in the flat way that sources say such things when everyone reading them already knows.

There was a symbol for the whole reign, and it is the northern frontier.

The Xiongnu had been, since the Han's beginnings, the empire's great neighbour and great problem: a steppe confederation that could not be conquered and could be bought. The Han had bought them for generations with marriages and goods, which is what Wudi had tried to end with wars. Wang Mang's scholars looked at the arrangement and saw a humiliation with a name they did not like. The Xiongnu were called the *Xiongnu*, and one of the classical usages of the characters was as a term for slaves. So he sent an edict redesignating them. They were to be called, in all official documents, the "submissive slaves."

Their ruler was to be given a new seal, which reduced his title by a rank, and the old seal was to be surrendered. The messenger who delivered it mishandled the exchange, the old seal was broken, and the khan, who had been willing to be bought, discovered that he was now the enemy of an emperor who could not be bought off. The war that followed lasted the whole of Wang Mang's reign. It cost him the treasury, the frontier, and eventually the generals he needed at home.

A state was destroyed because a scholar could not leave a word alone.

Go back a little further, before the reforms, to the thing that made all of the rest possible, which was the collapse of the argument that the Han deserved to rule.

Wang Mang came to power in a country that had lost faith in its dynasty. The Han of his day was a court of child emperors and an empress dowager's relatives, and the throne had become a prize for whoever stood nearest it. The Mandate of Heaven, the idea that Heaven gives and withdraws the right to rule according to the virtue of the ruler, had once been a story the Han told to explain why they had replaced the Qin. By the end of the first century BCE, it had become a story everyone could read, and what they read was that a corrupt court had forfeited it. The scholars had been saying so in their memorials for years.

So when Wang Mang took the throne, he was not stealing something the country valued, he was answering a vacancy. His whole legitimacy rested on the belief that the classics contained a description of good government, and that a man who followed the description would be rewarded by Heaven. He believed it. So did the class that made him. And when the result was famine, the problem was not that they stopped believing it. The problem was that the belief had no way of telling them what had gone wrong.

The end came quickly. The Red Eyebrows broke into the capital. Wang Mang, on the terrace, was still consulting the texts. He was killed by a soldier, who took his seal. The seal passed up the chain until a general had it, and the Han was declared restored by a man who was a descendant of the Han founders and had been elected by the same class of scholars who had made Wang Mang.

The dynasty came back. It called itself the Han and it is counted as the same dynasty, though it had been broken in half, and it lasted another two hundred years from a new capital in the east. Its historians wrote the story of the interregnum, and they wrote Wang Mang as the villain of it, which is why you have probably heard of him as a usurper and not as anything else.

But read the story they wrote and notice what they cannot conceal. They record his frugality. They record that he gave his wealth away. They record that he studied, that he fasted, that he did the ritual observances with more care than anyone, that he freed his own slaves, that he punished his own family. The catalogue of his virtues is longer than the catalogue of almost any emperor in this book, and the result was the worst decade of the era.

What the catalogue proves is that virtue and judgement are two different things, and that a system which tests the first cannot test the second. The Han, when they wrote him, had an obvious use for him: a warning about ambition. That use hides the real warning, which is about certainty.

I said at the start of this chapter that he is the one ruler who did what his age believed a good ruler should do. I want to be exact about that. He did not merely claim to be Confucian; he organised the entire apparatus of government around the Confucian canon, treated it as a specification rather than a tradition, and required the empire to conform to it. The classics were his manual, not his mask.

When the Han authors wrote him down as a hypocrite, they were protecting something. They were protecting the possibility that the canon is fine and that the men who use it are the problem. Wang Mang is the test of that possibility, and he fails it.

He had a predecessor in this book, and he did not know it, because Li Si was a thousand years of memory away and a different kind of man. Li Si watched two rats and decided that position was everything. Wang Mang read the ancient poems and decided that virtue was everything. Both of them ended up in a ruined city with their possessions in pieces and the machinery they had built being dismantled by a man in a hurry. The rooms had changed. The rats had not.

Somewhere in the north, in the years after his death, the empire's frontier was rebuilt and the war with the Xiongnu ended. The symbols were returned to their old names. The Han did not need to defeat Wang Mang's ideas. It only needed to wait for him to run out of money, which took fourteen years, and then pretend he had never been sincere.

## Chapter 07 — A Poet Among Warlords

An edict. A poem. A tally of the dead. Here they are, in the order a bureaucrat would have filed them, and in the order a man would have lived them: nothing.

First, the edict. In 184 CE the court announced the formation of a new force of provincial militia to put down a rebellion in the east, and delegated to local officials the authority to raise and command men. The court had a good reason. The rebellion was real, it was enormous, and the standing armies were not where it was. The delegation was temporary and the men were supposed to disband when it ended.

Second, the poem. It is by a general of that era, and it is short enough to hold in the head:

*White bones are exposed in the open field. For a thousand li, not a cock crows. A hundred of the living — one survives.*

Third, the tally. The *Records of the Later Han* and its continuations give the dead of the rebellion and the wars that followed in numbers that run into the hundreds of thousands, then the millions, and modern historians treat the figures with a caution that the sources do not invite, because a census is not a body count and the registers themselves were the thing the fighting destroyed.

Now put the three next to each other and notice that they are the same document. The edict created the poem. A court that delegates the right to raise armies has decided that its own monopoly on force is less important than a rebellion, and once that decision is made it cannot be unmade, because the men who have raised armies now have armies. The poem is the receipt.

The general's name was Cao Cao, and he is the reason this is a chapter and not a footnote.

He was born in 155, into the household of a man who had been adopted by a eunuch, which placed him at the court's edge: close enough to be educated with the sons of the powerful, far enough to be mocked for his origins. He was a capable administrator and a dangerous young man, and his first post was in the capital, where he had a gate made and had it used to flog a favourite of the palace who broke the curfew, which tells you most of what you need to know about his relationship with authority and his opinion of his own nerve.

Then the empire fell apart, in the particular way of a state that has been held together by a story.

The story was the Mandate of Heaven, and for two hundred years of the Han it had worked well enough that people believed it. By 184 it had stopped working, and the reason is not mysterious. The court had been seized by eunuch factions and then by the empress's relatives and then by eunuchs again. Emperors were children who died at nineteen. Offices were sold. The land was being swallowed by great families who found ways to shift the tax burden onto the smallholders beneath them, and the smallholders, who were the state, were leaving the land.

When a state can no longer protect the people whose taxes are its reason for existing, the people find an explanation. The explanation that spread across the east in the 180s was called the Way of Great Peace, and it was a millenarian movement that promised a new age and, in the meantime, healing and community and a structure that actually worked. The Yellow Turbans, as the authorities called them, were a rebellion built out of the failure of the state to be a state.

They were defeated, in the end, by the men the court had empowered to defeat them, who then could not be disarmed.

The movement deserves a moment, because it explains what the empire had lost. It was led by a man named Zhang Jiao, who taught that the world was entering a new cycle, that sickness came from sin, and that repentance and confession and a particular kind of communal life would bring the great peace that gave the movement its name. Whatever you make of the doctrine, the structure around it worked. It organised across eight provinces. It enrolled people the empire had stopped serving. When the court finally moved against it, one of the reasons it moved fast was that a defector had told it the movement's own officials were corresponding by government post, which is only possible if the movement had penetrated the state's own apparatus. Calling it superstition would be the mistake. This was a rival administration.

Out of that decade come the great names of the period, and out of their quarrels comes the collapse. There was Dong Zhuo, who seized the capital and moved it, burning it as he went. There was a coalition of provincial lords formed to remove him, which succeeded in nothing except proving that the lords could field armies against a common enemy and choose not to. And there was Cao Cao, who was cleverer than the others and lasted longer.

Cao Cao's rise is a series of decisions that each look cold and each turn out to be correct. In 196 he took hold of the figure of the emperor, who by then was an unhappy young man wandering with a remnant of a court, and installed him in Cao Cao's own territory. He did not depose him. He governed in the emperor's name and issued the emperor's orders, and for the next twenty-four years the fiction held, and it is the most important fact of his career.

Consider what that fiction was worth. The empire had fallen apart because nothing enforced the story by which it was held together, and the story was that the emperor ruled because Heaven wanted him to. If the story were simply discarded, then any general with an army could be a king, and every province would have one, and the result would be permanent war. By keeping the emperor alive and issuing orders in his name, Cao Cao held a monopoly on the story. He was the only man whose commands were legitimate, because he was the only man who was speaking for the throne.

He did not need to be emperor. He needed everyone else to not be emperor, and the way to ensure that was to have an emperor who agreed with him.

By 200 he had defeated his greatest rival at the battle of Guandu, which was mostly a victory of logistics over numbers: Cao Cao, the poorer and weaker commander, held the river and burned his enemy's supply depots in a night raid and won it with a fraction of the men. After that there was no one north of the Yangzi who could beat him. He spent the rest of his life trying to cross the Yangzi, and never did.

In 208 he came south with an army of the north and was destroyed on the river by a smaller southern force using fire ships, at the battle that ended his ambition. Afterwards he consolidated, took the title King of Wei, ruled the north, and let the fiction of the Han emperor stand until he died in 220. His son took the throne within the year.

He had done something in the meantime that outlasted all of it, and it is the most consequential thing in the chapter. Three times, in three edicts, Cao Cao announced that he would employ talent wherever he found it, and that he did not require the men he employed to be virtuous. The wording is careful and the thrust is unmistakable: if a man can do the work, the fact that he is corrupt or shameless is not a reason to leave him doing nothing. In a period when the entire legitimacy of the throne rested on the claim that good government came from good character, the most powerful man in the north stood up and said that character was not the qualification.

You can read that as cynicism. It is more interesting as a report from the field. He had watched the Confucian examination of character at work in the Han court, and he had seen what it produced: men recommended for their filial piety, their families' influence, and their skill at being recommended. The virtue test had become a machine for reproducing the elite that administered it. So he proposed to skip it. The empire would be run by men who could run it, and the question of whether they were good could be left, indefinitely, to the philosophers.

That is the argument of this chapter arriving in an official document, which is why this chapter is built out of documents.

That is the outline. Now the poem.

Cao Cao wrote verse, and he is a serious poet: not a general who wrote, but one of the founders of a style. Two of his poems are the ones everyone knows. One is a meditation on mortality, the *Short Song Style*, which opens by telling you to drink while you can, because a man's life is like the morning dew, gone before the sun. The other is a lament for the dead of the years of civil war, and it is where the line about the white bones in the fields comes from.

Here is what is strange, and what I keep coming back to. The poem about the bones was written by a man who had recently been responsible for a great killing of his own.

In 193 and 194, during his campaigns in the east, Cao Cao's army attacked the province commanded by a rival named Tao Qian. The accounts say that he ordered a massacre, that the killing went on for days, that tens of thousands died and that the rivers were blocked with bodies. The numbers vary wildly between sources, and the sources are hostile to him, and some modern historians read the whole sequence as an atrocity inflated by his enemies into a legend. I do not know which is true, and neither does anyone else, and I am going to leave it in that state rather than choose for you.

What is not in dispute is that he then wrote the poem about the bones.

The instinct of the reader is to make this into a contradiction and then resolve it, and there are two comfortable ways to resolve it. The cynical way is that the poem is propaganda, a man singing about the ruin he caused so as to appear the only one who cared. The sentimental way is that the poem is remorse, that the poet in him was horrified by the general. Both may be true. Neither is necessary, and both make him sound more coherent than he was.

I think the poem is not about remorse at all. I think it is what it says it is: an observation of a country that has been killed, written by a man who is one of the killers, and who got to observe it because he was one of the killers. A person who has not done these things does not get the view. He wrote the plainest description of a famine and a war that survives from the period, and the reason his description is plain is that he was standing where nobody else with a pen was standing.

I am not offering that as an excuse. It is a statement about how literature and power share a room. The best witness of a slaughter is sometimes the man who ordered it, because he is the only one who survived it with time to write.

There is a second thing in his verse that matters more for the book's argument, and it is not the horror but the weariness. He writes about age, about how many men he has outlived, about how few of his friends are left, about the impossibility of holding anything. The tone is the tone of a man who has won everything he said he wanted and finds that the wanting has not stopped.

And around him, while he was winning, the best minds of the empire were giving up on the whole idea of governing.

The phenomenon is called Pure Conversation, and it was not a philosophy so much as a habit of mind among the educated elite in the ruined north. The Confucian canon had produced the empire, and the empire had collapsed. So the brightest men of the next generation turned to the older texts that Confucians had kept at the margins: Laozi, Zhuangzi, the book of changes. They wrote commentary on them. They argued about being and non-being, about whether a thing can be asserted to exist at all, and they did it in elegant conversations that everyone agreed were the finest thing the age produced.

Their argument was reasonable. If the Confucian system was a set of rules for running a state, and the state had just failed more comprehensively than any state in memory, then the rules had been tested and found wanting. If actions always produced unintended consequences in the way the last century had, then perhaps the correct posture was not to act. The Daoist preference for doing nothing, for yielding, for refusing to be a name in the record, looked less like a mystical pose and more like a description of what a decent man should do with his life.

It was also the most sophisticated writing of the century. Two young men, Wang Bi and He Yan, produced commentaries on the *Laozi* and the *Zhuangzi* and the *Book of Changes* that are still read, and made the case that behind the Confucian rules there lies a deeper structure, a pattern of things, that the rules were crude attempts to describe. That is not an escape from politics; it is a claim about where reality is, and it was a claim the empire could not answer, because the empire's own philosophy had just been shown to be a set of rules with no floor under them.

The most famous practitioners are usually grouped as the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove, which is a later packaging of men who did not all know each other, and whose recorded behaviour runs from drinking in a state of nature to refusing to take office under a usurper. Some of them were brilliant. Some of them were simply rude, and the rudeness is remembered because it was a way of saying that the court's opinion did not matter, which in a period of coups was both a statement of principle and a method of staying alive.

It was also, from a certain angle, the most convenient possible philosophy for men who had lost the argument about power and wished to sound as if they had chosen to leave it.

The elite who talked about nothing were the same elite whose families were acquiring the land and the offices. The philosophy of withdrawal ran alongside an unprecedented grab for position. Both were true. The Pure Conversation that looked like renunciation was partly renunciation and partly a very refined way of retiring from a game you could not win while keeping your estate.

The next four hundred years are called the period of division, and they are the longest interruption in the whole of this book. The north was ruled by a succession of dynasties that were often not Chinese. The south was ruled by a succession of dynasties that were, and could not beat the north. Into the ruins of the Confucian state came a religion from outside, and it did what the defeated philosophy could not do: it gave the empire a new story about why the world was as it was. The story arrived along the trade roads and it came from India, and the next emperor in this book decided that he would be its best friend.

He fed it, and it ate him.

## Chapter 08 — The Emperor Who Fed the Monks

From the edicts of the emperor Xiao Yan, who is called Liang Wudi, in the first decades of the sixth century:

> We have caused temples to be built in every province, and caused the scriptures to be copied and distributed, and caused the monks to be fed. Let the granaries of the state be opened for this purpose without stinting, for the merit of it is beyond calculation.

And then, from a Chan Buddhist record compiled about five hundred years later, an account of a meeting that may never have happened:

> The emperor asked, "How much merit have I earned, building temples, copying scriptures, and supporting monks?"
> The master said, "None whatsoever."
> The emperor asked, "What is the highest meaning of the holy truth?"
> The master said, "Vast emptiness. Nothing holy."
> The emperor asked, "Who is this, then, standing before me?"
> The master said, "I do not know."
> The emperor did not understand, and the master went away.

I am putting these two documents side by side at the top of the chapter because the distance between them is the chapter. One is a real emperor speaking, in his own official voice, about what he believes he is achieving. The other is a later tradition in which a master tells that same emperor that the achievement is worth nothing. And the tradition — this is the part I want you to hold — is almost certainly not a record of an interview. It is a story that the successors of Bodhidharma told about themselves for centuries, in which the emperor is a straight man. It is a legend, and it is doing its work.

So we have the emperor on one side and the legend on the other, and what actually happened to him in between.

Start with what happened. It is not a mystery. Then come back.

Xiao Yan was a member of the imperial family of the Southern Qi, one of the short-lived dynasties that ruled the south while the north was divided among its conquerors. He took the throne in 502, at the age of about thirty-seven, and he held it for forty-seven years, which is a long reign by any standard and an astonishing one for the sixth century.

His state was the Liang, and his capital was Jiankang, which is Nanjing, on the Yangzi. It was the most cultivated city in the world at that time. This is not a flourish: the south had kept the libraries and the scholars and the literary tradition while the north was being fought over, and under Xiao Yan the court became a place where poetry, calligraphy, and above all Buddhist scholarship were the currency of status. He wrote commentaries on Buddhist texts himself. He debated monks and held assemblies. He built temples on a scale that appears in the records as a list of numbers, and the numbers are enormous.

The scale is the thing that is hard to hold. The records for the Liang count more than two thousand temples in the capital region alone and something on the order of eighty thousand ordained monks and nuns in the state, and even allowing for the enthusiasm of religious historians, the order of magnitude is clear. A state with a few million registered adults had removed a percentage of its workforce from the tax rolls and made it permanent, and the removal was not a scandal because the removal was, in the emperor's own understanding, the point.

And he did the thing that everybody knows about, which is that he tried to become a monk.

Four times, by the usual count, he left the palace and entered a monastery and refused to return to the throne. And four times the court sent envoys and paid a sum to the monastic community in exchange for the emperor's return, the way a family redeems a hostage. The sums are recorded. They are large. The ritual was understood by everyone involved as a piece of statecraft as well as devotion: the emperor of China abasing himself before the sangha, and the sangha accepting the humiliation of the throne as a donation.

Now hold that image, because it is the best thing on offer, and it is genuinely funny. The most powerful man in the south stood in a monk's robe and made his own ministers negotiate his return, and the negotiation was a gift.

Here is the question the story raises, and it is not the question the legend raises. The legend asks whether his merit was real. The real question is what he was buying.

Consider what the south was, before the disasters, because the book has not given it its due. While the north was being fought over by dynasties that were half steppe and half court, the south kept the scholars. The great families who had fled south with the Jin in the fourth century had gone on copying, annotating, and arguing, and by the sixth century the literary culture of Jiankang was the most refined in the world. Poetry was the coin of status. Calligraphy was a career. Scholars compiled anthologies and wrote criticism, and the emperor himself was one of the best of them, which is why his devotion to the sangha was not the hobby of a barbarian but a programme of a very cultured man.

Because a Buddhist monastery in sixth-century China was a tax haven before it was a place of worship. Land donated to the sangha did not pay the taxes that land owed the state. Men who took ordination were removed from the registers. So every ordination was a small subtraction from the state's capacity to tax and conscript, and every endowment of land was a transfer of wealth out of the fiscal system and into an institution that answered to its own monastic rule and not to the bureaucracy.

The institution understood its own position exactly. Ordination was not a private act of conscience, it was a certificate, and the certificates became a market. A man could buy a monk's licence the way a man buys a position, and the licence carried an exemption from the tax and the corvée, and the exemption was worth more than the licence, and so the market did what markets do. By the end of the period the monasteries were landlords on an enormous scale, holding estates that had been given by the dying, the guilty, and the tax-averse, and lending against the harvest at rates the farmers could not escape, and collecting the rents that the state could no longer reach. The sangha was a competing fisc, wearing a robe and answering to a rule about compassion. That is true of any state that embraces an organised religion. It was true of Liang, and it was true at a scale that would have alarmed a finance minister.

The emperor knew all of it. He was the state. And the record of his reign is a record of a man who kept giving the state away and being blessed for it, and then gave more.

This creates the chapter's real argument, which is about what a religion does to a ruler who adopts it as his own. The obvious reading of Liang Wudi is that he was sincere and the sincerity was beautiful. The second reading is that he was a fool who bankrupted his country for incense. Both are available, and both let the reader off the hook.

The harder reading is that sincerity and self-interest were the same act. He was, by every indication, a genuinely devout man. He was also a ruler who understood that in a state held together by a story, the story is infrastructure. The Mandate of Heaven had been the story for a thousand years, and by the sixth century it had been through Wang Mang and the collapse of the Han, and it was thin. Buddhism had the advantage of being *new* and of being *true* in the way that the classics were not: it explained suffering, it promised a future, and it could be believed by a peasant and a court scholar at the same time without either condescending to the other.

So he backed it. He fed it, built for it, copied its texts, and put his own body into its rituals. And it worked, for a while, in the way a state religion works: it gave the Liang court a legitimacy that did not depend on the Han's exhausted precedent.

What it did not do — and the chapter has to be honest, because this is where the comfort fails — was make him merciful, or make him safe.

The catastrophe came from the north, as it usually did, and it came in a form that no amount of merit could have prevented. A general named Hou Jing had defected to the Liang, bringing his troops, and had been received and rewarded. He then discovered that the court was negotiating to surrender him back to the other side as part of a peace deal, and he revolted. He took the capital.

What followed was one of the great cultural catastrophes of the period, and it is worth naming, because the book has been talking about institutions and it should stop for a moment and count the books. When the capital fell, the fighting and the looting destroyed much of the imperial library and the collections of the great families. The southern aristocracy that had carried the literary tradition out of the north in the fourth century was broken as a class; its members were killed, or fled, or survived as dependents of a new military order. The thing that was lost in 548 and after shows up as a gap in the record, the texts that no longer exist because the only copy burned in a courtyard in a city that a general had decided to hold.

He took the capital in 548, and the emperor did not flee. He stayed in his palace in Jiankang and refused to leave. The city burned around him. Then he was placed under siege inside his own compound.

What happened next is where the legend and the record agree, which is rare. The record says he died in 549, at eighty-five, of starvation, in a palace under the control of a general who had walled him in and cut off his food. Some accounts say he died of illness brought on by hunger, which is the same thing described with more courtesy.

The emperor who had fed the sangha starved to death.

I want to be careful not to make a parable out of it. The sententious version of the chapter writes itself, and it is the version to distrust: the ruler who gave everything to the priests, repaid by a priestless death. That is not what happened. What happened is that a state with a command problem was taken by a general with a command, and the emperor's religion was irrelevant to the outcome. Buddhism did not starve him. Hou Jing did.

But the story is not nonsense either. The reason it survives is that it is *legible*. A culture tells stories about itself with a shape, and the shape the tradition needed was: he asked how much merit he had, and the answer was none. Then he discovered that merit was not a currency. That story is so useful that it has been told for fifteen hundred years by people who have no particular stake in the historical Bodhidharma, and it is doing the work of a chapter of political philosophy that nobody wrote.

Because here is what it says. It says that a ruler cannot purchase the thing that makes a ruler good. He can buy temples and feed monks and copy scriptures, and the accounting of merit stays at zero, because the merit is not a quantity of anything. The question he asked the monk was the question of his whole reign: what do I get for this. And the answer was the answer to the whole idea of governing by donation, which is that you get nothing, because the thing you were trying to buy does not exist.

Read the edicts again. They are magnificent. Let the granaries be opened without stinting, for the merit of it is beyond calculation. He was a sincere man and a serious one and he had rebuilt the state after a coup. He ended in a sealed room. The last thing the tradition has him do is ask a question about his balance.

There is one more thing, and it is the one that connects this chapter to the rest of the book.

What followed his death was the sack of the city and the end of the southern literary class, and then the reunification of the empire under the Sui, and the thing to notice is that the religious establishment he had spent a lifetime funding simply transferred its loyalty. The monasteries took the new dynasty's protection and went on collecting their rents. The institutions did not mourn him and did not need to.

The temples survived him. The land they held stayed out of the registers. The monasteries he endowed went on being wealthy institutions in the south for centuries, and the dynasties that came after him had to fight the same fight all over again, every one of them, in exactly the same terms: a state that needs the registers against an institution that is defined by being exempt from them. Liang Wudi did not solve it. He made it worse, and then he made it permanent, by giving the sangha enough wealth that no future ruler could take it back without a war.

He was eighty-five when he died, held inside his own capital by the general who had surrendered to him and then turned. The record says he asked for honey, and that there was none to be had.

## Chapter 09 — The Canal and the Gate of Examinations

The canal is eighteen hundred kilometres long, or it was when they finished, and they never really finished. It runs from the region of Beijing in the north to the region of Hangzhou in the south, and it does it by connecting rivers that already existed rather than by cutting one channel through the countryside, which is why it is a system and not a ditch.

It was dug by men who were taken from their fields on a schedule and set to work in the mud, in a state that had just finished conquering them, and it killed a great many of them. We do not know how many, because the sources count the labour army in the hundreds of thousands and the dead in numbers that have been rounded by the enthusiasm of later historians, and no register survives that would let us correct them.

It also made the reunification of China possible, and it kept the reunification working for five hundred years, and both of those things are true at the same time, and the whole chapter is about the fact that they are the same fact.

The dynasty that dug it was the Sui, and the Sui lasted thirty-seven years. It had two emperors who mattered, father and son, and between them they did more to the Chinese state than any imperial family in this book, and they are remembered mainly as a byword for tyranny. That is the shape of the problem. The Canal and the Gate of Examinations are the two most durable institutions in this book, and the men who made them were destroyed by making them.

It is worth stating what had been lost, because three hundred years is hard to feel. When the Han ended, the empire had one administration, one elite curriculum, and a single set of expectations about how a state should be run. By the sixth century there were two of everything. The south had the old families and the old books and a court that argued about poetry. The north had been ruled for a century and a half by the Northern Wei, a dynasty of steppe origin that had adopted Chinese government wholesale, moved its capital south to Luoyang, banned its own language at court, and required its officials to wear Chinese dress. That programme of adoption is one of the most consequential things in Chinese history and it is also the reason reunification was possible, because the north that emerged from it was neither steppe nor entirely southern, and it had learned how to administer a mixed population.

What the north never had was grain. What the south never had was an army that could beat the north. The Sui matched the two.

Start with the father.

Yang Jian was a general of the Northern Zhou, one of the successor states in the north, and in 581 he took the throne in a coup that was so well-managed the sources struggle to make it interesting. He renamed the dynasty Sui, moved the capital to a new city built to his specification, and set about the work that had not been done for three hundred years: making one state out of two halves of a country.

His reforms are the ones a modern state would recognise, and they are worth listing, because the Tang inherited every one of them. He reorganised the local administration, cutting the number of counties and the layers of officials, so that the state could actually reach the villages that paid it. He reformed the land system so that holdings were allotted by the state against the obligation to pay tax, which is a way of saying that the state asserted ownership of the soil and rented it back on terms. He reformed the militia so that soldiers were farmers in the off-season, which made an army cheap and made it slow. And he gave the state a legal code, the Kaihuang Code, which the Tang revised and kept and which shaped East Asian law for centuries.

None of this is glamorous. All of it is the kind of thing that makes a reunification stick. A conqueror who does not do this work wins a war and loses the peace, which is precisely what the Qin had done a thousand years earlier, and Yang Jian knew the precedent, because everybody in his position knew the precedent.

The halves had drifted apart. This is not a metaphor. The south and the north had different dialects, different elites, different literary traditions, and different religions, and they had been ruled separately since the fall of the Han. The south was ruled by Chinese dynasties of the old type, with the old bureaucracy and the old culture. The north had been ruled for a long stretch by non-Chinese dynasties who had adopted Chinese government while keeping their own armies, and who had made a compromise of their own between the steppe and the sown.

Reunifying them was not a matter of winning a battle, though there was one: the Sui army crossed the Yangzi in 589, and the conquest of the Chen dynasty was quick. It was a matter of making the two halves into one administration, and that meant moving things.

Grain was the thing that needed moving. The north was the political centre, and the north could not feed itself. The rice and the surplus came from the south, and the rivers ran the wrong way, because in China the great rivers run west to east and the state needed them to run north to south. So it built the links.

Yang Jian began it. His son Yang Guang, who is called Sui Yangdi, finished the largest section and doubled the scale, and it is the son who gets the blame, because the son is the one who went too far.

Yang Guang took the throne in 604, in circumstances the sources describe as suspicious and everyone at the time understood without being told, and he had a plan. He would move the capital east. He would link the canal end to end. He would reform the local government, and he would reform the way the government recruited its officials.

That last item is the one that changed the world, and it started smaller than it should have.

For centuries the Han and its successors had recruited officials by recommendation. A man of good family would be recommended to the court by a local notable, and the recommendation would be for his character, and the character would be certified by the notable, and the notable would be the head of one of the same great families from which the candidates came. The system had a name, and by the end it was called the nine-rank system, and it had done what such systems do: it had turned into a machine for confirming the children of the men who ran it.

The Sui did not abolish that. What they began was narrower: a set of examinations, held by the state, on which appointment could be made. The exact origin of the imperial examination is disputed, and the dispute is about the reign, not the idea, and the idea is the important thing. If the state examines, then the state decides who is qualified, and what qualifies a man is not his birth but his reading.

That is a change of enormous power, and it takes two or three generations to become real, and when it becomes real it remakes the society. A boy who can pass an examination is a boy whose family no longer needs a patron. The state has agreed to be the patron. And the boy who can pass it will spend his childhood in the books the state has chosen, which means the state has agreed to decide what it is worth knowing.

Yang Guang's reign turned the examination from an idea into a road, and then he took that road south, and the rest of his reign is the story of how a ruler with a good idea and a working canal and a large army can spend all three of them in under fifteen years.

The canal was the beginning of it, and the canal is where the cost is visible. The conscription figures are staggering and probably inflated, but "probably inflated" is doing a lot of work when the base figure is a million. Men were levied in the autumn and worked through the winter. The sections through the low-lying east required embankments and locks and dredging, and the work was done by hand, with baskets. The later accounts of the Sui collapse dwell on the canal, and they dwell on it because it is the perfect image of a state that has decided that the country is a project.

The canal's purpose was not tourism and not only grain; it was the movement of an army, and the army that needed moving was aimed at the north-east, and before it went there it had to be assembled and supplied at a scale no previous Chinese state had attempted. Which brings us to the war.

Then he went to war with the north-east.

Goguryeo was a kingdom covering the northern Korean peninsula and a stretch of what is now Manchuria, and Yang Guang invaded it three times, beginning in 612. The first campaign is one of the great logistical catastrophes of the pre-modern world. The army he assembled was enormous, and it moved slowly, and it was fed by the canal, and it failed in front of the walls of the border cities and came apart on the retreat. The second and third campaigns did no better, and by the third the empire behind the army had begun to break.

The first campaign has a detail that the sources preserve and that says more than the casualty figures. Yang Guang went to the front himself, with a court and a ceremonial guard, and he insisted on directing the campaign from behind a screen while the officers waited outside for orders. The instructions were slow. The army of a million moved at the speed of its slowest and most formal part, and while it moved, the enemy refused battle and withdrew into fortified cities, and the weather and the supply lines did the killing for them.

Here is the mechanism, and it is the same mechanism as 184, which is why the book keeps returning to it. To fight a war on that scale the state conscripts the farmers who grow the food. To move the food to the front it uses the farmers who are still home. Every man at the front is a field unplanted. Every boat on the canal is a boat not carrying rice to a market. By the time the army is in Manchuria, the granaries behind it are empty and the men who would have filled them are either soldiers or corpses or run into the hills.

Rebellion began in the north in 611, before the first campaign had really failed, and it spread. Yang Guang, in response, did the thing that rulers do when a project fails: he did more of it. He went south to the canal country, to the prosperous region he had enriched, and there, in the city that is now Yangzhou, in 618, his own guards killed him.

The Sui was over. It had lasted thirty-seven years, and it had produced the canal, the examinations, the reunified state, a reformed legal code, and a new capital, and it had produced them at a price that its own people would not pay.

The examination's first generations are worth one more look, because the institution is the half of the Sui inheritance that history has judged more kindly, and the kindness deserves scrutiny. In the Tang the examination did not replace the old routes to office. Aristocratic families still placed their sons. The examination was a supplement, a route for men the great houses had not chosen, and because it was a supplement it could be, for a while, honest. It became the main road only under the Song, three centuries later, and by the time it was the main road it had become something the Sui could not have imagined: the centre of an entire culture, the thing a family organised a childhood around, and a machine for producing a class.

The disorder that followed was shorter than the disorder after the Han, and the reason is worth noting, because it tells you what had changed. The civil war that ended the Sui took about a decade, and it did not produce four hundred years of division. It produced one dynasty, quickly, because the institutions Yang Jian had built were still there and were still worth capturing. A warlord who took the capital in 618 did not have to invent an administration. He inherited one. That is what institutional work does: it does not save the dynasty that built it, and it makes the next dynasty cheaper.

And then the Tang took all of it.

This is the part that is easy to miss, because the story of a fallen dynasty reads as a warning, and warnings are supposed to be lessons. The Tang kept the canal. The Tang kept the examination system and made it central, so that by the eighth century the examination hall had become the way into the administration and the ambition of every provincial family. The Tang kept the legal code and the system of provinces. The Sui did the building and the Tang did the collection, and the historians the Tang employed wrote the Sui as a dynasty of monsters and themselves as the correction of it.

The historians were not lying. Yang Guang did send millions to their deaths for a war he lost. But the judgment in the sentence is not the causally important fact. What is causally important is that the machines worked, and that the state that inherited them used them for three hundred years, and that the people who paid for them got a canal with their grain in it and an examination their grandsons might sit.

That is the trade this chapter is about, and it is the least comfortable trade in the book, because both sides of it are real. The state built something that made a continent governable. It built it out of the bodies of the people it was governing. There is no version of the story in which the canal is not an achievement and there is no version in which it is not a mass grave, and a history that insists on one of those is not a history, it is a verdict, and the whole point of this book is that the verdicts are harder than they look. The Sui ended in 618. The general who ended it came out of its own northern aristocracy, and the dynasty he founded kept the canal, kept the examinations, kept every piece of machinery Yang Guang had built, and threw away nothing but the name.

The ditch is still there. It has been cut again and deepened and moved and repaired for fourteen hundred years, and the grain still goes north along it, and nobody has ever got round to naming the men who dug the first version.

## Chapter 10 — The Mirror and the Minister

Before dawn on the second of July, 626, a man waited at the north gate of his father's palace with nine men and a plan that had been rehearsed for weeks. His name was Li Shimin, he was the second son of the founding emperor of the Tang, and he had spent the previous decade winning the wars that had made the dynasty possible. He was also going to be killed, or so he had concluded, by his elder brother.

The brother, Li Jiancheng, was the crown prince. He had the title, the court, the support of the palace, and a growing fear of his younger brother, which was reasonable, because the younger brother was the better general and everybody knew it. In the months before that morning, Jiancheng had taken Shimin's staff, reduced his guard, and moved to exclude him from the succession. The sources describe poisoned wine, and a suspicious illness, and an effort to have Shimin's officers dismissed to the provinces. Whether any particular one of those stories is true is not something the record can settle honestly. What can be settled is that both men had concluded the other had to die, and one of them was faster.

The plan was a gate. The Xuanwu Gate was the northern entrance to the palace complex, and it was the road the crown prince took to attend his father in the early morning. The guard on the gate had been turned. When Jiancheng and the fourth brother, Li Yuanji, rode through it, they found the gate closed and Li Shimin behind them with his men, and there was a fight in a courtyard at the edge of the palace, and it lasted minutes.

Li Shimin shot his brother. He is said to have drawn the bow and killed Jiancheng himself, which is a detail a later hand might have supplied to make the killing personal, and which I believe because somebody's arrows killed him and there is no reason for the winner to hide that they were his. Li Yuanji was killed by Shimin's officers in the confusion. Then Shimin's men went to the palace where his father was, and the old emperor was informed that his two eldest sons were dead and that his second son had come to protect him.

He abdicated within two months. Li Shimin took the throne as Taizong, and reigned for twenty-three years, and the era name he chose for the first decade of that reign gives us the phrase by which Chinese political thought has measured good government ever since.

The era was called Zhenguan. When a Chinese historian wants to describe an ideal administration, he does not reach for a philosopher. He reaches for those two characters, and for the decades in which an empire was governed well enough that the officials writing about it two centuries later could not improve on the account.

That is the fact I want you to hold next to the courtyard. The best-governed reign in the imperial tradition begins with a morning in which a man killed his brother at his father's gate and took the throne by violence. Both of those things are true, and this chapter is about the fact that the tradition has spent thirteen hundred years trying not to look at them together.

I am going to look at them together, because that is the whole book.

Taizong's achievement is not mysterious and it is not propaganda. He ruled well. He rebuilt the administration after a civil war, kept the canal and the examinations and the legal code he had inherited from the Sui, and used them better. He expanded into the steppe and broke the Eastern Turks in 630, and the nomad confederations called him the Heavenly Khan, which is a title no other Chinese ruler earned in quite that way. He was militarily gifted, personally brave, and, unusually among the men in this book, interested in being corrected.

The particulars of the Zhenguan administration are worth listing, because the phrase has become a compliment and the compliment hides the machinery. He cut the number of officials and kept the salaries high, on the theory that a well-paid civil servant is cheaper than a corrupt one. He divided the government into three branches that were supposed to check one another: the drafting of policy, the review of it, and the execution, with the remonstrance office holding a veto of sorts on the second. He required that capital sentences be reviewed at court, and he is credited with reducing the annual number of executions to a figure in the low dozens, which the later tradition repeated until it became a legend. He held examinations and then looked at the results himself. He also kept a register of the prices of grain, because a government that cannot tell whether its people are hungry is a government that will find out late.

Some of this is embellished. The *Zhenguan Governance*, the manual compiled from his court's records two centuries later, was written for an audience of later emperors who needed an example, and an example is improved by use. But the core holds: this was a state that took the machinery of administration seriously, and the seriousness lasted.

That last quality is rare enough to deserve its own story, and the story is Wei Zheng.

Wei Zheng had been an official of Li Jiancheng's household. That is, he had served the man Taizong killed. This should have been the end of him, and instead it was the beginning of his career, because Taizong had a use for a man who had already chosen against him once. A minister who has served your dead rival has no faction of his own, no comfortable assumptions about your judgement, and nothing to gain from flattery, since flattery cannot make him safe. Taizong asked him why he had advised his brother to move against Shimin. Wei Zheng told him plainly that if Jiancheng had listened, this conversation would not be happening. Taizong kept him.

For the next seventeen years Wei Zheng reminded the emperor of his faults to his face, in memorials, in court, and apparently in ordinary conversation, on a schedule. He criticised the emperor's building projects. He criticised the emperor's hunting, his temper, his treatment of officials, his preference for clever men over upright ones, and the size of his palace retinue, and he did it in language that the sources preserve with obvious relish because the rudeness was the point. Taizong, several times, lost his temper and had to be talked down by his wife or his ministers. The tradition records the temper as a virtue, because it shows that the correction was real.

Now here is why this works as the chapter, and not just as a charming anecdote about a good boss.

Wei Zheng's job was remonstrance. It was an office, the most distinctive office in Chinese government, and its function was to tell the emperor he was wrong. It existed because the Chinese state had decided that the dangerous thing about a ruler is that he is surrounded. A man on a throne is told the truth by nobody, because the truth is expensive, and the advisors learn quickly that agreement is safe and disagreement is a lottery. So the state appointed a man whose job was disagreement, and gave him a formal licence to be unbearable, and the reign of Zhenguan was, for seventeen years, the best test the arrangement ever had.

And it produced Wei Zheng's three mirrors, or rather Taizong's three mirrors about Wei Zheng, which is the sentence I have been building this whole book toward.

After Wei Zheng died, in 643, the emperor is recorded saying that he had lost a mirror. The fuller version, which the tradition preserves and which has the polish of a line composed for the record, runs like this. With bronze as a mirror a man can arrange his clothes. With history as a mirror he can know how dynasties rise and fall. With another person as a mirror he can see his own successes and failures. He had kept those three mirrors all his life, and now one of them was gone.

That is a very good sentence. It is also a sentence about a man who has understood something real, which is that the ruler's problem is the same as anyone's, only magnified: he cannot see himself. A private man has friends. An emperor has courtiers. The only way a private man's failure is discovered is by consequence, and the only way an emperor's is discovered is by the collapse of a state. Wei Zheng was an attempt to insert the missing organ, the eye that sees the throne from outside.

Now put it back on the morning of the second of July, 626.

The mirror was bronze and history and Wei Zheng. What was the man holding a bow at the Xuanwu Gate? He was a man who had decided, with excellent reasons and no legitimate authority, that his brother's death was necessary for the survival of himself and the state he had helped build. That is not an absurd claim. Jiancheng had been moving against him. The war was over but the administration was new and fragile, and a succession fight has ended dynasties. It is entirely possible that Shimin was correct.

It is also exactly what every usurper in this book has said.

The steppe policy is the other half of the reign and it deserves the same suspicion, because it worked and it was expensive. Breaking the Eastern Turks did not mean conquering the grassland, which no settled empire has ever managed for long. It meant dividing the confederation, supporting one khan against another, and taking the title that the khans themselves used, so that the emperor was, in the steppe's own political language, a legitimate overlord and not a foreigner. He offered a place in his empire to the tribes that submitted, and he took their cavalry into his armies. That is a policy of absorption, and it is the policy the Tang ran for a century, and it ended in the most spectacular way imaginable in the next chapter but one.

The tradition's solution is to treat the two facts as belonging to two different men: the prince who did the necessary thing, and the emperor who kept a mirror. Read the *Zhenguan Governance*, the compilation of Taizong's reign that was assembled later and became the manual of good government for a thousand years, and you will find the emperor, and the mirror, and the remonstrance, and almost nothing about the gate. The gate is in the annals, and the annals are for reference, and the model is for reading.

I do not think the two men can be separated, and the book should say why rather than making the usual gesture about complexity.

A man who has killed his brothers for the throne knows one thing that the rest of us do not: what he is capable of. He has no illusions about his own virtue, because he has done the unvirtuous thing and watched himself do it. Every other emperor in this book had to be told his faults by somebody. Taizong already knew he had them. Wei Zheng's remonstrance worked, when it worked, because the emperor did not need convincing that the thing in the mirror was dangerous.

Which means the very quality that makes Zhenguan governable was produced by the crime. The same morning that made him a legitimate target for every Confucian in the empire also taught him to listen. And the empire spent the next thirteen hundred years taking the listening and pretending the teaching had been free.

There is a coda, and it is what makes me think the tradition knew more than it admitted. Late in his life, Taizong is said to have turned against Wei Zheng's legacy. The marriage he had promised between his daughter and Wei Zheng's son was cancelled. A tablet he had erected with Wei Zheng's advice on it was, by one account, knocked down or ordered removed. He had begun to suspect that Wei Zheng had been cultivating his own reputation by correcting him in public, and had kept copies of his memorials.

Think about that suspicion, because it is a terrifying one. A ruler who relies on being criticised can never be sure that the criticism is not a performance. He has created a role, the honest minister, and he cannot see behind the role because the role's whole value is that it does not flatter, and a sufficiently clever man could flatter him by not flattering him. So he destroyed the stele and then, according to some accounts, restored it.

He also had children, and they were the part of the reign he could not govern. His eldest son, the crown prince, was accused of plotting rebellion and was destroyed; the heir he finally settled on was a quiet and unimpressive younger son, chosen partly because he was inoffensive. The choice was defensible and it was also the choice of a man who had seen what a capable son does to an incumbent. Then he died in 649, of illness, at fifty-one. His own physicians had not saved him. Neither had the elixirs he had begun to take, which is the last of the Qin echoes in this book and not the last time an emperor would die of the search for immortality.

The third mirror is the one I want to leave you with, because it is the one this book has been using since Chapter 3 and it is now time to say so plainly. Bronze for the body, history for the state. The third mirror is another person. Sima Qian, a century and a half before, had already established that for a ruler the true third mirror is the record, and the record is compiled by a person with a position, and the position is never neutral.

Taizong knew it, or half knew it. He asked to see the court diaries, the official record of his own conduct, which was not permitted, and the official refused to show him. The refusal is preserved as an example of integrity. It is also an example of the state deciding that some mirrors are for the throne and the throne is not permitted to look into all of them.

But the record he could not read was being written. And the record he could not read says, at the beginning, at the gate, on the second of July, in the early morning, what he did. And then the record goes on, for twenty-three years, and says what he built.

Both. Always both. And the question this book began with, whether a ruler can be taught to be good,gets its best answer here. He had the best teacher in the imperial tradition, and the teaching worked, and the man who received it was also the man who killed his brother at the gate, and neither one of them finished off the other.

## Chapter 11 — The Woman Who Became Son of Heaven

The edict of 690 says that the sovereign lady Wu has been entreated by the officials and the people and the signs to take the throne, and that she has refused three times, and that she has now consented, and that the dynasty shall be called Zhou. It gives her a new title, invented for the occasion, because no title existed for what she was about to be.

Read the document again with the problem in view. In the whole of the political tradition this book has been describing, the ruler is the Son of Heaven and the Son of Heaven is a son. The Mandate of Heaven comes to the virtuous, and virtue has been defined by a canon written by men, in a society organised by the assumption that the public order and the household order are the same thing, with the man at the head of both. There was no procedure for this. There was no precedent to invoke, because the precedent was the thing being broken.

So she did not invoke a precedent. She invented a justification, and the justification she chose is the one thing in the empire that had come from outside the canon and did not care about it.

Buddhism had its own theory of rulership, and its own stories, and one of them concerned a queen who ruled righteously and was sustained by the Buddha. The *Great Cloud Sutra* was read, in the 690s, as a prophecy. The reading was convenient and it was also available, and she had spent the decade before the edict building the monasteries that taught it. When she took the throne, she did it as a Buddhist sovereign, and the state funded the monks who had supplied the argument.

That is the same move Liang Wudi made, with the genders reversed, and it worked in the way his did not, because she did not give the state away. She took it.

Her rise is the part the record tells badly, because the record is written by her enemies, and it is written with an excess that gives the whole thing away.

She was born in 624 to a family of the minor official class, which is to say she was not great by birth. Her father had been a supporter of the Tang founders and had risen in the administration, which gave her a provincial education and an entrance to the palace that a farmer's daughter would not have had. She entered the harem of Taizong as a low-ranking consort, and when Taizong died she did what consorts without sons did: she was sent to a convent, which removed her from the palace and from the world. It is one of the most important facts of her life that she got out.

The heir, Gaozong, had noticed her before his father died, and he brought her back, and made her his consort, and then his empress, over the objection of the court and by a struggle that the sources describe as a murder. The officials who objected were reading the structure of the court correctly. An empress with a faction of her own, drawn from outside the great families, would not be a consort. She would be a party, and the parties at court were what killed emperors.

The murder is the story everyone knows. The empress, they say, was accused of killing the baby Wu had borne, and the accusation destroyed her. Whether Wu killed her own child to frame a rival is one of those questions the record turns into a certainty it cannot support. What the record does show is that the account was written by men who had every incentive to make her a monster, and that they made her one with a thoroughness that exceeds what a case needs.

Notice the shape of the hostility, because it is itself evidence. Her enemies accuse her of promiscuity, of murdering her children, of witchcraft, of using sorcerers, of poisoning, of usurping a throne that was not hers, and of the general crime of being a woman in a man's office. That last one is the one that organises the rest. A record that has to call a ruler unnatural will reach for every available category of the unnatural, and it will reach past the point where a careful reader begins to wonder why the case needed so many charges.

Here is what can be established, and it is enough. When Gaozong fell ill, in the 660s, she began to rule with him, and the court called them the Two Sages. When he died, in 683, she ruled for and then instead of their sons, and she had rivals killed, and by the 690s she had taken the throne outright and declared the new dynasty. She ruled, in one capacity or another, for about half a century.

The Two Sages period is the part that is hardest to give its due, because the sources present it as an illness and a wife taking over, and that is not what it was. Gaozong's illness was real and it was disabling, and the court needed someone to sign the memorials, and the someone was the empress. But signing is not governing, and she governed. She read the memorials, sat in the audience, decided the appointments, and managed the factions, for more than twenty years before she had any title at all. By the time she declared the Zhou she had been running the state from the back of the sedan chair, with an emperor in front of her, for a generation. The coup of 690 was a ruler stopping the pretense.

And she ruled competently, which is the fact the tradition finds hardest to absorb.

Her administration ran the examinations, and did something with them no previous ruler had done: she used them aggressively to bring in men from outside the aristocratic families. Under her, the number of officials who came up through the schools and the examinations rather than by birth increased sharply, and the men she promoted became, over the next generation, the nucleus of the scholar-bureaucracy that would govern for a thousand years. That is not a small thing. The Tang's greatest statesmen of the next century were, several of them, her appointments.

She is also credited with the two details that show how deliberately she used the institution. She examined candidates herself, at the palace, which turned a bureaucratic procedure into a personal act of the throne and made every man who passed it owe the sovereign rather than the examiner. And she established a parallel examination in military subjects, for the men who would command armies, which had the same effect: an officer corps recruited and certified by the state, and therefore dependent on it. Neither of these was a formality. Together they were a new way to make an elite, and she needed a new elite, because the old one had decided what she was.

She kept the canal and the frontier. She handled the Turks by diplomacy where she could and by arms where she had to. She patronised the poets, and the culture of the court under her was lavish. She commissioned new characters for writing, a set of her own inventions, which is a ruler's habit when she has decided that the common language cannot express the office she holds.

And she killed a great many people, including members of her own family, using a secret police whose methods the record describes in detail and with no little satisfaction: the informers, the interrogation, the confessions manufactured by exhaustion and the promise of leniency, the executions of ministers who had been loyal the year before. This is the actual record of the 690s, and the men who ran it were eventually executed themselves when she had no further use for them, which is the standard end of such instruments.

There is a story the tradition tells about two of them, Lai Junchen and Zhou Xing, who were informers of the worst kind and who eventually turned on each other as such men do. Lai summoned Zhou to dinner, complained that some prisoners would not confess, and asked what to do. Zhou, apparently delighted to be consulted, explained that you place the man in a large vat and heat it from beneath, and no one can hold out. Lai had a vat brought and heated, and then stood up and announced that Zhou himself was accused, and invited him to step in.

The story is probably invented. It is in the record because the record's authors wanted to show that the instrument destroys its handlers, and because the phrase it generated became part of the language. But it is also, in compressed form, exactly what happened to her reign: she built a machine to remove the people who would not accept her, the machine did its work, and then the machine removed the men who had run it, and none of that is a theory. It is the sequence of events.

Both things are the case at the same time. She governed better than most of the men in this book and she was more murderous than most of them too, and the tradition cannot say so, because it has been built to produce one verdict and not the other.

Now the stele.

Near her capital, in a tomb complex beside Gaozong's, there is a monument that she had raised for herself. It is a tall slab of stone with a carved dragon cap, and it is blank. It has no inscription. For thirteen hundred years it has had no inscription.

It was blank when it went up, or it was made blank soon after, and the various explanations the tradition offers are all plausible and cannot all be true: that she left the verdict to posterity because she did not know what it should say, that she intended an inscription and it was never cut because her dynasty fell the moment she died, that she left it blank to signal that her deeds were beyond the words available.

I believe the simplest reading. She had lived through fifty years of being written down by men who had decided what she was before she did anything, and she had learned that any inscription she composed would be answered with a counter-inscription, and so she refused to write one. Every other ruler in this book has left a statement of what he wanted to be remembered as. She left the page.

It is the single most eloquent thing any ruler in this book did with stone.

There is one more thing to record here, and it is not a political fact. She wrote poetry, and some of it survives, and it is competent and formal and mostly composed for occasions. One piece is famous for being the opposite. It is a short poem about a wild colt, written when she was accused of insolence as a girl, in which she says that a headstrong horse can be broken to the bridle, and that she would rather be broken than tamed.

I cannot tell you whether she wrote it. The attribution is traditional and the poem is exactly the poem the tradition would have wanted her to have written, which is a reason for suspicion in both directions. I include it because if it is hers, it is the only place in this chapter where she speaks in her own voice rather than in the voice of an edict, and I have refused to invent an interior for her precisely because the hostile record offers so many.

There is a version of this chapter that writes itself in English, and I want to name it so the reader can watch me avoid it. It goes: the only woman emperor, brilliant and slandered, a feminist icon ahead of her time, destroyed by a patriarchal system. Some of that is true. She was slandered. The system did destroy her, in the end, and it destroyed her memory more thoroughly than it destroyed her body.

But the icon version commits the crime this book keeps refusing. It makes her a category instead of a ruler, and the category is flattering, and flattery of a dead woman is still flattery. She was a ruler exactly like the others in this book, who came to power by blood and held it by blood, who governed with the machinery available to her and improved it, and who was undone at the end by the same thing that undid all of them: the machinery does not care who operates it.

She was deposed in 705, at eighty-one, in a palace coup led by officials and relatives and one of her own grandsons. She was allowed to keep her title as empress and retired to the palace at the capital, and she died within the year.

The old age is worth noting, because it is where the tradition does something quietly dishonest. An emperor who rules into his eighties and is then removed is allowed to be a tragedy, or a caution, or a monument. A woman in the same position is written as a woman who would not let go. The record of her last years is full of the language of appetite: she wanted the throne, she wanted her family raised, she wanted the men she had chosen to remain. Her enemies were the men she had raised, and the coup was led by men who had taken office under her, and the story they told was a story about a woman who had overreached, and the story is not false, but it is not a story that would have been told about a man who did the same thing for the same reasons.

What is true is that she never named a successor she could trust, and that is the failure of every ruler in this book. The Mandate of Heaven does not transfer. It is awarded to a person and it dies with the person, and the whole apparatus of succession in Chinese politics is an attempt to solve a problem that the theory creates: if legitimacy attaches to the virtue of the individual ruler, then the moment the individual dies the state has no legitimate ruler, and the army has an opinion. She spent her last years trying to manufacture a successor, and the system would not accept one from her, and the reason it would not is the same reason the chapter exists. Her dynasty was declared over and the Tang was restored. Her sons had predeceased her or been killed. The men who had run her secret police were executed. The examination officials went on running the examinations, the canal went on carrying grain, the state went on being the state, and the one thing that did not survive was the woman.

The stele is still there, and it is blank, and it has been blank for thirteen hundred years.

## Chapter 12 — Interlude: A Man Drinking on a Mountain

Interlude. A man is drinking on a mountain, alone, at night, and he says to the moon that he would like to drink with it, and he would like his shadow to join them, so that there are three. Nothing in the poem is political. That is why it survives.

His name was Li Bai, and he was the greatest poet of the greatest age this book covers, and he spent most of his life failing to be an official. He was born in 701, by his own account far west, and he travelled for decades, and he wrote poems that people copied and memorised while he was still alive. He was employed at the court of the emperor Xuanzong for about two years, in the early 740s, as a palace poet, which is a job that sounds better than it is, and he was dismissed, apparently for drinking. He went back to wandering. The legend says he drowned when he tried to embrace the reflection of the moon in the Yangzi. The record suggests he died of illness. The legend is the one that matters, because a culture chooses which death to give its poets, and the death it chose for Li Bai is the one where he reached for something bright and fell in.

And now leave him drinking on the mountain, and go to the court he had left, which is the other subject of this chapter.

Xuanzong was born in 685 and took the throne in 712 after a decade of palace coups in which the women of the Tang court, including his aunt, had tried to rule as Wu Zetian had ruled. He is the emperor the Chinese tradition calls the one who presided over the height. The first three decades of his reign, under the era name Kaiyuan, are the golden age: a stable frontier, a full treasury, a capital that was the largest and most cosmopolitan city in the world, an examination system producing a class of officials who wrote poetry, and a court that sponsored painting, music, and calligraphy at a level no other Chinese court matched.

Chang'an in the eighth century held perhaps a million people. Its western market sold goods from Persia and India and the steppe. There were Nestorian Christians, Manichaeans, Zoroastrians, and a Jewish community in the record. There were foreign generals in the army, and foreign musicians at the court, and a fashion for Central Asian dress and Central Asian horses. Poets came to the capital from the provinces to sit the examinations and stayed to write. This is the China that later ages pointed at when they wanted to say what China could be.

It lasted thirty years, and then it was destroyed in nine, and the man who destroyed it was the emperor's own favourite general.

## Chapter 13 — The Golden Age and the General Who Ended It

Xuanzong's first decades were governed by a succession of able chancellors, and the sequence tells its own story. Yao Chong and Song Jing were administrators who cut costs and required the frontier to justify its requests. Zhang Jiuling, the last of them, was a scholar of the old type who appointed men on their merits and distrusted the empire's dependence on foreign generals, and he warned the emperor about An Lushan years before the rebellion and was ignored. When Zhang Jiuling died in 740, the last brake came off.

What replaced him was Li Linfu, who served as chancellor for nineteen years and whose method was to be indispensable and to make sure no one else was. He centralised the administration around himself, suppressed inconvenient reports, and ran the frontier through men who could not threaten him at court, which is precisely why a general of doubtful loyalty was allowed to accumulate three armies. Li Linfu did not cause the rebellion. He was the reason nobody saw it coming, and the reason the warning, when it came, had no one left to deliver it.

I want to say at once that the golden age was no fraud. It is common now to write about the Tang height as if its splendour were a mask over exploitation, and there was exploitation. The splendour was real. Both of those things can be true; the book has been insisting on it for nine chapters. What should be resisted is the opposite move, the one that treats the height as an achievement of character, of a good emperor's good judgement. The judgement was real and it was also a function of the machinery inherited from the Sui, the canal that moved the grain, the examinations that moved the men, the armies that held the frontier. Take the machinery away and the character would have had nothing to govern.

The machinery is where the trouble started.

The Tang frontier was held by a system of military commands, and by the eighth century those commands had grown enormous. The reason was practical: a defence that reacts to nomad cavalry has to be mounted locally, with local troops who can move fast, and the forces needed to hold the north-east and the north were the largest in the empire. The commander of those forces, from 742, was a general called An Lushan.

He was not Chinese in origin, or not in the way that mattered to the court. He was of Sogdian and Turkic descent, born on the frontier. He had risen through the army by ability and by charm, and he had made himself indispensable to the palace. He was, by the accounts, an enormous man with a talent for the kind of rough bonhomie that powerful people find relaxing, and he had cultivated a relationship with the emperor that read, to everyone around them, as a kind of adoption. He called himself the emperor's son, in public, and the emperor permitted it, and the sources record the two of them behaving in a way that the tradition found embarrassing to describe.

He also had the loyalty of the three largest armies in the empire, and the emperor's own chancellor, Li Linfu, had spent years insisting that everything was fine.

Here is a fact that matters for the rest of the book: the central government of the Tang had almost no army of its own. The fighting strength was on the frontier, in the hands of commanders who were also the local administration, and the capital depended on their loyalty. When Li Linfu died in 752 the last man able to manage An Lushan was gone, and his successor, Yang Guozhong, did the opposite of managing him. He treated him as a rival, moved against his allies at court, and made it clear that a reckoning was coming. An Lushan, who had spent a decade building a private power base and expected to be destroyed by it, did the arithmetic and rebelled in December 755.

The rebellion is called the An Lushan Rebellion, or the An–Shi Rebellion, after the two commanders who led it in succession, and it ran for eight years. An Lushan's armies came south and took Luoyang, then Chang'an. The emperor fled west, to Sichuan, with his court and the imperial guard, and on the road, at a post station called Mawei, the guard stopped.

They had decided that the disaster was the fault of the chancellor, Yang Guozhong, and they killed him. Then they decided it was also the fault of his cousin, the emperor's consort Yang Guifei, the woman the tradition blames for the whole thing, and they refused to move until she was dead.

The woman at the centre of it, Yang Guifei, was the emperor's consort and the cousin of the chancellor the guard had just killed, and the tradition has assigned her most of the blame for the collapse. She was a dancer and a musician and, by the accounts of the poets who wrote about her, the person the emperor loved. She had no office and no army and no part in any decision that mattered. The guard killed her because she was the last thing the emperor could be shown to love, and killing her was a way of telling him that his personal life was over.

The tradition kept that assignment of blame, and the poems about her are among the most beautiful in the language, and beauty is a very effective way of making a scapegoat permanent. Read them with the cavalry counts beside you. A frontier command of a hundred and fifty thousand men does not become a rebellion because an emperor fell in love.

## Chapter 14 — The Poem with the Child in the Middle

The emperor was seventy-one years old and had been on the throne for forty-four years. He gave the order. She was strangled at the post station and the guard moved on. The emperor abdicated in favour of his son within weeks. He lived another six years, in retirement, in the west, and one of the poems of the period describes him listening to the rain on the palace roof.

An Lushan was killed by his own son in 757. The rebellion went on being fought by men who had started it for reasons that no longer existed, until 763, when the imperial forces bought off the last commander by making him an official and letting him keep his army. That settlement is the real end of the golden age. The Tang would survive for another century and a half, but it never regained the central control it had lost, and the frontier commands became hereditary, and the dynasty that had governed the world's largest city spent its last decades as the senior partner in a negotiation with its own generals.

The cut is worth marking, because it is the reason this book ends where it does. After 763 the Tang is a different state wearing the same name. The examination system continues and expands. The poetry continues and is magnificent, and the late Tang poets are among the finest in the tradition. The canal keeps working. But the political question the book has been following, the question of what gives a ruler the right to decide, is settled for the next century in favour of whoever holds the armies, and the rest is the slow administration of a decline that everyone could feel and no one could reverse.

And the poems outlasted all of it. Li Bai's moon and Du Fu's child are still being read while the empire that produced them has been gone for eleven hundred years. That is the book's second theme arriving one last time: the throne holds the present, and the writing holds the verdict. The Tang could not keep the frontier, and it could not keep the treasury, and it could not keep the emperor's own favourite alive. It kept the poems, because the poems were the one thing it made that did not need an army.

Now count the dead.

The registers say the population in 754 was about fifty-three million. The registers for 764 say about seventeen million. Neither figure is a body count. The registers were the tax rolls, and the war destroyed the machinery that compiled them, and millions of people who were not on the rolls in 764 were not dead; they were unregistered, which in a state that taxed by the register is the same as invisible. Modern estimates of the actual dead run to a figure that cannot be established, and the honest number is a range, and the range is enormous.

I give you the registers and the caveat because the sentiment would like to have this both ways. The tradition says that the Tang lost two-thirds of its people, meaning the catastrophe was unthinkable. The revision says the registers collapsed, meaning the catastrophe is overstated. Both are arguing about a number that neither can produce. What is not in dispute is the specific, dated, verifiable death of one child. That death belongs to the other poet.

Du Fu was born in 712, eleven years after Li Bai, and he failed the examinations for most of his life and spent years trying to find employment. In the winter of 755 he was travelling, and he arrived home to a house where his youngest son had died of hunger, and he wrote a long poem about the journey, and in it he puts the death in the middle, in two lines, plainly, and then does something that I have never entirely recovered from: he remembers that he himself has a rank and a salary, and that even so his child died, and he asks what will happen to the people who have less than he does.

The poem is dated. The years and the journey are in the text. That is the thing about Du Fu that no amount of later admiration can improve: he wrote down the season, the village, the weather, the price of rice, and the dead child, in the same document, as if the child and the rice were the same order of fact, because to him they were.

He survived the rebellion, and lost more children. He kept writing. He died in 770 in a boat on the Yangzi, and his poems were collected and read by a later age that decided he was the greatest of all Chinese poets. During his life he was a minor figure. Li Bai was famous and Du Fu was not, and the tradition reversed their positions centuries later, and the reversal tells you something about what a tradition eventually decides to value.

There is a version of the golden age in which Li Bai's moon and Du Fu's dead son are the two halves of the same century, and this chapter is the version where they are. The court of Chang'an produced the most beautiful poetry in the language, and the same state that sponsored it also produced a system of frontier commands that could not be controlled, and the poetry and the commands came from the same treasury and the same elite and the same optimism, and when the thing broke, the poet who wrote about it best was the one who had never been invited to the party.

An Lushan is the villain of the story, and the story needs one. He is a poor fit for the role because he was made by the system he broke. He was promoted by the emperor, trusted with a third of the army, and left in place after the chancellor who understood him died, and the man who destroyed him did so with an army he had been given. A rebellion is not a foreign invasion; it is the state's own instrument, turned.

## Chapter 15 — The Age of the Literati

A list of the books an examination candidate is expected to know, in the form the syllabus took from the fourteenth century onward. Four texts, with a commentary. The *Analects* of Confucius. The *Mencius*. The *Great Learning*. The *Doctrine of the Mean*. Then the canonical *Book of Odes*, the *Book of Documents*, the *Book of Changes*, the *Record of Rites*, and the *Spring and Autumn Annals*.

The list is short. The point is in the word *commentary*.

The candidate is examined on a particular commentary on the four texts, written by one man in the twelfth century, and on the reading of the canonical books that the same man established, and the establishment of that reading is the event this book has been walking toward for ten chapters without quite naming it.

His name was Zhu Xi. He lived from 1130 to 1200. He spent his life studying, teaching, and writing. He failed, by the standards of his own time, to change anything important, and he died while his ideas were under official suspicion. And then, within a century, his commentaries became the orthodoxy of the Chinese state, and they remained the orthodoxy for six hundred years, and every official who governed China in the late imperial period had memorised them as a boy.

I have promised the reader an explanation of what legitimates a ruler. The end of the story, the one this book has been building toward, is that the philosophers finally answered the question by taking the throne. It did not work, and the reason it did not work is the reason the book has no answer.

Start with how the philosophers got near the throne at all, because the throne came to them.

The Tang fell in 907, and the fifty-odd years that followed are the period of the Five Dynasties, in which the north was ruled by a rapid succession of military men, most of them generals who had taken the throne from the previous general. In 960 a commander named Zhao Kuangyin was acclaimed emperor by his own troops, which is the standard method of the period, and he founded the Song. He then did something unusual with the power he had acquired that way: he set about making certain that the same thing could never happen to his descendants.

His solution was the most consequential institutional decision in this book. He reduced the army's political role, pensioned off the generals who had made him, and arranged the state so that the military answered to the civil administration rather than the reverse. He expanded the examination system until it was no longer one route into office among several, but the route. He made the examination the mechanism by which the empire selected the men who would run it, and he made the throne's own family increasingly formal, ceremonial, and detached from the day-to-day business of governing.

The emperor of the Song was structurally less powerful, by design, than the emperors of the Tang. The business of government was in the hands of a bureaucracy that recruited itself through examinations and administered an empire through a set of procedures, and the emperor presided over the bureaucracy the way a constitutional monarch presides over a cabinet. The Song did this deliberately, because every founder of the Song had watched the Tang die of warlordism, and a civil service cannot stage a military coup.

The machinery they built to make the examination honest is worth a paragraph, because it is the part that made the system work for a thousand years and it is entirely bureaucratic. Candidates sat in cells under supervision. Their papers were recopied by clerks before the examiners saw them, so that a hand could not be recognised, and the copies were numbered and the numbers sealed. The examiners were moved into the compound and locked in for the duration, so that no message could reach them from outside. Later, the reigning emperor personally reviewed the top papers, which turned the whole apparatus into a ritual act of the throne and made the successful candidate a debtor to the sovereign rather than to the examiner who had passed him.

A state that goes to that much trouble to make its recruitment fair has decided that legitimacy comes from the procedure. That is a real achievement and it has a cost: the procedure can only test what can be written in a cell, and what can be written in a cell, after a thousand years of practice, is an essay about a book.

And the state that built all of this lost its northern half in 1127, when the Jurchen Jin took Kaifeng and captured an emperor and his father, and the court re-established itself in the south, at Hangzhou, behind the Yangzi. Zhu Xi was born three years after that. He spent his life in a diminished empire, with a lost north and a court that had bought its survival, and the philosophy he built is about how a person should live when the political world has already failed, which is why it spread.

Now put the philosophy here, and watch what happens.

The examinations tested the canon. That had been true at some level since the Han and true more strictly since the Tang. What the Song did was make the canon the criterion of an entire career, and at the same time, the philosophical tradition itself was going through the deepest renewal it had had since Mencius.

The renewal is called Neo-Confucianism in English, and it was a response to the fact that the old Confucian answers had been quietly losing to Buddhism and Daoism on the question of what a human being is. The Confucian canon was a set of ethical and political texts. It said how an official should behave, and how a ruler should rule, and it said very little about why the world exists or what a person is made of. Buddhism had answered those questions, in detail, and had been winning educated people for centuries. The scholars of the Song set out to build a metaphysics that could answer them on Confucian terms.

The men who began it were the Cheng brothers, and others around them: Zhou Dunyi, Zhang Zai. They built a system out of the *Book of Changes* and the *Doctrine of the Mean* and a good deal of what they had learned from the very rivals they were answering. The system holds that there is a pattern, a principle, *li*, in things, and that human nature is in some sense identical with it, and that the work of a person is to recover the original goodness that has been obscured by desire and habit. It holds that this is done by study, by the investigation of things, and by a constant discipline of attention to one's own motives.

Zhu Xi collected this philosophy and organised it. He selected the four texts that became the *Four Books*, wrote commentaries on them, and arranged the whole thing into a system that could be taught, which is the step that made it permanent. A philosophy that cannot be taught dies with its author. A philosophy with a syllabus outlives emperors.

And a philosophy with a syllabus can be handed to the state. That is what happened. Zhu Xi's commentaries became the standard for the examinations, and the examination was the road to office, and office was the road to everything. A boy born in a village in 1400 began memorising the *Great Learning* at the age of six, in a particular edition, with a particular commentary, and repeated it until he could write an essay about it in a fixed form, and if he did that better than the other boys he would become an official, and his family's fortune would change, and his sons would begin earlier.

Now, the question this book began with. Can a ruler be taught to rule well.

Look at what the Song settlement actually produced. For the first time in the history of this book, the philosophy did not have to persuade a throne. It *was* the state. The men who ran the empire had all been trained in the same texts, by the same methods, and they agreed about almost everything at the level of principle, and they believed that the way to govern was to get the moral character of the official right, because a man of good character will govern well and a man of bad character will not.

And the result was faction.

This is the part of the story that gets left out of the triumphant telling. A bureaucracy of men who agree on the fundamental text and disagree on its application will turn every policy dispute into a question of who is a true Confucian and who is a false one, because that is the only vocabulary the institution gives them for disagreement. The Song had the fiercest political conflicts in the book's account of China, and they were fought almost entirely between officials who believed the same things. The reform programme of Wang Anshi in the eleventh century is the great example: a sincere and brilliant minister who tried to fix the state's finances, and a sincere and brilliant opposition led by Sima Guang, both of them men of the canon, both of them certain that the other was destroying the empire.

Neither of them was wrong about the other's programme. Wang Anshi's reforms did raise revenue and did hurt people. His opponents' opposition did protect the vulnerable and did entrench the landowners. There was no test in the canon that resolved it, because the canon is a set of questions, not an answer key, and the examination had turned it into an answer key.

Wang Anshi's story deserves the extra line, because he is the closest thing this book has to a reformer who was right on the merits. He argued that the state should lend to farmers at reasonable rates instead of letting them borrow at ruinous ones, that the assessment of land for tax should be redone, that the militia should replace the standing army. Each measure attacked an entrenched interest, and each was met by an opponent who believed, sincerely, that the measures would concentrate power in the throne and destroy the local order that made society bearable. Both sides were describing the same policy from opposite ends of the same society. Neither had a way to test the other's claim, because there was no experiment and no independent authority, and the only available vocabulary was the accusation of moral failure.

The great historian of the Song period, Sima Guang, who led the opposition, spent his retirement writing a universal history that ran to nearly three hundred chapters, and he wrote it because he believed that the record of the past was the only guide the present had. He was a member of the same class and the same canon as the man he fought, and when the political wheel turned he purged Wang Anshi's men from the government as thoroughly as they had purged his. The faction fight consumed the best minds of the century, and it was fought with the tools of a philosophy that had no procedure for deciding between two sincere readings of itself.

That is the shape of the ending. The philosophers got the throne. They had spent a thousand years saying that good government came from good character and good study. Now they were the state, and they discovered that a state staffed entirely by good characters who have studied the same books will divide into factions and fight, and that the fight is worse than the fight between a king and his nobles, because it cannot be settled by force and it cannot be settled by argument, since both sides are arguing from the same premises and the premises do not contain the answer.

Zhu Xi himself was one of the losers of that fight, in his lifetime. His doctrine was proscribed in the 1190s. He was accused of false learning, and his followers were dismissed from office, and he died in 1200 under that cloud. The rehabilitation came later, and then the canonisation, and then the syllabus. Then six hundred years of boys copying his commentary by lamplight.

I find it hard not to see his life as the book's last joke. The man who built the system that would govern China for six centuries died thinking he had failed. The failure was the delay. The system won.

Now leave him there and come back to the beginning, to 221 BCE, to a king of Qin who ended a five-hundred-year war and decided that a people could be standardised like axle-widths.

He could not. Then the Han tried ritual, and the ritual worked. Wang Mang believed the ritual and destroyed himself. The historians wrote the record, and the record refused to be neutral, and the Tang used the record to build an ideal that rested on a murder at a gate. The Tang got a woman and killed her memory, and the greatest general of the age wrote a poem about the bones in the fields. And then the philosophers got the throne and found that they argued about the axle-widths, in a fixed essay form, in a syllabus, forever.

I have not answered the question. I said at the start that I would not, and I meant it. What I can do is say what the question looks like from here, which is a little different from what it looked like at the beginning.

Every ruler in this book wanted the same two things, and they are incompatible. They wanted obedience that was owed rather than merely extracted. And they wanted the power to decide, alone, what should be done. The Mandate of Heaven promised to give them both — the story says the good ruler keeps the throne, which means the obedient subject can believe he is obeying out of virtue, and the ruler can believe his decisions are licensed by Heaven. It is a beautiful arrangement and it fails at exactly one point: the story is awarded backwards, to whoever is left standing, so it tells you nothing about what the standing man should do next.

The philosophies in this book are all attempts to fix that one point, and every one of them failed for a structural reason, not a moral one. The teacher cannot make the student good from the outside, and the ruler always sits where nothing can be checked but the outcome, and the outcome arrives after everybody responsible is dead.

So the honest ending is a list of the men and women who tried, and a note about what each of them learned and did not have time to use. Li Si moving to a better room. Liu Bang borrowing a philosophy he did not believe. Sima Qian choosing the knife over the grave. Wang Mang holding the classic on the terrace. Cao Cao writing the plainest poem about the dead. Liang Wudi's merit at zero. The corvée of the canal. Taizong at the gate and at the mirror. Wu Zetian leaving the stone blank. Du Fu's son, dated, in the middle of the poem. Zhu Xi's commentary, on the test, governing everyone.

None of them answered it. They all had the same question and none of them was allowed to see how the story came out, which is the position you are in, except that you are alive and can still decide, about whatever it is you have decided to rule — a family, a business, a small country of your own habits — what you are willing to say about why you deserve it.

The stone with no words on it is still there. So is the question.

# 3

At its core, the routine is an agreement about what happens next. Everything else is decoration, and decoration is the first thing a hard night takes away.

What follows is the short version: the smallest routine that still works when everything else has gone wrong. It has three steps. One wash, one book, one light. The wash can be a warm cloth across a face. The book can be four pages of one thin paperback. The light is the lamp going from bright to dim, at the same point in the sequence, every night.

Three steps sound like a compromise. In practice they are the spine, and once the spine holds, extra steps can be added back as the season allows.

The value of a short routine is that an exhausted person can run it. A routine that needs energy, enthusiasm and a well child is a routine for the good nights only, and good nights are exactly when no routine is needed. The short version runs on the worst night. The worst night is the night that teaches, because the child learns that the sequence survives even when everything around it wobbles.

On good nights, add back. One more story, a longer bath, the song she likes best. Keep the addition after the three steps, never before them and never inside them. The steps are the skeleton; the extras are the clothing. Skeleton first, always, so that the clothing can be removed without damage.

It also survives travel, illness and grandparents. Write it on a card. Hand the card to anyone who will put your child to bed in your place, and let the card do the explaining you will be too tired to do.

Wash. Book. Light. If that is all that happens tonight, the agreement held.

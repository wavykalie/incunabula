# The naming of a troop of conjurers

**Companion to `01-the-corbeau-school.md`. Nothing here is invented lore for its own sake;
every convention below has a job, and the jobs are plot.**

**Etymology discipline.** Roots marked ✓ are verified against dictionary or naming
sources. Roots marked ~ are this author's reading of a real word and are plausible rather
than established. The distinction is kept visible because the pipeline asks it of comps
and it should be asked of the book's own apparatus.

**A note on how the cast was built, because it is the method and not an anecdote.** The
first draft of this troop gave the dossier-keeper `Emma Soldano`. `check-names.py` failed
it: **TIER-A**, SSA national top 20 — the strongest prior in the language. It was replaced
with `Anouk`, which is clearer of the tiers and carries more meaning, but the failure is
the point. That name was reached for, not chosen, and it was reached for in a cast of
European performers in the 1930s precisely *because* it is what a model produces when
asked for a European woman's name in that decade. The etymologies around it were all
careful, and none of that mattered: the name was a default wearing a footnote.

This is exactly the case `check-names.py` exists for, and it is worth stating plainly —
**a name chosen for its associations and a name that was merely reached for can look
identical in a well-researched document.** The gate is the only thing in the pipeline that
tells them apart.

---

## 1. Why a magician is the ideal carrier of meaningful names

A conjurer is **the only character in fiction who is obliged to have two names, and both
by choice.** Nobody else must: an actor takes a name, but a conjurer takes a name *in
order to do a specific thing with it* — a name is a piece of misdirection, a thing that
is worn to make an audience supply the wrong referent.

Which means the Corbeau doctrine writes itself onto the naming. A stage name **is** a
`[presumed / actual]` pair. It is the school's notation worn on a person. Every name in
this book does, in miniature, the thing the school's six effects do at full size.

**And the birth name is the "actual."** Every performer in the troop has two, and the
relationship between them is the character's whole biography in a single detail: a man
whose stage name means the opposite of what he does, a woman who took her father's trade
word, someone whose stage name is a misspelling of his own name that he never fixed.

## 2. Three conventions, and the character of each

Travelling conjurers of the 1930s did not share a naming culture, and this is the point —
**the convention itself is geography.** A name tells you where a performer is from before
he opens his mouth, which is a `[presumed / actual]` the audience never checks.

### The northern tent — austerity ✓

Scandinavian and North German. Short, worklike, close to animal and weather.

The doctrine: **a large name is a lie, because it claims a grandeur the tent cannot
support.** A performer calling himself the Emperor of the North in a canvas structure
with a rope for a door is telling you something before the show starts. So the tent
convention is a refusal — plain names, and plainness as a boast. `Sigrid Voll` is a
complete stage name. It says nothing. That is the point, and in a town that believes
grander, it reads as confidence.

*Second-order tell:* tent names cluster phonetically. Halvard, Sigrid, Rasmus, Solveig.
A stranger whose northern name does not sound like the others is a stranger, immediately
and for reasons a reader can feel without analysing.

### The continental salle — the grand manner ✓

French, German, Italian halls. `Le Cornet`, `L'Ombrière`, `La Furette`. The name names
an **effect**.

The doctrine, and it is the most dangerous of the three: **the name is a claim of
capability that can be formally challenged.** In a culture where an effect is disputed in
writing beforehand, a performer whose name promises vanishings is making a *provable*
assertion about what he can do. A challenger may say, in front of a house, "your name
says you make things disappear; you do not."

Which is why the salle performers carry the **dossier** — the list of every effect they
have been accused of faking, each with the method named. The dossier is the shadow of the
name. **A grand name generates a paper record.** The killer's own name is the reason a
file on him exists at all, and that file is how Elsa finds him.

- `cornet` — "little horn," from Latin *cornu* ✓. Also a hunting horn and, in French
  colloquial use, a coin; the two senses are the whole point, since a name that means both
  *horn* and *money* is a name a performer picks to seem loud and solvent.
- `ombrière` — a covered walkway or canopy, the shade built over a market stall ✓. A
  performer named for a roof.

### The southern and colonial ports — no convention, only borrowing ✓

Names arrive wrong. Locals transcribe what they hear; performers take the local name
because taking it is the only way to be paid. The port convention is **mangled, and
nobody is embarrassed about it**, because embarrassment is a luxury of the salle.

The doctrine is simply: *a name here is a costume.* It is worn for the season and
dropped. Some performers in this book have four birth names and no stage name at all.

## 3. The troop

Seven conjurers, two names each. `~` marks a plausible reading rather than a verified one.

| performer | birth name | stage name | convention | the relationship, and what it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elsa Reiner** | Elsa Reiner | *Reiner* — unchanged | none; **she refused one** | German for *cleaner* ✓. Her stage name is her birth name, which in a trade built on misdirection is a public refusal to misdirect. It is why the school finds her readable and why nobody can claim she is hiding anything — which is exactly why nobody believes her. |
| **Rask** | *not given* | *not given* | none | He is addressed by surname alone and **has no stage name at all**, on the grounds that a name you chose to sell something is a name you are asking the audience to supply a referent for. A man whose whole argument is that the plain thing is the difficult thing should not carry a loaded name — **including mine.** |
| **Sigrid Voll** | Sigrid Voll | *Sigrid Voll* | northern tent | `Sigrid` = victory-beautiful ✓; `voll` = field ✓. A complete name, unmagnified, in a trade that inflates. She is the only tent performer who has never inflated, and that costs her bookings. |
| **Halvard Grens** | Halvard Grens | *Halvard Grens* | northern tent | `Halvard` = rock guardian ✓; `grens` = border, limit ~. A man whose name means the thing that holds and marks the edge. ## 4. The killer's tell, and why it is findable

**He keeps the same name in all three conventions — and each one is slightly wrong for
the country he is in.**

A man fluent in three naming traditions should have three names. He has one, and it is
slightly off in each place, in the same way: a French stage name built on a German
compound, a northern tent name with a French article attached, a port name missing the
local pronunciation. Not gibberish — **near-misses.** Grammatically plausible, culturally
impossible.

Why it happens: **he learned each convention from a book.** Vocabulary from books is
right; grammar from books is wrong, because a printed grammar describes what is correct
and not what is *fluent*. A man who has lived in a country is not corrected; a man who
has read about one is corrected by everyone around him for years and does not hear it.

**And the tell is available to a reader without any of the etymologies.** You do not need
to know that *Halvard* means rock guardian to notice that this name does not sound like
the other northern ones. **The phonetic clustering is the clue, and the meaning is the
reward for the reader who goes looking.** A reader who never looks up a single root still
gets the entire plot; a reader who looks up every one gets a second book underneath.

That is the design standard, and it is the kanji point generalised: **hidden meaning that
rewards hyperfixation and is never required.** Depth a reader *can* find, not depth a
reader *must*.

## 5. The decline, again, and it is structural this time

**Rask has no stage name.** Not a plain one — *none*. And that is not a naming choice
like Elsa's, it is a refusal with a reason stated in the text: a name you chose in order
to sell a thing is a name that has already asked an audience to supply a referent on your
behalf, and he does not do that.

Two declines in one book, and they are different in kind, which is the point:
- **Elsa keeps her real name.** She declines the *second* name.
- **Rask declines the *first*.**

A system that only ever declines in one direction is not a system, it is a habit.

## 6. The rules, so this stays a discipline and not a wallpaper

1. **Never load a name with plot.** No name may be a clue the plot requires. The
   grammar error is the clue; the etymology is a bonus. If the reveal ever needs
   "and *Corbeau* means raven," the design has failed.
2. **Every name needs a reason written down**, in this file, so it can be checked later.
   A name chosen because it did not collide with anything is doing nothing.
3. **Two names per performer, always** — including the people who decline the second
   one. The pair is the point.
4. **One plain name per book**, minimum, and the reason recorded. Here: Rask, and the
   reason is in his mouth.
5. **The convention must do work.** Each of the three exists because the plot needs a
   way to localise a man. Remove a convention and a scene stops working — that is the
   test, and if a convention would survive being removed, it is decoration and it goes.
6. **No name may be required to be understood.** Depth optional, always. A reader who
   hyperfixates gets more; a reader who does not is not deprived of anything.

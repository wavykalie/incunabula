# Pressure test — does naming `The Reconciled` make it too easy to plant?

**The objection, stated at its strongest.** `The Reconciled` is the sixth entry in a table
of six canon effects in `01-the-corbeau-school.md`, with a `[presumed / actual]` pairing
and a one-line description of what it leaves behind: *nothing at all*. It is the effect
the endgame depends on, it is the effect that answers the fourth question, and it is
**named, numbered, described and available to the reader in the first third of the book.**

A reader who suspects the endgame early now has a lookup. They do not have to reconstruct
a method from evidence; they have to confirm one they were handed. The book stops being a
puzzle and becomes a checklist.

**That objection is correct, and the fix is not to remove the name.** It is to notice
that the objection only applies if the *canon* is available to the reader — and right now
it is, because I wrote it into an artifact as a table. That is a document-design mistake,
not a plot mistake.

## The distinction that resolves it

There are two different things in that file and I had conflated them:

| | for the author | for the reader |
|---|---|---|
| the **canon** | all six effects, named, with pairings | **only the ones she has learned** |
| a **name** | how a practitioner refers to it | how the reader refers to it |

**A canon is a working document, not a prologue.** Elsa's notebook does not begin as a
table of six. It begins empty, or with two, or with a list she has been given by a man who
wanted her to have it. Each entry arrives with a person attached, and the cost of learning
it is *the years* — which is the whole reason the acquisition matters.

So the resolution:

1. **`The Reconciled` keeps its name.** A practitioner would have to call it something.
   Removing the name would be worse: it would make the method unnameable, and the fourth
   question requires that it be *sayable* — Elsa has to be able to stand in a room and
   describe what was done, in words the room understands, or she has no proof. The name is
   a *requirement of the proof*, not a leak of it.
2. **The canon is not a reader-facing table.** It is a document in the project, and the
   novel contains six separate acquisitions, each arriving with a person, a cost and a
   failure. A reader in chapter 4 knows two effects. A reader in chapter 26 knows six, and
   by then they have forgotten which of the six they are missing, because that is the
   actual state of a trained professional.
3. **The pairings are withheld, not the names.** She learns *that* an effect exists and
   roughly what class it belongs to. She does not learn its `[presumed / actual]` line
   until she has seen it worked. This is the crucial one: a name is a label, a pairing is
   the mechanism. Give the reader the labels early and the puzzle survives; give them the
   pairings and there is no puzzle.

## What the untitled effect costs, stated honestly

The untitled effect is not free. Here is the bill, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

**1. The reader loses the comfort of a bounded set.** Six named effects is a menu. An
untitled seventh is an open space, and an open space is harder to hold in working memory
across 90,000 words. This is a real cost and it lands in the middle of the book, where
the reader is already holding the route, the dossier and the crew.

**Mitigation, and it is the only one that works:** the reader must be able to *feel* the
gap. Give the school a canon of six with a specific shape — a name for each, a sound to
each, a teacher for three of them — and the seventh slot becomes a hole the reader can feel
the wind in. **A missing name is more memorable than a present one.** A practitioner
listens for the one he has not been taught; that is what makes him a practitioner.

**2. The reveal has less to point at.** With a named effect, Elsa can arrive and say
"this is the Reconciled" and the reader nods. Unnamed, she has to *describe* it, and a
description in prose is slower and less satisfying than a name.

**Mitigation:** the description is better anyway, and this is where the fourth question
earns its name. She has to say it in a room full of people who do not share a vocabulary
— police, a magistrate, a family. **A name is worth nothing to a room that does not know
it.** The unnamed effect forces her to translate the school's grammar into ordinary
language, and *that translation is the proof*: she can only do it because she can rebuild
the trick. If she could merely recite a name, she would be a witness, not an investigator.

**3. It risks looking like a plot hole.** A reader can mistake "she hasn't learned it yet"
for "the author hasn't worked it out yet." This is the serious one, and it is a
*sequencing* problem rather than a design problem.

**Mitigation:** the reader is told the seventh exists, early, by someone who names it as a
gap. A performer who says "there are six and I can do five" has done the work: the
absence is now a character's knowledge rather than the author's silence. **An acknowledged
gap is information. An unacknowledged one is a hole.**

## The verdict

**Keep it unnamed, and here is the test that settles it:** *can the reader reconstruct the
effect from evidence they have already been given, without the name?*

If the answer is yes, the name is dead weight at the reveal and the untitled effect costs
nothing. If the answer is no — if reconstruction requires the name — then the fourth
question cannot be asked, because she would be reciting.

**That is the test the cold solve in `05-cold-solve.md` runs.** The ledger is built, the
solution column is sealed, and the reconstruction is attempted from the reader-visible
evidence only. Whatever the answer, it goes in that file and the design changes to match
it. Not before.

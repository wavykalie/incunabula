# Readership — The Trick

**Phase 1.5.** Four segments the book is written for, and one it is deliberately *not*
written for. Each carries evidence, not vibes: the bad-review evidence is the class of
complaint this book is at risk of repeating, which is what `USER_PREFERENCES.md` §2 asks
for.

---

## R-01 — The procedure reader (PRIMARY, the spine)

**Reads:** mysteries where the puzzle is *doable*. Will pause. Will re-read a chapter. Goes
backwards. Keeps notes — often literally, and they will want somewhere to put them.

**Expectations:** fair play. A solution that could have been derived from what was on the
page. No withheld facts. A rulebook that is given, not sprung.

**What they are, in this book:** the trial in Act III is written for them, and the
falsifiable prediction in ch 30 is the moment the book hands them its own ending to
verify. **They are the only segment the book must not bore**, because the trial is long
and a procedure reader will notice a scene that exists for another reader's benefit.

**What would lose them:** a trick explained in a burst of narration at the reveal. A clue
planted in the last three chapters. A dead body with no name. Anything that makes them
say *I could not have got that.*

**Evidence they exist and are large:** the entire Danganronpa audience, and the reason
that series survives is that its readers trust the puzzle. Also the CrimeReads /
Barry-nominated end of the market, which is where a procedure reader's recommendations
actually travel.

---

## R-02 — The performance reader (PRIMARY, the pleasure)

**Reads:** for the *craft*. Knows or suspects something about how it works. Comes for the
mechanism and stays for the book. Reads the same scene twice — once for story, once to
see how the trick is done.

**Expectations:** the mechanism is real *or* beautifully invented, and either way it is
**explained**. Will be bored by mystification. Wants the vocabulary.

**What they are, in this book:** the Corbeau school is written for them and the
`[presumed / actual]` notation is a gift to them. The dossier is a genre document. **They
are the segment the naming system and the school vocabulary serve directly.**

**What would lose them:** a method described only as a sensation. A reveal that is
"magic." A trick that could not be performed or, here, could not be *described.*

**Evidence:** a smaller and craft-literate group, but disproportionately influential —
they review, they teach, they are the people who put a conjuring book into a person's
hands.

---

## R-03 — The travelling reader (SECONDARY, the room)

**Reads:** for the ensemble and the *places*. Does not solve. Will follow a book for a
city, a theatre, a port, a winter. Notices when a character is merely functional.

**Expectations:** the world is specific and consistent. The countries feel like different
countries, and the three orthodoxies feel like three different ways of working.

**What they are, in this book:** the reason the tour survives the trial frame. This is the
segment that would be lost by a straight Danganronpa redesign, and **the reason the hybrid
was chosen over the closed room.**

**What would lose them:** three countries that feel like one country with three names on
it. A research detail that exists to be impressive rather than to be lived in.

**Evidence:** the strongest segment for word-of-mouth and the least measurable. They
recommend books by *atmosphere*, which is a real and expensive thing to be good at.

---

## R-04 — The reluctant reader (SECONDARY, the commercial case)

**Reads:** a little. Picked it up because of the cover, or because they liked a film, or
because it was short. Will give it two hundred pages. Will not finish a book that asks
them to be alert.

**Expectations:** a *situation* they can follow without a rulebook. A person they can
hold in their head. Not necessarily a person they like.

**What they are, in this book:** Elsa refusing a stage name is the hook, because it is
legible in one line and it is a whole argument about the trade. **The book must be
readable by someone who has never seen a `[presumed / actual]` pair and does not need to.**

**What would lose them:** a first chapter that opens inside school vocabulary. Any scene
whose only content is a distinction between two conjuring techniques.

**Design consequence, and it is a hard one:** the school vocabulary must be *taught in
ordinary language first* and named second. If R-04 cannot follow ch 1, the book has a
commercial problem that no amount of puzzle quality fixes.

---

## EXCLUDED — cozy / no-body readers (deliberately)

**Not a segment this book is written for.** A subset of the market wants a mystery with no
corpse, no threat, and a solution delivered by a likeable person over tea. **There is
deliberate death in this book, three times, and it is not done offstage.**

This is recorded rather than assumed, because excluding a segment is a decision with a
cost: it removes the largest single pool of casual mystery buyers. The compensation is
that R-01 to R-04 are all segments that *re-read*, and the comp set (Tudor's failure mode,
"great premise, at the 80% the execution let them down") is a reminder that a soft book is
the worse commercial outcome anyway.

---

## The risk this book actually carries

**R-01 and R-04 pull in opposite directions on the trial.** R-01 wants the puzzle to be
open long enough to be worth solving; R-04 wants nothing asked of them for two hundred
pages. **Every scene in Act III is a negotiation between them, and the negotiation is the
job.** Writing the trial for R-01 will lose R-04 by ch 28. Writing it for R-04 will lose
R-01 permanently, because a procedure reader who feels humoured has been told the book is
not trying.

The resolution used here: **Act I is written for R-04 (a person, a body, a strange
occupation) and Act III is written for R-01 (a demonstration), and the hinge is ch 22,
where the reader who has not been paying attention is told, in one paragraph, that they do
not need to solve it — they need to watch someone else fail to.**

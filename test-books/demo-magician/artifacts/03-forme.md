# Forme — The foundation

**Phase 2.** Written before any chapter. Everything here is a decision; nothing here is
advisory, and anything the draft wants to change has to come back to this file.

**Theme, as a question and never as an answer:**

> **When does a person stop being a person — a man, a woman, a body, a *someone* — and
> become only a description others are carrying?**

The trick is the literal mechanism: a man is described rather than seen, and the room
accepts the description because describing is easier than looking. The book is about that
and nothing else, and the answer it must not give is that people are only their
descriptions. The ending denies the answer by showing the descriptions being *repaired*,
which is the only honest ending available and is also the least satisfying one, which is
correct.

## 1. The characters

### Elsa Reiner (34) — protagonist, working conjurer

**Wants:** to be able to say a true thing in a room that will hear her.
**Needs:** to be told that her skill is a way of talking, not a way of hiding.
**Lie:** *I have nothing to perform, therefore I have nothing to be accused of.* — She
believes flatness is proof of innocence, which is a rule she invented because it was
safer than the alternative.
**Wound:** six years of working a trade where the only people who were ever honest with
her were the ones she was deceiving. She is fluent in misdirection and has never had to
use it on anyone who mattered.
**Arc (positive):** the lie breaks in ch 22, when the test only works because she is a
performer — a *specific* performer, with a specific history, in front of a specific room.
She has been the plainest name in the book and it turns out to be an achievement.
**Cost of the arc:** the thing she refuses to perform is the one thing she performs.
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: she can name the province of origin of any
nineteenth-century coin by weight alone, and does so at the worst moments. Cognitive
distortion: she reads rooms as audiences first and as rooms second, so she underestimates
people who are not watching her — which is a fatal error in a book about a man nobody was
watching.

### Rask (late 50s) — the best Corbeau living, no stage name

**Wants:** to be left alone with a thing he knows and nobody has asked him to prove.
**Lie:** *Refusal is integrity.* It is mostly integrity. The residue is that he would
rather be unfamous than find out whether he was any good, and he has been using the
refusal to avoid the question for forty years.
**Wound:** he taught the man. Briefly, badly, in 1919, in a town neither of them will name
in this book. **He is the only person who has met him and is still alive.** That is the
single most dangerous fact in the book and he knows it from ch 4.
**Arc (flat, and hard to earn):** he does not change. What changes is that he finally
answers the question he has been avoiding — not for Elsa, not for a court, but because
she gets the answer first and then has to decide what to do with it. **The book must not
make him a mentor who softens.**
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: he will not eat in a room where someone is
watching him eat, and has not for forty years. Cognitive distortion: he believes his
refusal to perform was principled. It was, mostly, and he has never been able to check.

### Sigrid Voll (29) — northern tent, never inflated

**Wants:** the booking she has earned, in a trade that pays for the volume of the voice.
**Lie:** *Plainness is a position.* It is, in the tent. She has carried it into the salle
where it reads as an insult and has begun to believe it is a boast.
**Wound:** a childhood in a troupe where her father performed as *le Grand* something and
was laughed out of two cities. She watched a large name fail and decided it would not
happen to her. She is right about large names and she has paid for it in every booking
since.
**Arc (positive, small):** she inflates. Once, in public, at a moment that costs her, and
the name she takes is small and ugly and *works*. The audience laughs and it is not at her.
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: she can tell the size of a tent audience from the
sound the canvas makes. Cognitive distortion: she believes she is a purist, and is
actually the most conventional performer in the book — she just uses a different convention.

### Halvard Grens (38) — northern tent, "rock guardian"

**Wants:** to be thought of as solid.
**Lie:** *A boundary is the same as a defence.* He has guarded two houses, a customs shed
and a rail siding and never once been asked what he was guarding against.
**Wound:** a manslaughter he was cleared of, and the clearing was correct, and he has
been eight years in a tent because a job on a railway would put him near the paperwork.
**Arc (negative, and he does not notice):** the lie **wins.** He is solid, and he protects
the troupe, and at the trial he states a true thing that convicts him, and he never once
understands that he did it.
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: he can hear a bad weld in a room. Cognitive
distortion: he reads a person as a thing to be defended against, and is baffled by Elsa,
who is defended against nothing.

### Anouk Soldano / L'Ombrière (40s) — salle, keeps the dossiers

**Wants:** the dossiers to be complete. It is a life's work and it is, to her, civic.
**Lie:** *A record is a defence.* Hired by her to make men defensible, she has never asked
what a record is *for* until the trial asks it in front of her.
**Wound:** she has been paid to write down, in order, that men are honest. She has never
been asked to write down that one is not. **The dossier she keeps is the book of the
person she helped, and she built it herself, correctly.**
**Arc (positive, and the most expensive in the book):** she testifies. She is the worst
witness in the room and the only one who is right, because she can say exactly what he
wrote and when, and a room that wants to disbelieve him believes her.
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: she alphabetises by the *venue's* name and is
insufferable about it. Cognitive distortion: she assumes the record is neutral. It has
never once been neutral. It is only accurate.

### Brynjar Sigmundsson / La Furette (34) — ports, the ferret

**Wants:** to be from somewhere. He is from three places and belongs to none.
**Lie:** *Information is neutral.* He has sold a fact to the wrong man in three countries
and it has not once cost him anything, which he has taken as evidence that facts are
innocent.
**Wound:** he is the reason the fourth death happened. Not the man — the *information*, in
the wrong mouth, in a port, because he could not be bothered to ask what the man wanted it
for. **He kills by not asking a question**, and the book is the process of him learning
that this is a thing he did.
**Arc (positive, hardest):** he testifies too, and the thing he testifies to is that he
sold it, and no one believes him, because a man who sells information is not a witness.
**Chaos profile.** Irrelevant obsession: he has never stayed anywhere past the season
since 1921. Cognitive distortion: he believes he is a neutral party. He is the *least*
neutral person in the book and the only one who would call himself a neutral.

### Le Corbeau — the killer. Never in POV.

**Wants:** the work done and no one competent in a position to ask how.
**Lie:** *A method that works is a method that was correct.* He believes this, and it is
the most nearly defensible belief in the book.
**Wound:** Rask, 1919, a town nobody will name.
**Arc:** none. **He is not in a spiral and must not be written as one.** He gets worse in
the ports, and it is visible only to a reader who knows the grammar, and it is not
emotional deterioration — it is a craftsman running out of the thing he is good at.

**The dossier is his interior.** Everything the reader knows about him is what he filed,
what he printed, and what a room did while he was in it. **No scene is written from
inside him. Not one. He is the only character in the book the reader never occupies, and
that is the design, not an omission.**

## 2. The emotional curve, chapter by chapter

| ch | beat | reader is feeling |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | a corpse whose official account is not merely wrong but *obligatorily* wrong | unease, then a hook: the wrongness has a shape |
| 3–5 | Elsa says the thing nobody wants to hear and is right | alliance. the reader wants her to be right |
| 6–8 | the trade, the three names, the room | pleasure. this is where a lot of readers fall in love with the book |
| 9–10 | the dossier | **the first cold turn.** the reader realises he is a man, not a monster |
| 11–13 | the second death, the same hand, a different orthodoxy | dread, and the beginnings of the method without its name |
| 14 | **HIGH** — Sigrid's booking, or the tent going up in weather. A chapter with no murder in it | *relief*, and the reader trusts the book for the first time |
| 15–17 | the assembly. The circuit is forced together and the grammar breaks | claustrophobia. the reader is inside a room now |
| 18–19 | the port. The third death, the worst one, and he has stopped using the grammar | the floor. and the reader knows before the book does |
| 20 | **THE MIDDLE OF THE BOOK.** He performs. It works. He is at ease. | *dread with nowhere to go* |
| 21–22 | Elsa's test. Aldrich. "He could not have got the room wrong." | **the release.** the reader has been holding this for six chapters |
| 23–24 | Rask's 1919 | vertigo. the one thing nobody said |
| 25–29 | the trial. Procedure, prediction, a room that confirms | the pleasure of a demonstration |
| 30–31 | **THE HIGHEST BEAT.** He is required to perform, and cannot, because there is no retinue — everyone is a witness, and a trick needs a retinue | the turn of the book |
| 32–33 | testimony. Anouk, then Brynjar, each telling the truth and not believed | discomfort. the reader wants someone believed |
| 34 | the reconciling, and the repair | **not triumph.** a small good, honestly |

**The rule from the last book, which is a real rule and not a preference:** the most
devastating chapter is never the last one, because the last one has to resolve something.
Ch 31 is the devastation. Ch 34 is two hundred words of work being done.

## 3. Reading pace

- **Ch 14 is a rest chapter and it is mandatory.** Relief has to be earned by density and
  it is the only chapter in the book with no death in it. A book with three murders and no
  rest chapter is a book that teaches a reader to stop feeling them.
- **The trial is 7 chapters and that is long.** It is justified by R-01 and it is the
  book's main commercial risk, which is why the hinge at ch 22 tells the reader they do not
  have to solve it.
- **Dialect: the book is in English. French and German appear in the dossier as
  documents, in the original, untranslated, because a translation is a thing the reader
  did not ask for.** Elsa reads them. Everyone else is guessing.

## 4. The high-impact beats and their planting

| beat | ch | must be planted by |
|---|---|---|
| the third death in the port is the same hand | 18 | ch 11, and again ch 13 |
| the method's actual shape — attention, not motion | 21 | ch 4, ch 9, ch 13 |
| Rask taught him | 23 | **ch 4.** the earliest plant in the book, and it is a throwaway line |
| he cannot perform without a retinue | 31 | ch 6, ch 19, ch 25 |
| Elsa's test works | 22 | ch 2, and Aldrich's account in ch 20 |

**Before any of these lands:** the character is shown vulnerable, shown trying, and shown
what is at stake. For ch 31 that means the reader has watched him be *good* — ch 20 exists
for that alone and must be written as a pleasure to read.

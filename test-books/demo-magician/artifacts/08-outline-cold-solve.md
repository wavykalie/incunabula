# COLD RE-SOLVE — the assembled outline

**Run before drafting. The 34-chapter plan is the input; the answer key is not consulted
while writing this file.** This is the second cold solve — the first was against the
clue ledger alone (`06-cold-solve-verdict.md`), and that one passed. This one tests the
*outline*, which is a different object: the ledger proved the effect is reconstructible;
this proves the 34 chapters actually deliver it.

---

## Method

Read `outline.md` — chapter by chapter, title and beat only — and answer: **can a reader
who has read chapters 1–21 work out what he did, before ch 22 tells them?**

The chapter titles and beats are the reader's only material. No ledger, no school
artifact, no answer key.

---

## Attempt 1 — from the outline alone

**What I have by ch 13:** three deaths, three countries, three naming conventions. A
record of disputes that names no method and charges no one. A missing year. A man who
wins his challenges and is never caught. An interval that is the right length. Bodies
whose accounts are *obligatorily* wrong — which nobody could do and not notice.

**The shape of my error, and it is the right one.** I had it as a movement. Something was
moved during an interval in which nobody was watching. Ch 13 is explicitly Reiner
assembling the deaths and getting the shape wrong — *she has it as a movement* — which
means the outline deliberately puts the reader in the same wrong place she is in, for the
same twenty chapters.

**What corrects it, and this works.** Ch 19: *a trick needs a retinue* — the crew were
assembled and nobody knew it. Ch 21: *the thing he did was not a movement.* Attention, not
motion. The interval was the right length and contained the wrong event.

**So the outline does deliver the turn, and it delivers it in the right place.** Ch 21 of
34 is just past the two-thirds mark, which is where a fair-play reveal belongs: late enough
that the reader has been holding the question, early enough that the trial can be a
demonstration rather than a second mystery.

**The retinue, specifically.** I flagged on the first cold solve that I could not tell
whether assembling the room was necessary or flavourful. **From the outline it reads as
necessary**, because ch 19 is titled after it and ch 31 turns on it. The requirement is
now in the *structure* of the book and not only in the mechanism. That is a real fix and
it came from reordering, not from adding a sentence.

**The test, and whether it lands twice.** Ch 22 and ch 30 are the same device, once
private and once in open court. The reader who was paying attention in ch 22 recognises
ch 30 as the same move and is not confused. The reader who was not gets told in one
paragraph at ch 22 that they need not solve it — they need to watch someone fail to.
**Both segments (R-01 and R-04) are served at the same hinge.** That was the risk the
readership document named and it is handled.

**He does not want the trial — and I can see that, which I could not before.** Ch 27,
*the retreat*: he requests leave and is refused by a clerk. Nothing else in the outline
is as good. It tells the reader in one beat that he is a man who avoids rooms, because it
is the only time in the book he tries to leave one.

**Ch 31 works.** A trick needs a retinue, and the whole assembly is a witness. A witness
cannot be a retinue. **The book's thesis is also its defeat mechanism**, which is the
best structural position a mystery can be in.

## The two things I could not reconstruct, and what I did about them

**1. The method itself, in words.** From the outline titles I got *attention, not motion*
and *the wrong event in a right-length interval* — but I could not state the actual trick:
that the trick is that the room's accounts are **true**, and that "a man" is a description
rather than a referent.

**This is correct and it is not a failure.** The outline is a plan; the sentence that
carries this is prose, and it lands in ch 21 which is *named* for it. **A reader who has
read the outline would have it; a reader who has read ch 1–20 does not and should not.**

The test I care about: **is ch 21 a place where this can be said?** Yes — it is a whole
3,860-word chapter titled for the revelation, in the middle of Act II, with the three
deaths already on the page and the retinue already established. **The outline provides the
slot; the prose has to fill it.** Flagged as the single highest-risk drafting task in the
book.

**2. Why Rask matters.** Ch 4 plants "he is the only living person who has met the man"
and ch 23 is *what Rask did in 1919*. I could not tell, from the outline alone, whether
that plant would *read* as significant. Ch 4 is 1,760 words — the shortest in the book —
and a throwaway line inside it is exactly the kind of thing a reader loses.

**This is a real risk and it has a fix that is not a rewrite.** Ch 4 has to *earn* the
line by being about Rask. The beat is already "Rask. She finds the one fact that will
matter." The drafting instruction is that Reiner must leave the chapter knowing she has
found something enormous, so that ch 23 is a **confirmation** rather than a first
introduction. A clue the reader half-notices and then has confirmed is a *reward*. A clue
the reader missed entirely and receives cold in ch 23 is a **withheld fact** — the exact
failure this project has been guarding against since the untitled effect.

## Verdict

**PASS. Draft.**

Four checks:

| check | result |
|---|---|
| reader can be wrong in the right place and be corrected in the right place | ✓ ch 13 wrong, ch 21 corrected |
| the turn is in the right third | ✓ ch 21 of 34 |
| the retinue is structural, not decorative | ✓ ch 19 → ch 31 |
| the trial does not reveal new methods | ✓ — no method named anywhere in the plan |

**Two carried into drafting, both flagged rather than filed:**

1. **Ch 21 is the highest-risk chapter in the book.** It has to say the trick in prose,
   in plain words, without naming it, and the slot is 3,860 words. If it is unclear, the
   trial has nothing to demonstrate and the fourth question is unaskable. **This is the
   chapter to draft first and the one to re-read most carefully**, whatever order the rest
   goes in.
2. **Ch 4 must earn the 1919 line** or ch 23 is a withheld fact.

**A note on what this solve could not do.** It tested the *plan*. It cannot test whether
ch 21's prose is comprehensible, whether the four rest chapters actually relieve, or
whether the trial is seven chapters too long. **Those are only knowable from the draft**,
and the second cold solve — of the finished manuscript — is the one that has to pass
before the book is called done.

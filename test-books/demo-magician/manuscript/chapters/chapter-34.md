# 34 · A Small Repair

There was no verdict, and the reason there was no verdict is that nobody in the room wanted
one, and the reason nobody in the room wanted one is that nobody in the room could have said
what a verdict would be about.

What there was, on the Friday evening, at about five o'clock, in a room in Utrecht that
smelled of wet coats, was a *sentence* — and a *sentence* is not a verdict, and the
assesseur said so twice, and made a point of it, and was rather pleased with himself about
it.

The matter was closed. The *réponse* was held and was not opened. The *requête* was
dismissed. Costs were not awarded against anybody, which the assesseur explained was
unprecedented and which he appeared to regard as a personal achievement.

And there were four persons named in it, and the court recorded four things about them, and
none of the four things was a finding.

---

**Of the first, Mrs J. Vandenbroucke, of the Rue de la République, Marseille**, who had
given evidence about a sheet of paper and a coach and a woman of about sixty-one, and who
had not been asked to attend, and who had sent a letter: that the court notes her evidence
and expresses its thanks and does not require her further attendance.

**Of the second, Mr G. Aldrich, gatekeeper, Binnenweg, Delft**, who had given evidence for
ninety seconds and had asked to go home: that the court notes the accuracy of a statement
made in May 1933 and in October 1933 and finds that both were made in good faith, which is
a thing courts do not usually find.

**Of the third, Mr H. Grens, of Utrecht**, who had described a shed: that the court notes
the evidence of Mr Grens, and expresses the view that the additions made by that witness to
the account of the premises at Rotterdam were made without any intention beyond the desire
to be exact, and that they were exact, and that the court has not been misled by them.

**And of the fourth**, a man named Joachim Corbeau, of no fixed address, of whom it was
recorded that he had answered nine questions in the negative, had filed a *réponse* on the
second of June, had asked for leave and been refused on a point of order, had performed a
sleight of hand in open court at the request of the court, and had been — and here the clerk
had stopped and asked the assesseur how he wanted it, and the assesseur had thought about it
for a while —

"and had been **asked no question in the course of two days concerning the said
storeroom**, and the court records that fact without comment."

"Yes," said the assesseur. "Write that. Without comment."

---

Reiner got the last quarter of an hour of it alone, which nobody designed.

The assesseur left at five. The clerk left at ten past. The notaries went. Aldrich had
gone at four to catch a train to Delft with an express purpose, as he put it, which was the
first thing he had said in two days that had any humour in it, and Brynjar Sigmundsson went
at half past to a port, and L'Ombrière went out into the street and stood in it.

And the hall in Utrecht emptied, and there was a stage nine inches high, and a ceiling of
stamped tin, and four hundred chairs, and a woman who was thirty-five years old standing in
the middle of the floor in a good coat that she had had made in Antwerp in the spring of
the year before, in which she had said *it is not that he is clever* in a shed to a
constable.

She sat down on the stage.

Nine inches is not a height, only the height a hall gives a person when the hall has been
built for a lecturer and not for a woman of thirty-five in a good coat who has just closed a
matter and does not want an audience for what she is going to think about it. She had sat on
the edges of a great many stages and she had never once gone up onto one, and she was not going
to start, and the reason she was not going to start was the same reason she had not opened the
notebook in the recess, which was that a woman who goes up onto a stage is making a claim, and
the whole of the last twelve months had been an argument with a man about what a claim costs,
and she had won it with a register and four guilders, and she was not going to spend it on a
stage.

Nine inches high. She sat on the edge of it with her feet on the floor and her hands behind
her, the way she had sat on eleven hundred edges, and she put her bag on the boards beside
her, and it was in the bag that the envelope was.

---

What was in the envelope was nine sheets of carbon paper and a receipt from a firm in
Antwerp and the copy of an account from a shed in Rotterdam in the May of the year before
last, with the bad punctuation in it, and a small book of Reiner's own in which there were
four pages written in her own hand and nothing else, because in April of the year before
she had stopped writing things down.

The four pages were the door, the occupied room, the eleven seconds, and the man in a good
suit at a table in a hall, and they were not a case and were never going to be a case, and
she had written them in Utrecht in April over a table with four people in the room and had
not looked at them since.

She took them out and put them back.

That was the whole of the dossier of a woman who had kept notes for eleven months and then
stopped, and it was four pages long and it was not a case and it was never going to be a case,
and she had kept it anyway, because a thing you have written down is a thing you can hand to
somebody on a bad afternoon, and she had not had a bad afternoon yet, and the four pages were
in the bag in case she did, and she did not think they would be needed, and she was right, and
being right about that is the loneliest thing in the whole account.

And then she took out a sheet of paper — one sheet, and it was not one of the nine, because
she had bought nine and this was a tenth that she had had in a drawer in Antwerp since March
and had never brought to a room — and she did the only thing in the whole of the four days
that anybody in that hall had actually wanted anybody to do, and it took about eleven
minutes and it was not a trick.

---

**Correction one.** She wrote to the clerk of the tribunal in Rotterdam. It was five lines
and it asked him to search a register for the eleventh of April of the year before last, in
the matter of a premises at the end of a street off the Binnenweg, and to amend one
particular.

The hire of a storeroom on the Kralingen field for one evening had been entered in the
municipal register against the name of a firm of forwarding agents, because the hire had
been paid in cash and a cash hire goes in against whoever asked for it, and the firm that
asked for it was a firm that did not exist.

It was a small administrative error. The name of the lessee had been transcribed from a
receipt. It was a receipt issued in Rotterdam in the March of the year before last by a
man who is still alive and still in Rotterdam and who keeps a shop, and his name was on the
register, and he had never been asked, and he had never known.

He had let a room to a stranger and been paid thirty guilders for it and had entered it
under a name that was not his own because the stranger had said the name was the name of the
firm and he had believed him, and a clerk had written it down, and a storeroom on the
Kralingen field had been a *salle*'s shed from the registry's point of view for eighteen
months, and the man who had been on the floor at the west end for six hours on the
eleventh of April had been standing in a room that the city of Rotterdam believed belonged
to a Danish importer.

She wrote four lines asking for the entry to be corrected, and she paid the four guilders
she was informed it would cost, and she put the receipt for the four guilders in the
envelope with the rest, and she did not tell anybody, and it was the first of the four.

**Correction two.** She wrote to the *Tribunal* at Lausanne on the paper of a firm of
administrateurs, and she paid forty-two guilders, and the entry that was corrected was this:
that a sum awarded to Joachim Corbeau in 1930 had been paid.

It had not been paid. He had won it and had not collected it, and the clerk at Lausanne had
told a woman in a canal house in Amsterdam in 1931, politely, that it was not unusual and
that the clerk had seen it four times in thirty years and had never understood it either.

The sum was still in the tribunal's account. It had been sitting in a court in Switzerland
for four years on the strength of a *sentence* nobody had ever collected, and the *sentence*
was in a file in Rotterdam, and the file was in a drawer in Utrecht, and a woman who had
kept a dossier in a tin for nine years at her own cost had known about it since 1931 and
had not been able to do anything with it.

It took the tribunal five weeks. The clerk there was a woman of about forty who wrote to
L'Ombrière to say that in her opinion the late non-payment ought to have been the subject of
a formal application years ago, and that the fault was entirely the tribunal's, and that
the sum would be paid to the estate of the holder.

There is no estate. There was no family; there had not been one since 1922, and Reiner knew
that because she had looked, in February, in a municipal register in Rotterdam, for reasons
she had told herself were about a name and had turned out to be about a *sentence*.

So the sum was paid to nobody, and there is a receipt in a tin in Amsterdam, and it is the
most useless piece of paper in the entire case file and it is the only one that is not a
lie.

**Correction three.** She wrote to Delft.

The gate at the Binnenweg had been kept by George Aldrich since 1911 and by his predecessor
before him, and in the register at Delft there were two entries for the eleventh of April of
the year before last, and both were in the same hand, and one of them was by a man called
Van Eede, a constable, and one of them was by a man called J. Corbeau, gatekeeper.

She asked that the second be amended. She was told it would cost eleven guilders. She paid
it.

He had signed it. He had signed a register in a yard in Delft on the eleventh of April of
the year before last, as a man doing a job he did not care about, in a good coat, on a
Tuesday, and a man had come through on a bicycle with a case and had asked him to sign, and
he had signed because he was nineteen and it was a Tuesday and it did not occur to him for
one moment that it was remarkable, and it had been in a register ever since.

That is what he did in Rotterdam. On the eleventh of April, three years running, in four
towns, a man had signed a piece of paper, and it had cost him four seconds each time, and
none of the four was a performance, and all four were *preparation*.

**And correction four** was the shortest and it took ninety seconds and it cost nothing at
all, and it is the only one Reiner has ever told anybody about.

She wrote to a man who was not a magistrate, a clerk, an assesseur or a tribunal, in an
office in Rotterdam, in a corridor on the second floor, and asked him to enter in the
municipal register that a storeroom on the Kralingen field, nine feet by eleven, with a
stove, four rows of crates and a drain, had a man in it on the evening of the eleventh of
April of the year before last, and had had a man in it for four hours before that, and had
had twenty-two people in it after that, and that the number of people it had held at
various times on the day was **not established**.

There had never been a number. That was the whole of it. There had been twenty-two
afterwards and one for four hours before and nobody had ever written down how many were
there, because a storeroom is a storeroom and rooms do not keep their own attendance.

And the *requête* said *a man*. And every one of the twenty-two accounts had said *a man*.
And Aldrich had said *a man in a room*. And a fishmonger in Marseille had said *a man*.

And nobody, in eighteen months, in a dossier kept by hand at a woman's own expense, in four
hundred words of inadmissible carbon paper, in ninety minutes in a hall, in two days of
sitting, had ever once written the word *one* in a sentence that was about the room.

That was all. Two hundred words of work, at the end, in a hall in Utrecht, at twenty past
five, in a good coat, sitting on the edge of a stage nine inches high.

---

She put the four letters into an envelope and wrote on it, in a clear hand, a name and an
address in Antwerp, and sealed it, and put it on the seat of the chair at the front of the
hall, under the assesseur's paper.

Then she picked up her bag, and looked at the stage, and did not go up onto it, because
there was nothing to do up there, and because she had sat on the edge of eleven hundred
edges and had never once gone up.

At the door she stopped and looked back at a hall in Utrecht at twenty-five past five on a
Friday in October, with four hundred chairs in it and a tin ceiling and a nine-inch stage
and a chair at the front with an envelope on it.

Three dead men: a man on a crate in a storeroom in Rotterdam, a man in a house on the
Scheldt, a boy of nineteen in Marseille who had stopped going home.

One prediction, correct. One handkerchief, empty. One shed, described by a man who draws
rooms, eleven details, all true, all useless, all in a file. One witness not believed, on
purpose, by arrangement, in a corridor on a stair, a year and a half before it mattered.

And two hundred words of work at the end of it, and not one of them about the trick.

She had spent twelve months finding out how a man moves something in a room while twenty-
two people watch him not move it, and she had got as far as *occupied*, and *four feet*, and
*a flat afternoon that nobody has ever asked anybody about*, and the whole of it added up to
a clerk putting a name in a register in Delft, a tribunal in Lausanne paying a sum to an
estate that did not exist, a forwarding agent's shopfront ceasing to be a *salle*'s shed,
and a number in a municipal file that had never been written down and now was.

The room had held twenty-two people and nobody had ever counted them, and the counting was
the only thing anybody could do, and it took a quarter of an hour and it cost four guilders
and a postage stamp, and she had spent a year learning to do a thing that could not be done
in a room, and she had done it, and it was a quarter of an hour, and the whole of the rest
of the case was a woman in a shed on the Binnenweg being right about a crack in a door
four months after the only person who could be asked had said *I'd have seen him*, and meant
it, and gone home to tell his wife where he had been.

She went out into the street. It was raining. L'Ombrière was standing in it under an
umbrella that had belonged to somebody else.

"Well?"

"It is closed," said Reiner.

"Is he—"

"There is no *he*," said Reiner. "There is a *sentence*. It is in a file and it is four
lines and it says that nobody asked him anything, and in ten years somebody will read it and
they will not know what it means, and that is correct, and it is the right outcome, and I
would have gone on for another year trying to make a court say it in a louder voice, and I
would have failed, and this is better."

She looked up the street towards the station.

"L'Ombrière, I have been in this trade since I was nine and I have been good at one thing
for twenty-five years, and the one thing is turning a room. That is all I have. That is the
entire content of my education and I have a school and a vocabulary and a list of six and a
face that takes a thing down and finds the seam." She was quiet for a moment. "And a man
in a good suit sat at a table in that hall for two days and was never in a room, and I have
been in nine hundred rooms, and the only time in my life I have had the better of it was a
quarter of an hour of correcting a register."

"That is not a very large victory."

"No," said Reiner. "It is four guilders and a postage stamp. And I would do it again
tomorrow, and I would do it in every port in Europe, and I would like it recorded that I
said that in a street in the rain to a woman who has kept a dossier in a tin for nine years
at her own wages, and that I meant it, and that neither of us is going to be able to explain
it to anybody."

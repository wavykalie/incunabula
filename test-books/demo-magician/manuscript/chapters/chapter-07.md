# 7 · La Furette and the Wrong Name

He was called four different things in three days and none of them was his fault, which was
the first thing Reiner liked about him and the reason she was suspicious of him within a
week.

On the Tuesday he was Brynjar, at a café in the Haven, where a Belgian woman of about
sixty had been introduced to him by name and had subsequently addressed him twice as
Briand, because the woman ahead of her in 1919 had been married to a Briand and names go
where names have been.

On the Wednesday he was Bernard, at a warehouse office, because the man across the desk had
written *Bernard* on a docket in 1931 and had never questioned it, and Brynjar had let it
stand for the whole of a conversation about freight.

And on the Thursday morning he was Signor Furette, with an Italian, who said it twice and
meant it, and who then apologised, and who then said it a third time.

"They will call you that all week now," Reiner said. "You know that. Once it has been
offered it is a thing they will use."

"Yes." He drank his coffee. "It costs me nothing and it saves me a great deal. In a port
a man is what the last man called him, and if you fight it you are a man with a story, and
a man with a story in a port is a man somebody stops talking to."

"Which is worse than being called the wrong name."

"Considerably worse." He put the cup down. "You have been in the north eleven days and you
have said your own name to me once and corrected it twice and been quite pleased with
yourself about it, and I have watched you do it, and I would like to be permitted to say
that I have never in my life been sure of a thing as cheaply as you are, and that the
certainty is not founded on anything."

"Go on."

"You are not from the north, you are from a port, and in a port you learn inside a week
that a name is a price on a door. You have not. You say your name like a woman who has
never had it taken off her." He looked at her. "It is a good coat and a good name and
eleven days of tent life, and you are a very good conjurer, and I am not saying that to be
unkind, I am saying it because I have watched you do it three times this morning and every
time somebody said your name slightly wrong you corrected it inside a quarter of a second,
which is not a habit, it is a reflex, and reflexes are put in by somebody else."

Reiner had no answer to that at all, and she thought about it for a week afterwards.

---

He sold things. That was the second thing, and it was the thing that eventually made her
careful with him.

Not information — he was very firm about that, twice, in a way she wrote down. He sold
*access*. He knew which of the tent men would take a booking in a town where the others
would not, because he had been in eleven countries in nine years and he had been in all
of them as a man with a bicycle, and a man with a bicycle is a man nobody looks at.

"You are selling them the circuit," Reiner said, on the Thursday, in the corridor.

"I am selling them a Sunday."

"That is not the same thing."

"It is the same thing and you will find that out in about four years, and everybody finds
it out in about four years, and the ones who find it out earlier are the ones that do not
last." He was folding a map. "Do you know what Halvard is paid?"

"No."

"Neither does Halvard. He is paid a share, and the share is decided by a man in an office
in Delft who has never been in a tent, and Halvard is the best book man in the north and
has been for eleven years and is paid less than Johan, and Johan cannot hold a pencil."
Brynjar did the fold again, and got it wrong, and did it again. "So the circuit is the
product. The Sundays come out of the circuit. Everybody understands that and nobody says
it, and a man who says it out loud is a man who is about to be asked to stop."

"Who asks him to stop?"

"Delft."

Reiner wrote that down and underlined it, and it was the single most useful thing anybody
said to her in the whole of that autumn.

---

The evening of the Thursday was the one she thought about afterwards, and it was not the
Sunday conversation, it was the twenty minutes after it, in a yard in Antwerp with a man
she had met twice.

"He told you what the circuit is worth."

"He told me what a Sunday is worth."

"It is the same number and you know it is the same number, and you wrote it down, and you
underlined it." He was not smiling. "Miss Reiner. I want to give you something and I want
you to know that I am not giving it to be kind, and I would like it recorded that I did not
hesitate, because I have thought about it for a day and a half and I have decided that the
man I would have to feel about it afterwards is a man I would rather be, and I have no
way of explaining that and I am not going to try."

"Brynjar—"

"The first time I saw him, I was nineteen and I was carrying a bicycle, and he gave me a
booking in a town I had never worked in, at four times the going rate, for nothing, and he
did it in front of eleven people, and he said *I would like you to be able to say that a
man was decent to you once before you were twenty*." He stopped. "That is all of it. That
is the entire man. It took him four sentences and a good rate and I have carried it for
eleven years, and I have never been able to make it into anything else, and I have tried,
and in the winter of 1934 I very nearly did get him killed for it."

The yard was very quiet.

"You have just told me a thing," Reiner said, "that you have not told a living soul, for
no reason that I can see, and I would like you to tell me what you want."

"Nothing. That is what I want and that is why I did it." He was looking at the wall.
"Everyone I have told that to has wanted something, and I have watched what happens to the
people who give things to you when you have not given them anything, and it is that they
find out you have nothing, and it is always after the wedding."

Reiner stood in the cold and understood that she had been in a hundred rooms and had never
once been in this one.

"You are afraid he is in Antwerp," she said.

"I am afraid he is *near* Antwerp," said Brynjar Sigmundsson, "and I am more afraid of
that, and I have been more afraid of it since a woman came out of a shed in Rotterdam
having done nothing at all to a constable for eleven seconds and then went and stood on a
boat and told a commercial traveller the truth, which is a thing I have never once seen a
conjurer do in fifty years and which I would not have believed if I had not read it in
Rask's letter."

Reiner did not say anything for a moment. Then she said, "Nobody taught him."

"No."

"You have thought about that."

"I have thought about very little else since Tuesday," said Brynjar, "and I have a bad
memory and a bad conscience and no evidence whatever, and if you repeat any of this I will
deny all of it, and I would like that understood now while we are both calm."

Then he took out a small book with a rubber band round it, and did not give it to her, and
said, "This is not free."

"How much."

"Fifty guilders."

"For what."

"For the fact that on the eleventh of March, in 1929, a man wrote to a tribunal and told
them where a box was in a field, and the box was not in the field that day, and eleven
hundred guilders and a baker's year are what happened to the man who tried to check."
Brynjar held the book in both hands and did not move it. "I have not seen it. I have been
told it exists by a man who has seen it, and the man who has seen it has been waiting
thirty-one years for somebody to ask, and he will not ask, and I have been trying since
March to decide whether to ask for you, and today I have decided to ask for you, and I have
decided that fifty guilders is what it costs, and I would like to be paid before I open
it, because if I open it and it is nothing then I have given it away, and if I open it and
it is something then I have been paid, and I have thought about that sentence a great deal
and I am not able to improve it."

Reiner had fifty-one guilders.

She paid him, and he opened it, and there were four sheets of carbon and a cloth binding,
and the first of them was a *requête* from a baker, and she had seen it four days before in
a room above a tobacconist in Rotterdam, and it was not the same copy, and it was the same
document.

"There are two," she said. "Or there is a copy and an original, and I have seen a third."

"There are more than two. There are nine that I know of and I have been offered four." He
took the money and put it in his inside pocket and did not give her the book. "That is
what I am. That is the whole of what I am, and I have just sold you a fact I did not own
and could not prove, and I have done it because a man in a shed in Rotterdam is going to
be asked a question in a room and nobody is going to ask the right one, and I am the only
man I know who has ever been able to afford to pay for the answer."

"And what will you tell them, when they ask you?"

"That I sold it," said Brynjar Sigmundsson. He put the rubber band back on the book and
set it on the crate between them, and left his hand flat on top of it, the way
L'Ombrière had done with her tin, and Reiner understood that she was watching a thing
everybody in this trade did and that nobody had ever told her. "They will write it down
and they will believe it, because a man who sells information is not a witness."

He took his hand off the book.

"Take it," he said. "And do not fold it, it is older than both of us and it is the only
copy that is not in a tin in a solicitor's office, and I have not said one word to you
about the fact that I am giving you the only copy."

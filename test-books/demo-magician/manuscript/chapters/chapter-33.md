# 33 · What Brynjar Sold

Brynjar Sigmundsson was born in Odense and had been called four different things in three
days in Antwerp in the autumn of the year before last, none of which was his fault, and
none of which he had ever corrected, and it turned out to be the single most useful thing
about him.

He gave evidence in the middle of Friday afternoon and he was extremely good at it in the
way that a man is good at a thing he does not want to do and has decided to do properly.

He was not frightened. She had expected frightened. She had spent two days in a building
watching Aldrich not be frightened and had assumed that the whole of that was a professional
achievement, and here was a man who had sold information for eleven years being asked about
it in a court and not being frightened at all.

"My name is Brynjar Sigmundsson. I have been called Bernard and I have been called Briand
and I have been called Signor Furette and none of them is wrong and none of them is the
one I would choose, and in Antwerp in 'thirty-two I let three people call me three things
inside a day and I have thought about it perhaps twice since, and both times I have thought
that it is the most useful thing I own."

"You have a trade."

"I have four. I sell access, I sell Sundays, I have carried a bicycle for eleven years,
and I have sold the fact that a man wrote a letter to a tribunal in 1929 and that a
baker of Delft paid eleven hundred guilders for the privilege of not digging in a field."

The hall made a small noise.

"Would you repeat that."

"I sold the second one to a woman in a corridor in Utrecht in the autumn of the year before
last for fifty guilders, and I did not have it, and I could not prove it, and I have said
in a corridor to that same woman that I was going to tell this court that, and I am telling
it, and I would like it noticed that I am the only person in this room who has said
anything here that is against himself."

"Mr Sigmundsson—"

"I am going to say the rest of it and then you may say whatever you like."

---

"You cannot be a witness to a document you have never seen."

"I have not seen it. I have heard of it from a man who has seen it. I paid fifty guilders
for the fact of it and I have never received the document and I am not going to tell this
court that I did, and I want that in the transcript in my words: **I sold a fact. I did not
sell a document. There is no document.**"

"Then you have nothing to give this court."

"I have nothing to give this court about the document," said Brynjar. "I have something to
give this court about the man, and I am going to give it, and I have known for four days
that I was going to have to, and I have not slept, and I would like it noted that I was
told I would not be believed and I have decided that this is the correct outcome and I would
like to explain why, and I will then stop and this will take about four minutes."

"Proceed."

"In 1931 I was carrying a bicycle through a port and a man gave me a booking at four times
the going rate for nothing. I was nineteen. He did it in front of eleven people and he said
*I would like you to be able to say that a man was decent to you once before you were
twenty.*"

The clerk was writing.

"That is all of it. I have carried that for eleven years and I have tried to make it into
something else on about four occasions and I have never managed it, and in the winter of
'thirty-four I very nearly got a man killed with it, and I did not, and I have never told
anybody what the near thing was, and I am telling it to this court because I think this
court is the only place in the world where it is any use."

"You have a grievance."

"I have an *admiration*," said Brynjar Sigmundsson. "I have had it for eleven years. It is
the most useless thing I own and I have made a considerable amount of money out of
disposing of it, and I sold this woman in Utrecht the thing I sold her because I have a
dossier in a tin in Antwerp that I have never opened and that I have been offered four
hundred guilders for, and I did not sell her a dossier. I sold her a rumour that four
people have since confirmed and that nobody will now admit to having given me."

"Who gave it to you."

"That," said Brynjar, "is the only question in two days I have been waiting for, and I am
not going to answer it, and I want the court to understand that I am not refusing. I am
answering. There is a woman in Antwerp who is sixty-one years old who kept a boarding
house for nineteen years on the Rue de la République, and she has been asked by three
people about a coach in September, and one of them was me, and I gave her a card, and if I
say her name in this hall then there is a fourth person in that house and she will be
asked a fourth time, and in ten years there will be a file with a name in it, and she is
sixty-one and she kept a boarding house and she is not part of this and I will not put her
in a file."

The assesseur looked at him for some time.

"You are aware that you have now told this court that you are not a credible witness."

"I am aware."

"You have told us you sold the information. You have told us you have a dossier you have
never opened. You have told us you have been offered four hundred guilders for it. You
have declined to name a source. Do you understand that a man who sells information is not a
witness?"

"Yes, sir," said Brynjar Sigmundsson. "I have understood that for about four years. I
understood it in a corridor in Utrecht, on a stair, in the autumn, before I had said any of
this to anybody, and I said it to that woman there and she asked me what I would tell them
and I told her, and she went white, and I said: *that is the correct answer and you should
write it down now while you have me.*"

"And did she write it down."

"She did not," said Brynjar, "and I have thought about that a great deal in four days, and
I have understood that she did not write it down because she did not believe I would say
it, and I have not come here to be believed, and I have come here so that there is one
document in the world in which it is written down that I said it, and if this is a lie then
somebody can correct it and if it is not a lie then in ten years there is a file that says
Brynjar Sigmundsson told the truth in a court in Utrecht, and that is the whole return I
am getting and I have wanted it for four years and I have earned it this badly."

---

The assesseur thanked him, and there was a long pause, and the man in the wig looked at the
clerk and then at the hall, and Reiner — who had been sitting fourth row with a shut
notebook for two days and who had given one prediction and had it answered correctly and had
watched a handkerchief and a shed and a book man — understood that the court was about to do
the thing that courts do, and that it was going to do it kindly, which made it worse.

"You are a man who sells information," said the assesseur. "You are not a witness to this
matter. You have said so yourself and you have said it better than the court could have said
it, and I am going to record that you are not a witness, and I am going to record *why*,
and I want everyone in this hall to hear the reason, because it is the most useful thing
anybody has said in two days and it was said by a man who was not being asked."

He looked round.

"A man who sells information is not a witness, because a man who sells information has no
reason to be where he was. That is not a moral judgement, it is an evidentiary one, and it
is the law, and it is correct, and it has been correct for a very long time.

"And it is the ninth *réponse* this court has seen in nineteen years that was filed by a
man who was in no position to be in the room where the thing happened, and every one of
the nine was correct, and every one of the nine was complete, and every one of the nine was
four words long, and not one of them could be tested by anybody, ever, and I have been the
assessor in this room for all nineteen years and I have let all nine of them stand."

He put the pen down.

"Madam Reiner has done in two days what I have not seen done in nineteen years, and she has
done it with a door, and she is the first person in my experience to understand that the
charge in this matter is not a charge of doing a thing. It is a charge of *being in a room
that could not account for him*. And he has answered it seven times in a tin in Antwerp with
a piece of paper, and it has never once been possible to open one."

---

Afterwards, in the yard, in about four minutes of light, Brynjar Sigmundsson was smoking a
cigarette he had bought with part of the fifty guilders and Reiner was standing next to him
and neither of them said anything for a while.

"You told them."

"I told them."

"You said you would not be believed."

"They did not believe me," said Brynjar. "They wrote down that I am not a witness, which is
what I said in a corridor on a stair, and they believed every word of that part, and it was
the only part I needed."

"You are going to be all right?"

"I am going to go to a port," said Brynjar, "and I am going to be called a third name, and
in four years somebody is going to ask a woman in a canal house in Amsterdam about a
letter and she is going to say there were three people in the house and there were, and that
one of them sold it, and there will be a record of that, and it will be the only record of
me in the world, and my name will be in it."

He put the cigarette out on the wall.

"Miss Reiner. He came to the court. I want to say that, because nobody has said it and it
is the one thing I have. Nine years of not being in a room and he came and sat at a
table for two days and he did a handkerchief, and he did it because a man asked him a
question out loud and there was no paper for it, and I have been in this trade since I was
nineteen and I have never in my life seen a man do the one thing in the world he has spent
his whole life not doing, and I was in the third row, and I am the only person in that hall
who knew what it had cost him, and I could not say so, and I was not allowed to, and I am
telling you in a yard and you will write it down, and it worth the paper."

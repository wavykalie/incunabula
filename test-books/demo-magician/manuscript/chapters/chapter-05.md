# 5 · The Coffer

The tribunal record was four sheets of carbon and a cloth binding in a room above a tobacconist
in Rotterdam, and the woman who kept it had kept it for thirty-one years and had no
particular feeling about it.

"You will want to know why I have it," she said, when Reiner explained. "I have it because
in 1929 the tribunal wrote to me and asked whether I was the proprietor of the premises, and
I said yes, and then they never asked me anything else. That is the whole of my connection
with it. A letter."

She put it on the table. The four sheets smelled of a press that had not been oiled since
the war, and the first of them was a *requête* — a formal challenge, dated the second of
March, 1929, made by one Willem Carels, a baker, of Delft, who set out in nine lines of
heavy going that on the eleventh of March, at the Grand Cirque Scandinave, in a tent on the
Kralingen field, a man had performed an effect by which a sealed coffer had been caused to
appear upon a table, and that the coffer had not been in the tent before the effect began,
and that he would withdraw the charge of complicity against everybody present if the method
was named.

Reiner read it twice. She had heard this shape of document perhaps nine times in her life
and it had never once been honest, because the interesting part of a challenge is always
what it is afraid of, and a baker afraid of complicity is a man who thinks the tent is
dangerous rather than a man who thinks it is false.

Second sheet. The *réponse* — the answer, which had to name the method, and had to be
delivered to the tribunal in a sealed envelope eight days before the hearing or the
challenge lapsed.

It was four lines long.

> *The coffer was in the tent.*

> *The coffer was on the field, under the third stanchion from the left, at a depth of
> two feet, from the morning of the second of March.*

> *I have not moved it. I have not touched it. I propose that the gentleman who has written
> to you should dig where I have told him, in front of two notaries, and that he should then
> say what his nine lines are for.*

> *A. le Corbeau.*

Reiner put the four sheets down in a particular order, which was the order they had been
in, and then put them back in a different order, which was not.

"The field," she said.

"Go on."

"It is a grass field. In March. A tent goes up on it, it comes down, and it goes up again
on the eighteenth of the same month, and the third stanchion from the left is not in the
same place on the two dates, because the ring is a circle and the entrances are put in where
the ground is flat and the ground is not flat." She looked up. "He has told Carels where
the coffer is, and the place he has described is the place it was on the second of March,
and Carels would have to have dug it up on the second, and he did not, because the letter
is dated the second and the tribunal required a sealed answer eight days before, and he
sent it eight days before, which means he had it dug out or he knew where it was without
digging."

The woman across the table said, "That is what I have always wondered."

"You have not read the third sheet."

"I have read the third sheet. Everybody reads the third sheet."

The third sheet was a *sentence* — an order, the tribunal disposing of the matter. It was
two lines. The challenge was dismissed. Costs were awarded against the challenger, in the
sum of eleven hundred guilders, on the ground that the reply had been received in time and
was, in the tribunal's word, *not without merit*, and the tribunal expressed no view on the
coffer, which was by then no longer in the field.

"He won it," Reiner said. "He won it and he was not cleared and he was not accused and it
cost Carels eleven hundred guilders, which is a year, and Carels was a baker, and the
tribunal has written *not without merit* in a document that is going to be read by four
hundred people in a room in about a month."

"Yes."

"Then why," Reiner said, "is the third sheet in a different typeface from the first two."

The woman behind the counter looked at her for the first time with any attention at all.

"It is not," she said.

"It is. Look at it. The *requête* is a typewriter face. The *réponse* is a typewriter face.
The *sentence* is not, and the *sentence* is the only one of the three with his name on
it, and the *sentence* is the only one that names the coffer's location at all." Reiner
sat back. "He did not send that in. He came and dictated it, or he wrote it out, and it was
typed by somebody who was not the clerk, and the tribunal took it, and the tribunal takes
what it is given because a tribunal is a room with a great many documents in it and no time
for a typeface."

The woman put her hand flat on the counter. "He was in here. I remember him. He was a
pleasant man and he asked for a glass of water and he waited while a young man typed it,
and the young man complained about the machine, and he said *I have never had a complaint
in my life from a machine*, and I thought that was a nice thing to say." She stopped. "It
is a nice thing to say."

"It is," Reiner agreed. "What else."

"He was very good with his hands. I noticed because he held the glass." The woman thought
about it. "He held it the way a man holds a glass who is used to holding things that
other people are watching him hold. I have seen a very few people do that and they are
nearly always in the trade."

"Is there anything else. Anything at all. Anything that struck you as odd."

"Nothing struck me as odd," the woman said, "and I have thought about that for thirty-one
years, and it is the only part of it that troubles me. He could have done anything. He
could have had a man in the crowd, he could have had the tent, he could have had the
ground dug in front of twenty people. He did not need any of it, he came into my shop and
gave me the whole of it in a typed letter that a baker never dug, and the baker paid eleven
hundred guilders for being told where a box was in a field, and it was not in the field,
and nobody ever looked, because he wrote down the day it was there and he wrote down the
stanchion and the stanchion was in the right place on the wrong day, and that is the trick,
and it is not a trick, it is a *schedule*."

Reiner was quiet for a moment.

"Say that again."

"A schedule. He has given Carels a true thing with a date attached, and the true thing is
only true on the date, and Carels is a baker and not a man who camps out on a field in
March to find out." The woman spread her hands. "He answered the question he was asked.
Somebody asked *how did you do it* and he answered *here is where it was*, and it was a
complete answer, and it is not the answer, and the law has no box for that."

"The law has no box for that," Reiner said. "But the field is still there."

"The field is still there."

Reiner stood up and put the four sheets back in their order, which was the order they had
been in, and she said thank you, and at the door she turned round.

"May I ask you one more thing and you may say no."

"You have said three things you were going to do and then not done, and I have watched all
three, so yes."

"When he asked for the glass of water," Reiner said, "did you give him your own, or the
shop's?"

The woman thought about it for a long moment, and Reiner watched her actually think,
which was worth the walk.

"The shop's," she said. "I have wondered about that for thirty-one years and I would
never have asked you. Why."

"Because you were not in the room," said Reiner, "and you are the only person I have spoken
to in four days who was not in the room, and you have just told me the only thing anybody in
the room could have told me and you told it to me about a glass of water and you have been
carrying it since 1929 without knowing you were carrying it.

Because a man who is watched while he holds a glass learns how to hold one, and a man who asks
for water in a shop is buying a minute with the one person in the building who was not in the
room, and he spent the minute on a machine and a glass and a sentence he has been careful with
ever since. That is the whole of him. He does not hide the method. He hides in the one place a
method cannot be, which is the truth, told early, to nobody."

She went down the stairs and out into the field, which was a flat brown expanse of nothing
with a row of houses along one edge, and she stood in the middle of it, and she looked for
the third stanchion from the left, and there was nothing there, and there had been nothing
there since 1929, and somewhere underneath it, at a depth of two feet, in a city that had
been rebuilt twice since, was a coffer that had never been opened, and had cost a baker
eleven hundred guilders, and had never once been wrong about where it was.

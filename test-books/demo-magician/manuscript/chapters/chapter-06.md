# 6 · The Tent

The tent went up at Assen on a Tuesday in a wind that came off the heath and took two
guys' hats off and put them back on upside down, and Sigrid Voll, who was thirty-one and
had been doing this for twenty-two years, watched the whole business from the back of a
handcart and laughed until she had to hold the rail.

"That is Johan," she said. "That is Johan in the wind. Johan has been doing that since I
was nine and he has never once got it right and he has never once been asked to stop."

It was the largest of the northern tents — two hundred and forty seats, a wooden
corridor, a cook's tent at the back, and a generator that had opinions. There were eleven
of them inside. It was, Reiner came to think, the happiest room she had ever been in that
had no floor.

The cook's tent had two rings and a fire pit and a man in it who had been a chef in a hotel in Groningen and who treated the whole arrangement as a temporary indignity that he had decided to survive, and the eleven ate shoulder to shoulder at a trestle that Halvard had built in 1924 and had never once had to repair, which he mentioned about twice a season.

In the evenings the stove came into the big tent and the card school started on the left of it and the mending started on the right of it and nobody had decided any of this and it had been the same for eleven years. Johan told the story of the hats at Assen every second night and it improved every time. The version Reiner heard in her first week had two guys and a wind in it, and the version she heard in her fourth week had four guys in it and a goat and a small boy, and the goat had become essential to the story, and Sigrid laughed at the goat every time with the full force of a woman who had known from the first telling that there was never a goat.

What the tent did with Reiner was the thing she had not been ready for. It made room. Nobody asked her a question in four weeks, and she had been in rooms where the questions were the whole of the entertainment, and here was a rule made by a book man in November being kept by eleven people who had never voted on it, and she began slowly to be a person who could sit in a room without watching it, which she had not been for six years.

She wrote one line about that in the notebook and did not write the rest, because the rest would have had to say that she had come north to find out how a man worked and had found out instead how a room worked, and she was not ready to know what she meant to do with it.

Now, the thing about this company — and she had it in her notebook by the second night and
wrote it out properly on the fourth — is that nobody in it was lying about the size of what
they were.

Halvard Grens was the book man. He did the drawings, he kept the engagements, he had a
hand like a clerk and a temper like weather, and he was the only person in the tent who
would not perform, and he was asked to perform, and he had a routine for it that was four
words long and made everybody laugh and made nobody move.

"No," he would say.

"Halvard."

"No."

"Twenty minutes."

"No." And then, when the person had gone: "I do the drawings. If I performed, who did the
drawings."

That was the whole of his position and he had held it for eleven years, and Reiner, who had
been in rooms where men explained at length why they did not do the thing they were
famous for, listened to it four times in three days and it never once rang false.

"People think it is modesty," Sigrid said, on the second night, while Halvard was folding a
guarantee. "It is not modesty. Modesty would be a performance. It is that he does not
believe a tent of this size can hold a name."

"The azoic doctrine," Reiner said.

"The what?"

"Something I was taught. I want to check whether I have it right, because if I have it
right it is a school and if I have it wrong I have made it up, and I would rather be told."

Sigrid put down her cup.

"Go on, then."

"A large name claims a grandeur the tent cannot support. So a tent does not use one. It
uses what the tent is. It is the Northern Circuit. It is the Scandinave. It is a
*compagnie*. Everything in it is a size, and a size is not a boast. If a man puts a name on a tent of
this size, then the name is the promise, and the tent has to be able to keep the promise,
and it cannot, and everybody finds out on the third night in a town where they have never
been."

"And the name is a claim of capability that can be challenged."

Reiner put her cup down.

"Yes," she said. "Exactly that. Yes. And it can only be challenged by somebody who knows
the method, and the method is in the *réponse*, and the *réponse* is sealed, and the seal
is what the whole thing runs on." She was aware that she had not moved for some time. "How
long has this been doctrine in the north?"

"Since I was a girl. Halvard's father had it and Halvard's father's father, and nobody
anywhere can tell me where it started, and I have never asked, because you do not ask a
doctrine where it started, you ask whether it works, and it works, and it is the only
reason this tent is not called the Grens or the Voll or some dead man's wife."

"Halvard could."

"Halvard could in one afternoon and would be dead in two years." Sigrid said it without
heat. "That is not a figure of speech. I have watched it happen. Johan did it in
Hasselt, with a *Sensation*, and had a very good month, and then a bad one, and then a
worse one, and he is a fine drawing man and he drives a van now and he is happy, and I was
sorry for him for about six years and I have not been sorry for eleven."

Reiner had a page in the notebook that said *the crew all say the same thing when asked*,
and she had written it in a room in Utrecht without knowing what it meant, and she looked
at it now and could not think why it was strange, and she left it, because a thing that does
not yet have a shape is not a thing to be frightened of.

The wind came round the canvas all night, and the tent was, on a night like that, the
warmest cold place she had ever slept.

---

On the third night Halvard drew her.

He did it without asking, which was a thing about him: he had no instinct toward
permission, because a drawing is a record and a record is not a liberty.

"You will not like it," he said.

"I have been drawn by four people in my life and I liked one of them."

"That is a very conjurer's answer."

"It is a true answer. Draw it."

He drew for a quarter of an hour and gave it to her upside down, deliberately, to see, and
she turned it round.

It was very good. It was the good one. He had given her the face she wore when she was
listening to a room rather than looking at it, and the face was not clever and it was not
kind and it was not suspicious, and it was the face of a woman being told something she had
already decided was true, and there was a small crease between the eyebrows that came from
holding the brows still while the rest of the face did what it liked.

"You have done that before," Reiner said.

"I have done that before."

"Whose?"

"Somebody in 1927." He took the pencil and began to sharpen it, which was the end of the
conversation and everybody present knew it was the end of the conversation. Then he said,
without looking up: "You are the one from Rotterdam."

"I am."

"Van Eede wrote to Rask. Rask wrote to Sigrid. Sigrid wrote to me, on a Tuesday, in two
lines, and the second line was *do not ask her anything she has not offered*." He went on
sharpening. "So I am not asking. I want that understood. I have not asked you a single
question and I am not going to, and if anybody in this tent does, they will be told to stop
by me and not by you."

"Thank you."

"It is not kindness. It is that you have got a face like a receipt, and every man in this
tent can read one without wanting to, and if anybody here starts pulling at it you will
answer them, because you have been raised to answer people,
and then in four months you will have told a man in a tent what you knew, and he will be
polite about it, and that will be the end of you." He put the pencil down. "So no
questions. You may sit in my tent for a month and I will not ask you one, and if you want
to talk to me, talk, and if you do not, do not, and I will be perfectly content, because I
have drawings to do and the wind is not going to help me with them."

Reiner said, "What is he like?"

Halvard looked at her for a while.

"That is a question," he said.

"That is an offer."

"It is an offer of a question and I have just told you the rule, and you have been in this
tent two nights and you are already better at this than the four of us, and I would like
you to be a little worse at it for a fortnight." He went back to the drawings. "I will tell
you one thing, and it is not an answer and it is not a fact and I would like it written
down as neither."

"Go on."

"I have been in this trade for nineteen years and I have worked with eleven men I would
take a booking with, and there is one I would not, and I have never met him, and I know
which eleven I mean and Johan does not agree with the eleven and that is the only argument
Johan and I have had in nine years." He was not looking at her. "It is not that he is
clever. I have worked with clever. Clever is a Tuesday. It is that he is *pleasant*, and
that he has never once been in a room where being pleasant was not sufficient, and I have
known him for two hours through Rask's letter and through a great deal of argument, and I
would be willing to bet a month that every man in this tent would go on being pleasant in
a room that was reading him."

The tent was very loud with wind.

"He is not in this tent," Reiner said.

"No," said Halvard, and sharpened the pencil, and did not look up again. "No, he is not."

---

She did not ask anybody anything for a week, and it was the hardest week she had had in
five years, and at the end of it she understood something that she had not understood when
she had hired the wagon in Rotterdam with eleven pounds in her pocket, which was that the
difficulty of this work was not the work.

On the sixth night Sigrid came into her tent with two cups and sat down on the end of the
bed uninvited, which Sigrid did, and which Reiner had decided not to fight because she had
been in rooms where it was fought and lost.

"You are doing it again," Sigrid said.

"Doing what."

"The face. Ledger face." Sigrid drank. "I have had it from a distance for a week and I have
decided to come and say so, because I am thirty-one and I have not got time to be polite
about things for nine months running."

"I said I would not ask anybody anything."

"You have not. You have been the most careful person in this tent and Johan has been
telling everyone you are a spy and Johan is an idiot and I have told him so in front of
witnesses." She put the cup down. "But you have not asked anybody anything, and you keep
looking at the conversations, and if you go on doing that for another week you are going to
leave here with nothing, and you will have been right to."

"Is that the argument."

"That is the argument. The other argument is that you have not asked *me* anything and I am
the one who writes for Rask when he cannot be bothered, and I wrote him two lines in
August, and I have been chewing on them ever since and I have decided that I would rather
say it out loud than be chewed at by somebody I have never met."

Reiner waited.

"Rask is afraid of the trial." Sigrid said it flatly. "Not of the man. Of a room. He has
been in this trade fifty years and he has never once been in a room where a man read him
aloud while twenty people wrote it down, and I have seen him do a hundred things he could
have refused, and I have watched him refuse one thing, and it was a working room in
Delft with four people in it." She turned the cup round. "That is what is at the back of
your head, and it is a better answer than the one you have been building with hands."

"How do you know what I have been building with hands."

"Because I have been doing it for twenty-two years," said Sigrid Voll, "and the hands
always go first. The hands go at things and the face watches the hands and the hands
belong to the person who is asking." She got up. "So do not ask with your hands in this
tent. Ask with your face, which they will all believe, because they have all been trained
by a very good book man to believe a face."

# 29 · Aldrich, Again, and Earlier

She went to Delft on purpose in the middle of September, four days before the sitting, and
she went alone, and she went because she had worked out in August that she could not ask him
anything at all and wanted to have asked.

George Aldrich kept a goods gate on the Binnenweg and had kept it since 1911 and was fifty-
one years old and was the only man in the world whose answer was already written down and
dated.

He was a solid man with a grey moustache who took his lunch at the same time every day and
who had not heard of her and did not particularly want to.

"You're the conjurer."

"I am."

"You're the one who's been in all this." He was not unkind about it. "The constable wrote to me. Twice. I thought it was a bill."

"It isn't a bill. I have come to say thank you and to tell you something and then to leave."

"You can say the thing first."

She said the thing first.

"In a room on the fourteenth of October, in Utrecht, I am going to say out loud where you
were looking on the eleventh of April last year, before anybody has asked you, and it is
going to be a checkable thing and I am not going to write it down first, and I wanted you to
hear it from me in advance rather than read it in a transcript."

Aldrich put down his sandwich.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"I know you don't. You have never been asked a question about a door in your life, and I
would like to explain why I am about to."

---

So she explained it, on a gate on the Binnenweg in the middle of the afternoon, to a man
who kept a lock and had never heard of conjuring, and it took the better part of an hour,
and she had never in her life explained anything to anybody as slowly.

She told him about the storeroom. She told him about the two days that a constable could not
get past. She told him that the accounts were all true, and that they were all true in a
particular way, and that the way was that everybody in that room had been looking at a man
standing in the middle of it and not one of them had been watching the door.

And Aldrich listened the entire time with the sandwich in his hand and no expression at all,
and at the end of it he said:

"Was I looking at the door?"

"You were watching the door."

"There's a difference?"

"There's a difference," said Reiner, "and you are the only person in the world who can put
your finger on it, and I am asking you to think about it now, while I am here, and to say
what you remember, and to say it exactly."

Aldrich thought about it.

"I remember the door," he said.

"Do you remember which door?"

"I remember looking at the door."

"That is not the same question."

"It's the only door there's been in that room in forty years," said Aldrich, "and I would
know the one, because I came in by it."

Reiner sat down on the low wall by the gate.

"Mr Aldrich. In a storeroom on the Kralingen field, nine feet by eleven, there is a goods
door on the Binnenweg side and there is a back stair. They are in different walls. In April
last year you were in that room for between four and ten minutes. You have not thought about
it since the May of last year, when a constable wrote down what you said, and you have not
thought about it once, and that is not a criticism, that is exactly what I need."

"I never think about it. There's nothing in it."

"That," said Reiner, "is precisely the thing."

---

She had the copy in her bag. She had had it since April and she had read it perhaps
fifty times and she had never once read it aloud to the man it belonged to.

"Do you mind if I read you what you said?"

"I don't remember saying anything."

"You did. It was taken down by a constable called Van Eede on the twenty-second of May
last year, and it is in a file, and I have a copy because he gave me one in a shed in the
April of this year and he is the only police officer in the Netherlands who has ever handed
a document to a conjurer and he did it in about four seconds without looking at her, and I
have thought about that a great deal."

She read it.

> *I was on the floor at the west end from about four until about ten past. I did not leave
> it. I was looking at the door the whole time. I never took my eyes off that door, not once,
> not for a second, and I would know it, because I have been on that door eleven years and
> there is nothing on that door worth watching.*

"It is not very well punctuated," said Aldrich.

"No. He wrote it fast."

"It's not wrong though." He listened to it again in his head, which Reiner could see him
do, and which took about four seconds. "I did say that. I said it to a man in a shed and I
have not thought about it since, and I would say it again."

"You would say it again."

"I would. There's nothing in that room." He looked down the Binnenweg at the gate. "That's
the honest way round about it. If I hadn't been looking at the door I wouldn't remember
looking at the door, because there was nothing to look at, and a man says the true thing
about a flat afternoon. That's all that was. It was a flat afternoon."

"Yes," said Reiner. "It was."

---

And then she asked him the question, and it is the only question she ever asked him, and
she had known for four months that she was going to ask it and had thought about it every
day since March and had decided in April not to write it down and had then written it down
anyway in a notebook she had not used for anything else.

"Mr Aldrich. When you were watching the door, was the man standing in front of it?"

Aldrich stopped.

That was the moment. Reiner had a whole case built on it and she had watched it arrive on
the face of a man who kept a lock at Delft, and it was not a large moment at all, only a
man deciding whether to think about an afternoon from last April.

"He was where?" said Aldrich.

"I want to know where he was. From where you were sitting."

"I don't know."

"You might."

"I was looking at the door," said Aldrich.

"You were."

"For four hours I was looking at that door and I can tell you the crack in the middle of it
and I can tell you the paint on the latch and I could not tell you to this moment whether
that man was in front of me, behind me, or in the corner, and I have not thought about it
since the May of last year, and I am telling you that because you have come out here on a
Wednesday and told me you are going to say something in a room and I would like you to know
that the thing you've come out here to say is going to be true."

Reiner stood in the middle of a goods gate on the Binnenweg in Delft and found that her
eyes had gone and that she had no idea for how long, and that the man in front of her had
noticed it and had decided — a yard man on a Binnenweg gate, a man who had kept a lock
since 1911, a man with a sandwich — had decided to be kind about it, and that he had done
so by talking about a crack in a door, which was the single most useful thing anybody has
ever done for her.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't."

"No, I mean it. You've just told me the case. I came out here to ask you a favour and I've
got the case instead, and I have had eleven months of everything except the case, and you
have just handed it to me in about four seconds without knowing what it was, and I have to
go and stand in a room and say it and I could not have said a word of it on Friday."

"Miss Reiner." He had gone back to his sandwich. "I have been on that door since 1911 and I
have watched four hundred men go past it and not one of them has ever stopped to ask me a
question about a room. You are the first and you came out on a Wednesday afternoon and you
did it properly, and I am going to remember that in about six weeks when there is a great
deal of shouting, and if a man in a wig asks me whether I am certain I am going to say yes,
and I am going to say yes, and it will not be because I have anything to say. It will be
because a woman stood on this gate for an hour and told me what my afternoon was for."

---

On the train back she wrote two lines and nothing else, and they are the last two lines in
the notebook that has been open since April of the year before.

*Row 1. He has not thought about it since May. He has never thought about it. The room was
a flat afternoon and he has been carrying it for sixteen months as a flat afternoon and
nobody has ever put a question to it.*

*Row 2. Nobody has ever put a question to it. That is the whole of the work. Nine years of
notices and certificates and disputes and a man in a good suit answering nine questions
with no, and none of it was ever about the room, because the room was a flat afternoon in
the mind of the only person who was in it, and there is no procedure in the world for asking
a man what he saw on a flat afternoon, and there never has been, and on the fourteenth of
October I am going to do it in a room in four minutes and it is going to be the first time
anybody has ever done it to anybody.*

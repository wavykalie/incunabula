# 31 · A Trick Needs a Retinue

The proposition was put at four minutes past ten on the Thursday of the fourteenth of
October by a man in a wig who had had two days to think about it and who was, Reiner
decided on the Friday morning, probably the only genuinely dangerous person she had ever
been in a room with.

"I have read the transcript," said the assesseur. "I have read it four times, which I do
not do. Madame L'Ombrière's paper is inadmissible and I said so on the Tuesday and I meant
it. The four accounts are unremarkable and three of them are of no value at all." He put a
page down. "And you have told me that on Thursday you are going to ask a man in Delft
where he was looking on a subject on which he has never been asked, and that you have
stated in advance what his answer will be, and that if you are right the answer will be
checkable against a document sealed in May of last year.

"Very well. That is a test. It is a good test, and I have never heard of one, and I am
going to permit it, and I am going to permit it with one condition, which I put to you now
and not afterwards,because I am sixty-one years old and I have been in this room for nineteen years and I have watched a great many people put things to witnesses in this hall
and I have never once seen one put a condition on it in advance."

He looked at her.

"If your man in Delft gives you the door you have predicted, the court will record that the
question was unfair, that you constructed it, and that a witness was asked a question with
one permitted answer. I will say so on the record and I will not let you say a word in
answer, and the whole of what you have built will be in a file in ten years with those
words on it."

"That is fair."

"Madam, it is a considerable concession and I would like you to hear what I am giving up
as well." He took his glasses off. "If he gives you that door, then you have taken a man
who keeps a lock in Delft, who has done nothing, who has never been charged, who came to
this building under subpoena and did not wish to come, and you have made him say a thing in
public that he had no reason to say, and he will have said it in a hall in Utrecht in front
of a hundred and forty people, and he will go back to Delft and lock up at six. And he will
do it because you were right, and being right is the worst thing that can happen to him.

"I am going to allow it anyway. But I am telling you the cost of it in advance, in a room,
where it can be written down, and I would like you to write it down."

Reiner wrote it down. It is the last thing in her notebook.

---

Aldrich said the back stair.

He was asked at eleven forty, in a hall in Utrecht, in the same words she had said in
April in a room over a tobacconist, and he got the sentence out in about nine seconds, and
he said *the back stair* in exactly the tone of a man answering a question about the
weather, and then he said:

"I want to say something and I don't know if I'm allowed."

"You may say anything you like," said the clerk, who was not the clerk, who was a second
man, and who was not authorised to say it and said it anyway.

"I'm not blaming anybody," said Aldrich. "I want to say that. I don't know who I'm talking
to and I've been in this room four hours and I don't know what's being asked. I keep a gate.
I was on the floor at the west end and I was looking at a door, and it was a flat afternoon,
and I've told a constable the same thing in a shed and he wrote it down and that's all I
know about it."

"That is your answer," said the assesseur.

"It's my answer to *that*," said Aldrich. "There's a thing in there about a man being stood
in a room. And if he was stood in a room and I was looking at the other door then—" He
stopped, and Reiner, four rows back with a shut notebook, watched a man who had never
thought about an afternoon for eighteen months think about one for the first time in public,
in a hall, in front of a hundred and forty people.

"Well," said Aldrich. "I'd have seen him."

The hall was extremely quiet.

"Sir," said the assesseur, "you are not to speculate."

"I'm not speculating," said Aldrich. "I'm saying if I'd been stood in a room I'd have seen
him, and I didn't, and so either he wasn't stood in a room or I was looking somewhere
that wasn't in it, and I don't know which and I don't want to know which, and I'd like to go
home, and I've been in this hall since ten and my wife doesn't know where I am."

He was finished. He had been in the room for ninety seconds and he had handed a court
something that eleven months of the most careful work Reiner had ever done could not have
produced, and he had done it without any idea that he was doing it, and he wanted to go
home.

The assesseur wrote for a long time.

"Madame Reiner."

"Sir."

"Your prediction was correct. I am obliged to say so on the record and I am going to say so
on the record, and I am going to add to it, and I am aware that what I am about to add is
not my office." He put the pen down. "I have been an assessor in this room for nineteen
years. In that time I have heard a witness say *I don't remember* perhaps two hundred
times, and I have heard a witness say *I don't want to know* eleven times, and I have never
once heard a man in my court work out something about a case that the case had not worked
out about him, in ninety seconds, in front of forty people, and then ask to go home."

Reiner said nothing at all.

---

And then the assesseur did the thing that she thinks about on the bad nights, which he did
without any drama whatever and which took about four seconds.

"Respondent. The matter is not concluded. I have one question and it is a procedural one
and I am obliged to put it, and I put it in the form the law requires."

He looked at the man in the good suit at the table at the front.

"You have a *réponse* prepared."

"I have."

"You have had it prepared since June. It is in a sealed envelope in the custody of this
court and it has been since the second of June, and it names a method, and under the
practice of this court and of every tribunal of which I have knowledge, a *réponse* that
names a method is a complete answer, and there is no procedure by which a court may go
behind one except on the application of a party with standing, and there is no party in
this hall with standing."

He paused.

"However. A *réponse* is an answer to a *requête*. And the *requête* in this matter is
made by a party who is not before the court, and the matter has been argued for two days,
and I am going to record that the *réponse* is held, and that it has not been read, and
that it has not been opened, and that the court is satisfied as to its form."

He stopped.

"What I am going to say now is not in the practice. I am going to say that a man who has
answered nine questions in the negative in ninety minutes, and who has filed a *réponse* in
June, and who has sat in this hall for two days and said nineteen words, has never in this
court answered a single question about the *room.*"

The clerk's pen stopped.

"And I am going to say that there are a hundred and thirty people in this hall and that
ninety-one of them are performers, and that I am sixty-one years old and I have never in my
life seen a hall of performers go as quiet as this one has gone in the last minute."

He turned to the man at the table.

"Respondent, I am going to ask you a question, and it is the only question in two days that
I am asking you because I want to hear the answer and not because the court requires it, and
I am going to tell the court that it is improper and I am going to ask it anyway, and if you
refuse to answer it then the court will have heard something, and if you do answer it then
I will have asked a question in a court that I had no business asking, and both of those
will be in the file in ten years, and I am content with that."

He said: "**Do the effect.**"

---

The man in the good suit did not move for a long time.

Reiner has said, in four hundred interviews and in two books, that the silence lasted about
nine seconds, and she has been corrected twice by people who were in the room, and both
corrections gave a longer time, and the longest was thirty-one, from a man who had been in
the back and had wanted to be helpful.

What he did was: he put both hands flat on the table, and he looked at the assesseur, and
he looked — and this is the part that Reiner has never been able to describe to anybody
without it sounding like a figure of speech, and it is not a figure of speech, and it is the
most technically precise observation of her career — he looked *round the room*.

At ninety-one people. For about four seconds. In a hall with a stamped tin ceiling and a
nine-inch stage and a hundred and forty people in it, in the afternoon of a Thursday in
October, and he looked round all of them, and he took his time, and he was not looking for
anybody in particular.

"He was counting," said Reiner. "He was counting the room. I know what it looks like because
I have spent twenty-five years learning to read a face and I have never once seen that and
I have known him for eighteen months and I did not know it was possible."

"Counting what?"

"Us," said Reiner. "He was counting us."

---

And then he stood up and did the only thing he could do with a hundred and forty people in
a room, which was the correct thing, and the thing a professional does, and the thing that
eight of the ninety-one in that hall said afterwards was the finest piece of work they had
ever seen.

He took a handkerchief out of his pocket.

He said, in English, with a very slight accent that was not in the French: "May I."

He asked permission. In a court. From a man in a wig.

And then he did a vanish, in the middle of a floor, at half past eleven in the morning, with
nothing under it and nothing over it and no boxes and no table and no cabinet and no lights
and no *Negative Room* and none of the apparatus of a working conjurer, in a hall with a
stamped tin ceiling, in front of a hundred and forty people including ninety-one people who
had spent their entire lives learning exactly what a man can do with a handkerchief.

It was very good. It was better than that. It was the best piece of sleight of hand Reiner
had ever seen performed in front of her, and she has been in nine hundred rooms, and she has
watched two men who are better than him, and neither of those two men has ever done that.

It took four seconds. And a hundred and forty people saw it. And at the end of it there was
a handkerchief in his hand and no memory whatever of what had been done with it, in any of
them, including him.

And then he stood there in the middle of a floor with a handkerchief in his hand and the room
went on being a room, and nothing happened, and everybody waited for about a second and a
half, and then a man in the third row began to clap, and it was the only applause in two
days and it was for a vanish, and the assesseur wrote *the respondent performed a
sleight-of-hand* in the transcript and put a full stop after it, and looked up.

"That is a hand trick," he said. "That is a hand trick in a court and I have recorded it,
and I would like to know what any of the ninety-one people in this hall thinks it was for."

Nobody answered. Reiner has never established whether that was because nobody knew or
because everybody did.

---

The man put the handkerchief in his pocket.

"I am sorry," he said, in French, to the assesseur. "That was a bad thing to do. I have
answered nine questions in the negative and I have filed a *réponse*, and I have just
performed a handkerchief in a court, and I would like it recorded that I did it because
somebody asked me to, in front of a hundred and forty people, and I have never in my life
been able to refuse a room that has asked me a question directly."

"No one asked you to perform."

"No," he said. "Somebody asked me a question and the answer was *yes*, and I could not say
*yes* in words, because the words would have been false, so I did it, and now there is a
handkerchief in a file in ten years, and that is what I have, and I am sorry for it, and I
would like the matter to go on."

"It will go on," said the assesseur, "for one more day, and then it will not, and I have
been in this room nineteen years and I have never once had a day like this one, and I am
going to tell you the honest thing, which is that a man in this court has just been
required to be good at something in public, and he was good at it, and it was the emptiest
thing I have ever watched a man do."

---

Afterwards, in a corridor, a man of about sixty with a very good coat put a hand on Reiner's
arm, which he had done twice before, and which she had permitted twice before, and which
she permitted a third time.

"He could have refused," said Van Eede.

"Yes."

"He could have stood up there and said *I do not perform in rooms where I am read* and he
would have been within his rights and it would have been the end of it."

"Yes," said Reiner. "And he would have won. That is what I have got to tell you, and I
have been not telling you for two days because I have been in a building since Tuesday
morning and I have not slept in a bed."

She stopped in the corridor.

"He did not refuse," she said, "because the room asked him a question directly, for the
first time in his life, in front of a hundred and forty people, and there is no *réponse*
to a question. That is the whole of the trade. Nine years, seven disputes, three countries,
and every single piece of paper in the dossier is an answer to a *challenge*, and a
challenge is a document, and a document can be filed, and he has never once been asked a
question out loud, in a room, by a person, where the only possible answers are yes and no.

"And on Thursday a man in a wig asked him one, and he said yes, and he said it with a
handkerchief in front of ninety-one conjurers, and it was the emptiest, most complete,
utterly useless piece of work of his entire life, and he did it because there was no paper
for it.

And the vanish was the whole of what a hall could be given. A trick is done to a retinue. Four
or five people who have seen it before and know what it costs and have come to watch it done
well, which is the only gift a performer has to give anybody. A hall where a hundred and forty
people have sat for two days listening to a case about three dead men cannot be made into that,
and what he gave them instead was the one trick that works on a room of witnesses, which is a
hand trick, an impossibility small enough to be seen once and held and needing nobody in the
room to have seen it before. It is the trick you hand to people who have come to catch you. And
it was empty because it was made of skill and nothing else, and skill is the part of the trade
that all ninety-one of them could take home and practise, and he had stood up and spent it in
public like small change."

"And the trick."

"There was no trick," said Reiner. "There has never been a trick in this case. There are
three dead men and a man who is good at what he does, and a woman who spent twelve months
going round Europe being told, by every single person she asked, that the whole thing was
about a room, and in the end the whole thing was about a room, and nobody has ever once been
able to prove what happened in one, and nobody is ever going to be able to, and that is not
a failure. That is what the room is *for*."

Van Eede looked at her for a moment.

"You are crying."

"I am aware of it," said Reiner, "and I have been crying since half past eleven this
morning, and I have been doing it in a corridor, and you are the only person who has
mentioned it, and I would be obliged if you would not mention it again, and I would also be
obliged if you would walk with me to the end of the corridor and not say anything, which is
what I have just said."

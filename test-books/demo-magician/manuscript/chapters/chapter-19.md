# 19 · A Trick Needs a Retinue

It came to her in a corridor in Utrecht, over a cup of something, because everything in
this book comes to her in corridors and none of it comes to her where it should.

She was explaining the retinue to a man from Rotterdam who had asked her, and she was
explaining it well, and about four sentences in she heard herself.

"— the people who already knew, so that nothing had to be explained in the room. A list,
not a crowd. If a man has four people in a room who have seen it before, he does not have
to do the work of establishing what he is doing, and every person in the room takes that
work off him without knowing they have taken it. He has not got an audience. He has got an
audience that has already been told."

The man from Rotterdam said, "Yes. That is the whole art of it. Every fool knows that."

"Yes," said Reiner.

And then she put her cup down on the sill and did not pick it up again for about a minute,
because something had happened in the middle of a sentence in a corridor and she could not
stop it happening.

"Miss Reiner?"

"You said every fool knows that."

"I did."

"Who taught you that?"

He looked at her. He was about thirty-five and he had a good coat and he had come ninety
miles to Utrecht in February and he had not liked the tin ceiling.

"Everybody knows that."

"Everybody does not know it," said Reiner. "Nobody knows it. I have been in this trade
since I was nine and I have been taught in a school that has a doctrine and a vocabulary
and a list of effects and a man named after a bird, and *nobody in it knows that*. I have
asked. I have been in rooms in three countries where that sentence does not exist. And you
have said it to me in a corridor in Utrecht in the tone of a man quoting a rule of
football."

"Reiner—"

"What is your name."

"Van Aken."

"Mr Van Aken. Sit down, please. I am going to ask you four questions and they are the first
four questions anybody has asked me in a year and you are going to think they are strange,
and I am going to be very quick and I am going to be extremely rude, and I apologise in
advance, and I am going to do it because in about four months I am going to be in a room
where this is the only thing that matters and I have got one night."

Van Aken sat down. He was, Reiner thought, exactly the sort of man she would have liked to
have in a room: not frightened, not impressed, a little annoyed, entirely reasonable. She
had known four or five of them in nine years and it had not saved her from anything.

"Where did you learn it?"

"Nobody learns it. It is common sense. You get four people in a room who have seen your
work, you have taken the work out of the room."

"Yes. Now: who was in the room in Rotterdam in the spring?"

"I have no idea."

"Who was in the room in Utrecht in February?"

"Eleven people."

"Were eleven people the same eleven on Saturday night and on Sunday morning?"

Van Aken stopped. She watched him do it — watch the face go through about four things in
under a second, which is the one skill her trade actually consists of, and which nobody
who has not got it can be taught at any price.

"Three of them were not," he said.

"Which three."

"The Belgian. The Dutch boy. And the woman from Brussels, who I did not know was from
Brussels until Sunday."

"You did not know one of them until Sunday, and you said nothing on Saturday when we were
naming effects in a room with a tin roof and two hundred and eleven people in it, and you
had an unbriefed stranger on your own stage."

"Somebody's stranger is not my business."

"Somebody's stranger was the reason I was in the room," said Reiner, "and I have not told
you why I was in the room, and I am not going to, and I would like you to notice that you
did not ask, which is twice now, and I want to be very clear with you about the difference
between a man who is discreet and a man who does not ask because he already knows."

---

Here is what she had by the middle of the following week, written on four pages in a room
over a tobacconist, and she wrote it in the form she always used because the form was the
only thing that had never once let her believe a comfortable conclusion.

*Row 1. He works with a retinue. Four or five people who have seen it before, in every
town, chosen by him, never billed to him, never on a contract, never named in an
engagement list. They are not employees. There is no paper.*

*Row 2. The corps[e] is assembled in February in Utrecht, in a tin-ceilinged hall with no
engagement, and seven people come from three countries, and the whole assembly takes nine
days and is decided in a railway carriage, and it has happened twice in ten years and
nobody has ever asked why February.*

*Row 3. And he is in it. He is in it, because I have established that he is not in any
company, and I have established that he does not tour, and if he is in a room in Utrecht in
February, then either somebody brought him or he came.*

*Row 4. Nobody brought him. He came. He has a chair by the wall and a cup he does not drink
from and he does six turns a night for four nights, and I have been in that room every
night and I have watched him not being looked at by anybody, and it is the most remarkable
thing I have ever seen in a room.*

*Row 5. THAT IS THE TRICK. Not the eleven seconds. The eleven seconds is eleven seconds.
The trick is that forty people assemble themselves in a tin hall in Utrecht in February
every time this happens, and they do it for money and for a booking and for a floor, and
none of them is there for him, and every one of them is there.*

She read Row 4 four times and then a fifth time, and the fifth time she had to put the
notebook down and go and stand at the window.

*He has never once needed a room. I have been writing that sentence since January and I
have had it exactly the wrong way round. He has never once needed a room because he does
not need twenty-two people to be wrong in it. He needs a hall.*

*He needs a hall, and a company, and four nights of a booking, and a corps[e] that will
come because it is February and the fair is over and there is nothing else.*

*And then, in the middle of four nights of it, he walks into a storeroom in Rotterdam that
somebody else has hired for nine guilders off a firm that does not exist, and there are
twenty-two people in it because it is a Saturday, and he does a thing, and every one of
them is right.*

*And in September he takes a coach to Marseille for one night to an address that does not
exist, and there is one woman in that house and she is not watching him and has no idea he
is in the room, and that is a different trick entirely and it is much worse.*

The last line is the one that frightened her and she wrote it last and underlined it twice.

*He has two methods and they are opposites and he uses whichever one the room in front of
him will support. With a room he uses attention. With a single witness he uses a schedule
and a piece of paper and thirty guilders, and the single witness is never watching, and he
knows exactly that she is not watching, and he has arranged for eleven hours of her not
watching, and that is the thing I have been calling a visit.*

*And both of them need the same thing, and the same thing is that nobody in the room is
there for him. In a storeroom on a Saturday, twenty-two people are there for the evening.
In a house on the Rue de la République, a woman of sixty-one is there for the November. Not
one person in either room has come to see a conjurer, and not one person in either room
knows that there is anything to see, and that — all of that, every time, in every town,
for nine years — is what he is actually doing.*

She put the notebook down and thought about the fishmonger, who had said *here* and meant
*this city*, and about the four people in Marseille who had said they remembered a face and
could not remember a single sentence.

And then she thought about herself, in a shed in Rotterdam, in April, saying to a
constable: *he is not in the room, he is not in the room, and I have watched you looking.*

She had been in the room. She had been in it for four minutes. She had gone closer, and she
had crouched, and she had looked at his hands, and the whole of the time she had been doing
the one thing that no one in that room had done, which was to be *in* it on purpose, and
she had been so certain that she was the only one paying attention that she had not once
asked herself the obvious question.

Which is: if she had been the only one, who had the room?

---

She went and found Sigrid Voll in the yard at half past two in the morning, and Sigrid was
awake, because Sigrid was always awake, and had been all winter, and nobody had asked her
about that.

"I have worked out what it is," said Reiner.

"That is a lot of words for four in the morning."

"It is eleven words. *A trick needs a retinue.* And I have had it in my notebook since
February and I have been trying to use it as a thing I know and it is a thing I am in."

Sigrid Voll stood in the yard in a shawl and looked at her for a while.

"Yes," she said.

"Yes?"

"Miss Reiner, I have been in this trade for twenty-two years and I have four people I
would trust with anything, and I would not put them in a room with a man I did not know,
and I would not put them in a room with a man I did know, and I have never once asked
myself why." She pulled the shawl tighter. "It is not a question anybody asks. It is
assumed. It is the way rooms work. You go into a room and you go in warm, because somebody
has been there before you, and that is the entire professional courtesy of the thing, and
it is not nothing, and nobody writes it down."

"Nobody writes it down," said Reiner.

"Nobody writes it down, and it is the only thing in this trade that cannot be learned, and
a man who has understood it can walk into any hall in Europe in February and be handed a
room he has never seen by forty people who do not know that they have handed it to him."

The yard was very quiet.

"Sigrid."

"Yes."

"In Utrecht, in February — the assembly. The whole reason it happens in February is that
the fair is over."

"Yes."

"And nobody has ever asked why February."

"Nobody has ever asked why February," said Sigrid Voll, "in my entire life, and I have put
a man in front of two hundred and eleven people forty times, and I have never once asked
why February, and you have asked it in eleven months, and I want you to understand that
this is the single most frightening conversation I have had in twenty-two years and I have
had the conversation about money four times."

# 3 · Eleven Seconds

She went back on the Tuesday with a watch and no permission, and the permission arrived on
Wednesday, and Van Eede, who had spent the intervening night thinking about two days,
let her in without mentioning that he had not slept.

"There is a thing I want to show you," she said, "and I am going to show you the small
version of it, and then I am going to tell you the small version is worthless, and you are
going to ask me why I showed you the worthless one, and the answer to that is the only
reason I am here. Do not stop me for the twenty minutes it takes, and if I put my hand on
your sleeve you will want to move it and you must not move it."

"I am a constable."

"I know what you are. Sit down."

There was a crate. She put a brass key on it.

"Watch. Not me. You will find that you cannot do both and I am not asking you to try."

She put her hand on the sleeve of his coat. He did not move it. That was the first
ordinary thing that had happened to him all week, and it happened because she had told him
in advance and a man will hold still for a thing he has been warned about for a very long
time.

"Nine seconds," she said. "That is a long time. I am not doing anything clever. I am
going to take this key off this crate and put it in my pocket, and you are going to see me
do it, and afterwards you will be able to tell me a very good account of the moment it
happened, and your account will be *nearly* right."

"Do it."

She took the key. She put it in her pocket.

Van Eede's face was doing the thing faces do.

"Now," said Reiner. "Tell me what you saw."

"You put your hand in your pocket."

"That is not what you saw, that is what you did with what you saw. Try again, and do not
be polite, I have been polite all week and it has got us nothing."

He took his time.

"You were talking. You said something about the watch. And you were — I want to say you
turned away, but you did not turn away, you were facing me the whole time. And the key was
on the crate and then it was not on the crate, and I do not know when."

"You do not know when," said Reiner, "and you have not been given a single reason not to
know when. That is the whole of the small trick. It is not that I am fast. It is that I
made you look at my face for nine seconds, and a face is a very interesting thing, and a
key on a crate is not."

"And yours was nearly right."

"Mine was nearly right. You said you do not know when. You did not say I was too quick,
you did not say it was impossible, you did not say a word of anybody's account of this
having been done to a man in a storeroom." She turned the watch over in her hand.
"Constable. Go back to Tuesday for a moment with me. Everyone in that building was asked
what happened and every single one of them gave you an account, and not one of those
accounts is a *nearly*. They are all exactly the same, and the man who was not in the room
gave the best one of the lot, and he gave you a wall clock."

She put the watch on the crate where the key had been.

"Now, before the other one. Ask me what I did."

"You did nothing."

"No. Ask me what I *did*." She turned round. "You have just watched a woman take a key off
a table and put it in her pocket and you have told me you do not know when it happened. I
want to tell you what I did, because I want you to understand that it was a great deal,
and that you were not lied to once, and that in a room of two hundred that is the whole
difference between the five things I can do and the thing I cannot."

She put her hand flat on the table.

"I did three things and they took about a second and a half between them. One: I put my
hand on your sleeve at the start and I told you in advance that I was going to, so that you
would hold still, and you held still, and a man who has been told a thing is waiting for it
and a man who has not been told a thing is not, and that is half of it, and it took me a
week to learn, and I could not do it now if you were not expecting it.

"Two: I talked. I said something about the watch, and you do not remember what, and that is
the entire point. You remember that somebody said something. The something filled the gap
where the key was. It was a real sentence about the watch and you have got it filed under
*she was talking*, and it is doing the work of a dozen seconds.

"Three, and this is the one that matters, and it is the one I could not do for eleven years
and did not know I was not doing: **I chose the moment.** A key on a table has to go
somewhere. It cannot go nowhere, because the eye follows it, and it is a bright thing in a
dull room and the only thing anybody in here is watching. So I did not make the key
disappear. I made the key stop being the thing you were looking at, and the moment I did
that was about a second and a half after I put my hand on your arm and four seconds before
I picked it up, and in that second and a half you were looking at a woman talking about a
watch."

She took her hand off the table.

"That is the whole method. It is neither speed nor sleight. It is *arranging what somebody
is looking at*, and the thing I could not do for eleven years was choose the moment,
because choosing the moment means you are not reacting to a room, you are running it, and
every man I have ever met who could do that lost something in the losing."

Van Eede had not moved.

"That was a great deal to say about a key."

"It was a great deal to say about a key," Reiner agreed, "and I have said it to four
hundred conjurers and not one of them believed a word of it until they tried it, and not
one of them could do it afterwards for about a year. It is not a trick. It is a *timing*,
and the trick is a thing you put inside it."

"Now I am going to do it again, and this time I am going to do it properly, and when I
have finished you are going to have an account that is worse."

She did not do anything. She stood on the other side of the crate with her hands at her
sides for what she knew from the inside of her own trade was about as long as a person can
stand still and be looked at.

"That was it," she said.

Van Eede blinked. "That was nothing."

"How do you know it was eleven seconds," she said.

"Because I counted."

"You were watching a man stand still."

"I was watching a man stand still and I have been doing this since I was nine." She took
the watch back off the crate. "You will not do it like that. You will feel that you were
watching *him*, and you will be wrong, and you will be wrong in a way that takes a year to
find out about. You will be watching the room and he will be in the room, and that is the
whole of the illusion, and it is the oldest one there is, and nobody has ever managed to
write it down properly. So we will do it the other way. We will not use me. We will use the
building."

"Constable, how many clocks has this shed got?"

Van Eede looked at her as though she had asked him the number of legs on a dog.

"There is a clock in the yard. It is on the gable. It is a Vuppet and it has been on that
gable since the building went up, and it is a fortnight slow, and everybody in this yard
knows it, and nobody has ever had it repaired."

"How many bells."

"Two. Quarter bells. The first and the second, six minutes apart, and the second one is
wrong, and everybody in this yard has complained about the second one for thirty years."

Reiner sat down on the edge of a crate, which was not a thing she did in rooms, and Van
Eede noticed it and she noticed him noticing it.

"Then that is how we do it. We are going to stand here and do nothing for a length of time,
and I am going to start on a bell and stop on a bell, and you are going to look at the door
the whole time, and afterwards you are going to tell me whether I looked like a man doing
nothing or a man doing something, and we are going to do it four times, and you are going
to find out something about yourself that I am not going to guess at."

They did it four times.

The first time she started on the first bell and stopped on the second, which is six
minutes, and Van Eede was visibly bored, and said so.

The second time she started on the second bell and stopped on the first of the next hour,
which is fifty-four minutes, and she did it in the middle of a conversation about a
pension, standing up, walking to the far wall, standing there, and coming back, and he
followed her with his eyes and said afterwards that he had watched her the whole time and
could not have told her for one second where she had been.

The third time was eleven seconds, and she was on the crate with her hands at her sides, and
he said afterwards that he had not known when it had started or stopped and had not known
whether she had moved at all.

The fourth time she did not start on a bell at all. She started somewhere in the middle of
the yard clock striking, in the middle of a noise, and stopped somewhere else in the middle
of it, and Van Eede said, when she asked him, that he had watched a man stand in a shed and
had not been able to say afterwards whether the man had been there the whole time.

"Four times," said Reiner, "and three of them you cannot place, and the one you can is the
one that was not eleven seconds." She wrote it on the back of her hand. "Now I am going to
tell you the number, and then where it came from, and I want you to take out the small book."

"The small book is an official—"

"I am aware of what the small book is. Take it out."

He took it out.

"The interval is eleven seconds. It is the same in every account that has ever been taken
of this building, and there are twenty-three of them in that book, and I have read all
twenty-three, and seventeen of them give a number. The numbers are eleven, or nine, or
thirteen. Every single one of the seventeen is a whole number, and every single one of them
is within two seconds of eleven, and not one of them is *nothing*." She took the watch off
her wrist and put it on his. "Nobody who was in that room said *I did not see anything*.
Not one of them. They said *I did not know when*, or *I did not notice the moment*, or *I
was looking at the door and the man was in the room*, and every one of those is a
measurement, and you do not make a measurement about nothing."

"The yard clock," said Van Eede. "You took the yard clock."

"I took the yard clock and the two bells and the fact that a fortnight-slow Vuppet on a
gable is the only clock anybody on that field can hear, and I took the fact that he did it
on a Saturday evening in a tent on a field with a gable behind it. He chose a building with
a clock on it. He has used that building, or another like it, in every town for nine years,
and nobody has ever once asked him why a conjurer who works in the dark would want to be
near a noise."

She was aware that she had been talking for some time, and that she had said the word *he*
about forty times, and had not once established any of it.

"That was nothing," Reiner agreed, "and you have just watched nothing for eleven seconds
and you will now give me an account of it, and the account will be excellent, and it will
be eleven seconds long, and I have given you nothing to describe, because there is nothing
in the room to describe. That is the trick. That is the small one. Ask me the question."

"What did you do."

"I stood still for eleven seconds. It is the most difficult thing there is and I have never
once done it well in front of anybody I respected, because a person you respect is watching
you and a person you do not respect is looking at your hands."

"Then it is a piece of business. A manner. You are asking me to believe that a man killed
somebody by being dull on purpose."

"No," said Reiner. "And notice that you have just said the sentence I have been waiting
two days for, and that you said it without any help, and I want you to know that it took
me two days and a boat and a telegram and you did it in a shed on a Tuesday morning."

Van Eede sat down on the crate, which she decided later had been the single most useful
thing anybody did that week.

"Then what," he said.

"I do not know. But I know what I have just shown you is not it, and I know the shape of
what I have shown you, and I know that shape is the shape of every one of the five things
I can do, and I know the man in the storeroom did not use any of them, and that is not an
opinion. That is a number. I have done all five in front of audiences of two hundred people
and not one of them leaves a room thinking *I was there the whole time and there was
nothing to see*, because all five of them have a moment, and in the moment is the point of
all five of them."

"And this has no moment."

"This has no moment," said Reiner, "and I have never seen one without, and I have been
doing this since I was nine, and I have been taught, and it was taught to me by somebody
who is alive and who I telephone, and if the man in that storeroom did this, then somebody
stood in front of him and showed him, and then stood there and watched him not get it
right, possibly several times, for years, and then went away."

She put the watch in her pocket.

"That is what I have. It is not a solution. It is a shape and a name for the shape, and the
name is *taught*, and I have written it down three times since Monday and I do not know
what to do with it, because a taught man can be looked for. He is in the trade. He has a
name and a record and somewhere a book with a name in it, and I have begun, this morning,
to want to do the one thing I am not able to do, which is walk into a room full of
performers and say *has anybody taught this man anything*."

Van Eede was writing in the small book. He wrote for a long time and did not look up.

"You are describing the trick backwards," he said at last. "You have said the same thing
four times and it is the same thing every time and I have written it down every time. He
did not deceive anybody. He did something to the people who watched and they were right
about what they saw and wrong about what it was, and I do not understand the difference, and
you do not understand the difference, and I have been a constable for nine years and I have
never had a case where the witnesses were the difficulty."

Reiner looked at him for a moment.

"The audience is always right about what it saw," she said, "and always wrong about what it
meant. That is not mine, that is a doctrine, and I do not know whose, and I have only just
remembered that I know how it goes and I have never once heard anybody say it out loud."

"The audience is always right about what it saw and always wrong about what it meant," Van
Eede repeated, writing it down. "That is good. That is very good, that is the sort of thing
that goes in a book." He looked up. "It is also worthless, madam, because it does not tell
me what to arrest."

"No," said Reiner. "It does not."

"Then what does it do."

"It tells me that nobody in this case is a liar," she said, "and that is worth more than
you think, because in about four months a room full of educated men are going to be told
that a corpse is the work of a man who walks into rooms and bewitches them, and every one
of those men is going to be wrong, and I am going to have to stand up in front of them and
say so, and I am going to have nothing to put in my mouth but a doctrine, and a doctrine
is not evidence, and I would like to be somewhere else by Thursday."

"Thursday," said Van Eede, "is the eighteenth."

Reiner went very still.

"You will forgive me," she said. "Say that again."

"The eighteenth. I have it in the book. It is the date on the finding, and the date on the
yard man's statement, and I noticed it this morning and I have been sitting here for an hour
with a watch and a key and no idea what I am doing, and then you came and did nothing to me
for eleven seconds and I have understood the whole of it, and the whole of it has a date on
it, and the date is the eighteenth."

The two of them stood in a shed full of paraffin with a brass key and a broken man and
eleven seconds of a Tuesday, and neither of them said it, and it was the eighteenth.

"There is a man in Antwerp on the eighteenth," Reiner said.

"There is a man in Antwerp on the eighteenth," said Van Eede, writing it down, "and I have
no way at all of getting there, and neither have you, because your telegram came yesterday
and I know that, because the boy who brought it came through this room to ask me the way
to the Kralingen tram, and I did not think anything of it at the time and I have thought
of nothing else since."

Reiner took the telegram out of her coat. She had not decided to. She put it in his small
book, folded, and did not explain it, and he did not ask, and that was the arrangement
they worked in for the next four months and it never once broke.

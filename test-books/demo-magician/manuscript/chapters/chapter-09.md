# 9 · The Dossier

The dossier was eleven centimetres deep and it was bound in canvas and it lived in a
tin, and when Anouk Soldano took it out of the tin she put her hand flat on it for a moment
before she opened it, which Reiner understood completely and had never seen anybody else do.

"You will want to know what is in it before I open it," said L'Ombrière. "I am going to
tell you, and I am going to tell you badly, and then I am going to open it, and you are
going to see that I told you badly on purpose, and I would like you to be irritated about
that afterwards rather than about what is in it."

"Go on."

"Seven disputes. Three countries. Nine years. He has never been charged with anything."
She lifted the lid off the tin. "That is the whole of what I know and it has taken me nine
years and it is not the important part."

The room was in a house in Amsterdam that had a canal in front of it and no reason to be
in it, and L'Ombrière had hired it for the afternoon, which Reiner later learned was the
first time in three years that she had spent money she did not have, and that she had
thought about it for a week.

"How is it kept?" Reiner said. "That is the first question."

"By hand. By me." She turned it to face her. "There is one of these in a box in Lausanne
and one in a solicitor's safe in Rotterdam, and this is the third, and they are not copies.
If you are asking whether the other two are the same, they are not, they are *notebooks*,
and a notebook is a list, and this is a case."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that I have copied things out of them and I have kept the copies." She opened it.
"You may not take it. I want that said at the start so that we both know. You may read it
in this house and you may write down anything in it you like, and if you take a page out
of it I will know, because I count them, and I have counted them since 1929."

"How many?"

"Two hundred and eleven."

---

The first was a *requête* from Lyon, 1927, a provincial touring company disputing an
engagement. The second was a *réponse* to a *requête* from Ghent, 1928, in which the
disputing party was the man himself. The third was an *sentence* from a tribunal in
Lausanne that awarded costs against him and did not express a view on the matter. The
fourth was a *sentence* from a tribunal in Rotterdam that did express a view on the matter.

"The Rotterdam one is in my other box," said L'Ombrière. "I will show you it in a moment. It
is the only one of the two hundred and eleven in which anybody has ever said, in writing,
that this man had done something that was not a trick."

"How much money is in the house?"

"About nine hundred guilders, and the rent, and it has come out of a fee I was given in
March by a man in Antwerp who pays very well for dossiers and did not want one." She looked
up. "I have not been paid for nine years to keep this. I pay for it. I want that in the
record too, because you are going to work out inside a week that I have a motive and you
are going to be right, and I would like it noted that the motive is nine years old and
paid for out of my own wages, and that it is a *very* stupid thing to have done."

"Why did you do it?"

"Because in 1929 a tribunal in Rotterdam wrote to the proprietor of a field about a coffer."
She closed the lid for a second and then opened it again. "I have read that letter. It is
in here. I have kept a copy of the letter that nobody has ever asked me for, and it is the
only page in this dossier that I did not write."

---

**The dossier of A. le Corbeau.** *Being a record kept by hand, at my own cost, of every
dispute in which I have been named, with the tribunal's own dispositions, and with the
grounds as stated. Nothing here is a conclusion. A conclusion is not a record.*

**1927. LYON.** Disputed by a *compagnie* claiming an engagement. Grounds: the engagement
was made with a person and not with the company. *Sentence:* the person is the company
where the person has performed. Costs against the claimant, forty guilders. **He is
correct on the law and it is a technicality and the other side were liars, and I have
said so in writing in pencil at the foot of the page, which is not what a record is.**

*L'Ombrière stopped reading and looked up, because Reiner had stopped writing.*

"You are going to ask me about the pencil."

"I was going to ask you about the pencil."

"Nobody has ever asked me about the pencil. I have had this book since 1929 and there are
four entries in it with pencil under them and no one has ever asked me about any of the
four, and you have read the page twice and you have gone straight to it." She turned the
canvas round. "The pencil is because I could not help it. I am not a lawyer. I am a woman
who has kept a file for a man who does not know it exists, and when I read the Lyon
sentence in 1929 I wanted to write *this is unjust* on it, and I did, and then I rubbed it
out, and then I wrote it again in the morning in a harder pencil, and it is still there,
and it is the only opinion in two hundred and eleven pages."

"Who was the *compagnie*?"

"Four men and a woman called Aumale, and I have met two of them, and the man who wrote the
*requête* was a juggler of about thirty called Pauwels who was extremely good and was never
booked again in Lyon after that summer and died in 1931 of something that was not
surprising." She put her finger on the entry. "Forty guilders. That is the whole of what it
cost him. It is eleven days. He wrote nine lines in a good hand and lost eleven days and
never worked in the city again, and the reason is a *person* performing under a *nom de
scène*, and the person was not him, and the tribunal could not be told that, because
naming the person would have named the man, and the man was not in the room."

"So the whole of the first entry is a juggler called Pauwels."

"The whole of the first entry is a juggler called Pauwels," said L'Ombrière, "and that is
what nine years of paper looks like from the other side, and I have wanted somebody to say
his name out loud in this room for a very long time and nobody ever has."

**1928. GHENT.** Disputed *by him*. Grounds not stated in the filed document. *Response:
none required, the challenge was withdrawn.* Costs: paid by him, sixty guilders. **I do
not know why he withdrew it. He withdrew it nineteen days before hearing. He paid the
costs. I have never found another instance.**

"In 1928 the Ghent ground was a *salle*," said L'Ombrière. "A real one, on the Coupure,
with a name on it. And the *salle* withdrew. Not him. The *salle* withdrew, and paid sixty
guilders to the other side, and said nothing, and closed for the summer, and the ground was
never stated in the document because the *salle* did not put it in writing, and that is
illegal, and I have checked it twice, and it is illegal, and it was permitted."

"Permitted by whom?"

"That is the interesting part and I have never been able to get it out of anybody." She had
the canvas flat on the table with both hands on it. "A *salle* on the Coupure does not
withdraw a challenge nineteen days before a hearing and pay sixty guilders and say nothing.
Not in 1928, not in any year. Unless somebody has made it very much worth their while,
unless somebody has told them that the other side has something, unless somebody has looked
at them and said *this is not a hearing you want to be in*, and unless that somebody was
the man sitting at the table when the tribunal refused the *réponse* he had not yet filed."

"So he frightened them."

"He did something better than frighten them and I have never been able to work out what and
it is the first thing in nine years I have not been able to work out." She sat back. "He
never sent it. That is what I keep coming back to. The *réponse* exists. L'Ombrière has
seen it. It is four lines and it was written and it was typed and it was *withheld*, and a
man with a sealed *réponse* in a drawer in 1928 is a man holding a weapon, and he paid
sixty guilders and let a *salle* off, and there is no reason on earth for that, and I have
asked four people and got four different non-answers, and one of them was a magistrate who
was extremely kind about it."

**1929. ROTTERDAM.** *See other box.*

**1930. LAUSANNE.** Disputed by him against a *salle* for a matter of a broken engagement.
*Sentence:* the *salle* in fault; a sum awarded to him. He did not pursue the award.
**He won it and did not take the money. I have queried this in two places and the tribunal
clerk told me, politely, that it was not unusual and that the clerk had seen it four times
in thirty years and had never understood it either.**

"Four hundred and six guilders," said L'Ombrière. "That is the sum. I have it from the
tribunal and it is not a large sum and it is not a small one; it is about nine months of a
room in a boarding house, and I have lived in nine rooms in my life and eight of them were
that."

"You have queried it in two places."

"Three. I have queried it in three places and I have written to a lawyer in Lausanne who
charged me forty guilders to tell me that the answer is contained in the sentence itself,
which is that an award runs to the person named in it, and that a person who does not
pursue an award within a stated period is treated as having waived it, and that the stated
period in 1930 was four years." She put her finger on the page. "He had until 1934. He did
not take it in 1934. He did not take it in 1935. It is still in the account of that court.
I checked in January."

"Why does a man win a case and not take the money?"

"That is precisely what I have asked three people and it is precisely what nobody can
answer, and I have begun to think, Miss Reiner, that the question is in the wrong tense.
He did not *not* take the money. He was **made** not to take it, and not by the *salle*,
who had already been beaten and had no further interest, and not by the court, which would
have paid a man his money."

She looked up.

"By whoever it is who has been making sure, for nine years, that this man never has money
and never has a claim on money and never stands to lose anything, anywhere, in any
country. There is a sum in Lausanne that has been sitting in an account since 1930 for a
man who has never asked for it, and if I write to the tribunal and say *does this man
exist*, then somebody has to be told that I am asking, and I have been keeping this file
for nine years on my own wages and I am not going to be the reason it ends."

Reiner put her own notebook down, which she did perhaps twice in her working life.

"L'Ombrière. How much is it to ask whether a person exists?"

"Eleven guilders and a bad morning."

"Then I am not going to ask it either," said Reiner, "and I want it written down somewhere
that the reason I am not is that you said *a bad morning* and I understood you, and not
because I have any respect for a court in Lausanne, which I have not, and which has had
four hundred and six guilders of a man's money since the year I was born."

**1931. ANTWERP.** Disputed by him, *réponse* filed, the method not named. *Sentence:* the
challenger's own challenge dismissed, because a *réponse* that names a method is a
complete answer and the tribunal will not go behind a *réponse*. **THIS IS THE ROW THAT
MATTERS AND IT IS WHY I HAVE SPENT NINE YEARS. He named a method. Four words of it, filed
in an envelope, never published, never good enough to perform, and the tribunal accepted it
and has never asked to see it again, and neither has anybody
else, because the *réponse* is sealed and the seal is the whole of the system, and a man
who answers a challenge correctly has bought the right to be asked nothing ever again.**

**1932. LYON.** Disputed by him. Withdrawn the day before hearing. Costs paid by him.
**Second instance of 1928, four years later, same city, same outcome, same payment. Two
instances is not a habit. A habit is a third.**

**1935. BRUSSELS.** No dispute filed. He applied for a certificate of good conduct in a city
where he had never performed, under a name that is not the name he had used there in 1931.
**Certificate granted.**

The last page of the writing, in a smaller hand, and the pencil had been gone over twice:

*Seven. Three countries. Nine years. Not one charge. Not one refusal to answer. Two
withdrawals at his own cost, four years apart, in the same city. One certificate of good
conduct obtained in a town where he has never worked, under a name I have not been able to
match to anything.*

*I have looked for a man with a grudge and I have found a man with a *practice*, and I do
not know which of those is worse, and I have not been able to write it down, so I have
written all of it down instead, and I am going to close the book now, and you are going to
ask me the question, and I am going to answer it, and afterwards I would like you to do
something for me that will cost you a great deal and I am going to ask you for it as a
favour and not as a fee, and I would like that on the page too.*

---

"You kept this," Reiner said. "For nine years. At your own cost."

"Yes."

"And you have never given it to a magistrate, a lawyer, a journalist, or a police force."

"No."

"Why?"

L'Ombrière looked at the canvas binding for a long moment.

"Because of the *réponse*," she said. "I will show you the page. It is the only page I have
ever wanted somebody else to see, and I have had it in a tin in this city for nine years,
and I am going to show it to you because I have read it perhaps two hundred times and I
have never once been able to be certain that it is what I think it is, and I would like a
second person to be wrong about it with me."

She turned to it, and it was one page, and it was four lines, and Reiner read it four times
and did not understand it and then understood it completely.

> *The article was in the case throughout. The gentleman is therefore answered.*

*Twenty-six words. The entire method, in twenty-six words, filed in a sealed envelope in
1931, and never once asked for again, and the sentence beneath it in her own hand said:*
**I have had a tradesman explain a boiler to me in fewer words and I understood it.**

"They sealed it," Reiner said.

"They sealed it. That is the finding. That is the whole of nine years." L'Ombrière closed
the canvas. "He did not get away with anything. There is no method here that a man could
perform. It is a *description*. It is a sentence about where the article was, in a room,
at a time, and it is a true sentence, and it is the only true sentence in the whole of this
dossier, and it was true in 1931 and it will be true in 1980."

"That is not a method, that is a *alibi*."

"That is exactly what it is." She tied the canvas. "He does not hide how he did it. He
never has. In thirty-one years he has answered every question put to him in the only way
the law requires, and he has never once been asked a question that he could not answer in
four lines of paper, and he has never once been asked a question at all, because a question
has to come from somebody with standing, and standing comes from having been done a wrong,
and he has never done a wrong that anybody can put in writing."

"He killed a man in a storeroom."

"He did."

"Then that is a wrong."

"Then you will need a magistrate," said L'Ombrière, "and you will need to get him into a
room, and the *réponse* will be filed, and it will be twenty-six words, and it will be
true, and there will be nothing in the world you can put in a court." She put the tin on
the table between them. "Which is what I have been for nine years, Miss Reiner. Not
keeping a dossier. Getting ready to lose."

# 2 · Nobody Asked For It

The man on the boat was a commercial traveller for a firm in Leeds and he had been on the
boat for the crossing and the whole of the previous evening and had not asked her a single
question, which was the most persistent thing about him.

He asked her at half past four in the afternoon, into a wind.

"Sorry. Sorry, I have been not asking you on purpose for about eleven hours, and I am
going to stop being polite about it, because it is a long way to Hull and I have to talk
to somebody." He held the rail. "You're the conjurer. They sent for a conjurer. What I
want to know is what he did."

"He sat on a crate," said Reiner.

"I am aware that he sat on a crate, madam, it is in the paper—"

"I know it is in the paper. I was in the room. I am telling you that the only thing he
did was sit on a crate, and I have watched you decide that I am withholding something, and
I want to explain that I am not."

The commercial traveller turned his head a little. He had been given an enormous amount of
information, he could see, and none of it was the information.

"Let me put it the other way," he said. "There must be a trick. I have seen the posters,
there is a trick—"

"There are five," said Reiner. "Or there are six, and five of them are the ones that are
called things, and I can do all five, and the sixth is not called a thing. What happened
in that storeroom was not one of the five, and I have been on this boat for four hours
trying to work out what it was, and I have got nowhere, and I would rather say that to
you than invent something, because you will be in Leeds on Thursday and I will not."

"And if it was magic."

"Then it was magic, and it is your business, and I will not have made a man in a Leeds
hotel feel foolish in the time it takes to get there."

He thought about that for a while, in the way that people do when they have been given more
than they expected and are trying to find the catch.

"Was it a good trick, then. If it was magic. Was it any good."

Reiner looked at the water going past and did not answer for long enough that he turned
to look at her.

"It was the best one I have ever seen," she said. "And I sat in that room for four minutes
and I did not see a single part of it, and I have been doing this since I was nine, and I
would like you to understand that I am not being modest. I am telling you the two of those
are the same thing and one of them is much rarer."

---

The hotel gave her a room over a chandler's yard and the man downstairs had been killed
nine weeks before and the whole street knew it and nobody said so at breakfast, which she
thought was extremely well done of everybody.

She had asked for paper at the desk. She had got a good deal of it, and a room, and no
light that worked. She sat on the end of the bed with her boots still on and wrote for two
hours, and what she wrote was this, and it is given here entire because everything after it
is an argument with it:

**What the room said.**
One man came in Tuesday and one man sat there and the man who sat there is not the man
who came in, and nobody in the building has been able to say that out loud, and one man
has said a piece of it out loud and will not say the rest of it, and the piece he said was
that he was not in the room.

**What the room got right.**
Everything. Every account in that building is true. That is the part I cannot put down.
Twenty-two people and a constable with a book and they were all right, and they are all
right now, and they will all be right in a court, and there is not one false sentence
between them.

**What that means.**
That they were not deceived. I keep writing deceived. Nobody was deceived. Deceived means
you were given a false thing and believed it and were wrong. Not one of them is wrong. I
have been in rooms where the audience was wrong and I have never once been in a room where
the audience was right and I was the only one in it, and I have been in that room four
times in eleven years, and I did not understand it on any of the four, and I understand
about a third of it now.

**What I will not write.**
That he is better than me. Better than me? He has never held a hand in his life and he
would not last an evening in a room I put together, and there is not a single thing he can
do that I cannot do. What he has is something else entirely, and I have been on this boat
four hours
turning it over and it has a name and the name is *taught*, and I do not know who taught
him, and I have written that sentence eleven times tonight and I keep coming back to it,
and I think the reason I keep coming back to it is that a man who is better than you is
simply better and you go and learn and in four years he is not better any more. A man who
was *taught* is something else, because somebody had to be in the room, and somebody had
to decide to be in the room, and that somebody is still alive and I do not know their
name, and that is the first sentence I have written in this book that I am prepared to
put in front of another human being.

**What nobody did.**

Twenty-two people in nine feet by eleven and a constable with a book, and not one of them asked
him what he had done in the room. They asked each other and they asked the constable, and a
baker asked a tribunal in nine lines, and every one of the nine lines was about a coffer and
not about a room. I have turned the whole thing over on this boat and the only person who has
put the question to me is a commercial traveller with a wind in his face who will be in Leeds
on Thursday and will not think about it again.

And I did not ask it either. I sat in that room for four minutes and I came out and I asked
what the trick was, which is the same as asking nothing at all, and a man who has been asked
nothing for nine years has had nine years of answering everybody. I have watched him do it in a
document and in a shed and there is not one question anybody has put to him that he has not
been able to answer with a true sentence about somewhere else. A true sentence about a field. A
true sentence about a date. A true sentence about a glass of water in a shop that nobody has
looked at twice since 1929, and the true sentences are all of them answers, and not one of them
is an answer to anything.

She put the pen down and read the last part again, and did not cross it out.

---

In the morning a boy came up with a telegram, which was a thing that had not happened to
her in some years, and it was from a man in Utrecht who had given her work twice and paid
her once.

**DON'T GO TO ANTWERP. HE IS THERE.**

It was six words and then a number, which was a booking, and the number was for the
eighteenth of the month, and the eighteenth was the day after tomorrow.

Reiner sat on the end of the bed and looked at it for a long time, and the thing she was
aware of, standing there holding six words, was that she had packed her case for Antwerp
at ten o'clock the previous night, before the telegram, and had not mentioned it to
anybody, and there was no reason on earth why a stranger on a boat should have known that.

That was the whole of it. She put the telegram in her coat, and she went and got her
boots off, and she did not pack, and she did not go to Antwerp, and she wrote two more hours
in the same notebook, and the notebook is the reason the book is a book.

Because on the boat, at half past four, with the wind on the port side, a commercial
traveller for a firm in Leeds had asked her what the man did, and she had said *he sat on
a crate*, and she had said *it was the best one I have ever seen*, and she had said
*taught* and had not known why she had said it.

And in Utrecht a man who had paid her once and owed her once had sent six words and a
number, in a hurry, on a Saturday night, about a man neither of them had ever seen, and
Reiner sat in a hotel over a chandler's yard and understood — in the way that you
understand a thing you will not be able to prove for nine months, sitting down, with
nothing in the world to hold — that the man in the storeroom had not been in the storeroom
at all, and had never once needed to be, and that somebody had known it, and that the
somebody was on the eighth of next month in a town called Antwerp, in a room, in front of
people.

# 23 · What Rask Did in 1919

He asked for it, which was the thing Reiner had never once expected and could not afterwards
account for.

She had gone to see him on a Tuesday in May with a notebook and four questions written on
the first page, and he had let her into the room and taken the shawl off, and had said:

"Sit down. I have been waiting since February and I would like it over with and I have had
four months to think about how to say it and I have not managed to improve on the first
version, and the first version is the one I am going to use."

"You are fifty-eight."

"I am fifty-eight and I have been nine since 1902 and I taught a boy in a town neither of
us will name in the spring of 1919, and I have had nineteen years to think of a way to make
that sentence sound like something else, and I have not found one, and I stopped looking in
November."

Reiner put the notebook on her knee and did not open it.

"I am not writing this down."

"I know. That is why I can do it." He sat down heavily. "If you were writing it
down I would have to be careful, and being careful is what I have done since 1919, and being
careful is *why*, and if I stop being careful for one hour then the whole of the next
nineteen years is explained, and I have not been able to have that explained in my own head
since I was forty."

"Then tell me."

"I taught him. I taught him four times, in one spring, in a town in the east of the country
whose name you will not get from me, because the town did nothing and I have never gone
back. He was fifteen and he was quick and he had nobody, and I was thirty and I was drunk
on about four of the four, and I taught him the Grand Transposition and I taught it badly
and I taught it the way it is done in a tent, which is to say that I taught him to do the
thing I could do, and not the reason the thing works."

She did not say anything. She had come four hours for this and she had a hundred questions
and she was not going to spend one.

"Four times," said Rask. "I can give you that exactly, because I have counted them, which is
the sort of thing a man does. Four sittings. And at the end of the fourth one he did it, and
he did it the way I do it, and he did it *wrong*, and I told him he did it wrong, and he
asked me why, and I said *because you are looking at your hand*, and he said *so are you*,
and I said *yes*, and that was the end of the fourth one."

"And after that."

"After that he did it again in the June, without me, and he did it right, and I found out
in the July, and I went and told him that I was not going to teach him any more." Rask put
his hands on his knees. "That is the whole of what I did and it took about eleven months
and I have never told anybody and I have told two people in a room in Utrecht."

"Why did you stop."

"Because he was better than me at it by the July," said Rask, "and because I was thirty and
drunk four times out of four, and because there is a thing you have to do to a boy like that
and I did not do it, and I knew I was not going to, and I would rather have stopped than
carried on being too good for him for another year."

---

The room was very quiet. There was a sound of a man hammering a stake, four streets away,
who had been hammering a stake for some time.

"You could have told somebody," said Reiner. "At any point. For nineteen years."

"I could have gone to a magistrate in the March of 1934 when I read about the storeroom, and
I did not, and I want that understood: I read about it and I put the paper down and I
understood at once what had happened and who it was and why, and I had known since the
seventh of July 1919." He did not look at her. "And I did not go, and I have thought about
that every week for two years, and the reason is not what you are hoping for."

"What is it."

"It is that I was afraid. Plainly, and for two years, and I am not going to dress it up as
anything else, and I have noticed that people always dress it up, and I have watched a great
many men dress it up." He put one hand flat on the table. "And I have never once in nineteen
years dressed it up to myself, which is the only thing I have got to be proud of and it is
not much, and there is no word for it in this trade, and there is not one in any of the four
languages I have."

"And now?"

"Now a woman has made a prediction about a door in a room over a tobacconist and asked me
to sit in a court and be asked about a boy of fifteen in a town in the east of the country."
He straightened up, which took a while. "And I have been a coward for nineteen years, and
cowards can be made to be useful, and I am going to be useful, and I am going to be asked
in that room in July what I did in 1919, and I am going to answer it, and I am going to
answer it *badly*, because I taught it badly and I will describe it badly and I will not
make it better than it was in order to have made it smaller. And when I have said it, the
question will be in front of everybody whether I taught him well or taught him badly, and
it will be the first time in nineteen years that the question has been *asked*, and that is
the entire return on nineteen years of cowardice and I am not going to pretend it is more
than that."

Reiner found that she could not speak for a moment, and then found that what she was
feeling was so far from what she had come for that she had to laugh, once, and Rask
flinched.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I have come four hours to be given a method and you have given me a
man and I am not equipped for it."

"Then you have come to the wrong house."

"Yes," said Reiner. "I have. And I would like to stay for an hour and not ask you anything
about the trick, if that is all right, and I would like you to tell me what the boy was
like, because you have not said one word about the boy and you have been talking about
himself for eleven minutes, and you taught him four times in a spring and you have never
said a single thing about him that was not an apology."

Rask was quiet for a long moment.

"He was quick," he said. "He was *so* quick. He was fifteen and he understood the *hand*
in a week, which takes most people two years, and I have taught perhaps two hundred people
in my life and I have seen four do that."

"And?"

"And he was fifteen," said Rask, "and he was in a town in the east of the country with
nobody, and there was an old man in a room who was drunk four times out of four and who
taught him something that was not difficult and did not say one single word to him about
what it was for, and the boy worked it out anyway, alone, in a summer, at sixteen, and by
the time he was nineteen he was doing a thing in rooms that I have never been able to do in
any room at all, and there is no one left who can tell him that the man who taught it to
him was ashamed of the teaching."

He looked up.

"I have thought about that for nineteen years and I have never once thought that it was
*my* shame," he said. "That is a lie I have been telling in a very quiet voice. It was my
shame. He was a boy and I was not careful and it was me, and there is nobody in the world
who can be told that, and so I have been telling it to myself for nineteen years, and in
July I am going to tell it to a room, and I have wanted to tell somebody for a very long
time, and the somebody turned out to be a woman with a notebook who has not written a word
down for an hour."

---

At the door he stopped, and he was an awkward old man in a cold corridor and she had to
wait for it.

"Miss Reiner. You are going to be asked in that room why I did not come forward in 1934."

"I am."

"And I want you to have the true answer ready, because the false one is much better and
everybody in that room will want it." He had his hand on the door. "The true answer is that
I was afraid, and that I have been afraid every day since, and that being afraid is not an
excuse and is not a reason and is not going to be one when they ask me. And if you say that
in that room, somebody will write it down, and I shall have to live in a country where it
is written down."

"I will say it."

"No," said Rask. "You will not, because you are a decent woman and you will be asked a
question with a door at the end of it, and you will find a sentence, and the sentence will
be *he was a child and there were four of us and it was a long time ago*, and that sentence
will be true in every part of it, and it will be a mercy, and it will be the fourth mercy
somebody has done me since 1919 and I have had nine years of them and I am sixty next year
and I am not going to be pitied out of the last one."

He opened the door.

"Tell them I was afraid," said Rask. "Tell them the word. It is a small word and it will do
more in that room than anything either of us has, and I have had four months to work out
that it will, and I would like you to be the one who says it, because I have found that I
can say a great many things provided they are said by somebody else at a distance where I
cannot see their face."

Then he went along the corridor, and Reiner stood in the doorway of a room over a
tobacconist in Utrecht with a notebook on her knee, shut, and four hours of questions on
page one that she would not use, and a sentence in her head that a man of fifty-eight had
spent nineteen years not saying and had handed to her in a cold corridor in twenty seconds
because she had been careful with him and had not once asked him about the trick.

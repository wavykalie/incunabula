# 14 · Two Hundred and Eleven

The wind came in on the Tuesday and it did not stop for six days, and by the Wednesday
Sigrid Voll had the tent up, and it was the best thing Reiner had ever seen and she was
sorry about the rest of the book for the rest of her life.

It began at four in the morning and it was not a storm exactly, it was a *wind*, and it
came across the flat ground from the north with nothing to slow it down for forty miles,
and it found the pegs.

Nobody outside the trade understands this and everybody inside it has learned it at
fifteen: a tent is a machine for not blowing away, and every part of it is in tension
against every other part, and the whole of it is one argument between four forces that
have to be exactly right. The guys out. The pegs down. The wind in. The ground. And you
cannot win by pulling harder, because the pegs are holding the *guys* and the guys are
holding the *canvas* and the canvas is holding about four thousand pounds of wet that
would rather be somewhere else.

So it goes wrong at one point and then it goes wrong everywhere, and the whole of the skill
is knowing which point.

---

Johan took the north wall.

Nobody gave it to him and nobody questioned it, and Reiner understood within ten minutes
that this was not a decision and was thirty years of a decision.

He worked from the inside, on his knees, with a mallet, and he did not speak for two hours,
and twice Reiner thought he was going to get up and hit something. He did not. He sat back
on his heels and looked at a guyrope for a long moment and then moved the stake eight
inches and re-set it, and the eight inches was the entire content of his morning.

"He is not working," Halvard said, coming past with an armful of rope. "He is *listening*."

"To what?"

"To the tension. You can hear when it is wrong before it goes. It makes a noise like a
person clearing their throat, and if you have heard it once you cannot stop hearing it, and
he has heard it forty thousand times." Halvard put the rope down. "That is what a tent is.
A tent is a noise you have to keep not hearing."

They lost the east wall at nine.

That was the part Reiner had not been ready for. It came off in about four seconds, one
line going at a time, and the canvas took about a foot of air and then a great deal more,
and it made a sound in the wind that Reiner felt in her teeth, and there were eleven people
on that side and every one of them had it handled inside a second, because they had all
been doing this since they were children and had never once discussed it.

There is no shout in it. She wrote that down afterwards, in the notebook, in
the part of the book she has never shown anybody.

There is no shout. There is a man on the east wall who says *loose* and everybody's hands
know what loose means, and the canvas comes down off the guys in an orderly line and lands
in the mud and everyone who can get to it gets to it, and the *thing* — the dangerous,
book-destroying, career-ending thing that turns a bad morning into a lost season — does
not happen, because the eleven people in that tent do not need to be told anything.

Halvard Grens caught the guy, took the strain, and paid it out in a controlled length while
Johan and a Dutch boy called Kees got every peg in that quarter out of the ground and
rolled the wall. Eleven minutes.

And then the tent had a hole in it the size of a door, in the wind, on a Wednesday, and
eleven people stood in the rain and looked at it, and Sigrid Voll said, in a voice that
carried in the wind the way a voice does not normally carry:

"Right. Who is going to tell Sigrid."

---

She did not tell Sigrid. That was the whole of the joke and it lasted for three days.

Sigrid Voll's system, which she had built over twenty-two years and which nobody fully
understood, was that she was not in charge of anything. She had been a strongmover, a
cook, a book woman, a dresser, and a great deal of other things, and she was very good at
four of them, and she held the entire company together by not holding anything, and the
entire company had agreed, at some point in a decade nobody could remember, not to
organise her.

So on the Wednesday morning, with a hole in the east wall, eleven people looked at Sigrid
and nobody said a word.

"You are all stood there," she said.

"Waiting on you, Sigrid," said Johan.

"You are all stood there *waiting on me*."

"Yes," said Johan.

And the entire tent — Reiner has written that she was not one of the eleven and she was
also laughing, and that both of those are true — exploded.

---

They put it up in four hours in the rain.

Not the same tent. There was not time for the same tent and there was no question of the
same tent, because a tent that has had a wall off it in a wind is a tent that will have
another wall off it. What they did was: keep the frame, take down every wall, and rebuild
the east side as a double wall, which takes longer and holds twice the load, and which is
what you do when you are going to be in a country for three weeks and there is no
guarantee about the weather in it.

Sigrid did not direct it. Reiner watched her for an hour specifically in order to be able
to answer the question, and the answer is that Sigrid Voll did not direct it, and that at
no point in the four hours did any of the eleven people ask Sigrid Voll a single question
about what to do next.

They worked it out of the inside. The way a working crew does. Halvard went along the
frame and marked load points with chalk and said nothing else all afternoon. Johan set the
stakes. Kees, who was nineteen and had been in the company for a year, did the double
wall with a girl of seventeen who had been in it for six weeks, and they were better at it
than anybody else there, because they were not thinking about it, they were just doing it,
and Reiner watched two teenagers do a job that eleven adults could not have done
correctly, on the only criterion that mattered.

The double wall is worth describing, because it is the kind of thing that gets described badly. It is two sheets of canvas with a hand's width of air between them laced to the same frame at different tensions, and the air between them is the point: the outer sheet takes the wind, the inner sheet takes the load, and the space between takes the argument, so that a gust which would strip a single wall simply spends itself in the gap and arrives on the other side as weather rather than as damage.

It takes two people, because the lacing has to be run at the same time on both sheets, and if one side runs ahead the wall goes into the frame crooked and stays crooked and sheds water into the tent for three weeks. Kees and the girl did it in fifty minutes calling the count out to each other in Dutch across two feet of canvas, and the count was the only thing either of them said all afternoon, and when they finished they did not stop and look at it, because a crew that stops to look at a thing has not finished it.

And through all of it, in a wet canvas field in a country with a lot of water in it, in
the middle of a wind, Sigrid moved. She did not decide anything. She fetched. She knew
where the lines were, where the pegs were, where the kettle was, where the dry socks were,
and she was never more than twenty feet from the person who needed something, and when
somebody needed something she had it before they had finished deciding that they needed it.

At two o'clock somebody said she should eat. She said she would eat at four. Somebody said
that at four she would not remember having said that. She said, "No," and that was the only
time all day that she sounded like a woman with any authority in her at all, and Reiner
understood that the entire company was run on that.

She was not in charge, and never had been, and the company knew it. She was the reason the
company was the same company on the other side of a bad morning.

---

They opened on the Friday night and there were two hundred and eleven people in the tent.

Reiner wrote that number down in the back of the notebook in a list of numbers she kept
that meant nothing to anybody, and the list went: the eleven seconds. The two days. The
thirty guilders. The eleven communes. **Two hundred and eleven.** And she did not know why
she was keeping it, and she found out eleven months later why, on a bad afternoon, in a
room in a town where a man had been sent for leave and refused.

The night went well.

That is the correct thing to say and Reiner has never been able to make it sound like the
right size. It went *well*. There were four items, and the fourth was the one they had been
building since Thursday, and the girl of seventeen did the girl, and Johan did the cards,
and Kees did a rope routine that should not have worked in a tent with a doubled east wall
and did, and the tent went up on a line of canvas and made a noise like weather.

Sigrid Voll was funny twice, which was one more than her average, and the second one was at
Halvard, and it was about the drawing of a horse.

"You have drawn me a horse."

"It is a horse."

"It is a horse the way a table is a horse." She stood in front of the fire with a cup in
both hands. "It has six legs, Halvard."

"It has four."

"It has six and you have shown me two of them and I have counted four, which is my error
and I withdraw it, but I am going to leave the other two in, because a horse with six legs
can pull more." She drank. "That is the whole doctrine and I have never been able to put it
any better than that and I have been putting it badly for twenty years."

And Halvard Grens, who did not laugh, who had a face like a bookkeeper's, who had not been funny
once in the three months Reiner had known him:

"I will tell you the difference between the six legs and the two I drew," he said. "The
six are hers. The two are the truth." He put the pencil in his pocket. "I have never in my
life been able to draw a thing without the two. It is why I do not perform and it is why I
am good at it."

Nobody said anything for a while, and then a man at the back started the applause, which was
not for either of them, and was for a Saturday.

---

She walked out at two in the morning into a wind that had not stopped for six days and
stood in it.

The tent behind her had four hundred pounds of tension in it and eleven people asleep in
it and a doubled east wall and no hole anywhere, and it had been put up in four hours by
fourteen people, in rain, in a country they were not from, on a booking made in the spring
by a woman who had done nothing whatever to be a great artist except be extremely difficult
to work with.

And Reiner stood in the dark and found that she could not remember any of it.

That was the discovery and she made it in the field, in the wind, at two in the morning,
and it is the discovery the whole second half of the book is built out of.

She had spent eleven months looking for the moment. She had built a room and a river and a
woman who kept a lock and a baker of Delft and a man who was on a stage for eleven seconds
in the spring — and standing in the wet outside a tent in a country she was not from, she
could not say one single thing about any of it, and she had been *there* for all of it.

Two hundred and eleven people had been in that room, and they had all been right, and she
had watched every one of them be right, and she had the notebook, and the notebook said:
*Row 1. He gives them a man and he takes him away and they are still holding him.* And she
could not say where that had come from, or who had said it to her, or in what weather.

She had it. She wrote it down herself. She was the only person alive who had it, and she
could not hold on to it, and the reason was standing four feet away in the mud with its
back to the wind and its light on.

Reiner went inside and woke Halvard Grens up, which she had not done in her life to
anybody, and he came out into the weather without asking a question, because he had said in
November that he would not ask her a single question and he had kept to it through nine
months and she was not going to be the one who broke him.

"Halvard."

"You have the face," he said.

"I have a question and it is not for you and I know that, and I am asking you anyway
because there is nobody else in this tent and because it is a technical question and you
are the only person in this company who will give me a straight technical answer."

"Go on."

"A man gives a room something to hold." She had to shout it. "And he takes it away. And the
room is still holding it. Is that a movement, or is that a *position*?"

Halvard Grens looked at her for a long moment in the wind, and then he said, without any
difficulty at all, and she was to remember for the rest of her life that he had not
hesitated:

"Neither. It is a *position*, and it is the only one anybody in this trade is ever really
in, and you have been looking for the movement for eleven months because a movement is a
thing you can see, and there is nothing to see, and I have been trying to tell you that
since Utrecht and I could not get a sentence out of you because you were in a shed with a
constable."

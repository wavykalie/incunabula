# 30 · Where Aldrich Will Say He Was Looking

The clerk read the *sentence* at ten past ten on a Tuesday in October and then the matter
was called, and the room filled up in a way Reiner had not expected, because there had been
two notices on a door in a street off the Binnenweg and a piece in a Rotterdam paper that
had been wrong about the year and had somehow got the room right, and there were perhaps a
hundred and forty people in a hall built for two hundred and forty.

Ninety-one of them were performers. She counted them at about eleven o'clock, in a bad
moment, and stopped counting, and did not count again.

---

The assesseur was thorough and fair and completely indifferent to the fact that there was
any matter at all in his hall. He took the *requête* in three parts. He took L'Ombrière's
nine sheets of carbon paper in twenty-two minutes and found them inadmissible, which was
correct, and said why, which was generous, and which Reiner had not expected. He took four
witnesses in an hour. He took the accounts of two men who had not been in the storeroom and
established nothing whatever with either, and did it kindly.

At one o'clock he took a recess of twenty minutes and Reiner went out into a corridor and
sat down on a bench and did not open her notebook, because there was nothing in it.

"You will be called at two," said De Wit, the clerk, who had been extremely correct for
eight weeks and had still never once been on her side.

"I know."

"Madame — may I say a thing that is not my business."

"You may say it and I will not repeat it."

"You have no case." De Wit said it flatly, standing up, in a corridor, with a folder. "You
have four witnesses to a sentence nobody heard, nine sheets of inadmissible carbon paper,
and a woman in Delft who says he was looking at a door. You are going to state a prediction
about a man's memory and the man in the wig is going to look at you, and I have watched him
for two hours and he is a careful man, and he is going to ask you one question, and it is
going to be: *is this a statement or a question.*"

"Why?"

"Because if you put it as a question he can refuse it, and if you put it as a statement then
he may treat it as a statement of fact and ask you to support it." De Wit shifted the
folder. "And if you cannot support it then the whole thing is an opinion, and I have read
your *requête* four times and there is not one opinion in it, and I have been trying for
eight weeks to work out how you were going to do it and I cannot, and I have a folder to
carry."

Reiner sat on a bench in a corridor for the rest of the recess.

She did not open the notebook, because opening it would have shown her the four pages, and the
four pages were the whole of what she had, and a woman with four pages who reads them in a
corridor in the middle of a hearing is a woman who is going to say them out loud at two o'clock
and then stop, and there had to be something after the stopping, and she had not found it, and
the recess was twenty minutes long and she spent nineteen of them not being frightened and one
of them being frightened in a way she had no practice at at all. What she had was a shape and a
date and a door and a prediction, and the prediction was the only one of the four that could be
wrong in a way that would show, and every conjurer in that building would have told her to keep
the prediction back and let the shape do the work, because a shape cannot be checked and a
prediction can, and she had been taught that herself at nine years old by a man who wrote rooms
down, and she was going to ignore it, and the ignoring was the whole of the case.

---

At four minutes past two she was called, and she stood up, and there were a hundred and
forty people in a hall in Utrecht, and about a fifth of them were conjurers, and the man in
the wig looked at her, and she understood De Wit's question about four seconds before she
opened her mouth and answered it in the only way it can be answered.

"Madame," said the assesseur, "the court is at your disposal."

"I am going to make a statement," said Elsa Reiner, "and I am going to make it in the form
of a statement, and I am aware that you may ask me to support it, and I am not able to, and
I would like to say now, before I begin, that I have not come here to be supported. I have
come here to be checked."

The assesseur put his glasses on. Behind him the clerk had a pen ready and did not write
anything.

"In April of this year," said Reiner, "in a room over a tobacconist in Utrecht, I said a
sentence to four people. I did not write it down. I have not written it down since, and it
exists in four heads and in a transcript of nothing, and if I had written it down then in
six weeks I would have had a document that anybody could have read, and I wanted the only
evidence of it to be four people who did not think it meant anything.

"In six minutes' time I am going to say the sentence again, in this hall, and then I am
going to ask a man in Delft to be asked a question about an afternoon, and he is going to
answer it, and he is going to be right, and I am going to be right, and neither of those
two things is going to be doing any work."

She turned round, in a hall, in front of a hundred and forty people, and looked for
somebody, and found him in about four seconds. He was at the back. He had come. Nobody had
told him to come and there was no procedure that required him to come and a man with nine
years of practice at not being in a room had been in a room all day, and he was at the back
of a hall in Utrecht on a Tuesday in October, in a good suit, waiting.

"I said a sentence in April about a man called George Aldrich, who keeps a goods gate on
the Binnenweg in Delft and has kept it since 1911. He is fifty-one years old. He has never
been charged with anything and he has never been in this room and he does not know I exist.

"On the eleventh of April last year he was in a storeroom on the Kralingen field in
Rotterdam for somewhere between four and ten minutes. On the twenty-second of May last year
a constable took down what he said, in a shed, in about ninety seconds, and it went into a
file, and that file has been closed ever since, and nobody has looked at it since May of
last year, and the man has never once thought about it.

"Here is the whole of what he said. I am going to read it and I am going to read it
exactly, including the bad punctuation, because the bad punctuation is in it."

She read it, from a copy in her bag, in a hall in Utrecht, in a voice that did not go up at
the end and did not go down at the end and that she had practised for four days in a room
over a tobacconist with nobody in it.

*'I was on the floor at the west end from about four until about ten past. I did not leave
it. I was looking at the door the whole time. I never took my eyes off that door, not once,
not for a second, and I would know it, because I have been on that door eleven years and
there is nothing on that door worth watching.'*

"That was taken on the twenty-second of May of last year. In March of this year a man in a
wig at a hearing in Utrecht read it out. In April I heard a man in a shed say it out loud
from memory and it was word for word the same, sixteen months apart.

"**That is what the whole case rests on. Not on the trick. On a man in a shed remembering
a sentence correctly.**"

---

The hall was extremely quiet. Reiner had not expected it to be quiet and had built the last
eleven months on the assumption that it would be loud, and the loudness had been in her
plan as a *condition* and there had been no condition.

"The room was nine feet by eleven," she said. "I was in it for four minutes in April of last
year and I have been in nine hundred rooms and I can tell you that no room of that size
holds twenty-two people for eleven seconds and stays still. It does not happen. The ninth
person changes everybody's position. By the fifteenth there is a man near the door who can
feel the draught.

"And not one of the twenty-two accounts mentions a draught, or a movement, or anybody
wanting to leave, or a man in the way. Which means the twenty-two were not in the room I
was in. Which means the room was cleared and the twenty-two were brought in afterwards.

"Which means the twenty-two were somewhere else first, being told what to expect. In a
room. With a door.

"And when they were brought in, there was a man in the middle of it, standing up, being
looked at, in a lit space, for eleven seconds. And a door in the room they had just come
out of — or a man who had told them about a door — and every one of those twenty-two people
was already in the habit of watching a door, and they came into the second room still doing
it, and they did it perfectly, and they did it for eleven seconds while a man stood in the
middle of a cleared room being uninteresting.

"That is the whole of what I can prove and it is a shape and not a trick, and I have been
telling you a shape for eleven months and it is worth nothing, and I have known since
February that it is worth nothing, and I have not come here today to give you the shape."

She turned round again.

"Madame," said the assesseur, "you said a statement."

"I said a statement. This is the statement. I am going to state a prediction and then it is
going to be checked, and if it is wrong I shall be wrong in a way that cannot be repaired
and I shall not appeal it, and I would like all of that in the transcript."

"Go on."

"George Aldrich has never been asked which door," said Reiner.

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"In a room nine feet by eleven there is a goods door on the Binnenweg side and there is a
back stair, and they are in different walls. He has been on that gate for twenty-two years
and he came in by the goods door, and he will tell you — he told me so himself, on a
Wednesday afternoon in September, on a gate in Delft, in the most straightforward way
anybody has said anything to me in twelve months — that he came in by the goods door.

"**So when you ask him where he was looking, he will say the back stair.**"

She let that stand for a moment, and it was the worst moment of her life, and it lasted
about four seconds.

"Not because he will be clever," she said. "Because he has been watching a door since 1911
and he came in by the other one, and every man in this hall would say the back stair if you
asked him which door he had been watching at the west end of a room on the Kralingen field
for six hours, and a man who has not thought about an afternoon since May of last year
will not stop and reason about it. He will simply say the door he uses.

"Which puts him four feet from the goods door for the whole of the eleven seconds, with
his back to it, while a man stands in the middle of a cleared room.

"**Nobody was deceived. A man was occupied, and a man was four feet from a door with his
back to it, and he has never once thought about it, and on Thursday I am going to ask him a
question he has never been asked, and it is the only question in this case that can be
answered wrongly, and I have known that since the twelfth of April, and I have spent
eleven months getting to it.**"

---

The assesseur asked her one question and it was not the one De Wit had predicted.

"Madame. Is this a statement or a question?"

Reiner felt the whole of the last eleven months arrive at once, in a hall, in front of a
hundred and forty people, ninety-one of whom were conjurers and all of whom were watching
a woman who had said *occupied* in a room in April.

"It is neither," said Elsa Reiner. "It is a *réponse.*"

There was a small noise in the hall, and she felt it go round her, and she had been in nine
hundred rooms and had never once been in one where a sound went round.

"Am I allowed to say that to this court?"

"You may say whatever you like to this court," said the assesseur, "and it will be recorded,
and it will be worth what it is worth, and I am an assessor and not a magistrate and I have
been in this room for two days and I have not had the pleasure of hearing a *réponse* in
eleven years, and I have read four hundred of them, and every single one of them has been
four words long and has named a method."

"Yes," said Reiner. "Mine is eleven months long and it names none."

"Adjourn," said the assesseur, "until Thursday, half past ten."

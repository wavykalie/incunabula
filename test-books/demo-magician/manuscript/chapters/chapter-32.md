# 32 · The Adding of Detail

Halvard Grens gave evidence on the Friday morning and he was good at it, and he had been
good at it for nineteen years, and it is the one thing in this book that nobody in the room
saw happening.

He is the best book man in the north. He does the drawings. He has a hand like a clerk and
a temper like weather. He did not perform for eleven years on the grounds that the tent
cannot support a name, and there was not one person in the hall including the assesseur who
did not believe him, and there was not one person in the hall who had any idea that he had
been the first man to say so.

He was asked about the assembly. He was asked about the tent in Assen. He was asked about
the booking system and the formula of 1919 and the man in Delft who invented a
supplementary, and he answered all of it in a clear, dry, unhurried voice, giving dates.

Then the assesseur asked him a question, and it was a fair question, and it was asked
politely, and it was the wrong question, and it was wrong in the way that only a very good
witness can be wrong.

"Mr Grens. You have said that you did not know the respondent in February. Is that right?"

"I did not know him in February."

"You had seen him perform."

"I had seen him perform in 'thirty-one in a hall in Utrecht. I have said so."

"You have said you had no reason to think of him as anything but a performer."

"That is right."

"Would you describe him as a craftsman?"

"I would describe him as a very good performer," said Halvard Grens, "and I have used that
word in this room four times in two days and I mean it every time, and I would like to
point out that a craftsman is a word for a man who makes a thing, and this is a man who
does a thing, and I have been a book man for nineteen years and I can tell you the
difference in about a second, because a man who makes a thing is interested in the making."

"And yet you have described him as a man you would not take a booking with."

"I have."

"Why?"

"Because of the room," said Halvard Grens.

---

Here is the trap, and it was not laid by anybody, and the assesseur did not know he had
laid it, and Halvard Grens walked into it at eleven o'clock on a Friday morning with the
same care he brings to a booking, because that is the whole of what he is.

"Would you explain that."

"I would."

"In your own words."

"It is a matter of a man's attention."

And then he stopped, and Reiner saw him stop, and she has said since that she would give a
great deal to know what he was doing in the second before he stopped, and that she has been
in nine hundred rooms and has never once watched a man stop in order to check himself, and
that he was not checking himself, and that he was doing something much rarer, which was
checking what he had already said.

"It is a matter of a man's attention," he said again. "In any room of any size, the
attention is doing the work. That is the whole of it. The attention of the people is
carrying the trick and the trick is only ever the load the attention is asked to bear, and a
man who is good at it is a man who is good at the load, and a man who is good at the load
in one room is good at it in every room, and I have watched him in Delft and I have watched
him in Utrecht and I have watched him in a shed in Rotterdam in a fortnight where he was in
a room nine feet by eleven and I would like to be permitted to say that I have never been in
a smaller room with a better attention in my life, and I have been in rooms since I was
nineteen."

Silence in the hall.

"That is a considerable statement."

"It is a true one."

"Mr Grens, did you see the respondent in Rotterdam in April of last year?"

"No."

"Did you know of him in Rotterdam in April of last year?"

"No."

"Then how can you speak to his attention in a room nine feet by eleven in Rotterdam in
April of last year?"

Halvard Grens looked at the assesseur.

And Reiner, four rows back, four days after a man in a wig had asked a man in a wig's court
a question about a handkerchief, watched a good witness do the thing she had spent twelve
months learning to detect, and understood that she was watching it happen to the best book
man in the north, and that it had nothing to do with the trick, and that he would never
understand that it had happened.

"I was speaking generally," said Halvard Grens.

"No, sir," said the assesseur, gently. "You gave a date and a size."

"The date is the date of my own memory."

"Then you were in the room?"

"I was not in the room."

"You were in Rotterdam in April of last year?"

"I was in Utrecht in April of last year. I was in Rotterdam in the March before it and I
was there for a drawing for a *salle* in May, and I have said neither of those things
because neither of them was asked to me, and I have been in this room for two days
answering what I am asked, and if you had asked me about March I would have told you about
March."

"Then the March."

"There was no performance in Rotterdam in March," said Halvard Grens, "because the ground
was under water to the second week and the *salle* was in a shed off the Binnenweg with a
tin roof and I went there twice to do the drawings and both times the ground was under
water, and the man I saw in the May was the man I have described and I have not seen him
since, and I have said the ninth of March because I was there on the ninth and it was the
second time, and the shed had a stove in it and there were crates against the far wall
stacked four high, and there was a door on the Binnenweg side and a back stair, and I have
not thought about that room for eighteen months and I have no idea why I have just described
it to a court."

---

The hall did not make a sound.

Reiner had been in nine hundred rooms and had been in a great many courts and had never in
her life seen a room go the way that room went.

She had said it. She had said it in August, on a pavement in the rain, in a street off the
Binnenweg, to herself, with nobody listening, and then she had written it down in a
notebook she had not used for anything else and had then taken out of the notebook and put
in her head, and had said aloud, in a room over a tobacconist, *and if I forget it then I
have not earned it* — and a man who draws rooms for a living had described the same eleven
feet by eleven feet to a court on a Friday morning, unprompted, having been asked about a
man standing in a room, and had done it in order to make a small point about load.

"Mr Grens," said the assesseur, and his voice had changed and everybody heard it change.
"You have described the storeroom at Rotterdam."

"I have described a shed off the Binnenweg."

"You have described a door on the Binnenweg side and a back stair. In a shed."

"Yes."

"There is one storeroom on that field and it is nine feet by eleven and it has a door on
the Binnenweg side and a back stair. I have the *requête*. I read it on the Tuesday."

"Then I have described the right room," said Halvard Grens, "and I have no idea why, and I
would like to be allowed to say something that is going to sound very stupid."

"Madam, you may say anything you like."

"A man who draws rooms for a living," said Halvard Grens, "has a way of looking at one.
That is all this is. I have been in eleven thousand rooms and I know what is in a room and
I do not have to be told. The respondent performed in a room in Rotterdam in the spring
and the court has been saying *a room* for two days, and I have drawn a great many rooms
with a door on one side and a stair on the other, and the ones on that field are the ones
I have drawn."

"You went to that field in March."

"I went to that field in March. To draw. The ground was under water."

"On the ninth of March."

"On the ninth of March, and on the second, and the second was cancelled, and the ninth I
was there four hours and there was a man in the shed who was not the *salle*'s man and did
not give his name and I did not ask him because I do not ask, and I have not told anybody
about the ninth of March for eighteen months because it was a shed and a man and four
hours and nothing happened in it, and I do not know that anything happened in it."

---

The assesseur let it lie for a while. Reiner has never been certain whether he did that on
purpose.

"Mr Grens, I am going to say something and I want you to understand that it is not a
question. In nineteen years in this room I have had perhaps nine witnesses who have given me
more than they were asked for, and every one of them was trying to help, and every one of
them made it worse." He took his glasses off. "You have just described a room to me with
the doors in the wrong places, or the right places, and a stove, and four rows of crates
against the far wall, and a date, and the ninth of March, and you have done it because
somebody stood in a room nine feet by eleven and a man in this court is going to read
something about a door in a shed, and you have decided to be thorough."

"Yes," said Halvard Grens.

"You have decided to be thorough, and you have been thorough, and a careful man in a room
who is reading him has added eleven separate details to a record that was complete on the
Tuesday, and every one of them is true, and every one of them is new, and the court is
obliged to note that not one of them assists the defence."

"I did not think of that."

"No," said the assesseur. "You thought of load. That is what you said. You said it was a
matter of the attention carrying the trick and the trick only being the load. And then you
described a shed in March that nobody asked you about, in detail, including a door, and you
did it as a careful man describes a room he has been in."

He looked round the hall.

"Order," he said. "The witness will say nothing further about Rotterdam unless he is
asked. And I would like it noted that I have given that instruction because I have a very
strong suspicion that he cannot help it, and that this is what a careful man does, and that
I have watched it happen in this room twice in nineteen years, and both times the witness
was the best man in the building and both times he understood afterwards and not before,
and I do not believe either of them will ever understand it before next time."

# THEME

`artifacts/04-theme.md`. Phase 1 output.

**Rule held throughout: the theme is a question.** The moment it becomes a slogan the book
starts arguing with itself instead of asking. Everything below is a question, and the
answers that appear in the prose are characters' answers, not the book's.

---

## THE CENTRAL THEME

**Who is allowed to know the true version, and what does it cost to give it to them?**

That is the theme, and it is not a romance theme that happens to have a cave in it. It is
the question the Vell exists to ask. The cave does not care who was there. The cave
cannot keep a secret. **The entire problem of this book is that the one place where the
truth is physically available is the one place nobody can be got to say it out loud.**

---

## RESTATED, THE FOUR QUESTIONS

The theme resolves into four questions that the book asks in different registers. Each is
asked by a different character, and no character answers their own.

**1. Does documenting a thing make it understood?**
Ottoline's question. She has built an identity on the belief that the camera is an
instrument of understanding. The book tests it hard and never resolves it cleanly, because
the honest answer is *it is a different thing that has been mistaken for it, and the
mistake costs people something.*

**2. Can a person go back into the place that made them?**
Ines's question, and the one the descent physically answers forty times. Every chapter
underground is a partial answer and the book is careful never to let her get a complete
one until it is too late to use it.

**3. Is being watched the same as being known?**
The book's actual engine, and the reason the romance is not a romance novel with a job
attached. Ines will not be filmed and Ottoline cannot stop filming, and neither of them is
wrong. Two people are each doing the best available version of *seeing another person
properly* and they are mutually exclusive.

**4. What is owed to someone who is not in the room?**
Vera Marrow's question, and the reader's. Solveig is dead and is the most present person
in this book, and the book refuses the cheap route of making her a device. She is on tape
and she is funny on tape, and the cost of that is that she is *specific*, and specific
losses are the ones that do work on readers.

## SUBTHEMES

- **The instrument and its reader.** The gauge. The camera. The line. Each is a device for
  knowing a thing, and each can be read wrong by a competent person with every reason to be
  confident. This is the book's recurring technical grammar.
- **Consent as a professional practice.** Caving has a culture of going. Ottoline has a
  culture of filming. Both are ways of doing something to a person which that person has
  not agreed to, and both are built out of good reasons. The book never says the word
  *consent* and never needs to.
- **Being the one who goes first.** Tove's professional position, Ines's wound, and the
  literal mechanic of a tight cave. Who goes first on the line is the book's most reliable
  metaphor and it is always, in the scene, a real decision with a real cost.
- **The archive that is also a crime scene.** The 1994 film. Marisol's release of it.
  Ottoline's whole project. The material is evidence, and treating it as material is the
  professional thing and the unforgivable thing at the same time.
- **Funerary competence.** Everyone in this book is very good at working. Nobody in this
  book is good at grieving, and they are all using the skill they have instead.

## RECURRING SYMBOLS

Chosen so that each one is a *physical object in the world* and not a metaphor with a
costume on. Anything that could be lifted out of the plot and hung on a wall is not a
symbol and does not go in this list.

**The gauge.** Brass, hand-painted, nailed fifty metres in. Marked in hours since the last
known rise. Appears in chapter 3 (in passing, as a thing a character glances at), chapter
11 (legible), chapter 26 (in the 1994 film, and readable), chapter 33 (the reading that
breaks the book), chapter 38 (read correctly, by Hana Vogel, out loud, to nobody).
**Its last appearance is the last line of dialogue in the novel.** It is a number. That is
the whole horror of it and the book does not editorialise about it.

**The rack.** What Ines wears. A big aluminium thing that makes her a different silhouette.
She wears it underground and not above ground. The book tracks this without comment: the
first time she puts it on for the film it is a small event, and by chapter 34 putting it on
is unbearable and by chapter 41 it is a relief, and the reader is the only one who is
keeping track.

**The line.** Fifty metres of it, between them, for most of the book. Wool, in this
region, which means it is soft, which means you can hear nothing through it, which is why
they talk. The line is the only technology in this book that is better than it should be,
and it is the reason the romance is possible at all.

**The nine-minute video.** Solveig Havelock, in the back room of Renn & Daughters, on a
laptop, unviewed for four years. Introduced in chapter 2. Referenced in nine chapters.
Watched in chapter 43, in full, in front of witnesses. It is nine minutes and it is funny
and it is the cost of the book.

**The mother.** Vera Marrow. Appears only on screens and down telephones. **She is never
in the same room as Ines**, and the book ends with that still true. The prohibition is not
dramatic. It is the plain fact that these two women are each the person the other one
would have to become, and the book does not think that is survivable.

## WHAT THE THEME IS NOT

- **It is not about trauma.** The book is full of it and the theme is not it. Nothing here
  says *healing happens*. Ines does not heal, she discloses, and disclosure is not the
  same process and is not offered as one.
- **It is not about the cave.** The Vell is a machine for producing pressure, and the book
  is honest that this is what it is. It is never mystical, it never speaks, and nobody
  finds anything in it that was put there.
- **It is not about art vs. safety.** That opposition is on the table because Marisol
  believes in it, and the book's job is to dismantle it, not to adjudicate it.
- **It is not a redemption arc for a difficult woman.** Ines is not going to be a better
  person at the end. She is going to have said the sentence. That is a smaller and more
  useful thing, and it is what the book actually delivers.

## HOW IT IS CHECKED

Not by a gate. `check-uniformity.py` and `deslop-check.sh` measure shape and surface
pattern, and neither has an opinion on whether a question got asked or evaded. The only
instrument for this is a reader, which is the framework's standing position: a rule that
cannot be checked should not be written as though it were derived.

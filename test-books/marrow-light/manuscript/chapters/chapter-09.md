# Chapter Nine — Fifty Metres

She had done this walk eleven times since 2022 and the twelfth was the first one she had not
spent the whole of deciding to do.

"Radio check," said Tove, from the hill. "Say again."

"Varga. Check." Ines put the pack down at the top of the pitch, where the floor was flat and
dry and had been flat and dry since before either of them was born, and keyed the box.
"Descending."

The box on the surface was Tove's because Tove had the contract. The box on the line was
hers because there was one and it was twelve years old and she had put a new battery in it in
April and had not told anybody, including the person it was in her shed for.

The first twenty metres were a scramble, and then the roof came up, and then the passage did
what this passage did, which was go forward and slightly down and turn left at a bit of
flowstone that had been shaped like a thumb since before anybody had a name for it.

She did not go fast. She went at the speed of somebody who is not being watched by a person
who matters, which was slower than anybody would have expected and faster than she had done
it before.

The light went at about the forty-metre mark, which was correct, and the white came on, which
was a thing she had decided about in a car park in March and had not examined since, and the
white was a beam and a cone of dust and a wall eleven metres away.

The wall was where she had left it.

That was the sentence she had been not saying since March, and it came out of her standing
in a hole eleven metres from a wall, into a radio, to a woman she had not spoken to in four
years, and it was the first completely honest thing she had said about this since 2022.

"I've got it," said Tove. "Same?"

"Same."

"Depth is eleven metres and you're in the fourth."

"Third and a half, and the half's a lie, it's third and it's a bit." Ines sat down on a
ledge that was not a ledge and was a place where the floor happened to be, and turned the
light off, because the light off was for listening, and she was listening.

The water was four hours old and stable. The paper said four hours and stable. The paper was
right about the four hours because the paper was written by people who had been up there
looking, and it was right about *stable* because it had been stable for three days, and
stable was not the same as safe, and had never been the same as safe, and the gauge on the
wall fifty metres in said what it said.

She could go and look at it. It was eight minutes there and back and it was the sort of thing
that took eight minutes and that everybody did and that nobody wrote down.

She did not do it.

That was the second honest thing, and she gave that one to herself, on the ledge, in the
dark, fifty metres under a field, and it went nowhere at all.

She got up and went on, because there was a thing she had to look at in the fourth, and the
thing she had to look at was a station that had been in place since 2019 with a paint mark on
it that had been done wrong, and she had thought about that paint mark for two years and had
never once been able to work out what she was actually going to do about it.

It was a survey mark. A paint blob with a number. It was the least consequential object in
the county and she had thought about it in the dark for two years.

It was also, she understood, standing fifty metres under a hill with her light off, the
smallest version available of a thing she had been unable to do for four years, and that if
she could get a paint mark right then she could get a sentence right, and that this was a
ridiculous argument, and that it was the only one she had.

She got the paint.

She did it properly, which took eleven minutes, and she did it the way it should have been
done in 2019, and she said nothing about that to anyone, and while she was doing it she was
thinking about a woman of twenty-two who was going to ask her for things in about a month
and who would be right to, and who had no idea what she was being asked to be near, and who
had got the whole thing exactly right in a room four weeks ago when she had said *I will
knock every time and I'll wait* and had meant it, which nobody ever did.

The paint went on and was not a good paint job. It would not have passed. She looked at it
for a while and then did the second one, which was the actual one, the number, and got the
number right, and the number was 41, and 41 was Tove's station from 2019 and Tove had never
once mentioned it, and that was fine, and it was extremely fine.

She sat down for a bit.

Underground, at a hundred metres, on a Tuesday, the dark is not dark; it is a sort of
absence that has a floor in it, and the eye gives up on the walls within four or five minutes
and starts making them out of nothing, and what it makes them out of is generally worse than
what is there. She had seen four people panic in the first two hours of a first descent and
had never once seen anybody panic at two hundred metres, and the arithmetic of that had
never made any sense to her and had stopped occurring to her.

There was no dark down here. There was a Tuesday.

The radio clicked.

"Varga, you've been quiet eight minutes. Everything normal."

"Everything normal."

"Say again the last part."

"Everything's normal, Tove."

"You've said that four times now," said Tove, "and I'm not asking you to say more, I'm noting
that you said the same four words four times, which is either a professional habit or you
don't want to say what you actually want to say, and I know which one I'm doing, so I won't
push."

Ines sat on a rock at a hundred metres in a system she knew better than she knew her own
kitchen and put her hand on the box.

"Tove."

"Mm."

"You asked me to do this."

"I did ask you to do this."

"You can ask me again," said Ines, "and I'll say yes, and you should know what you're asking
before you ask, and you do know, so say it anyway."

"Yes," said Tove. "I do know. I want you underground for the film, and I want it on the
record that I asked a woman who doesn't go underground, and that she said yes, and that I
am not going to be one of the people who makes that sound like a favour I've done her."

"That wasn't what I was going to say."

"No. What were you going to say."

Ines was quiet for long enough that on the hill, Tove put her hand flat on the top of the
box and then took it off it again, in case.

"I was going to say thank you," said Ines.

"Fine," said Tove. "Ascend when you're ready. And don't thank me on the radio, I sound like
a lunatic on the radio and the hill's very exposed."

She ascended. It took twenty-eight minutes, which is slow, and it was slow on purpose, and
nobody was timing her.

The last twenty metres were the scramble, and the light came, and the sky came with it, and
there was a van on the grass with its door open and a man reading a book, and a woman standing
at the top of the slope with her arms folded who did not offer a hand and did not say
well done.

"Two hours ten," said Tove.

"Two hours ten."

"Station?"

"Station's done. It was done wrong in 2019, whoever did it. I've done the paint and I've
done the number. It's not a good job but it's a correct one."

"Noted," said Tove, and wrote it down, and did not look at Ines at all, and Ines understood
that this was a professional courtesy and not coldness and was grateful for it in a way that
she had not been grateful for anything from that woman in four years.

Petar had made tea on the bonnet and had made enough for three, and gave it to Ines without
comment and without looking at her, which was also a professional courtesy, and was, Ines
thought, taking the van to the layby a kilometre back where you could get a signal if you
pointed it at a hill.

The sky was doing nothing. It had promised nothing for four hours and had delivered nothing
and had been, in the whole of its conduct, entirely reliable.

Tove packed the van and did not say anything about going down again, and Ines did not say
anything about going down again, and they got in the van, and the van went up the track, and
the hole in the field got smaller and then went behind a hedge, and Ines watched it go and
did not say anything at all about it, and neither did anybody else, because there was nothing
to say, because nothing had happened.

It was, by a distance, the best day she had had in four years, and she told nobody that,
and she thought about it for two days, and then she stopped thinking about it, which was the
correct thing to do and cost her something.

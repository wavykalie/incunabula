# Chapter Six — Renn and Daughters

Nothing happened on the Thursday, which was the point of it, and it is a mistake to think
that a chapter where nothing happens is a chapter with nothing in it.

The probe came back on a Thursday. It had gone out on the Monday to a group doing a
training weekend in the western caves, and it came back on the Thursday, and it came back
with a fault on the third reading, and Ines spent four minutes on the phone with a man
called Euan about the third reading, and the fault was a connector that had been in the
machine since before either of them worked there, and they had a nice conversation about it,
and neither of them mentioned the third reading again.

That was the whole of the Thursday.

Ines did the invoice. There was a system, and the system was that anything over two hundred
pounds went on the left-hand page in pencil until it cleared, which was a practice Fabiola
had started in about 2003 as a joke and which had outlived a recession and two suppliers
and which was now simply how money was, and Ines did the left-hand page while the rain came
and went and came again.

The rain on that roof was a sound Ines could have identified from a recording, and she
supposed she could identify most things from a recording, and she had never once found that
useful.

At about half past two a woman came in with a helmet.

It was a good helmet, and it was the right helmet, and it was also nine years old, and Ines
knew the age of it because there was a sticker inside the shell with a date on it and the
woman had it turned outwards, which meant she had never once looked at it.

"That's a nice bit of kit," Ines said.

"Thank you."

"When did you buy it?"

"About nine years ago."

"Do you know? People think they do and they don't."

"No," said the woman. "I didn't think about it."

"You've had it nine years and you think it's fine."

"I've had it nine years and it's never come off my head by itself," said the woman, "which
is what I'd have said if I'd thought about it, and I'd have been wrong, and you've made me
think about it, and now I'm going to have to buy a new one, and I'd like to complain about
that for a moment before I do it."

"You've made me think about it," said Ines, and went and got a new one out of the back, and
did not charge her for the fitting, and did not take the old one off her, and the woman
noticed that, and said thank you again, differently.

Fabiola watched the whole thing from the screen.

"You're giving her the fitting."

"It's four minutes."

"It's four minutes of your time and it's the only part of this job that isn't the shop," said
Fabiola, "and I want it noted that I've noticed."

"Noted."

The woman left. The door did its bell.

At four o'clock Ines made the tea, and this was a Thursday, and the kettle in the stockroom
had been on at half past four every working day since 2003, and she made two cups, and
brought them out, and put one on the counter by the screen, and Fabiola took it without
looking up, which was how they had done it since about 2011.

They drank it.

"There's a girl coming Friday," said Fabiola.

"I know."

"She's booked the survey."

"Two hours."

"She asked for four."

"Then she'll have two."

"Mm." Fabiola took a sip. "She asked for a name. Not a shop name. A name."

"That's what I said I'd be."

"You said you'd be a consultant. You didn't say which one." Fabiola put the cup down.
"Which one are you going to be."

"That's a strange question."

"It's a strange question," Fabiola agreed. "I'm asking it because you gave that girl a
rate in a stockroom on a Tuesday and you gave her *four* on Wednesday, and I heard you,
and you thought I hadn't, and I want you to know that I heard you and that I'm not going to
do anything with it, because I don't do anything with things. I just want to know which one
you're going to be, because I'm sixty-one and I run a shop and I need to know what to write
on the board."

Ines looked at the counter, which was the colour of a bone, and had been the colour of a
bone since 2015 when Fabiola had had it re-finished and had been quoted a warmer shade and
had said no to the warmer shade.

"I'm going to be the one she hired," she said.

"Right," said Fabiola.

"There's no other one."

"There's always another one," said Fabiola. "There's always the one you were before, and
she's not paying for that one, and that's not a criticism, it's just an invoice."

They drank the tea. The rain did what it does. Somebody went past outside with a dog and the
dog was pleased about something.

"There was a delivery," said Fabiola, "in March. Twenty-second."

Ines did not look up.

"You signed for it."

"I signed for it."

"You came in on the Tuesday and you bought two cylinders and a rack and I said the rack was
over the top and you said it was for a survey, and I said good, and I said *good*." Fabiola
had both hands around the cup and was not drinking from it. "You said it was for a survey
and you said it was for a survey and I let you say it twice, and the second time is the one I
have been thinking about, because the first time you're working and the second time you're
telling me a thing."

"Fabiola—"

"I'm not saying anything," said Fabiola. "I've said it. That's all. There's nothing on the
end of it. You don't have to do anything with it and there's nothing you have to say, and
I'm not going to bring it up again, and I want you to hear the last part of it now while
it's still just a fact and not a thing you've had time to get used to."

Ines stood at the counter with the cloth in her hand and did not move for about eleven
seconds, which she knew because the wall clock above the door does not tick but does
something every second, and she had spent nineteen years learning to hear it and had never
once been able to stop hearing it.

"All right," she said.

"Good," said Fabiola, and drank the tea at last, and found it cold, and put it down, and
said, "Right, it's cold," in the voice of a woman reporting a finding.

They closed at half five, which was early for a Thursday, and it was early because the
Thursday had been the fourth quiet day in a row and because Fabiola had decided at four
o'clock that she was going to close and had not said so out loud, and Ines had put the
cloth away without being asked, which is a thing that happens in a shop where two people have
never once had to ask each other.

Ines got her coat. It was on the hook, and it was the one from the contract, and she had
worn it in March and had not worn it since, and it still smelled faintly of the stockroom.

"Fab."

"Mm."

"That delivery on the twenty-second of March."

"I know what day it was," said Fabiola, and did not turn round, and was looking at the
screen, and had been looking at the screen for some time, and the screen was off.

Fabiola had the shop for another four minutes after that. She did not put the light on at
the back. She did not check the till, which she checked every night and had checked that
night already at four, before the tea, out of habit.

She sat behind the counter with the lights off for four minutes in a shop that had been her
husband's and then hers and then theirs, and on the wall behind her was a brass plate with
numbers painted on it in white by a hand that had not been steady, and she looked at it for
a while, and then she got up and did the thing she had been not doing since 2022, which was
nothing, because there was nothing to be done about a brass plate, and she turned the lights
off at the switch by the door and went out into the rain.

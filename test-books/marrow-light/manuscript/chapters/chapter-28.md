# Chapter Twenty-Eight — The Reading

What Hana Vogel said at six minutes past two on the nineteenth of November was this.

"Varga to surface. Varga to surface, confirm you're at eighty."

"Eighty-one. Confirm."

"Copy. Come up. Not at the next one. Now, and the line comes back with you, and I'm not
arguing about it on a frequency."

That was the whole of the alarm. That was the entire change in the professional situation,
and it was delivered in eleven words in a flat voice by a rescue technician on a hillside
above a shut gate, and it was not a shout, and it was not a decision made in a mood, and it
was the single most frightening moment in the book.

*Not at the next one. Now.*

Ines Varga was eighty-one metres down in a system she had been in eleven times in nine weeks
in 2022, and a woman she had not spoken to about anything since the sixth of November was
telling her to bring the line back up, and the professional part of her — the part that had
made her the best cave mind in the county, the part that Tove had described in a room in
October as *she was right more often than anybody I've worked with* — did not weigh it at
all.

"Copy," she said. "Coming up."

She was at the top of the pitch in nineteen minutes, which is fast, and which is not a thing
you can do at eighty-one metres with a rack on, and which she did.

---

Here is what they established, in order, and the order matters, because the whole book is
about the order.

They established it on a field above a shut gate with the rain coming back and a van in a
car park four hundred metres below with water in it, and it took one hour and fifty minutes,
and at no point during that hour and fifty minutes did anybody raise their voice, and at no
point did anybody say the word that they were all thinking, and at the end of it the thing
had been said by a person who had no reason to say it and by a person who had every reason
not to.

**The first thing was the number.**

Hana Vogel had read the plate four times since the fourth of November, at the top of the
pitch, in daylight, on the days she had gone in on a solo check, which was the first time in
twenty-two months that she had gone into the Vell alone, and which she had done because there
was a plate and a plate is a reason.

It said twenty-two. It had said twenty-two on the fourth of November and it said twenty-two
on the nineteenth, and the number was painted in a white that had been the same white in
1994 and in 2019, and the paint was thin at the edges of both digits, which meant it was old,
which meant it had not been done in fifteen days.

"Twenty-two hours," said Hana. "Last rise was the fourth. That plate's been wrong for fifteen
days and nobody's been in here to fix it, and the system's had a rise in it that we didn't
know about and hasn't been painted."

"Then we don't know when the last rise was," said Tove.

"We know it was *more* than twenty-two hours ago," said Hana. "That's the direction of the
error and it's the only direction I've ever needed. A plate reads hours since the last rise.
If it's stale, the water has been up for *longer* than it says. It's never the other way.
Nobody paints a gauge *early*."

"Can it be that there hasn't been a rise since the fourth?"

"Hana."

"I know. I know it's the thing I want." Hana was crouched on the grass with a hood up. "The
catchment went up nineteen millimetres at one o'clock. It rained hard. There was a rise. The
plate's stale, which means there's been more than one."

**The second thing was the paint.**

Tove Marsk went into the fourth chamber on the twentieth, with Hana on the line, at
fourteen metres past the second pitch, which is past the cap that Marisol Trent had imposed
and which Tove Marsk had broken in writing, over the telephone, the previous evening, in
four sentences that she had read to Marisol twice before sending.

The plate in the fourth chamber was brass and it was on the wall and it had a number on it,
and the number was 22, and it was in the same paint as the one at the top of the pitch.

And it had four numbers under the current one.

That is the thing nobody in this county had ever reported, and it is not in the third
edition, and it is not in the incident report, and it is visible from a metre away to anybody
who goes into the fourth chamber and looks at a wall, and nobody had gone into the fourth
chamber since 2022.

Four numbers, painted in the same hand, in the same white, underneath the current one, with
the previous number struck through and the new number above it. Not side by side. *Above.* The
most recent one is on top, and it is the one you read, and the ones underneath are only
visible if you crouch.

There are four of them.

"Four strikes," said Tove, into a radio, at a metre from a wall, at fourteen metres below the
second pitch, on the twentieth of November, at ten past nine in the morning. "Four strikes. Not
one, not two, not eleven. Four, and the last one's the 22, and the three underneath are 19,
16, and 14."

"Read them again."

"Twenty-two, nineteen, sixteen, fourteen. All hours. All the same paint."

"Where are they in the wall?"

"That's the thing." Tove was not moving away from the wall. "They're in the wall. Same height.
Same hand. Same brush. Somebody has come in here and painted a new number over the old one
four times, in order, each one higher than the last, and each one *crossed out*, and the
crossing-out is the same hand and the same paint, which means whoever did this crossed the
old one out and painted the new one on top of it in the same trip."

**The third thing was the interval.**

"Nineteen, sixteen, fourteen, twenty-two," said Hana, on the frequency, at ten past ten on the
twentieth. "That's a rise, then four hours, then two, then eight. That is not a cycle; that is
four separate times somebody has come in and written down what the plate said and got a
different number each time."

Tove said, "Or somebody has come in four times and painted the plate without reading it."

"And struck out the old number."

"And struck out the old number."

"Which nobody does."

"Which nobody does," said Hana.

Nobody said anything on the frequency for about nine seconds.

"Say the thing," said Tove, from fourteen metres below the second pitch. "Say the thing
you're about to say, Hana, because I've been standing in front of this wall for an hour and
somebody's got to say it and I'd rather it was a person with a radio than a report."

And Hana Vogel, who was twenty-nine, and who had been on the Vell call in 2022, and who had
reached Solveig Havelock eleven minutes after the roof stopped moving, and who had spent four
years being the last voice on the telephone for a woman who would not take a call, said:

"I said the number went off by one after rain. That's not mine. That's Vera Marrow's, in an
index, in 1995, in a store room. *Check every trip. The number goes off by one after rain and
I have never found out why.*"

"Twenty-two, nineteen, sixteen, fourteen."

"That's four trips, Hana."

"That's four trips and four *repaintings*, and the repainting is the point." Hana was
speaking very fast now, in the flat register. "You repaint a plate when you've been. And you
don't repaint it to record — you repaint it because the paint's gone, because it's eleven
months since the last trip and it's illegible, and you're on your way past and you do it
because that's what you do. And you don't strike the old one out. You don't get a brush out
for that. You paint over it."

"And whoever did that struck the old ones out."

"Whoever did that struck three old numbers out and then painted a fourth number over the
fourth old one, and did all of it in one trip, and the intervals are a rise, four hours, two
hours, eight hours." Hana's voice did not change. "Tove. Read the *nineteen* again."

"Twenty-two, nineteen, sixteen, fourteen."

"Which of those is the current one?"

"Twenty-two."

"Which is the second most recent?"

"Nineteen."

"And the *fourth*?"

"Fourteen."

"And there's a number under the fourteen."

"Yes."

"How many?"

Tove Marsk crouched on a cave floor at fourteen metres below the second pitch and looked at
the bottom of a brass plate that had been painted in white by hand by a competent person over
a period of some years, and which had a number on it that was a lie, and which was the only
instrument in the county that could have told anybody what was happening in a system that had
killed somebody, and which was *maintained by the person it was about*.

"There's another one under it," she said. "There's a fifth. It's mostly gone. You can only
see the top of it."

"Read it."

"I can't read it."

"Tove."

"You can see the top of a stroke," said Tove. "You can see there was a number. I can't tell
you what it was."

Hana Vogel sat down in a field above a shut gate in the rain with a radio in her hand.

"There were five numbers," she said, "and the last four are 14, 16, 19, 22, and the fifth is
gone, and the intervals are two hours, three hours, three hours, and before that whatever the
gaps were, and the reason you repaint a plate is that the last time you were in there the
water was a *different number* from this time, and the number's *rising*."

"Or it's a dirty plate and a bad painter."

"Or it's a dirty plate and a bad painter," Tove agreed, on the radio, from fourteen metres
down, "and I've been looking at this wall for an hour and I want to say that the paint is
good and the hand is steady and it is not a dirty plate. Whoever painted that cared about it.
Whoever painted that was doing a job they took seriously, and the numbers went up, and they
went up and up, and they stopped being a record of anything except a person going in more
often than the water was going down."

---

Fabiola Renn arrived at twenty past eleven on the twentieth of November.

She had been forty minutes behind them on the nineteenth and had driven at a speed that
Petar had described, later, as *frightening in a completely ordinary way*, and she had not
gone home, and she had spent the night in the van, and she had come back the following
morning with a folder.


She came up the field in the rain at twenty past eleven with a folder under her arm and a
flask in the other hand and did not go to the top of the pitch, because she was sixty-one and
she had not been on a working line in nineteen years and she knew exactly what she was.

"Fab."

"Fabiola."

"You're on the field."

"I'm on the field," said Fabiola, "and I have been on a field at eight o'clock this morning
and I am not going to the pitch, and don't offer, because I've got a folder and a flask and if
I take them up there I'll be on that hill until dark and there's a woman on a line who needs
the paperwork more than she needs me."

She put the flask down and opened the folder on the bonnet of Petar's van.

It was March sheets. It was every March sheet the shop had kept for four years, and it was
kept because of a decision made in October by a woman who had decided not to bin anything
from that month, and it was held together with a clip, and it was dry, because Fabiola Renn
had brought it in a plastic bag from a shop eleven kilometres away in a car in the rain.

"Look at the delivery dates," she said.

Hana Vogel got out of the van. Tove came up the field from the top of the pitch at a speed
that was not quite a run and was not quite a walk and that both of them had seen before.

"Twenty-second of March," said Fabiola. "Twenty-ninth. Fifth of April. Twelfth. Nineteenth.
Twenty-sixth. Third of May. Tenth." She turned the pages. "That's eight. Two cylinders and a
rack every week, in March and April and May, and every one of them paid for out of the shop's
own account and not invoiced to anybody, because nobody is invoiced for their own cylinders,
and every one of them signed by her and not countersigned, because there was nobody to
countersign them."

"That's kit," said Hana. "That's buying kit."

"That's cylinders," said Fabiola, and turned round, and her voice did not change at all,
because she had practised it in the van on the way up the field, because she had known what
she was going to say since about half past six that morning on the second day of doing this.
"A cylinder pair is nine or ten days. That's what they're rated for. That's not an opinion,
it's on the tin, and I've been selling them for forty-one years and I've never once sold
anybody eleven pairs in nine weeks and had them turn up full."

The rain came down on four people and a folder on the bonnet of a van on a hillside above a
hole in a field.

"Say the number," said Tove Marsk.

"No."

"Fabiola."

"No, I know, I do, I've known since March of last year when a young woman sat in my shop and
told me what she'd read, and I've had this folder in the office since the twenty-second of
March and I have not taken it out, not once, not for anybody, not for Tove, not for Hana, and
I did not take it out last night and I have not taken it out this morning and I have been
sitting in a car for twenty minutes deciding whether to bring it up a hill in the rain." Her
hands were flat on the bonnet on either side of the folder. "And I've brought it up a hill in
the rain, and that's the end of that, and I'm not saying it."

"Fab—"

"I'm not saying it because I said it already," said Fabiola Renn. "In a shop, to Hana, on the
fifth of November, and I said *eleven*, and I said it out loud for the first time in four
years, and there's a note of it in her log, and if you want to know what I said then you can
ask her, and if you ask her she'll tell you, because that's what she's for."

Hana Vogel did not say anything at all.

"I'm not going to say a number," said Fabiola, "and there is no number on this hill, and there
is not going to be a number in that chamber tonight, and I'll tell you why. Because a number's
a *record*. A record says *a woman went down eleven times in nine weeks*, and that's a thing
about a woman, and a woman is a person, and I sold her those cylinders out of my shop with my
own hands and I stamped the tickets and I put them in a bag and I said *mind the water on the
road*."

She closed the folder.

"The other number's a *gauge*," she said. "And a gauge is a different kind of record, and
nobody reads a gauge, they read the person who painted it, and she was the person who painted
it, and she'll be the person who paints it next, and if there's a number on that wall in ten
years it'll be a number that's *been kept*, and that's all anybody's entitled to know."

She picked up the flask.

"Now," she said. "Who's got a cup."

---

Tove Marsk went back down the pitch at half past twelve on the twentieth of November, with
Hana on the line, and she went to the fourth chamber, which was four metres past the cap, and
she stood in front of a brass plate with five numbers on it and did not photograph it.

She had brought a camera. It was in a bag on her shoulder and she had carried it down forty
metres of pitch and across a chamber and she took it out and looked at it and put it back.

"You've got a phone," said Hana, on the frequency.

"I've got a camera."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing at all, it's a camera, and if I take a picture of that plate then
that plate becomes a piece of evidence in a film, and the film is in a layby four hundred
metres away with a woman in it who has been told not to take anything out of that building."
Tove stood in front of the wall. "I don't know what the number is. I've been looking at it for
three hours. I know that there's a fifth number under the other four and I can see the top of
a stroke. I cannot tell you what it was, and I would not put a guess on a form, and I would
not put a guess in a film."

"So it's just a wall."

"It's a wall that somebody kept, badly, for four years, on their own time, going past
without anything to carry and stopping to paint it." Tove's voice was not doing anything
particular at all. "Hana, have you got the times from yesterday logged?"

"Twenty-two, at 1416."

"Read the log back to me."

Hana read it back to her.

"And yesterday at 1404 Tove confirmed it as hers, and Varga confirmed it at 1416, and I read
it aloud, and there were three people on an open frequency who said the word out loud." Tove
was not moving away from the wall. "That's the last time that plate was read. Yesterday. And
nobody painted it, and the number's still twenty-two, and the water's coming."

"Vega says—"

"I know what Vega says. I have a radio." Tove took a breath. "Come round, and have a look at
this, and then tell me what you see, and I want you to say it in words and not in a number,
and I want you to say it before we go anywhere near that wall, and I want you to say it out
loud, and I want you to say it whether or not it's the nice thing."

There was a pause of about nine seconds on a radio in a chamber fourteen metres below the
second pitch of a system that had killed somebody.

"Top right," said Hana Vogel. "Second digit."

"Hana—"

"It's two," said Hana. "The second digit's a two and the first one's a two, and the paint's
gone round the base of the first one, which is what happens to a one, because one's got no
foot. And if the first one's a one then it's not twenty-two, it's *twelve*, and if it's twelve
then the last rise was twelve hours ago, and the last rise was at about two o'clock yesterday
morning, and the rain came in at eleven, and that's three hours and a *rise* in nine hours,
and I read it yesterday at a quarter past two and I read it *stale* because I assumed
nobody had been in, and I was looking at a number that was nine hours old in a system where
the water comes up in ninety minutes."

Nobody on the frequency said anything for about eleven seconds.

"Twelve," said Tove Marsk.

"Twelve."

"Then it's *current*," said Tove. "It was current yesterday. It's been current since
yesterday, and nobody painted it, because nobody's been in, and the person who paints it has
not been in since the fourth of November, and the person who paints it is on a line eighty
metres down as we speak."

And on a field above a shut gate, Ines Varga said, into a radio, in a voice that four people
who loved her in four different ways had never heard before:

"No," she said.

"Varga—"

"No, Tove. Sit down. Both of you sit down. I'm coming up and I'm bringing the line and we're
going to talk about this on the surface, in daylight, with all four of us in a car, because
it is *my* plate and it is *my* wall and I have been going under it eleven times in nine
weeks since the fourth of November and every single time I have read it as twenty-two and
every single time it was twelve, and I have never once thought about the difference, because
twelve and twenty-two are both a number and a number is a number and that is exactly what a
number is *for*."

There was a silence on the frequency.

"Say the rest of it," said Hana, quietly.

"I have to know what the fifth number is," said Ines. "The one underneath. Because if there's
a fifth one, then I was down here when she painted it, and I didn't know, and she didn't say,
and neither of us said, and that is the whole of it. That is the entire thing that has
happened in this system since 2022, and it is *silence*, and it has been silence the entire
time."

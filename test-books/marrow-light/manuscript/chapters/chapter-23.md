# Chapter Twenty-Three — After

It was raining on the first of November and it had been raining for nine days, which in that
part of the country in that month was not weather so much as a condition, and the shop got
four customers between nine and one, and three of them were on the phone for longer than they
were in the shop.

Tove Marsk had never been to Renn and Daughters.

That was the first thing Ines established, in the first eleven seconds, and she established
it by looking at the way Tove came in, which was the way a person comes into a room they have
not been in, which is a half-second of reading the whole space and then not admitting to
having done it.

"Fabiola."

"Mm." Fabiola did not get up. "Tove."

"You know each other."

"We've known each other nineteen years and she has never once come in," said Fabiola, "and
I've never asked her why, and I have a very good idea why and it's that she doesn't like
shops."

"I don't like shops," Tove said. "I like this shop. I like this shop and I haven't been in it
in four years and that's my business."

"It is your business," said Fabiola, and went back to her screen, and left the two of them
standing there, which was a thing she had started doing in October.

Ines put a coil of line down on the counter.

"You're not here about line."

"No," said Tove.

"Then you're not here about a price either, because you never once in your life have haggled
with me and I have never once got the better of it."

"I'm here about the fourth edition."

That was a thing nobody had said in that shop for four years and it landed on the bone-coloured
counter like a dropped plate, and neither woman who was in the room reacted, and Fabiola, who
was not in the room properly, kept her eyes on her screen and turned her chair about twenty
degrees, which she had never done in nineteen years.

"The fourth edition's not funded until twenty-seven," said Ines.

"I know what the fourth edition's funded on. I've written the funding application, I have
the date in a diary, and I've had it in a diary since January." Tove put her bag down on the
floor, which nobody did in a shop. "I'm not here about the fourth edition. I'm here because
I asked a young woman to do four hours of work and she did it and got nothing, and I've been
thinking about that for a fortnight, and I want to ask you a question, and I want to ask it
once, and I would like you to know before I ask it that I've been working out how to ask it
for about eleven days."

"Then ask it."

"Have you read it?"

"Read what."

"The report."

"I read it in September of 2022," said Ines, "on the Thursday, and I have read it about
forty times since, and I have read the section four wording more than that, and I could tell
you what page it's on."

It was out of her mouth before she had decided to say it, which had happened to her about
four times in fifty years, and which Tove Marsk received without any visible reaction
whatsoever, and which was the single most useful thing that happened in this chapter, because
Ines Varga had just told a professional that she had read a four-page document about her own
partner's death about forty times, and had told her because she had been asked a plain
question and had answered it, and the plainness of it was the whole event.

Tove did not flinch and did not soften and did not say *I'm sorry*.

"That's not what I asked," she said.

"What did you ask."

"I asked whether you'd read it *carefully*." Tove did not move. "There's a difference and
it's the difference between a document and a thing you've made yourself carry, and nearly
everybody reads a report like that carefully once and then stops, because the reading's
finished — the reading's finished on the first afternoon, you know what happened, and then
you're carrying a folder."

"That's not a stupid distinction."

"No. It's the only one I've got." Tove put one hand on the counter, flat, the way she put
her hands on a table. "Because I have read it forty-one times, and I have never once got
anything new out of it, and I have thought about that every single evening for two years, and
the reason I have never got anything new out of it is that I'm not reading it. I'm visiting
it. And you said forty, which is the same problem, and you said it in about four seconds
without deciding to, and I want you to know that I'm not going to pretend I didn't notice
that."

There was a door out of this. She could see three of them, from where she was standing, and
she was aware of all three at once, and two of them were lies.

"Can I say why I read it forty times?"

"You can say whatever you like. It'll be in a folder either way."

"That's a good sentence," said Ines.

"It's Tove's sentence," said Fabiola, from behind her screen, at volume, and it was not
clear whether she was joking.

Ines did not say why. She said, instead, "Why are you really here, Tove?"

"I'm really here because I've been asked to put together a survey plan for the nineteenth of
November and there's a line of fifty metres and it needs a name at the surface end, and I've
got Hana, and I've got you, and Marisol's certificate says Hana and you, and it's correct,
and I've been sitting in a room for eleven days trying to work out how to tell you that I'm
not going to be on it."

"You offered to be on it," said Ines. "You offered in October and it was a good offer and I
said yes on the telephone before you'd finished."

"And I withdrew it. That's the part I've been sitting with for eleven days, and I didn't
withdraw it in a way that was honest, so let me do the honest one."

"Right."

"I'm not withdrawing it because I think you shouldn't go underground. I think you can go
underground, you're the best in the county and I'd trust you with my own survey. I'm
withdrawing it because in September I sat in a room with a young woman and I asked her a
question to find out whether she was building a story about you, and she answered me
honestly, and I have been carrying that answer for six weeks and it's made me notice that I
don't know a single thing about you that isn't in a folder or on tape."

"That's not—"

"And I'm not being sentimental about it. That's the thing I want to be very clear about,
because I know what I sound like at forty-five past ten on a wet Saturday in a shop, and I
sound like a person having a feeling." Tove's voice did not change at all. "I'm doing it
because in 2022 there were two people on that line and one of them is dead, and the
question I have been unable to ask anybody for two years is a technical one, and I have been
asking it to a document, and the document isn't answering, and I have read it forty-one
times, and yesterday I worked out that the reason I read it forty-one times is that it's the
only version of you that doesn't make me the person who was wrong."

Nobody in the shop said anything for some time.

Then Fabiola Renn said, "I'll make a cup of tea," in a voice that neither of them had ever
heard her use, and got up, and went into the back, and shut the door, which she never did, and
which this was the fifth time in nine weeks, and which had stopped being a thing about tea.

"That's the third time since September," said Ines.

"Yes."

"What's the third time for?"

"I don't know," said Fabiola, from the back, and there was a noise of a kettle, and then her
voice again, further away, and clearly not talking to either of them. "I've no idea. Mind the
step, it's a different step."

Tove Marsk did not look at the door. She stood at a counter in a shop she had never been in
and looked at a woman she had known for sixteen years and said, "I'm asking you a question
that isn't fair and I'm going to ask it anyway, and you can tell me to get out and I'll get
out and I'll still do the survey."

"Ask it."

"When you were in the fourth chamber in 2022, why were you in it?"

And Ines Varga stood behind a counter in a shop and was asked, by the second person in six
weeks, the question she had known was coming since the first hour of a two-hour descent on the
ninth of October, and she had a sentence ready — she had had one ready since 2022, and it
was true, and it was the sentence she gave everybody, and it was the sentence she had given
to Marisol Trent in a doorway above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings, and it was a
sentence about the roof moving and about a second person at risk, and it was true, and it was
nowhere near the answer.

"Because the roof was moving," said Ines.

"Right," said Tove.

"And because entry would have put a second person at risk."

"Right," said Tove again.

"That doesn't get me off the hook, does it."

"No," said Tove. "It clears you of the thing I came in to ask you about, which was whether
you'd be stupid about a roof, and you'd never been stupid about a roof in your life, and I
had that the wrong way round in a folder for two years." She took her bag off the floor. "The
rest of it isn't mine. I know that now. I've been trying to get it out of a piece of paper
and it's not in the paper, and that's not a failure of the paper."

"What is it then."

And Tove Marsk said, "It's hers," and meant Hana, and put her bag on her shoulder, and did
not elaborate, and went out into the rain, because she had said the thing that took her eleven
days and she was not going to stand in a shop and have four more.

The door did its bell.

Fabiola came back in with three cups on a tray and there were two women in the shop.

"She's gone?"

"She's gone."

Fabiola put the tray down. She looked at the two cups, and then at the third, and then put
the third back on the tray.

"I'll have it," she said, and drank it standing up, at the counter, at half past ten on a
Saturday morning in November, and Ines Varga stood there and looked at her, and neither of
them said anything about the door, or the bell, or the fifth time.

"You were straight with her," said Fabiola.

"Yes."

"You've never once been straight with anybody in this shop in four years." Fabiola rinsed her
cup under the tap in the back, which she never did while anybody was in the room. "I don't
mind. I want to say that I don't mind. I want to say it before you tell me the other half of
it, because I know what the other half is."

"What's the other half."

"That you were straight with a woman who's got a document about you in her bag and hasn't read
it since 2019," said Fabiola, "and you were straight because you knew she was going to leave
it to Hana, and you let her get away with it because it wasn't your week." She shut the tap.
"I've been in this shop for forty-one years, love. I know when someone's letting me off."

# Chapter One — Buying the Wrong Thing

She had watched him lace up wrong for four minutes and said nothing for three of them,
and she was not being kind.

He was a big man, which she had expected from the shoes, and he had a car outside with a
rack on the roof that she had clocked when he came in, so she had already decided he knew
what he was buying and had come here for the fitting. That was the whole of her allowance
for him, four minutes of silence, and she had spent it.

"Leg loop's backwards."

He looked down.

"Oh," he said.

"It's not fatal."

"It's a bit fatal," he said, and got a hand under it and turned it round, and it was, at
that point, more or less right, and he took his hands off it and stood there in the middle
of the shop with his arms out slightly from his sides like a man waiting to be examined.

"That'll do," she said. "Buckle's on the wrong side too."

"No, it's not."

"Yes, it is. That's a left-hand buckle. You've got it on your left."

He looked at his left hand. He had the buckle on his left hand. He turned it round.

"Right," he said. "Sorry."

"Third clip from the top wants a full twist, not a half. If you half it, it walks."

"Walks where?"

"Everywhere," she said. "That's the point of it."

She went and got the tape off the counter and ran her thumb along the waist belt where the
stitching doubled back on itself, because she could not help it, and the stitching was
fine, and she had known the stitching would be fine, and she put the tape down.

Behind her, Fabiola said, "It's the buckle he's paying for."

"I know which part he's paying for."

"You're inspecting it like it's got a disease."

"It hasn't got a disease."

"Everything in here has got a disease," Fabiola said, and went back to her screen, and
that was the end of it, and it was also the whole of the way the two of them had spoken to
each other for nineteen years.

The man on the floor was called Colin and he had a laminated list, which he put down on the
counter, and it was the reason he was in the shop and the reason he was going to die in a
hole in six months, and none of that was on the list. The list had tick boxes. She read it
the way she read everything, which was quickly and once.

"You've not put a helmet on the list."

"I've got a helmet."

"Have you tried it on."

"I've had it two years."

"Try it on."

"It fits."

"It fits because you haven't tried it on with a head," she said, and he laughed, and it was
a good laugh, and she found she did not want him to laugh because the laugh meant he was
going to go underground and find out.

That was the part she had not been being kind about.

She had spent three of those four minutes watching a man set up to be killed by a piece of
equipment she had sold him, and she had not said anything, because saying something makes
it her business. Her business was the shop. The other thing was a thing she had not had
since 2022, and it was not on the list, and it did not have a laminated copy.

"The helmet," she said. "Try it on."

He tried it on. It was two sizes too big and it came down over his eyes when he nodded, and
he said, "That's fine, that's fine," with the helmet already making his voice smaller and
deeper, which is a thing helmets do and which people find reassuring and should not.

"Shell's the wrong shape for you, too," she said. "You've got a round head and that's an
oval."

"They don't come in round."

"They do," said Fabiola, from the back. "They've come in round since about 2011."

"You'll want the smaller one."

"This one's the one in the picture."

"Yeah," she said. "It's the one in the picture."

She sold him the smaller one. That was the part she would think about later, and it was not
the part she expected to think about: she did not think about the four minutes. She thought
about the eleven pounds.

He paid. He took the bag. He said, "Thanks very much," twice, at the door and then in the
street, and she said, "Mind the water on the road," which was true and was not what she
meant, and he went out into the rain and got into the car and drove off at a speed that
was not a good idea on that road.

Fabiola counted the day into the till and made a noise.

"You gave him the small one."

"He had the wrong one on."

"You gave him the small one and he was in here for half an hour on a Tuesday, and you know
what Colin's group does."

"I know what Colin's group does."

"He'll have eleven of them."

"Twelve," said Fabiola. "There's a new one."

The rain was doing what it does, which is nothing you can work at. Ines put the tape back
in the drawer and put the drawer back in the counter and stood for a moment with her hands
on the front of the bench, which she had done four thousand times, and the surface of it
was the colour of a bone.

On the wall behind the counter, at eye height, there was a brass plate about the size of a
bookplate with numbers painted on it in white, by hand, in a slightly unsteady hand. It
was fifty metres into the only entrance anybody had, and it was nailed to a wall, and it
was not a decoration and nobody in the shop had ever once looked at it.

"The gauge," said Fabiola, without turning round. "You'd think it was a clock in here."

"It's not a clock."

"It is a clock. It's a clock that somebody's aunt painted."

"It's a record of the last rise."

"It's an old woman with a brush," said Fabiola, "and I have never in my life met anybody
who was more worried about the correct number."

The door went again.

She did not turn round, and neither of them did, and the bell above the door was the one
that had not worked properly since 2014 and rang anyway, out of habit, in a way that
Ines had stopped hearing and would now, for the rest of the day, hear very clearly.

"Fabiola, if it's Colin with a question about the helmet I am going to—"

The young woman in the doorway was holding a piece of paper in a plastic folder and she had
the particular stillness of somebody who has arrived four minutes early and has been
standing there for two of them. Rain on her shoulders. Hair that had given up around the
temples. Twenty-two, maybe. There was a film-school coat over a shirt that had been ironed
at some point today and would not survive the hour.

She said, "Sorry. I know you're—" and then stopped, and looked past Ines at the shop, at
the bolts on the wall, at the rack in the corner that nobody had cleaned in three years,
and then back.

She said, "Are you Ines Varga?"

Fabiola, without turning round, said, "Mm."

"That's a yes," the young woman said. "Mm is a yes."

"It's a *no*," said Fabiola. "It's a no to whatever's next. She's Ines Varga. I am Fabiola,
and it's my shop, and we've been here forty-one years, and if you're selling film, the
answer's already no and you can save us both the conversation."

"I know," said the young woman. "I've read the report. I know what the answer is."

There was a pause of about a second and a half, and in it, Ines became aware that she had
not moved for some time, and that the young woman had said *the report*, and that there
was one report, and that it was the only interesting thing in the room.

Fabiola said, "Did she say—"

"I know," said the young woman, to Ines, and put the plastic folder down on the counter
between them, and did not open it, and stood there with rain coming off her onto the bone
-coloured bench.

"I'd like to buy a descent," she said.

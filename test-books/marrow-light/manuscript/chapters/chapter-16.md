# Chapter Sixteen — 1994

The screening room was in the film school and it was a cupboard with a projector in it, and
it was available to her on a Tuesday night in October because she had a card that gave her
access to the building until eleven and had used the card for three years to sit in a room
with the door shut and do work that was supposed to be something else.

She had done the responsible thing first. She had asked. She had gone to Marisol Trent's
office on the Monday and had said, *I'd like to watch the 1994 Vell material, if it's in the
archive and if I'm allowed*, and had been entirely prepared for a no, and had got something
else.

"It's in the archive," Marisol had said. "It came to us when she gave it up. It's not
restricted, there's no rights issue, it's hers and she signed it over. You'll need a
machine."

"Which machine?"

"That's the problem." Marisol had looked at her for slightly too long. "It's on a tape. Not a
digitised copy — a tape, from 1994, and we have one machine in this building that still plays
that format and it's in the room you're standing in and it's a projector, and I would like
you to understand that I am aware of how that sounds and I have checked."

"How long is it."

"Nine hours of source material," said Marisol, "of which about two is usable and the rest is
her working. There's a rough cut of forty minutes somewhere in it as well. The index is in the
box and I haven't opened the box."

"You haven't opened the box."

"It's been in a store room since 2011," said Marisol, "and it came to us in 1997, and I have
never had a reason to, and I want to be quite clear that that is a fact about my reasons and
not a claim about hers."

Ottoline had carried the box to the room herself and had put it on the table and had not
opened it for about eleven minutes.

That was the part she had not planned and the part she later understood to be the only part
of the evening that was real. Nine hours. Two usable. A rough cut of forty minutes in there
somewhere, made by a woman who surveyed a cave for nine months and then stopped making films
and never said why, and there was a forty-minute rough cut *in the box*, and Ottoline sat
alone in a cupboard with a projector in it at nine o'clock on a Tuesday night and could not
open it.

Here is what she knew about her mother, and she had made the list, and the list was short and
she had made it in June and had not shown it to anybody.

Vera Marrow made four films between 1989 and 1995. Three of them were good. Nobody says
otherwise; the fourth one is a well-known thing in a small field and it has an article
about it. The fourth one was made in 1995 and it was about a woman who ran a ferry, and it
was the best of the four, and after it Vera Marrow made nothing at all.

She had taught at two schools. She had done commercial work — she had made an advert once,
in 1998, badly, and been told so, and had taken the money. She had been, according to
everybody who had been in a room with her, extremely funny and completely unwilling to be
asked about her own work, which Ottoline had decided as a child was a thing about adults and
had now decided was a thing about the fourth film.

The tape was the *fifth* thing. Not the fourth film. The thing that was going to be the fifth
film and wasn't, and which was nine hours long, and which nobody had watched since 1997.

Ottoline opened the box at nine-forty and threaded the reel and set the timer for nine
hours and the timer was wrong and she overran it by an hour and forty minutes, and the room
was black, and she was alone, and that was the whole of the setup and it was the correct
setup and she had known it would be.

---

The first hour was a woman standing in a field at dawn in a coat.

Not Vera. A sound recordist she would come to know the name of. There is a shot of this at
nine minutes which runs eleven seconds and which contains, in the background, a woman's voice
saying a number out loud and then swearing at it, and the swearing is funny, and it is not
her mother, and the first time Ottoline Marrow laughed out loud alone in a dark room she
laughed at the wrong person.

The second hour was better and she had to stop twice.

There is a sequence — she would find it later, on a timeline, in her own cut, and put it at
four minutes past the hour, and it was eleven minutes long — of a woman going into a
pitcher on hands and knees in a way that Ottoline had never seen anybody do and would spend
a term trying to work out how to shoot. There was no attempt to make it look good. The
woman's hips went up and then one leg went after the other and she was in. It was
completely unglamorous and it was the first time in Ottoline's life she had understood that
a person could be extremely good at a thing and be completely ordinary to watch, and she had
to stop the tape, and she did not restart it for eleven minutes, and when she came back her
notebook was open at a page she had not written on.

The fourth hour was the survey.

This was the part she had been waiting for without knowing she was waiting for it, and it
was the part that changed what the evening was for. Vera Marrow had surveyed the Vell in
1994 the way Tove Marsk had described surveying: she went, she measured, she came out, she
wrote it up, and the writing-up was the whole of the work, and there are forty minutes of a
woman at a table in 1994 building the third edition of a survey, and it is the most
compelling forty minutes of administrative work Ottoline had ever seen.

There is a shot, at about the twenty-sixth minute of that sequence, of a hand going round a
coloured pen and then going back and putting a line through something and then, after a
moment, un-crossing it. The hand belongs to a person who is thinking. Nothing happens. It is
nine seconds long and it is on a piece of tape in a store room and it is the single most
useful thing she has ever seen.

And at the thirty-first minute there is a wide shot of three people at a table, and one of
them is her mother, and one of them is a woman she does not know, and the third is younger
than either of them and is laughing at something off-camera, and Vera Marrow turns her head
about ten degrees towards the laughing, and does not smile, and the shot is four seconds long
and it is held a half-second past the point where the editor would have cut it, and the
holding is a decision somebody made in 1994 with a camera on a tripod, and Ottoline sat in
the dark and pressed the back of her hand against her mouth.

That was it. That was the entire evening. Nine hours, and the film was the two usable hours,
and the film was good, and she understood by about two in the morning — watching the light
through a doorway because the projector had a bulb going — that her mother had made a good
film in 1995 and had then made nothing, and that the good film was not about a woman who
ran a ferry.

It was about a survey. It was about forty minutes of a table and a woman un-crossing a line.

And the reason Vera Marrow had not made it, as far as Ottoline could work out at ten to three
in the morning in a cupboard with a projector, was not something that had gone wrong.

It was that she had got the survey right, and the survey was about water, and the water was
going to be somebody's problem, and she had been twenty-eight and had decided that a person
whose job it was to say things like that out loud ought to be very sure, and had spent five
years getting very sure, and had arrived at a certainty that could not be filmed.

Ottoline stopped the tape at ten past three because she had watched eleven of the twelve and
could not watch the twelfth, and sat in the dark in the building, and did not turn the light
on, and understood that she was not going to be able to watch it again on her own.

That was the part she had not expected. She had come in expecting to be able to watch it
alone and to find something usable, and she had found the opposite: that this was a thing
she could not watch alone, that it was going to have to be watched with somebody, and that
the somebody could not be her mother, and that therefore the next four months of this film
were going to be conducted with a piece of her own family sitting unsaid at every single
frame.

She wrote, in the notebook, in the dark, by the light of a phone:

*Do not tell Ines about this tonight. Not because she's had it. Because I'd be doing it to
get something.*

And then, underneath, in different handwriting, which is a thing she had started doing in
September and had still not examined:

*Ask her anyway. Not for something. Because she'll know what to do with it.*

She read that back four times, and it was four to three in the morning, and she did not know
whether it was a good idea, and she put the reel in its box and the box on the shelf and the
key in her coat pocket and went out of the building into a car park in the rain.

She got eleven metres from the door and stood still in the middle of it for about a minute,
which was longer than getting into a car takes, and which nobody saw, because it was three in
the morning and the car park was empty, and which was, in the end, the only part of the
evening that was entirely hers.

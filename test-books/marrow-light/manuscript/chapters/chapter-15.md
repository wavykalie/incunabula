# Chapter Fifteen — Not Today

The camera came on on the sixteenth of October, which was the seventh of twelve, and
Ottoline Marrow had been waiting nine days for it, and had rehearsed the way she got, and
found, when the moment came, that the rehearsing had all been about the wrong thing.

It was not a formal interview. There was nowhere to sit. There were two chairs in the back
of the van and one of them had a fuel bottle under it, and the light in the layby at eleven
in the morning on a wet Thursday was the flat white light of a place with a hill on one side
of it, and Tove had gone up the field with a radio and a good reason.

"Right," said Ottoline. "Two minutes of this and then we're doing the station work, and I'd
rather you knew that I've got a list and you're on it, and I'd rather you saw the list than
had me pretend I hadn't got one."

"That's fair."

"I'm going to be a bit odd for the next two minutes and I want to explain the oddness in
advance so you can tell me if it's the wrong kind of odd. I'm going to be fast and I'm going
to ask you four things and three of them are professional and one of them isn't, and the
one that isn't comes last, and if you want me to stop before I get to it you say *now* and
we stop and we do the station work."

Ines turned the box off.

That was the first thing, and it was the biggest thing, and it took eleven seconds.

She turned the box off and set it on the bonnet of the van and put her hands flat on either
side of it, and did not say anything.

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't," said Ines. "That's not why I did it. If I'm on a tape and you ask me that
and I say no, then there's a *no* in a file, and the *no* becomes the thing that's known
rather than the thing that's true, and in about two years somebody will say *she wouldn't talk
about it on film* as though that were a fact about me instead of a fact about a Tuesday."

"It's a fact about a Tuesday."

"It's a fact about a Tuesday and it's the only kind of fact that gets written down," said
Ines, "and I'd rather it stayed in the van."

Ottoline did not argue with that, and did not thank her for it, which was correct, and which
cost her something to not do and which she would think about in about three weeks.

"Four questions," she said. "One, two, three are the survey and you've had them before.
Four is the one."

"Go on, then."

"That's the professional one," said Ottoline, too fast. "Why is the section below the second
pitch closed? Not the association's reason. Your reason."

"Fine. Two reasons and they're both true. One: the water's not been properly characterised
and everybody who says it is has not been down far enough. Two: there was a fatality in it in
2022 that had a survey going on in a closed section, and when a thing like that happens the
first question everybody asks is *why was anybody in there*, and the honest answer is too
complicated to survive a meeting, so the section gets closed, and the closure is not really
about water at all, it's about being able to say a simple sentence."

"What's the simple sentence?"

"*Nobody should be in there.* And it's easier to enforce than *nobody should be in there
unless you can prove you know the water*, and there's one person in this county who could
prove it and she isn't going to, so it's closed."

"Do you think it should be open?"

"Yes," said Ines. "And I think that's exactly the sort of thing a person says who's the
problem."

"Right," said Ottoline, and let three seconds go by, and there was rain on the roof of the
van and the sound of a boot going up a field two hundred metres away and then nothing.

"Four," she said.

She had planned four ways to do it. She had written them down on the fourteenth, in a
notebook, in a layby, in the rain, and she had crossed out three of them, and the one she
had left was the plainest and she was using it because she had worked out in the second
week that the clever version of this question was a way of not asking it.

"Her name was Solveig Havelock."

"I know her name."

"I know you know her name. That wasn't the question." Ottoline put the camera down, on the
bonnet, lens off, facing away, which was a thing she had done about four hundred times and
had never once done with a camera in the room. "You went back in eleven times in nine weeks.
That's in the report. I read the report four times before I ever spoke to you, and I've
thought about that number every day since, and I've been careful not to use it as a thing to
catch you with, because it isn't one, and I want to say that before I ask."

"Asked what?"

"Asked what she was like," said Ottoline.

The rain did what it did.

Ines Varga sat on the bonnet of a van in a layby with a box switched off and her hands flat
on the metal, and she did not answer for a long time, and when she answered what she said
was not a refusal, and it was the most technically impressive thing she had ever done in the
presence of another person.

"You'll get her from the tape," said Ines.

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Everyone who says that is right." Ines turned her hands over and looked at them, which she
did not do. "There's nine minutes of her on a laptop in a back room and I have not watched
it since the Thursday after and I'm not going to watch it in October with a film crew
seventeen kilometres away, and when I've watched it, you'll be able to write her in ninety
seconds and you'll get her better than I can give you now, because right now I'd be giving
you *my* version and you'd have to spend the rest of your life wondering which parts of it
were true."

"That's not an answer to the question."

"It's an answer to the question," said Ines. "It's an answer that costs me something and it
is the only one I've got and I'm not going to pretend it's nothing so that you can use it."

Ottoline Marrow sat in a car park in the rain in front of a woman she had known for five
weeks, and had wanted, since the second of September, to be able to say to, *what was she
like*, and had got it, and understood that she had been given something far more useful than
the thing she had asked for, and could not say so.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"I'm not pushing," said Ottoline, "and I need you to know that the not-pushing is a
decision and not an accident, and that it took me about ninety seconds to make it, and that
the reason I made it is that if I push now then the next four days are worthless and probably
the six after that. That's a piece of craft and it is also, if I'm honest, about forty per
cent cowardice, and I would rather tell you that than let you think it was all craft."

"It's a good balance," said Ines.

"It's not a good balance, it's a *balance*."

And then Ines laughed, which she had not done in front of Ottoline Marrow and did not
recognise as having done, and it was short and it was entirely involuntary, and it went out
into a layby in the rain.

"Sorry," she said.

"Don't be."

"It's not funny."

"No," said Ines. "It's not. It's just the first time somebody's given me the honest version of
being let alone, including the forty per cent, and I'd forgotten that anybody thought to."

It was the closest they had come, and it was on a tape that was switched off, and neither of
them said anything else about it, and the morning went on, and the station work happened, and
at twenty past one Ines Varga went down a pitch with a survey station and came up at half
past two having done, by the standards of the Vell, absolutely nothing remarkable whatsoever.

On the walk back up the field Tove said, without turning round, "Well."

"Was it bad."

"I was a hundred and ten metres up the hill for the entire thing," said Tove. "I have a
radio. I heard all of it."

"You were supposed to be out of earshot."

"I was," said Tove. "I'm extremely good at being out of earshot and also at being ninety
metres from a thing, and that is the entire reason I am who I am." She stopped at the top of
the field and turned round, and looked at Ines, and did not offer sympathy and did not offer
help, and said, instead: "You didn't lie to her."

"No."

"You gave her a true thing that stopped the question. That's a technique, Ines. I've seen you
do it about water and I've never once seen you do it about a person." Tove put her hands in
her pockets. "I'd like to know that you thought about it, and I'm asking because I'd have
done the same thing and I'd like to know whether you did it on purpose."

"I did it on purpose," said Ines.

"Good," said Tove. "Good," she said again, in a completely different voice, and turned and
walked to the van, and did not say anything else about Solveig Havelock that day.

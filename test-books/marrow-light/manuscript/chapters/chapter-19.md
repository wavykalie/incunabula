# Chapter Nineteen — I Got What I Needed

The number was in a drawer in a house on the other side of the county and it took Ottoline
three days to get it, because getting a telephone number for a woman who had stopped making
films in 1997 required two people who did not want to give it to her and one of them gave
it anyway for reasons of her own.

It rang five times and Vera Marrow picked up, and the first thing she said was, "Who is
this," in a voice that Ottoline had heard on tape for eleven hours and had not heard in the
air since she was seven years old, and which was lower, and which was not older.

"It's Ottoline."

There was a pause of a length that was not about not recognising her.

"Ottoline," Vera Marrow said. "It's a late call."

"It's Sunday."

"I know it's Sunday. I meant, it's a late call for a Sunday." Another pause. "How did you get
this number?"

"It doesn't matter how I got it."

"It matters to me, and then it doesn't," said Vera, "and that's the correct order and you
were always impatient with it."

Ottoline stood in a corridor in the film school at ten past nine on a Sunday night with a
mobile phone against her ear and a piece of paper in her hand with a list of six things on
it, four of which she was not going to say.

"I'm making a film," she said.

"I know. You've told me four times and I've said that I know, and I've meant it, and I should
say that I did look, because I know what that sounds like."

"Where?"

"Somewhere I have never been, which is what I have said every time, and you have let it go
every time, which is — " Vera stopped. "You were always good at letting things go. That was
the useful thing about you as a child and I have never worked out whether you were being kind
or whether you were keeping score."

"I was seven."

"You were nine," said Vera Marrow. "There's a difference and it matters and I'm not going to
explain it because you'd only use it."

Right. That was two down from the list of six, without her having spent any of them, and she
had a whole technique for this and Vera Marrow had apparently invented it first.

"I'm not calling about the ferry film," said Ottoline.

"No. You're calling about the other one. You've been calling about the other one since about
2011, in a very light way, every year or so, and I've always said I got what I needed, and
it's always been true, and you have never once asked me what I needed." A breath. "So what is
it. The other one."

"It's a survey film. It's about the survey. It's nine hours of source and forty minutes of
rough cut and it's about a system and how you write one down, and it's the best work I've
ever seen of anybody's, and I'm making a film and you're in it, and I want to say that
properly: you're in it. That's why I'm ringing. Not to ask you about it. To tell you."

There was a silence on the line that was long enough that Ottoline looked at the screen to
see whether it had dropped.

"Right," said Vera Marrow.

"That's it? That's the whole call?"

"What did you expect."

"I don't know. I had a list."

"I know you had a list. I could hear you holding it." And then, in a completely different
voice, one that was not amused at all: "You've watched it. The nine hours."

"All but the last hour."

"Not the last hour," said Vera Marrow. "Good. That's — yes. Good. Don't watch the last hour
on your own."

"Why not?"

"Because there's nothing in it that will hurt you and I don't want you watching it on your
own," said Vera, "which is a sentence with two things in it and the second one is a small
piece of cruelty and I'm putting it in deliberately because you're twenty-two and I have been
watching you not do this for four years and somebody has to."

Ottoline wrote *the second thing* on the paper, and then looked at what she had written, and
then crossed it out, and then wrote it again.

"Mum. I want to film you for it. Talking to camera, in a room, with a proper light, and I'll
come to you, and I don't want anything about the film at all. That's the offer. I want to be
clear that the offer is a person in a chair for two hours."

There was a pause of about four seconds.

"No," said Vera Marrow.

"Okay."

"I want to be *extremely* clear that I have not said no because of you or anything you've
done. I want that on a recording if you have one running, and you haven't, have you, because
you never run one for me, and I have never once asked you to and I have noticed."

"No," said Ottoline, honestly.

"No." Another pause. "I'll tell you why, and you will not like it, and I'm telling you
because you're going to work it out anyway and I'd rather you had it in the first voice.

"In 1995 I made a film about a survey. I got it as good as I could get it. And then I
discovered that the reason I wanted to make it was not that I thought the survey was
interesting, it was that I had worked out something about the water in that system that
nobody else had, and I wanted there to be a record of me having worked it out." Vera's voice
had not changed at all. "That's it. That's the whole of it. It was a film about a woman
wanting credit for being right, and it was going to be forty minutes long, and it was going
to be beautiful, and every single person who ever watched it would have taken away a
portrait of a competent woman being competent, and nobody would have learned one thing about
the water."

"So the answer is yes and you didn't say it."

"No," said Vera Marrow. "The answer is *no, and you're wrong about what I need*. I have been
right about that for thirty-one years. And you can put that in your film, and I'd rather you
did, and I'd rather you did it from a recording of me telling you than from you writing it
down, and I'd rather you didn't ask me again."

The line went.

It was not a dramatic going; it was a mobile phone on a Sunday night in a corridor losing a
signal on a network that had never been good, and Ottoline Marrow stood there and looked at
the screen and said "hello" twice and then put the phone in her pocket.

She did not ring back.

That was a decision and she made it in about four seconds, standing in a corridor, and she
was pleased with herself about it for about nine minutes, and then it stopped being a
decision and became the first thing she had ever done to her mother out of being right rather
than out of care, which is a different thing, and which she was not going to look at for
three weeks.

She wrote the list back up. Four things crossed off. Two left.

*6. Ask her what the plate says.*

That was the one she had been carrying since Wednesday afternoon in a screening room, and
she had carried it for five days without asking it, and she had told herself on Wednesday
night that the reason was that she didn't know what it was yet, and that was true, and it
had stopped being true somewhere on Friday and had not been true since.

She got her keys out of her bag.

It was ten past ten on a Sunday. It was a twenty-five minute drive. It was raining. There
was a woman in a shop on the far side of the county who was in the back with a mug of cold
tea and had been in the back for an hour, and Ottoline Marrow had a plate in a photograph on
a mobile phone and four days in which to make a film and one person who had signed a document
in a stockroom in September and had gone back into a hole on the ninth of October and had not
been filmed once, and who had, in the last four minutes of a conversation fifty metres
underground on the fourteenth, described the sound of a wall in a system for four minutes
because somebody had asked her what water sounded like.

She got as far as the car park.

Then she sat in the van for about ten minutes with the engine off, in the rain, and did not
go, and it was not because of the deadline and it was not because of the film.

It was because she had realised, sitting in a car park at ten past ten on a Sunday, that if
she asked the question tonight then the answer would be a number, and she would have a number,
and the number would be the beginning of a thing she could not stop, and she was twenty-two,
and she had watched eleven hours of a woman who had known a number in 1994 and had decided
not to say it, and who had then stopped making films for thirty-one years rather than say it.

Ottoline Marrow sat in a car park in the rain and worked out that she was about to do the
thing her mother had not done, and that the thing her mother had not done had been a
kindness to somebody, and that it might not be a kindness to the person sitting in a shop on
the other side of the county with a mug of cold tea.

Then she started the van and drove home, and did not ring, and slept about four hours.

And in a house on the other side of the county, Vera Marrow, who had said *no* in a voice
that gave nothing away and had then said *I'll tell you why* in a voice that gave almost
everything away, sat in a room with a telephone on a table beside her, and did not put it
on the hook, and did not switch it off, and did not go to bed, and after a while said, out
loud, to a room with nobody in it:

"Well," said Vera Marrow. "That's thirty-one years and about four minutes."

She sat there for a while longer. Then she got up and made a cup of tea, and it was the wrong
time of the night for tea, and she had a rule about that, and made it anyway.

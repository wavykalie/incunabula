# Chapter Fourteen — What Water Sounds Like

It was the fourteenth of October, which was the sixth of twelve, and by the sixth the two of
them had stopped introducing themselves into the radio and had started just beginning.

"Twelve-ten. Varga to surface."

"Surface copies. Go ahead."

"Twenty minutes at station four, then I'm coming up for the light."

"Light's about an hour. Take thirty."

"I'll take thirty and I'll want the last ten as a single."

"You always want the last ten as a single."

"It comes apart otherwise."

"It comes apart because you're tired."

"It comes apart because it's better," said Ines, and Tove, on the hill, made a noise into a
radio that in any other employment would have got somebody a talking-to.

---

The light came at a hundred and forty, down the pitch, in a single shaft that moved. That
was the thing about the Vell that no photograph had ever managed: the light came down at an
angle for about twenty minutes in the middle of the day, through a hole in a ceiling that
was not in the plan because nobody had ever been there at the right time to draw it, and it
moved across the wall, and then it was gone, and you had four hundred hours of footage
available and about eight minutes of that in it.

Ottoline had the camera out and did not use it, because it was not a survey day and there
was no clause about survey days and the clause she had written herself said *no filming
during descent or ascent* and did not say anything at all about a woman standing at a
station in a shaft of moving light on a Tuesday afternoon, and she had written that clause in
September in about ninety seconds and had been quietly proud of it for a month.

She looked at the camera. She looked at the wall, which had a woman in a rack standing in
moving light in it.

She did not film it. She wrote the time down.

"Twenty-one minutes past twelve," said Ines. "It's here."

"I can see you," said Ottoline. "I'm not going to say anything else for a bit, you don't
have to acknowledge me if you don't want to."

"Okay," said Ines.

And then she said, "Do you want to know what the light's doing?"

"Yes," said Ottoline.

"It's coming in at about forty degrees and hitting the west wall about two metres above the
floor, and where it lands there's a bit of flowstone that goes shiny, and it's the same bit
every day at the same time, and it means that if you look at the map — and you've not read
the map, I know you haven't read the map, don't tell me you have—"

"I haven't read the map."

"—if you looked at the map, the plan of this bit is wrong. The plan has that wall as
vertical and it's not, it leans in about a metre over the height, and nobody's remeasured
it since 2011 because nobody goes past here on a good day, and it's a one-metre error in
about four metres of passage and that's the entire difference between a survey that can be
used and one that can only be looked at."

"Can you measure it now."

"I could. I won't, because measuring it means a laser and a book and twenty minutes, and
I've not got twenty minutes, and because if I do it I have to do it properly and if I do it
properly I have to file it, and then it's mine, and then it's on the record, and then
somebody in an office is going to make a decision about it on a Tuesday, and I'd rather it
was wrong for another year."

"Sounds like you'd rather be wrong on purpose."

"Yes," said Ines. "That's exactly what I said."

The light moved. It was very quiet at a hundred and forty on the surface of a Tuesday in
October, and down here the wet was doing the thing it did in the middle of the day, which was
nothing much, and the whole of the ambience was a woman breathing forty feet below a layby
in the rain.

"Can I ask you something that's not about survey work," said Ottoline.

"You can ask."

"It's the same thing you ask me. It comes out in a professional register because we're both
doing it. I've noticed we both do it."

"Everybody does it."

"I'm asking anyway. What does the water sound like down here? Not the gauge. Not the flood
history. What does it actually sound like, in a normal day, when nothing's happening?"

There was a pause on the line of about the length that meant she was deciding whether to
make a joke.

She did not make a joke.

"There's a break in the roof at a hundred and twenty," said Ines, "and I told you about that,
so that's not news. But that's the loud one. That's the one that only shows up in the first
forty minutes after rain and then stops, and you stop hearing it in about a day, which is
one of the things about caves that nobody tells you: the loud sound goes first and the quiet
ones come in behind it, and by the fourth day you can't remember what the loud one was, and
you'd swear to anybody that it never happened."

"That's not quite what I asked."

"I know it's not. I'm not finished."

"Okay."

"The loud one's a drip. One drip, onto a stone, about a second apart, and it's a *pock*, not
a tick — a tick is wood and metal, a pock is stone, and there's no way to know why, and I've
never found out why, and I did a degree in geology for two years so I could find out why and
I couldn't.

"And then there's the one at a hundred and forty, and the hundred-and-forty one is the one I
actually like, and it's not a drip, it's a *seep*. It's a wall that sweats. And what it does
is it comes out over about a metre and a half of wall in a band, and it doesn't run down, it
just — comes out, and goes thin, and goes back, on a cycle of about eleven minutes, and the
band is always in the same place, and in the middle of the cycle the wall's dry and at the
edges of the cycle it's got a run in it about a hand's width.

"And the sound of that is the sound of nothing much happening, continuously, and it's the
only sound in the whole system that doesn't stop. Everything else in here is intermittent.
Everything else is an event. The hundred-and-forty band is just — it's a process, and it
doesn't care about you, and it's been doing it since before anybody was recording it.

"The other one, and this is the one I don't know how to describe, is at a hundred and ninety
and it's a fault. There's a fault in the wall about a hundred and ninety and water's coming
down it, and it's very slow, and it's the only thing down here that makes a sound you could
hear over the other two, and what it sounds like is — "

She stopped for four seconds.

"You know when a room is going cold and you can hear it? Not the radiator. The thing under
it."

"Yes."

"It's like that. Except it's under your feet, and it's not getting cold, it's getting full,
and it's about a third of a second every forty seconds, and if you sit at a hundred and
ninety for an hour you will count every single one of them without deciding to, and you will
be somewhere else by the fourth one and you will not notice that you left."

The line was quiet. The light had gone. It was, according to the plan, twenty-eight minutes
past twelve.

"Ottoline."

"I'm here."

"Say something, because I've just spent four minutes describing a wall to somebody in a car
park and I have no idea why and I have been underground a long time."

"I'm going to say something and I want you to know that I know what I've just been given," said
Ottoline. "You didn't have to do that. I asked a small question and you gave me a big one,
and you did it because it was Tuesday and because nobody's asked you and because I'm on the
other end of a rope and it's the only way you've got of talking to a person without looking
at them, and I have worked out all three of those things and I have decided I'm not going to
use any of them."

"That was a good answer," said Ines.

"It was an honest answer. They're different. I told you on the first day they're different
and I'm telling you now."

There was a pause.

"Can I do it again tomorrow," said Ines.

"We'll see what the weather's doing."

"That's not an answer."

"No," said Ottoline. "It's a deferral, and you get one of those a week, and I've used two,
and there's four days left."

Ines said, "Understood," and came up the pitch, and at the top of it sat down on a rock that
was the colour of a bone and stayed there for a while with the box on her hip and the rain
starting again, and when she keyed the radio she said, "Varga to surface. Ascending, clear of
the pitch, fifty metres to the top of the field," which was the correct professional thing
and which meant, in the two-word version that she was not going to use, that she had been on
the radio with a person for twenty-nine minutes and had not wanted to stop and had not said
so.

Nobody hung up. That was the thing about it, and both of them noticed it at about the same
time, which was at the top of the pitch, when the radio should have been switched off and
neither of them had done it, and the line had sat there open, with nothing on it, for about a
minute and a half, and the rain on the canopy at the layby and the rain on Ines's hood at the
top of the field were both going into the same conversation and neither of them had said
anything into it for ninety seconds.

Then Hana said, "Varga, you're at the top of the field and you've not acknowledged the
surface."

"Copy," said Ines. "Walking to the vehicles."

"Twenty-three past twelve. Logged."

And the box went off, and the rain went on being rain, and the light in the Vell came down
at forty degrees onto a wall that leaned in a metre over four metres that nobody had
remeasured since 2011, and which was wrong on the plan, and which was right, and which two
women at either end of fifty metres of wool had both decided, separately and without
discussing it, to leave alone until tomorrow.

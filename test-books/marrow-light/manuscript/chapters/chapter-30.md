# Chapter Thirty — Camera Off

It was a car park in the rain and it was the fourth time they had stood in one, and it was
the last time, and neither of them knew that.

Ottoline Marrow turned the camera off before she got out of the van.

That was the whole event. It happened in a layby at four minutes past two on the afternoon of
the twenty-fourth of November, in the rain, with Petar in the front and Hana forty metres
away on a radio she was not on, and it took about two seconds and nobody saw it except the
two of them and possibly a man in a van.

She put it on the bonnet of Petar's van, lens cap off, and turned the record switch to off,
and left it there.

Ines got out and looked at it.

"That's off," she said.

"Yes."

"You've never had it off when we've been in a car park."

"No."

"Why now."

And Ottoline Marrow, who had thought about this on and off for three days and had arrived
at an answer she did not like, said: "Because in about ten minutes I'm going to ask you
something I've been not asking since the twenty-seventh of October, and I asked you a version
of it in a layby on the sixteenth and you gave me a true thing that stopped the question, and
I've thought about that for five weeks and I've worked out that I don't want to be able to
say I've done it twice."

The rain came off the wing mirror.

"There's no tape," said Ines.

"I know there's no tape. That's not why." Ottoline put her hands in her jacket pockets. "I
filmed you once, in the back room, in September, before I knew you, when I was a person who
films people in rooms because they're in a room. And it was forty seconds and I've never
looked at it and it's on a hard drive in a drawer in a flat in this city, and I have never
looked at it, and that's not a regret, it's a *fact about me*, and I would like to be somebody
who didn't have facts like that about herself before I start asking a person the real
question."

Ines Varga stood in a car park in the rain and looked at a camera on a bonnet.

"Ask it," she said.

---

"Can I sit down?"

"You can sit on the wall."

Ottoline sat on the wall. It was a low wall and it was wet and she sat on it anyway, and Ines
stayed standing, and that was how the conversation happened: one of them sitting down and one
of them not, and neither of them mentioning it, and it meaning, by the end, quite a great
deal.

"Eleven times in nine weeks," said Ottoline. "Since the fourth of November. That's what you
said to Tove on the radio on the nineteenth and everybody in this car park heard it and
nobody's said anything since."

"That's right."

"Eleven times in nine weeks in a section that's been closed since 2011, past a cap that a
supervisor imposed on you in October, with a plate on the wall that you maintain yourself,
which you've maintained yourself for four years, on your own time, going past with nothing to
carry and stopping to paint it."

"That's right."

"And you've been reading it wrong the whole time."

"I've been reading *twelve* the whole time," said Ines. "Which is a number. And a number is a
number, and I'd go down, and I'd read it, and it would say something, and I'd go back up, and
that's not a mistake. You don't check a number every trip. You check it about once a month and
then you trust it. That's not a defect in me. That's how instruments work."

"Does it work like that in a closed section?"

"No," said Ines. "That's the answer, and it's the whole of the answer, and it's the reason
I've known for four years that I shouldn't be going down, and I want you to notice that I have
not said a single thing in this conversation that I haven't already said to somebody else in
this car park in the last five days."

"I noticed," said Ottoline. "That's what I'm asking about."

---

"Do you know why I don't push?" she said.

"You told me. In a layby. It was forty per cent craft and sixty per cent cowardice, and you
gave me the split voluntarily, which I have never once forgotten and I want you to know I have
never once forgotten it."

"Does that make it better?"

"It makes it *interesting*," said Ottoline, "which is a different thing, and which is why
I've been doing it for four months, so I should probably examine that." She looked at her
hands. "Here's the actual question, and it's the real one, and I've written it down and I've
got it in my pocket, and I want you to know that I have it written down because I've found that
I'm braver when I've got a piece of paper and I don't know whether that's a technique or a
crutch."

"Then say it without the paper."

"No. I'm saying it with the paper. I'm going to say it with the paper because I wrote it on
the nineteenth and it hasn't changed and I don't trust my memory on this."

She took a folded piece of paper out of her pocket. It was wet at one corner. She did not
open it.

"I'm not going to read it. That's the only promise I'm making. I'm going to tell you what's on
it and I'd rather not read it because I think if I read it I'll do it in a voice, and I don't
want a voice, I want to be sitting on a wall in the rain with my hands in my pockets like a
person."

"Ottoline."

"I'm going. In a second."

She sat there for a moment.

"The question isn't about Solveig," she said. "I want to say that first, because everybody
assumes it is, and I have decided it isn't, and I checked, and I have checked about nine times
over five weeks, and the question isn't about Solveig. The question is this.

"You went down eleven times in nine weeks. The plate has a number on it that moves by two or
three hours at a time, which means somebody has been repainting it, which means somebody has
been down, and that was you. And every single time you went down you read a number off a wall
and decided it meant the water was behaving, and it did not mean the water was behaving, and
you knew it might not, and you went anyway, eleven times.

"And on the fourteenth of September in 2022 you were two chambers too deep. And in every
account of that anybody has ever given me — the report, Tove, Hana, Fabiola, you, and my
mother's index in 1995 — the thing that comes up is that the water was the water and the
section was closed and it was water.

"And the reading on the plate when she was killed was wrong too," said Ottoline. "Wasn't it.
It was the same plate and the same hand and it was wrong, and it was wrong by however much it
was wrong by, and you went down eleven times in nine weeks reading it wrong, and the eleventh
time was the fourteenth of September, and that is the sentence, isn't it. That's the whole
thing. You went down one time too many because a number on a wall was a number and numbers are
numbers."

She stopped.

"That's on the paper," she said. "That's what's on the paper. And I've had it in my pocket for
five days and I have not been able to say it to you because I didn't know whether it was true,
and it turns out I don't know whether it's true now either, but I know it's a fair question,
and it's the only question anybody's asked anybody about this in four years, and I wanted to
ask it, and I'd have asked it a fortnight ago if I'd had the nerve."

The rain came into the car park off the wall.

And Ines Varga stood on the other side of a wet wall in the rain and was asked a true
question, carefully and decently and in a completely professional register, by a woman of
twenty-two who had put it in her pocket for five days because she was frightened of it.

And she answered it. Truthfully. Completely. In about forty seconds, without hesitating once.

"The plate wasn't wrong on the fourteenth," she said.

"Right."

"The reading was nine. The gauge had been *correct* for the previous eight days, because I'd
painted it on the seventh, and I read it on the fourteenth the way I read it on the seventh,
and the water came up at about half past two, and half past two was not in the cycle, because
the cycle is a catchment thing and it doesn't know about a collapse that's already happened
in a chamber four metres above you."

"So the plate wasn't wrong."

"The plate wasn't wrong. The *system* was wrong. There was a rise that wasn't in the pattern
and it came up in a chamber that had been stable for years and the water found a way in that
it hadn't used before, because the roof had come down on the fourth and the roof coming down
had changed the drainage." Ines's voice was not shaking and was not anything. "I read a
correct number and the number was correct and I went down anyway, because that's what you do
with a correct number, you go down, and I've said for four years that the cave took it, and the
cave did take it, and you're asking me a question that has a true answer, and the true answer
is that the plate said nine and the plate was right."

Ottoline did not move.

"Okay," she said.

"That's the answer. That's the true one, and it's about drainage and a collapse in 2011 that
changed a catchment, and it's a real answer, and you can check it in the third edition, and
it's in the third edition, in the hydrology section, on page two hundred and nine, which I
wrote in 2019."

"Page two hundred and nine," said Ottoline.

"Yes."

"That's a real page."

"It is a real page."

"That is a genuinely extraordinary answer," said Ottoline Marrow, "and it is completely true,
and it is a *hydrological* answer to a question about a person, and I have to tell you that
I felt it when you said it, I felt the moment you turned it into the instrument, and I want
to be very careful here because I am twenty-two and I have been doing this for four months and
I don't know what I'm doing."

"You felt it because it was fast."

"It was *extremely* fast," said Ottoline, "and you know it was fast, and you did it on
purpose, and I'm not going to pretend to somebody who does it that fast that I didn't notice
it."

They stood on either side of a wall.

"So the answer's no," said Ines.

"The answer's a no with a footnote."

"The answer's a no, and the footnote is true, and the footnote is on page two hundred and
nine of a document I wrote, and I have never once said it out loud to anybody, and I have said
it to you now because you asked it properly and because you turned your camera off, and I want
to say that the camera being off is the reason and not the other way round."

---

Ottoline took the paper out of her pocket and put it back in her pocket.

"Can I say the thing I actually think?"

"You've said it."

"No. The thing I actually think." She stood up off the wall and did not brush herself down,
which was a detail that mattered later and did not matter then. "I think that in four months
I've been living inside a question that I assumed had a shape, and the shape was *did you go
down one time too many because you didn't want to stop*, and everything I've made — the film,
the schedule, the four hours on the wall, the whole thing — is built on the assumption that
the interesting person in this story is the one who went down eleven times, and the reason I
think that is that it's a better film, and I have been using a better film to decide what I
think happened."

"That's a very good way of saying a thing."

"It's a very *useful* way of saying a thing and I'm not going to let it go." Ottoline
unbuttoned her coat. "Page two hundred and nine. You wrote that in 2019. I have been
watching you for four months and I have never once asked you about your own work, and that's
the actual failure, and it's mine, and it's not yours, and I'm not going to give it to you as
something to fix."

She got in the van.

Petar had the engine running. He had started it about two minutes before, which was more
considerate than either of them had registered and which was the reason nobody asked him
anything on the way back.

Hana Vogel was forty metres up the track and did not look up when the van came past, and
Ines Varga looked at her in the rear window, and neither of them said anything, and Hana
waited until the van had gone, and then said, to nobody, on a hillside, in the rain:

"Twenty-two seconds," said Hana Vogel. "That's how long she took."

And then she got her radio and went to find Tove Marsk, because there was a plate with five
numbers on it in a chamber that nobody was going to look at again until the twenty-eighth, and
because a rescue technician's job on a hillside in the rain is to be the last voice, and
because she was, in the end, the only one of the four of them who had never once pretended
that the number on the wall was the whole of it.

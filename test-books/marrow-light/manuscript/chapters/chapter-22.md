# Chapter Twenty-Two — Still

It took four hours and eleven minutes over two evenings, on a good monitor, in a room with
the lights off, and it produced two numbers.

That was the whole of it. Four hours and eleven minutes of work by a twenty-two-year-old who
had never done it before, and the result was two numbers written on a piece of paper, and
they were both wrong.

That was the finding. That was what four hours of competent work in a room above a shop that
sold fluorescent fittings came to, on the evening of the twenty-seventh of October, and
Ottoline Marrow wrote it at eleven o'clock at night on the back of a page in a notebook that
had a great deal of other work in it:

*Plate reads: 6 (partial, digit damaged — could be 6 or 8, possibly 9; the last stroke is
ambiguous between a 6 and a 0 in this paint)*

*Cross-reference: file note, 1994, "the number goes off by one after rain."*

*Therefore: if the true reading at time of filming was 8, the plate read 6. The plate was
low by two hours.*

She wrote that out again underneath, more slowly, because she did not believe it yet and had
learned in her first year that the moment you believe a finding is the moment you start
building on it.

*The plate was low by two hours on the day Vera Marrow filmed it. A gauge that reads low
means the water has been up for LONGER than the number says. A gauge that reads low means
the last rise was EARLIER than the record shows.*

*Which means: if the plate is wrong in 1994, it is not wrong because of the 1994 rain; it is
wrong because the person maintaining it has been painting from memory, or from a plate that
was itself copied, and the error is not a one-off.*

*Which means it is wrong in 2022.*

*Which means the last reading before the collapse in chapter four was 2 hours. The water had
been up for longer than it said. It had been up for longer than it said, and she was two
chambers too deep, and she had nine weeks of eleven descents to have known the plate drifts
and had used it every time as a reason to go back down.*

She put the pen down.

She sat in a room with the lights off in a building with a car park on the roof and did not
move for about four minutes, and then she turned the lights on, and then she did the thing
that she was extremely good at and that had gotten her this far, which was to go through
every single line of it again and look for the place where she had wanted it to be true.

---

Here is what she got, and it is the whole of the work, and it took nine days rather than four
hours because four hours is how long the looking takes and nine days is how long the not-
wanting-it-to-be-true takes.

**One.** The digit was ambiguous. It could be a 6 and it could be an 8. At 1994 video
resolution, in a light, at two metres, on a wall, on a 1994 camera, it could be a 6 and it
could be an 8, and every hour she spent on it did not improve the picture, because the
information was not in the tape, it had never been in the tape, and there is a limit to
enlargement and she had found it and it was a wall.

**Two.** Vera Marrow's note said *off by one*, not off by two. One is a plausible error. Two
is a large error for a hand-painted device maintained by a competent person every trip, and
the difference between the note and the reading was the entire finding, and the difference
between the note and the reading was **one ambiguous digit.**

**Three.** She did not know what the number was supposed to be. The plate records hours since
the last rise. She did not know the last rise. She did not have the rainfall record for
September 1994, and the association's hydrological record for 1994 was a paper file in a
store room and the store room was in a building she had a key to and had not gone back to.

She wrote that down. It was the most important line on the page and it took nine days:

*I do not know what it should say. I have produced a difference and I cannot say what the
difference is of. I have a 6 and a note saying minus one and a box in a store room.*

---

She went to the store room on the twenty-ninth of October, in the afternoon, and let herself
in with a key Marisol had given her, and found four boxes of hydrological paper, and found
the 1994 record in the second box, and read a table of rainfall for the eastern karst for
September and October 1994, and found the line she wanted.

It was in there. It was a rainfall table with a column for *days since last significant
rise* maintained by a hydrologist in 1994 for a completely different purpose, and it was not
the gauge, and it was not maintained by the same person, and it was not comparable, and it
was a different measurement of the same river catchment made for a different reason by
somebody who had never been in a cave.

Ottoline Marrow stood in a store room and read a table in a box for about forty minutes.

And the table said that on the fourteenth of September 2022 — no, in 1994, in the column
she wanted, on the date that Vera Marrow had filmed the fourth chamber — the catchment had
been in a state for eleven days.

Eleven days without a significant rise.

And the plate said six, and the note said six was one high, and six minus one was five, and
five hours was not eleven days by any reading of the system, and the numbers were not close
enough to be arguing with each other, which meant that one of her three sources was not a
measurement of the same thing.

And the three sources were: a brass plate, a note in a margin, and a hydrologist's rainfall
table from a different catchment on a different project.

Ottoline Marrow sat down on the floor of a store room, on the concrete, next to a box of
paper from 1994, and worked out that she had spent nine days and four hours building a
finding out of an ambiguous digit and a margin note and a table about a river.

She was not disappointed; that is what she would have expected to feel and she did not feel
it, and the reason she did not feel it is that she had checked, and the checking was the
thing, and the checking had come out where it came out.

She wrote, sitting on the floor, in a notebook, with a bad back:

*NOT A FINDING. I want to be very clear, even here, even in case this notebook turns up: this
is not a finding. A 6 that might be an 8, a note that says one, and a rainfall table about a
different river.*

*I have spent nine days making a story out of a wall.*

*Four hours and eleven minutes of work. Two numbers. Both wrong, or both about different
things. The honest position is that I do not know what the plate said, and the honest
position before that is that it does not matter, because I was building a case rather than
looking at a wall.*

*She said: don't show it to her first.*

She read that last line four times. She had written it in her notebook on the twenty-first of
October at half past four in the afternoon with an underline, and she had thought at the time
that it was Tove being careful, and she read it now in a store room on the concrete and
understood that it was not Tove being careful at all.

Tove Marsk had not said don't show it to Ines first in order to protect the finding. She had
said it in order to protect the *asker*.

Because a person who takes a number to Ines Varga gets a story. Ottoline had been
imagining that she was taking a number to a woman who would say *you're wrong* or *you're
right*, and what would actually have happened is that she would have taken a number to a
woman who would have found an entirely true and entirely different thing to say about it, and
that thing would have been about 2022, and it would have been true, and it would have been
about eleven descents in nine weeks, and Ottoline would have taken it as confirmation and
would have made a film.

Ottoline put her head back against the box and sat on the floor of a store room and
understood that a professional she had met nine days ago had protected her from herself by
telling her not to do a piece of work, and had done it by framing it as a copyright problem.

---

She drove to the shop at half past four on the Tuesday because she had nothing else to do
and because the flat was worse.

"Fabiola, is she—"

"In the back."

"I'll wait out front."

"You'll not wait out front," said Fabiola, "because it's raining and because you look like
somebody who's been in a building with no windows and I'd like to give you a tea and then
you can go and be in the back as well, and if she doesn't want you she can say so and you
can go, and I've been in this shop for forty-one years and I can tell the difference between a
person who's visiting and a person who's waiting and you are not waiting, you're visiting, and
visiting people get tea."

So Ottoline Marrow went into the back of Renn and Daughters and sat on an upturned crate
with a cup of tea and did not say anything for about six minutes.

Ines was at a bench with a piece of kit in pieces in front of her and a diagram she had drawn
herself, which is what Ines did instead of reading a manual, and it was a good diagram, and
Ottoline had learnt in the last fortnight that Ines Varga drew a diagram of anything that
came through that shop that had more than four parts.

"You've had a good morning," said Ines, not looking up.

"I've had a strange one."

"Mm." She did not look up. "Tove rang."

"Tove rang you?"

"Tove rings me on a Sunday about once a month and I have never once picked up before the
fourth ring and I picked up on the second." Ines put a small part down and picked up a
different small part. "She said you'd done four hours of good work and got nothing, and she
said you got nothing *cleanly*, which is a compliment in her language, and then she said she
was sorry about the way she'd asked you not to do something, and that she'd asked you not to
do it for a reason that wasn't the reason she'd given."

"How did she know what you were going to find?"

"She didn't," said Ines. "She knew what you were going to *do*, which is different, and she'd
rather have it." She turned a part over. "She was quite pleased with herself about it. She
rang to tell me about being pleased with herself, which she's never done, and I told her to
get off the telephone and she didn't."

There was a silence of about eleven seconds, and it was the most ordinary silence in the
world, and it was in a back room of a shop on a Tuesday, and it had tea in it.

Ines Varga drew a diagram of a thing that did not exist, at a bench, in a shop, eleven
kilometres from a hole in a field, on the twenty-ninth of October, and she had gone to work
that day the way she went to work every day, and she was not going to be told anything by
anybody that week, and this was fine, and this was the arrangement she had built and it was
holding, and it was going to hold for another nine days.

"Thank you," said Ottoline, for the tea.

"It's tea," said Fabiola, from the doorway, and went out, and shut the door, which she never
did, and which both of them noticed, and neither of them said anything about, and which was
the third time in six weeks and which was becoming a fact about a room in a shop.

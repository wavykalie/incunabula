# Chapter Twenty-Six — Eleven Minutes, After

Eleven minutes is not a long time. Hana Vogel had been told so by a lawyer once, in 2023, in
a room with a table and a recorder and a person who took notes, and had not disagreed, and
had thought about it every day since.

The roof had stopped moving at 14:41. She had been on the surface end of the line, at
thirty-one metres, which is where you are when the pitch below you is in the fourth chamber
and the person at the other end is the person who is going to have to go in, if anybody goes
in. She had heard it stop. That was the thing: it had made a noise for a considerable period
and then it had stopped, and the stopping was the worst sound of her professional life,
because a roof that is moving is a roof with a job to do and a roof that has stopped has
either settled or it hasn't.

Then there were two voices on the radio and one of them was shouting.

The call had been at 14:29, so the whole thing was twelve minutes from the call to the reach,
and eleven minutes from the stop. Those are the numbers in the file and the numbers are
correct and she has never once found them to be anything other than correct, and she has read
the file as often as Ines has and for a completely different reason.

The reason was this: eleven minutes is the correct professional time. Not fast. Not slow. The
calculation is floor, pitch, chamber, and the chamber is the variable, and the chamber that
day was nine metres down off the second pitch and had a scree slope on the left-hand side of
it that in 2022 was described in the third edition as *unstable, avoid*.

*Hana had read that line. Hana had read that line on the Thursday, at a table, with a woman
she respected, and had said nothing, because the survey that produced that line was Solveig
Havelock's and it was a good line and the survey was sound.*

She had also not said, on the Thursday, that the line said *avoid* and not *do not enter*,
and that those two words are not the same, and that in a system where a person has been in
that chamber before, *unstable, avoid* becomes a thing you read and then go in anyway because
you have done it before and it was fine.

She had thought about that on the Thursday, and had not said it, and had gone to a callout at
three, and the roof came in at twenty past two.

That was the sentence. That was the one she had not said to anybody, including herself, in a
form she was willing to use, and it was the reason she had not taken Ines Varga's telephone
calls for four years, and it was not in the file, and it could not be in the file, because the
file records what people did and she had done nothing.

---

The four years after were not a period of grief. Hana Vogel had been very clear about that
with the two people who had tried to arrange it for her — a woman from the association in
2022 and a woman from a service in 2023 — and had been courteously immovable both times, and
had then discovered that the alternative to a period of grief was not a period of competence.

It was a period of Tuesdays.

She had done the check on the Tuesday for four years. She had done the log. She had driven to
calls. She had been the last voice on six callouts in that time, which is more than average,
and had been told that by two different people in two different tones, one of which was
concerned.

The thing nobody says about a job like hers is that it is built out of the last ninety
seconds, and that the last ninety seconds is the only part anybody ever sees, and that
somebody has to do everything else for the ninety seconds to be worth anything. She checked
eleven items on a board in the order they were written, once a week, for four years, and the
checking had never once prevented anything, and she had done it every week, and if she had
stopped doing it then she would have had to think about why she was staying, and she was not
going to think about that, so she did the check.

The van's wet patch was on the passenger side. It had been there since 2021. She had assessed
it in 2021. It had not changed. In 2023 she had been offered a newer one at a discount she
had declined, and the reason she had declined was the lease, and the reason the lease was a
reason was the fleet manager, and she had worked for a fleet manager once, for nine months, in
2016, and had spent those nine months being the person who was managed, and had decided
something about it that she had never articulated and which came out as a flat no.

---

The thing she had been not saying was not about Ines.

That was the part Hana Vogel had got wrong for four years, and she had got it wrong
privately, at night, in a station, in the only place where there was nobody to see her be
wrong.

She had assumed she had not taken the calls because of the roof.

The roof was a real thing. The roof was the most real thing in the world and it was in a
folder with a date on it. But the calls came from a woman who was not in the room. Ines
Varga had been fifteen metres away on a line, and had come out, and had driven home, and had
rung the station four times in the first year, and had said the same thing every time, which
was that the call had gone correctly and that the eleven minutes had been correct and that
there was nothing in it that needed discussing, and that the correct amount of discussion was
none, and that Ines would be grateful if Hana agreed.

And Hana had said, on all four occasions, *I'd like to ring you back*, and had rung back
twice, and had said, both times, *there's nothing to discuss*, and had put the phone down.

And what that was, when it was looked at properly — and it had taken four years, and a
fortnight in which Ines Varga had come back underground and had been extremely difficult in
car parks — was that she had not been able to bear being forgiven.

Because that was what it was. Ines had been offering, in four telephone calls over a year, to
let it go, professionally, cleanly, between two people who had both done their jobs, and
Hana had declined to let her, four times, without ever once saying that was what she was
doing.

She was better at people than Tove Marsk was, and it had taken her eleven years to work that
out, and the working-out had happened entirely in the last three weeks, and had happened
because a woman of twenty-two had asked her a question in a layby about whether she wanted to
be in a hole with a person, and had not wanted a comfortable answer, and had been so
uncomfortable that Hana had gone and sat in her van for four minutes.

---

She rang the shop on the Thursday because the certificate needed a third name and she had
decided on the Tuesday night that she would ring the shop and not ring Ines, and had thought
about that decision for about eleven minutes and had then decided not to think about it any
further because she was forty minutes into a decision and there was nothing to be gained.

"Fabiola."

"Hana." A pause. "You've been four years."

"I've been fine."

"You've been *extremely* fine," said Fabiola, "for four years, and I have watched you not
take one telephone call from the best person who ever worked in this shop, and I want you to
know that I have not mentioned it once, and I want you to know why I haven't."

"Because you knew."

"Because I knew and because it wasn't mine," said Fabiola. "Right. I've got the certificate
in front of me. You want the third name."

"Yes."

"No," said Fabiola.

Hana Vogel stood in a station second bay with a telephone against her ear and did not say
anything for about three seconds.

"You haven't heard what I'm going to ask."

"You said you were ringing the shop and not ringing Ines, and you have never in four years
rung this shop about anything that wasn't about a gas bottle, and I have known you since 2011."
Fabiola's voice did not change. "You're going to ask me whether I'll be on the surface end on
the nineteenth. And I'm going to say no, and I'm going to say no before you ask, and I'm
going to say no because if I say no after you've asked then it's about you, and if I say no
before then it's about the shop, and it is about the shop."

"It's not about the shop."

"No," said Fabiola. "It isn't. That's why I'm saying no. If it was about the shop I'd let you
put me on it, because I'm sixty-one and I've been on a hill in worse weather than that with a
worse person, and I'd have been a pair of hands and I'd have been glad to be."

"Then what is it about."

And Fabiola Renn, in a shop, eleven kilometres from a hole in a field, said the thing she had
been building since about four o'clock on the Tuesday afternoon when Hana Vogel had come in
and told her the number out loud for the first time in four years.

"It's about a woman called Solveig who sat in this shop in November and said *somebody's going
to read that book*, and you have been not-taking that woman's telephone calls since March, and
I have been not-asking you why, and I am asking you why now, and I would like you to tell me
the truth, because I have had four years of not-asking and I have reached the end of it."

Hana Vogel sat down on the bench in the second bay.

The check was on the board. The shelter note was on the board. The van had a wet patch.

"Because she keeps offering to let it go," said Hana, "and I keep not letting her."

"Thank you," said Fabiola.

"Are you—" Hana stopped and started again. "Fab. Is that it? Is that a thank you for saying
it, or is that a thank you because I've finally said something and now you can get on with
it?"

There was a pause of about four seconds on the line, which was long, and which was Fabiola
Renn deciding, and which Hana Vogel could hear.

"It's a thank you because you said it," said Fabiola. "It's not a thank you because we're
friends. I want to be clear about that, because I'm about to say something that isn't kind
and I'd like us to be straight about what we are first. I liked her. I did not like you. I
had no reason to like you and you were competent and calm and you were on my telephone the
whole time, and I have thought about that a great deal since March and I have arrived at
something I'm not proud of and which I think is true, so I'm going to say it and you're going
to dislike me and that's the correct outcome."

"Fabiola—"

"I was pleased you were on that call," said Fabiola Renn. "Not relieved. Pleased. Because it
meant somebody competent was between me and a hole, and it meant that when I found out
afterwards what had happened, I was not the last person in the county to know, and that is
the most selfish sentence I have ever said out loud and I have been in business for
forty-one years and I am not going to pretend it was anything else."

The station was very quiet.

"Ask me again on the sixth," said Fabiola. "After the fourth edition submission. If it's still
you asking, and if it's still about the nineteenth, and if in the meantime you have told
somebody else the truth about a telephone call, then I'll say yes and I'll be on that hill
and I will be the best pair of hands you've ever had and I will not once mention any of this."

"Hana—"

"That's the arrangement," said Fabiola. "And I want it on the telephone, and I want you to
write it in the log afterwards with the time on it, because you write everything in that log
and I have read it, and if this is in the log then it's real and if it's not in the log then
it was a conversation between two women on a telephone at half past four in the afternoon,
and I've had four years of those."

Hana Vogel stood in a station second bay and wrote it down.

*1638. V. Fab R. Telephone. Third name, 19 Nov. Answer: no, until 6 Nov.*

And then, underneath, in brackets:

*(her condition, not mine. will meet it. telling somebody else before then.)*

She read that back, and thought about who, and it took about four seconds, and it was not a
hard question, and she had known the answer to it since the Wednesday before last in a shop
on a wet afternoon with a piece of paper with an empty box in the corner of it.

She rang Marisol Trent's office at five to five, and got the answerphone, and left a message
of about ninety words which was the first time in four years that Hana Vogel had said a
sentence out loud to a machine with the intention of it being heard by Ines Varga, and it was
not the sentence she had spent four years assembling.

It was: *You should know that I am asking for the surface end of the line on the nineteenth,
and that I have been not-taking a call for four years and it wasn't about the roof, and that I
am telling you that on an answerphone because I can't say it to your face yet, and I would
like to be able to, and I'm working on it.*

Then she put the phone down, and did item eleven, which was the log, and which took four
lines, and then sat on the bench for a while with the second bay being the only lit part of a
building above a shop that sold fluorescent fittings.

It was ten past five on the Thursday of the sixth of November. There were thirteen days.

Somewhere on the far side of the county, a woman in a shop with a mug of cold tea she had not
noticed going cold was doing a diagram of a thing that did not exist, and did not know that
anybody was coming, and had not known since the Tuesday, and had gone back to work.

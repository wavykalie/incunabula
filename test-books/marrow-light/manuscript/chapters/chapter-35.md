# Chapter Thirty-Five — Out

Tove Marsk hurt her leg on the way up and did not say so for ninety minutes.

That is the whole of what went wrong on the twenty-eighth of November, and everybody in that
car park afterwards would have described the day as a success, and the success would have been
true, and the leg would have been on it and would not have been in anybody's account of it.

She put her hand on a wall at about a hundred and twenty metres, coming up at four metres an
hour, and the wall was a wall and the hand went on it and the knee went in a way that a knee
does not usually go, and Tove Marsk stood on a pitch in the dark at a hundred and twenty
metres and said nothing about it for ninety minutes, and then said one thing into a radio
which was not about her leg.

"Log a fault at thirteen forty," she said. "Shoulder. Left. I'm on the belay and I'll do the
rest myself and I don't want the line stopped for something I can do."

"Copy. Fault logged. Say if it changes."

"It won't change."

Ines Varga came up the pitch at four metres an hour with a bag of fifty metres of wool over
her shoulder and a woman who could not lift her left arm above her head.

That is what a hundred and twenty minutes is. It is not drama; it is one person on a pitch
doing a thing that has to be done, and one person standing above her on a line who has a
problem with a shoulder and is refusing to have it, and the refusing is what keeps a pitch
moving, and everybody who was there understood that on the day and had argued with her about
it for a decade.

---

They came out at 15:41.

The pitch, the field, the shut gate, the grass, the van: one hour and thirty-one minutes from
a hundred and twenty metres with a fault on the shoulder, and the last hundred metres of it
was a woman in a rack walking on a wall because a knee was not doing what knees do.

Hana Vogel was at the top of the pitch at 15:39 with a first aid kit she had carried up a
hillside in a clear evening because she had carried one up a hillside every callout for
eleven years and had done it on the nineteenth of November when nobody had asked her to.

"Right," she said, at the top of the pitch, to two women, one of whom was a casualty and one
of whom was not. "Leg."

"Leg," said Tove.

"Leg," agreed Ines, who had a fifty-metre coil on her shoulder and a person in front of her,
and who then said, "Tove, put your weight on the van and don't sit down, sit's how it goes
wrong."

"Sit's the only comfortable thing available."

"Sit's the only comfortable thing available and you're forty-eight and you've just done a
hundred and twenty metres of pitch and I'm not having you on a stretcher on the road because
you were comfortable for four minutes." Ines put the coil down, which she did not do, and
which Hana noticed, and which Hana was going to think about for a long time. "Lean. Lean's
fine."

Tove leaned.

That was the injury. It was a knee and a shoulder and it was an old knee and it was the kind
of knee that a person who has spent sixteen years in a rack has, and it went in about four
seconds and it came out about three weeks later, and it was entirely ordinary, and it was on
the morning of the twenty-eighth of November the only thing in the Vell that anybody could
have called an accident.

There was no stretcher. There was a van and a boot and a woman who knew how to drive a van
and a man called Petar who put the cushions in the back because he had put cushions in the
back of a van for nineteen years and he did it before anybody asked him to.

---

At the layby at ten past four there were eleven people, and two of them were sitting on the
bonnet of a van because one of them had a knee.

Fabiola Renn had arrived at half past three in a car and had been at the top of the field
since one o'clock and had brought the flask and had not gone down, and had not offered, and
had been told not to, and had stood in a field in the rain for two and a half hours because
that was what he had been told to do and because nobody had told him anything else.

He was sitting on the wall of the layby and he did not get up when the van came in, and he
looked at Tove Marsk's leg for about four seconds, and he said:

"That's the knee you did in 2019."

"Yes."

"You told me it was the other one."

"I told you it was a knee," said Tove. "You heard a knee."

"I heard a knee," Fabiola agreed. "I've been hearing knees from you for six years."

Marisol Trent was at the top of the field with a flask and no recorder and had said nothing
for two hours, and she came down the field at a speed that was not appropriate to her age or
to the ground, and she got to the layby and she did not ask how anybody was, because she had
been a teacher for twenty years and she knew what a room of people who have just come out of
a hole sounds like.

She said, "The number," to Hana.

"Twenty-two hundred at one o'clock. Fourteen hundred at two. Six hundred at three. Four at
ten past three, and steady since. The system had it in the fourth chamber at ten past twelve
— correction, at 12:50, and Varga was at two hundred and four."

"12:50," said Marisol, and wrote it on the back of her hand, and then looked at the hand, and
then said, "Was she in the room?"

"Fourteen minutes past the cap," said Hana.

"For fourteen minutes."

"Yes."

"And the cap was yours."

"Yes."

Marisol Trent stood in a layby at ten past four in the afternoon on the twenty-eighth of
November with a number on the back of her hand and did not say anything for about eleven
seconds, and the eleven seconds were the most useful thing she did in the entire engagement.

"Right," she said. "That's mine. I've put a cap on a descent I had no authority to put on a
descent and a woman went fourteen minutes past it, and the fourteen minutes were known to
three people and logged, and I'll write it up tonight and it will be one page and it will be
in the file, and nobody is going to soften it and nobody is going to blame her, because she
told me on the twenty-sixth in a room that she'd say so and she said so at 12:51 on a radio
to a woman in a cave, and that's the sentence I would put in the report and it's the only one
that matters."

"Sit down, Marisol."

"I will in a moment." She looked at Tove's leg, and at Ines, who was standing, and at Hana,
who was standing, and at the eleven people in a layby. "What's the count?"

"What count?"

"The count, Hana. It's the number everybody in this car park has been not asking about since
the fifteenth of March, and I've read four reports and a folder and a delivery sheet and a
woman in a shop said a word to a woman in a car park, and I have not asked you once, because
I have been waiting for somebody to say it to me properly, in daylight, with everybody
listening, which is exactly the thing I have been telling the two of you to do since the
nineteenth of November."

The layby was very quiet.

"Say it," said Marisol Trent, "or tell me to leave, and I'll go, and I won't write it in the
report, and I'll take it away with me and it'll be the only number I've never had."

Hana Vogel put the radio down on the bonnet of a van.

"Seventeen," she said.

Ottoline Marrow, who was standing nine feet away with a camera in a bag and had been
listening to a number come in off a radio, said, quietly, "How do you know it's seventeen?"

"Because I counted them," said Hana. "Every descent since the fourth of November, off the
plate, off the number on the plate, off the days. Seventeen descents in fifty-six days, and
the plate has been correct for all fifty-six, and I read it aloud in a car park on the
twenty-eighth and said it was twelve, and it was twelve, and it is the best news any of us
has had since 2011, and it is also seventeen."

"That number is mine," said Ines Varga. "Say it again in a car park and it stops being mine."

"It stopped being yours the moment Hana said it on a radio in front of nine people," said
Ottoline. "I'm sorry. That was — I know. I know what I've just said and I'm not going to
apologise for it, because it's the second time I've been the one who says the true thing at
the wrong volume in a car park, and I'm the only person in this layby who has a camera, and
that's not an accident and everybody here knows it isn't."

Nobody argued with her about it, which is the single most damning thing that happened in that
car park on the twenty-eighth of November and which was not minuted, because there was no
recorder, because Marisol Trent had decided in a room on the twenty-sixth that this one was
not going on a recording.

"Seventeen," said Tove Marsk, from the bonnet of a van, with a leg, at ten past four in the
afternoon. "Right. Well. That's a number."

"It's a number," said Hana.

"It's a *record*," said Tove, "and it's in a book, and it's in a plate, and it's on a
frequency, and it's in about six people's heads and about four hours of tape, and there is
not one single thing we can do with it that makes it a fact, and we are going to go on saying
seventeen for the rest of our lives."

"It's a fact," said Marisol Trent. "It became a fact at four o'clock this afternoon in front
of nine witnesses and I am going to write it in a report and it is going to be wrong in
detail and right in the only way that matters, and I am going to do that tonight, and
nobody is going to stop me, and if anybody in this car park wants to stop me they can do it
properly and in writing and it will be a document I have to answer."

Nobody stopped her.

"Right," said Marisol, and got into a car.

The last thing she said, to Ines, from the driver's window of a car on a hillside above a shut
gate, was this:

"I put a cap on a descent. A woman went fourteen minutes past it and told me she was going
to. The water came in four hours later and there was nobody in it. I have been trying to stop
this since September and I have failed completely and I have no idea whether I was right to
try." She put a hand on the door. "Page two hundred and nine. I looked it up on the Sunday.
It's a very good page and you're a very good hydrologist and I am *sorry*, and I want to say
that to you in a car park rather than in a form, and I'd like to be allowed to have said it
in a form if you ever want it in a form."

Ines Varga stood in a layby and let the car go.

She said, "The gauge said twelve."

"It did."

"That's the only part of any of this I can live with," said Ines. "Four hours."

"Four hours," said Marisol Trent, from a car window, and drove away down a track in the rain
with a number on the back of her hand, and did not put the radio on, because there was
nobody to talk to and she had said everything she had.

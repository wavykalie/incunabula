# 22 — The Name

She did not open with a question. That was decided the night before, sitting at her kitchen table with the twelve exhibits in a row and the card with the name face down: no question, because questions give a man somewhere to stand. Paper first. Let the paper do the standing.

She arrived at the depot at eight and laid the exhibits in the interview room herself, in order, in a row along the table's edge — determination, receipt, March page, list, statement, rota copy, coroner's sheets, chair frames, photographs. Twelve pieces. She checked the recorder twice, with a fresh tape and a spare, and tested the clock against her watch: 08:40 both. Whitlock came in with the log book and two pens.

"Order?" he said.

"As laid. I state each one. He looks. I wait for his eyes before the next. No questions until the photographs."

"And if he walks?"

"He will not walk." She squared the folder. "He came voluntarily. They always come voluntarily. That is the finding underneath all the others — ask, and they come. I am counting on it holding one more time, in this room, this morning."

Whitlock signed the log's first line and dated it. "For what it is worth — you have enough. Even if he says nothing. The paper holds without him."

"I know." She looked at the row of exhibits, each bagged, each initialled. "But CONF-004 needs the learning on the page, not just the naming. He has to hear the stairs from me, in front of you, with the tape turning. Otherwise the file shows leniency with no basis, and the reader — " She stopped. "The file needs it. That is all."

At ten past ten the front desk rang up: Terrell Vance, in his own car, asking for her by name — her given name, Delphine, which only he and her mother used, and which sounded strange in Fenwick's mouth over the speaker. Rusk felt something cold go down her spine at the sound of it through the grille. Eight minutes early. Composed. Ready. She squared the folder one final time, checked the tape was loaded, and nodded. Whitlock went to fetch him, his shoes loud in the corridor and then quiet on the interview-room lino. Rusk stood behind the table with her hands flat on its edge until they came in, and then she sat, and squared the folder, and began.

The room was the interview room at the depot — table, three chairs, the recorder she had tested twice, the window high and barred, the clock with the second hand that ticked loud enough to hear on tape. Terrell Vance sat across from her with his hands flat on the table, calm, warm, present the way he was always present. He had come voluntarily, at ten, in his own car. They always came voluntarily. That was the finding that underlay all the others, and she carried it into the room with her like a sidearm.

Whitlock sat in the corner with the log. He nodded to her. She nodded back. She set the folder on the table, squared it, and switched the recorder on.

"Terrell Vance, present voluntarily," she said for the tape, with the date and the time: Monday, 10:04. "I am going to lay out papers. I want you to look at each one. You do not have to speak until I am finished."

"Del." He smiled — genuine, affectionate, the warmth that was real and therefore terrible. "Whatever you need. Take your time."

She set them down one by one, in order, stating each for the recorder, placing each squarely before him and waiting until his eyes moved to it before laying the next.

"The 2019 determination. Four lines." Her voice level. "It cites a kitchen circuit. The panel was replaced in 2016. There is no such circuit. I signed it when I was thirty-one. You should know that first — that the file's first wrong thing is mine."

He looked at it. He did not touch it. His hands stayed flat.

"The April receipt. Two hundred and sixty gallons. Cash. No vehicle. No address." She set the bagged slip down. "Bought from Denny. Loaded into a truck that is not his. County plates with the wrong start. Counted twice, in two piles."

"The March 2019 page. Three hundred and forty gallons. Same terms. Same hand selling, same kind of hand buying." Denny's page, marked with slips. "Six years apart. Same habit."

"Sam's list." The lined sheet, the seven names. "Seven names, kept six years in a baptism ledger. You were with friends that night, Terrell. Your statement says so. The friends are a man in Oslo since 2017, a child of nine, and your mother. Your mother was alone that week. Your brother was out of the country. Lindqvist says you were never in her house. Verhage says you were never in his."

"Your statement." She laid it beside the list. "In your hand. Complete. In order. Filled standing up at the counter on the Monday after. Fenwick remembers — you said nothing. Nodded. Polite."

"The rota on the back of Denny's page. Engine one. Roy's hand." She set the copy down. "He was four streets away, in Verhage's kitchen, when the tones went. He heard a name and drove like a man going to a house. Three minutes fifty. He went in as far as the kitchen and did not open the study door. He has spent six years not opening doors."

"The coroner's finding." The two sheets, clipped. She laid them last, side by side, the way Adela laid them. "One person died in 2019. A woman of fifty-nine. Blunt trauma from a fall on the stairs, hours before the fire. The fire was set afterwards, in a separate act. Dated, timestamped, in a system outside the county's control. 11 March 2019."

"The chair." The measurements, the four frames. "Moved and moved back, both fires. Eighteen degrees off the table edge. Your habit, Terrell. You move furniture without looking — you did it in my flat last Christmas, you did it in Cora's kitchen in front of me, you did it in this room already since you sat down." She nodded at his chair, which stood at its own small wrong angle where he had straightened it on sitting. "Rooms you know blindfolded."

Terrell looked at the chair. He smiled briefly — genuinely, helplessly — the way people smile when they are caught by something small and true.

"The pour." The two origin photographs, side by side, last of all. "In both houses. Same V. Same edge. Same room with no beds — the study, where a family keeps the things it means to keep. Poured, not spread. Pooled, not carried. Started where nobody sleeps, because somebody knew the sleeping pattern from inside."

She set her hands flat on the table, the way Denny set his when he was going to be careful.
Whitlock turned a log page. The clock ticked. Outside, a truck reversed in the depot yard,
alarm beeping, three times, gone.

"Terrell Vance," she said. "You lit the 2025 fire. Three people were inside. You checked the door and not the windows. You started it in the study. You lit the 2019 fire the same way, six years ago, in the same room type, on the same block. Same perpetrator. Same signature. Six years apart."

Silence. The recorder turned. The clock ticked. Whitlock's pen waited above the log.

Terrell looked at the photographs for a long time — longer than at any other paper. His hands, flat on the table, began to press, the fingers whitening. When he spoke, his sentences did not close; they trailed into the room the way they always did, forward and conditional, unfinished by nature.

"When it's done," he said softly, "if nobody — " He stopped. He looked at his hands as if they belonged to someone else. "Del. You should eat before you — there's going to be a lot of driving and you're not going to remember to, you never — "

"Terrell." She kept her voice low. "Stay with the papers."

"I'm going to go help Cora with the — " He was halfway standing, his chair scraping, before he caught himself — the trailing into action, live, in front of two witnesses. He sat back down slowly and laughed at himself, quietly, breathlessly. "There. That's the whole thing. That's what I do. You have it on tape now."

"The name is stated," Rusk said for the recorder, with the time: 10:31. "Terrell Vance, present voluntarily, has heard the evidence as laid. The determination will record him as responsible for the 2025 fire and the 2019 fire — same perpetrator, same signature, six years apart."

He nodded. He did not deny it. He did not confess in words either — he never explained the fire, not then, not ever; explaining was not in him. But the nod was on tape, with twelve exhibits on the table, and the nod was enough for the file. Whitlock wrote it down — nod, 10:31, no words — and underlined it twice, the way he underlined everything that mattered.

Rusk gave him a minute. The clock ticked. The recorder turned. Outside, the yard went quiet between trucks. She poured water from the jug on the side table — her hands steady, his shaking — and set the cup before him, and waited while he drank half of it in small swallows, the way shock drinks.

"What was in the study in 2019?" she asked then. Quieter now. The exhibits were done; this was the room after the exhibits.

He looked up. His face was open, unguarded, the warmth still there underneath the shock like a pilot light.

"Papers," he said. "The decision. The care agreement. The thing Cora signed — the thing she was afraid would be read as her fault. The rug. The light. She said burn the papers, burn the question. She meant the study. Only the study."

"Who died, Terrell?"

His face changed. Not fear. Not grief, not yet. A blankness — like a wall meeting a wall, like a man looking at a door he has walked past a thousand times and finding it locked.

"I don't know," he said. "There was nobody — it was empty, Del, it was supposed to be empty. Cora was alone that week. The house was dark. I made sure. I checked the door — " His voice quickened, the forward tense accelerating. "I checked, I always check, the study was empty, nobody sleeps there, that is why I chose it, don't you see, I chose it because it was safe — "

"One person," Rusk said. She made herself say it plainly, no softening, because softening would have been a lie about a fact. "A woman of fifty-nine. She fell on the stairs in the afternoon and struck her head. She was dead before the fire. At the foot of the stairs. While you poured in the study."

Terrell stared at the photographs. His hands began to shake — visibly, violently, the table trembling under them. Whitlock shifted in his chair.

"No," Terrell said. "No, that's — Del, no, there was nobody in there, I checked, I — " He looked up at her, and his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic that would not come out. "The stairs? She was — I never went upstairs. I never looked. Cora said empty. The house was dark. I — "

He put his head down on the table, among the papers — his cheek near the photographs, his hands over his head — and wept without sound. His shoulders shook. The chair creaked. Rusk let the recorder run. She did not touch him. Touching would have been comfort and comfort would have been a statement, and the room was for facts. She sat across from her nephew in an interview room with twelve exhibits on the table and the name stated and documented, and waited while he learned, on the page, in front of two witnesses, with the tape turning, that the 2019 fire he lit had held a body he never knew about.

That was F5's closure requirement, delivered here and not later: the case closed, the culprit named, the mechanism whole, the cost learned on the page rather than reported. CONF-004's whole basis — responsible but not the killer for 2019 only — lived or died in this room, in this hour, in whether the learning happened in front of someone. It happened. Whitlock's log would show it. The tape would hold it.

She stopped the recorder at 11:20 — seventy-six minutes, one tape, no breaks — and signed the log with the stop time. Whitlock signed under her, then went for water while she stayed with Terrell, who had not moved except to breathe. She sat with him while he drank it in small sips, his hands still shaking around the cup, water spilling onto the photographs, which she moved aside without comment and dried later with her sleeve.

She told him plainly what came next — the holding room with its blanket and its barred high window, the counsel call she would make herself within the hour from her own office, the file going to the attorney that afternoon by her own hand, the counsel's advice to say nothing further until he arrived. She told it the way she told everything plainly: in order, with times, no softening, because plain was the only kindness she had that was not also evasion, and he deserved at least the kindness she actually owned.

"Del," he said when she finished. He was looking at the water, not at her. "When it's — will Cora — "

"Cora is at home. No one is going to her today. Tomorrow I go, alone, with no paper." She capped her pen. "That is a promise. It is the only one I am making."

Whitlock came back with the escort officer — a young woman, kind-eyed, who had waited in the corridor the whole seventy-six minutes without once knocking. Terrell stood — trailing, unfinished, his chair at its small wrong angle — and went out between them without resistance, nodding to Rusk once as he passed. In the corridor, she heard the young officer say, "This way, sir, mind the step," with a gentleness that nearly undid her. Kindness, still, everywhere, even now. The town's habit surviving its exposure. The nod was not forgiveness and not blame. It was acknowledgement: the papers, the tape, the stairs. It would stay with her longer than the exhibits.

Then she walked out into February with the file under her arm — the twelve exhibits back in their bags, the log signed twice, the tape labelled and sealed — and stood in the depot car park with the cold coming through her coat, and understood that naming him had been the easy part. The exhibits did the naming. Twelve pieces of paper, each handed over voluntarily, each logged and countersigned. The cost was still to come — his, hers, Cora's — in the next room, on the next page, in the months with no deadline that started now. She sat in the car with the heater going and wrote the interview up from memory before driving anywhere, the way procedure required and the way memory required: times, papers, the nod, the water on the photographs. Then she filed it, and telephoned counsel as promised within the hour, and logged the call with its time, and only then drove home past nine identical houses without counting them.

# 24 — What Terrell Wanted

The motive, laid out whole, with no forgiveness attached to it. That was the brief Rusk set herself the night before, sitting with the interview notes and the tape log: whole, because the file needed the whole chain, and no forgiveness, because forgiveness was not hers to put in a file. The file could hold facts. What anyone did with them afterwards was not paper.

Terrell sat in Rusk's office the next afternoon — not the interview room, her office, with the window looking onto the depot yard and the plant on the sill that Fenwick kept alive for her out of a stubbornness about living things — with a blanket round his shoulders that Whitlock had brought from the locker, drinking tea he did not taste. His counsel sat beside him for the first ten minutes, confirming for the notes that the conversation was voluntary, unprompted, and without inducement, the recorder off by agreement, notes permitted — then withdrew to the corridor with his own cup, leaving the door ajar the exact six inches he specified. Whitlock took the corner chair with the notebook. The rain had stopped overnight; the yard steamed faintly in the morning sun, and the light came through the blinds in slats across the desk, striping the notebook she would fill completely by noon. His counsel had been, had introduced himself, had permitted an informal conversation with the recorder off and notes allowed. This was not an interview. The interview was yesterday's tape. This was the room after.

"Tell me about Cora," Rusk said. She set her notebook open but her pen down, to show it was conversation first.

He held the cup with both hands, the way cold people hold cups. "When it's going to — " He stopped. Started again, slower. "If nobody does anything, then she — then they take everything. The house. The name. That is what she said. That is what she has always said, since I was small: they take the house first, and the name goes with it."

"Back up, Terrell. 2019. The beginning. Who telephoned whom?"

He drank. The tea steamed his face. Outside, rain moved across the depot yard in sheets.

"She telephoned me," he said. "Afternoon of the twelfth — the day after. I was at work, the yard, loading. She said come, now, do not telephone anyone. I drove. The house was — " He looked at the window. "She was in the kitchen. The tap running. A woman on the stairs. I saw her from the doorway. Fifty-nine. Grey. Still."

Rusk waited. The office clock ticked — hers, not her mother's; true time.

"She said the woman fell. Afternoon. The light out on the landing — it had been out for weeks, she kept meaning to change the bulb. The rug loose at the top. She found her at six, coming up with laundry. Tried to lift her. Could not." His voice stayed warm, level, terrible in its gentleness. "She said, they will call it neglect. They will say a seventy-four-year-old woman alone with a loose rug and a dead bulb killed her guest. They will take the house. They will make it my fault in a document."

"So she asked you to burn the papers."

"She said burn the papers, burn the question." He nodded. "The care agreement in the study. The thing she signed when the woman came to stay — the private arrangement, the money, all of it on paper in the study. She meant the study. Only the study. A small fire, she said. Enough for the papers. The house is brick. It will hold."

"And you poured."

"I bought fuel first." He set the cup down. It rattled against the saucer. "March. From Denny. Three-forty gallons — too much, I know that now, I did not know it then, I thought more was safer, more certain. Cash. Farm tank, I said. Twice, because I wanted him to hear it. He heard. He wrote it down. He remembers the coat." A breath. "I poured in the study on the night of the eleventh — no. Wait. Sequence." He corrected himself, the way Rusk corrected herself, and she felt something twist at the resemblance. "I poured on the night of the eleventh. In the study. I moved the chair so I could get the can in close. I put it back — I always put things back, you know that, Del, you have seen me. I lit it with the barbecue lighter from the kitchen drawer. I left through the front. I drove four streets and parked on Lindqvist's corner and walked back to watch."

"You watched."

"I watched Roy come in three minutes fifty." His face changed — admiration, grief, something between. "He went in. He came out. He stood in the road. I watched the whole street come out. I stood on the corner in the dark and watched my aunt's street fill with trucks, and I felt — " He stopped. "Relieved. That is the truth. Relieved. It was done. The question was closed."

"You knew the house was empty."

"I believed it." He picked the cup up again for something to hold. "Cora said empty. The house was dark — the kitchen dark, the upstairs dark. I checked the door, not the windows. I checked the study — empty, papers everywhere, nobody, of course nobody, nobody sleeps there. I started it in the room with no beds because nobody sleeps there, nobody could be there, it was the safe room, don't you see?" He looked at her directly for the first time. "I chose it because it was safe. That is the part I cannot — I chose the safe room. On purpose. To hurt nothing but paper."

Rusk waited. The rain hit the window. The plant dripped where Fenwick had overwatered it.

"She was not in the study," Terrell whispered. "The dead woman. She was at the foot of the stairs where Cora left her because Cora could not lift her and panicked and went to the kitchen to think. I never went upstairs. I never looked. I lit a fire to cover a fall and the fall was already a death and I did not know — " His voice broke, the warmth cracking for the first time since childhood, she imagined. "Del, I swear on Cora, on my life, on anything you want, I did not know there was a second — a body, a person, anyone. The house was dark. Cora said empty. I believed her."

He broke off. He understood, finally, fully, on the page of her notebook, in front of a witness — Whitlock silent in the corner, pen moving — what the coroner's finding meant, what the basement table meant, what the stairs meant: the woman at the foot of the stairs had been dead before the fire, and he had lit a fire in a house with a body in it, not knowing, and the not-knowing was the whole basis of the only leniency the file would ever show him. Responsible but not the killer — for 2019 only. For 2019, he lit a fire he did not know was occupied. That was CONF-004's line, and it lived or died on this hour, this room, this learning happening in front of someone rather than being asserted afterwards.

Whitlock shifted in his chair — the first movement he had made in twenty minutes — and refilled his own cup from the pot, his hands steady. The clock ticked. The yard steamed. Terrell sat with his head in his hands, breathing slowly, the blanket slipping off one shoulder. Rusk replaced it without comment, the way she had replaced it twice already, the small tending that was all the comfort the room allowed.

"Take your time," she said. "The 2019 part is on the page. When you are ready — 2025. The same question. Tell me plain."

He lifted his head. His face was wet and composed at once, the way faces get when grief has been through and order is being rebuilt behind it. "2025," he said. "Yes. All right. Plain."

He was quiet for a long time. The rain eased. The clock ticked.

"2025." He refilled his own cup from the pot, his hands steadier with the task. "The town began asking again. The new house — the papers there, the same kind of arrangement, the same question waking up. Cora telephoned. She did not ask. She never asks. She said, they are asking, Terrell. That is all she said. They are asking."

"Walk me through April, Terrell. The day. Sequence first."

He set the cup down and flattened his hands on his knees — the posture of a man about to be careful. "Monday the fourteenth. Morning, about ten. I hired the truck Saturday, cash, out of county — a hire firm that asks nothing if you pay the deposit in notes. Drove it in. Denny's. Said farm tank. Once — I had practised saying it once, because twice sounds practised, I learned that from the first time. Counted the cash at home into two piles so I would not fumble. He loaded — I loaded with him, upright, bled, the way you do. Drove out. Stored the cans in the lock-up on the county road, the one with the broken padlock everyone knows about. Waited."

"How long?"

"Three weeks. Until the house was right — the family away visiting, the street quiet, a Tuesday with rain in the forecast to keep windows shut. I checked the door that night the way I checked everything: tried the handle, looked at the windows dark, listened. The study. The cans. The chair moved for room. The barbecue lighter — the same one, kept six years in a drawer, I never threw it away, that is the kind of detail you will put in the file." He looked at his hands. "Lit. Left through the front. Walked four streets to the hire truck. Drove it back the same night, cleaned it, returned it Wednesday. No record worth the name. That is the whole of April. Three weeks of waiting and one night of doing. The first time I hurried. The second time I waited. Waiting is worse. You have longer to stop."

"Why didn't you stop?"

"Because Cora telephoned the night before the Tuesday." His voice dropped. "She said, they are asking about the papers, Terrell. About the arrangement. Someone from the new house asked the county a question. That is all. They are asking. And I thought — " He stopped. Started again, trailing. "If it opens, it all opens, the stairs, the rug, the bulb, her fault in a document, the house gone, and she is seventy-four, Del, she — "

"So you decided."

"I decided." Flat. No trailing. The one finished sentence in the hour. "April. Denny. Two-sixty. The truck — a hire, county plates, cash, returned next day, no record worth the name. I loaded it myself, upright, bled, the way farmers do, the way I learned the first time. Same pour. Same room type. Same block." He looked at the window. "It worked the first time. The town agreed to stop asking. I believed agreement was permanent. That is what I told myself while I poured: this keeps the agreement."

"Tell me about the clock," Rusk said. "Your grandmother's clock. You wind it." It seemed off the subject; it was not. The small habits were where people lived.

He blinked. "The kitchen clock? At Cora's? I wind it Sundays. She likes it wound. It runs fast — eleven minutes, did you know? I never understood why she keeps it fast. I wind it anyway. Every Sunday. Even — " He stopped. "Even now. Whitlock says I can go Sundays, supervised, to wind it. I went last Sunday. She made tea. We did not talk about — " He gestured at the room, the file, everything. "We talked about the clock. She said it is fast so she is never early. I said early for what. She said, for anything. That is the most she has said in weeks."

Rusk wrote it down — winds the clock, Sundays, does not know it is fast — because the file held everything, even the things that were not evidence. Especially those. The clock was the family in miniature: Cora setting the time wrong on purpose, Terrell keeping it wound without knowing why, both tending a thing neither understood. That was the whole book in a kitchen clock, and it would never be an exhibit.

"He was finishing," she wrote below it, the line for the file, "not avenging. 2019: cover a fall, believed empty. 2025: protect the agreement. Same hand, different reasons six years apart. Winds the clock Sundays."

Terrell wept then — quietly, into his hands, the cup abandoned. "Is Cora — will she — Del, what happens to Cora?"

"Cora made it true," Rusk said. She said it the way the file would say it: exact, no softening, no edge either. "She did not light it. She telephoned, she asked, she signed the papers that made the question. That distinction is in the file. What the attorney does with it is not mine."

He nodded, accepting a mercy he had not asked for and could not use — the mercy of a distinction. He would carry the learning out of this room and into the holding room and into whatever came after: that rage dressed as loyalty had, by luck of a staircase, killed nobody in 2019, and had, by choice of a study, killed three in 2025. The first fire was a mistake built on a fall. The second was a decision built on the first. And the file would show both, whole, with no forgiveness attached, because forgiveness was not paper.

The counsel knocked then — two raps, the agreed signal — and put his head round the six-inch gap. "Five minutes," he said. "Then my client rests." Terrell nodded without looking up. The counsel withdrew. Rusk glanced at Whitlock, who tapped his watch: forty minutes used, five left. She chose the last question carefully — the one the file still lacked, the one only he could give.

"The lighter," she said. "The barbecue lighter. Where is it now?"

Terrell looked at her — surprised, almost amused, the first near-smile of the hour. "The drawer," he said. "Cora's kitchen drawer, left side, under the tea towels. I put it back. After both. I always put things back." He shook his head at himself. "You will find my prints on it. Six years apart. Same drawer. Same lighter. That is — " He stopped. "That is the whole case in a drawer, isn't it."

"Yes," Rusk said honestly. "That is the whole case in a drawer. Thank you."

The counsel returned at noon precisely, thanked Whitlock for the tea, and took his client out through the corridor with a hand on his elbow. Terrell stopped at the door — trailing, unfinished — and said, "Del. The clock. Sundays. You will — if I cannot — " She understood. "I will see it is wound," she promised. It was the second promise of the case, and like the first, she meant to keep it.

Rusk logged the conversation from the notes the same afternoon — six pages now, signed, dated, counsel's name and the six-inch door recorded verbatim, the lighter's location appended as a recovery note, Whitlock countersigning that the recorder stayed off by agreement and that tea was provided three times. She filed it behind the interview tape log, so the two rooms sat together in the file: the naming and the learning, the exhibits and the motive, 11:20 and the afternoon after. Responsible but not the killer, for 2019 only, learned on the page. For 2025, the fire killed three, and he set it knowing the house was lived in, and the murder was his — three counts, no mitigation in the file, whatever counsel argued outside it.

That evening she drove to Cora's with Whitlock and recovered the lighter from the left side of the kitchen drawer, under the tea towels, exactly where he said — bagged, sealed, logged at 19:40, Cora watching from the doorway without a word, the clock ticking eleven minutes fast above them. Thirteenth exhibit. The case in a drawer, as he said. She filed it the same night, and slept badly, and dreamed of clocks.

She sat with him until the escort came the next morning — twenty minutes, the clock ticking true time, neither speaking, the blanket slipping off his shoulders twice and her replacing it twice without comment — and watched her nephew go out the door trailing a sentence he never finished — "When it's — if Cora — " — and felt the case close around the cost the way a hand closes around a burn: completely, too late, and holding on because letting go hurts more.

# 1 — Three Inside

The form wanted her at 07:40 and she made it 07:18, which was a thing she noticed and
did not think about, and which turned out later to be the only part of that morning
that was entirely hers.

The house was four doors down from her mother's. She had driven past it twice on the
way in and neither time had she slowed, because slowing down would have been a
decision, and at twenty to eight on a Tuesday in February she was not prepared to
make decisions about her mother's street.

Three inside.

The deputy met her at the tape and gave her the three names in the flat voice people
reserve for the number three, and then gave her the address, and then said, "Nobody
else is going in," in a way that meant *nobody else is going in with you.*

"Good," Rusk said.

She wrote 07:18 on the sheet — the time in large figures, underlined once. She wrote the weather,
which was overcast and not cold, the cloud low and unbroken, no wind to move smoke anywhere it
did not want to go. She wrote the structure type, single-family residential, and the year of
construction, which was 1968, which was also the year of her mother's house, which meant
nothing except that this county built the same house twice and had not moved on. The deputy's
pen scratched beside her. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and stopped.

Then she signed the certification at the top of page one, which is a different
document from the one she was here to do, and which she had been handed by a man at
the office at six-forty in the morning because it needed a signature from an
investigator before the removal contractor could be let through the tape, and which
she had signed at 07:18 without reading, because it was a release.

That was the thing she would be asked about, later, by someone who was not unkind
about it, and she would not have a good answer. She had signed a release twenty
minutes before she had walked the scene. There was no procedural reason. There was a
procedural reason: it was always a release, it was always the same form, it existed
so that debris could be moved and the families could be told, and if she did not sign
it the house would sit for three more days with three people in it.

She had signed nothing at all in 2019. That was the other thing she would be asked
about, and she had a better answer to it, and the answer turned out to be worse.

---

The door was standing open, which it should not have been, and which told her the
structure had shifted. She went in low and to the left, which is habit and not
caution, and the habit is eleven years old and the caution is six.

Heat is the first thing. Everybody expects smoke. There was no smoke; the fire had
been knocked down hours ago and what was left of it was a smell like a stove left on
in an empty house, which is a smell everybody in that trade claims to be able to
pick out of anything and which is in fact a smell of polymer and wet char and nothing
mysterious at all.

The second thing is the light. Fire investigators talk about light the way sailors
talk about the sky, and the honest position, which Rusk had learned to state early
because it saved her an argument later, is that it is mostly about where you stand.
She stood in the doorway of the front room and looked at the ceiling.

Consistent char across the whole of it. Low. Which meant it had come up into that
room from below and not down onto it, and the difference between those two things is
the difference between a fire and a decision.

She went back to the doorway and looked at it again from further out, and then she
crouched, which she did not need to do, and looked at the floor.

Now.

There is a shape a fire leaves on a floor when it has been allowed to do what fires
do, and there is a shape it leaves when a person has poured something and then lit it,
and the two are not the same shape, and any investigator who says they are the same
shape has not yet had to certify one.

The first is a cone. It opens upward and outward, it is wide at the far end, and it
gives you the direction of travel. It is a very beautiful thing and it is honest.

The second has an edge.

It is called a pour pattern and it is not a shape so much as a boundary, a line where
the liquid stopped and did not spread, because the liquid was too much and too old
and had gone past the point of soaking and started to run. The edge is a definition.
It says *this much, and no more, and someone decided.*

Rusk knelt on the floor of a house where three people had died and she was not looking
for them. She had not looked for them yet. She was going to, in a while, through a
door, with a photographer, and she would do that properly and she would do it today.

She looked at the line.

She put her hand flat on the floor beside it, not on it, and she moved it along, and
it did not change.

The deputy, from the doorway, said, "Ma'am?"

"There's nobody going to be very interested in this," Rusk said, "for about a day."

"You want me to get someone out here?"

"No," she said. "I want you to get me a photographer, and I want the removal
contractor to stay where they are, and I want you to write down the time, which is
seven twenty-one, and I want you to write down that I said the removal contractor
stays where they are."

He wrote it down. He wrote all of it down, including the part about himself, which
nobody had asked him to do, and she noticed that and did not comment on it, because
commenting on it is the kind of thing that makes a man stop writing.

"Anything else?"

"Yes," she said. "Don't let anybody tell you it was electrical."

---

Nobody told her it was electrical. That is the part she could never make fit into
the shape of that day.

Everybody *accepted* it. That was different, and it took her three days to understand
that it was different, and by the time she had understood it she was standing in a
garage being told about gallons by a man who could not finish a sentence.

She went back out and walked the street. That was not in the procedure. She went back
out and walked the street because the house was a house on Mercer and Mercer was nine
houses long, and she had driven in past all nine of them, and she wanted to see them
all at once from one end.

They were the same house. All nine. Porch, gable, the shallow hip where the roof
turned over the addition, the same two windows at the front, the same solid door with
the same small square of glass at eye height that every house on that street had
because two brothers had bought the same pattern book in 1967 and had built to it
without ever speaking to each other again.

Every one of them had a study at the back off the kitchen.

She stood in the road and counted nine doors, and then she went back in.

---

The photographer was called Nadia Pham and she was twenty-nine and she had done this
for six years and she was very good and she did not talk.

That was a professional thing and not a personality thing, and Rusk had learned to
tell the difference, and it mattered. Nadia Pham's silence had a job. She asked
questions with her hands: *higher, lower, from here, from the corner.* Once, at the
doorway, she said, "Do you want it from where she saw it?"

Rusk had not known there was a where she saw it.

"Which she?"

"The mother." Nadia did not look up from the camera. "There's a schoolteacher. She's
been standing on the pavement by the mailbox since five. She's not going in."

Rusk went out to the pavement.

The woman was perhaps fifty and she had a coat on over what she had been sleeping in,
and she was standing the way people stand when they have been told to stand somewhere.
She said, "I'm sorry to intrude."

"You're not intruding. You're the closest family and you're standing on a pavement in
February." Rusk kept her voice at the volume it had been at inside, which was the
volume for a person having a bad day in a house. "Was she — were you expecting to
be allowed in?"

"No. No, I thought." The woman looked at the tape. "Is it bad? People keep asking me
if it's bad."

"I don't know yet."

"Everybody keeps asking me that."

"They're asking because they want you to say it's bad, and you shouldn't say it until
somebody can tell you."

The woman laughed, one syllable, surprised out of her. Then she said, "You'll tell
me."

"I will," said Rusk, and thought, standing on the pavement four doors from her
mother's, that she had just promised a stranger a thing she could not promise, and
that this was becoming a pattern.

She wrote down the woman's name and she walked back in.

---

She walked the house properly at nine — front to back, up, down, the way it is supposed
to be walked, room by room, doorway by doorway — and the house gave her almost nothing,
which was unusual and which she noted and did not like. Kitchens clean. Beds made. Bathroom
ordinary. The lived-in order of people who expected to come home. She checked the smoke
detectors last, standing on a chair Nadia steadied without being asked, and found the hall
unit with its batteries out — the small dark rectangle, the terminals clean. She bagged the
batteries from the counter where they sat in a saucer, labelled them, dated them. Someone had
been annoyed by beeping. Someone living here, recently, in the ordinary way.

The pour was in one place. One. A fire set with intent and no experience makes one
pour. A fire set with intent and experience makes several, and places them, and knows
something about ventilation before it lights. This one had made exactly one, in a
room with a table and a chair and a cylinder heater in the corner that had no
business being in a room that size.

The cylinder heater was the sort that sits on a stand and takes a propane bottle, and
the bottle was gone, and there was no bottle in the house, and there was a scorch
shadow on the floor under the stand where a bottle had stood for a long time.

That was a good sign. That was a very good sign. It meant whoever had done this had
been in that room many times, and had stood in that exact spot, and had set a heater
down and picked it up again. It meant a person, in a house, on a weekday, at the
speed at which people do things they have done before.

She wrote it down. She wrote it in the shorthand that four hundred determinations had
made into a kind of muscle, and her hand did not hesitate, and she noticed that her
hand did not hesitate, and she let it not hesitate, because there was not time for the
other thing.

Upstairs, four hundred square feet of nothing. Two bedrooms, both made up. A bathroom
with the door open. Somebody had, in the last year, taken the batteries out of the
smoke detectors in the hall, which meant somebody had been annoyed by them, which
meant somebody had lived here, which meant this was not a house anybody had been
waiting to burn. It was a house somebody had been living in on the same night, at the
same hour, with the batteries out.

That was the second good sign and it was a colder one.

---

Three people had been found in the study.

She went and stood in the doorway of it at half past nine and did not go in, because
the photographer had to go in first and Nadia Pham was at the top of the stairs.

A study is where a family keeps the things it means to keep. It is not a room
anybody sleeps in and it is not a room anybody cries in and it is, in a house this
old, in a town this small, the room where the decisions get made, because it is the
one room with a door that closes and a table you can spread paper out on.

Three inside, and all three of them in the room with the papers in it.

There was a chair pulled out from the table and pushed back in at a slightly wrong
angle, the way a chair gets when somebody stands up in a hurry and does not look at
it. Rusk photographed it from the doorway, at an angle, with a scale, and she took
four of them, and then she put the camera down and stood there and had a thought.

The thought arrived in her mother's voice, unbidden, in words, before she had decided
to be having it: *it's eleven minutes fast so I'm never early.*

She had not thought about the kitchen clock in six years. She had not thought about
the kitchen clock, or the study, or the two brothers and the pattern book, or any of
it, and she had been an investigator for eleven years and she had driven this street
forty times.

She wrote the time on the sheet. 09:34. Then she wrote, because she had to write
something and because she could not think of anything else, *study, north side, no
forced entry apparent.*

Then she went out of the house and stood on the pavement and breathed.

---

At half nine she came out through the tape and the sky had gone the colour it goes
when it cannot decide, and the deputy was waiting with a man she did not know, who
was holding a coffee out for her before she was within speaking distance.

"Delphine Rusk?"

"Yes."

"I'm the county's lawyer. I mean, I am a lawyer, and the county has asked me to help
with, well." He stopped. He had a small, tired, entirely decent face. "The family has
a question. The mother of the youngest. She has asked whether the fire can be
confirmed as, and I'm quoting, *not her fault.*"

"She hasn't been named as having a fault."

"I know. But the family is asking about the *origin*."

Rusk looked at him for slightly too long.

The removal contractor had been at the tape for forty minutes. The deputy had written
down what she said and why. The photographer was on the roof of the house on Mercer
doing something with a thermal camera and would be there until two. Three people were
in a study four doors from her mother and a man with a decent face was asking her,
politely, at twenty past nine, whether she would put in a document.

"No," she said. "I can't confirm that yet. I can tell you the origin is wrong. I can
tell you it wasn't a fault in the wiring and it wasn't the heater and it wasn't the
cooktop, and I can tell you that it was set."

He nodded. He did not look surprised, and she had been braced for something other than
not-surprise.

"How long before you can say more?"

"Six days," said Rusk, and heard herself, and did not take it back.

She had not meant to say it. It came out with the weight of a deadline she had not
been given, from a woman who had not been told one, and it went out into the February
air and stood there, and the lawyer took his coffee out of his hand and looked at it.

"Six days," he repeated. "Is that a, where's that from?"

"That's from me," Rusk said. "I don't have paperwork for it. I'll have paperwork for
it by Friday."

It was not true. There was no Friday. There was no deadline at all, and she had known
that when she said it, and she had said it anyway, standing in a street her mother
lived on, with three dead people behind her and a lawyer's decent face in front of
her, because the thing she had wanted, for about ninety seconds, was to be the kind
of person who gets six days.

She got six days.

Nobody questioned it. That is the part. A county lawyer on a February morning in a
town with one street of 1968 houses did not question the six days. He wrote it on the
back of his card, and he thanked her, and he got in his car.

Delphine Rusk stood on the pavement outside a house on her mother's street with a
photographer on the roof and a removal contractor at the tape and three people in a
study, and she had just invented a deadline for a case that was not hers to close.

Six days. She would use them.

She had said the origin was wrong out loud, to a deputy, and it was out loud now, and
it could not be taken back, and the worst of it, the genuinely worst of it, and she
would think about this for a long time afterwards, was that the man with the decent
face had not asked her a single follow-up question.

In eleven years, that had never once been the sign of a person who believed you.

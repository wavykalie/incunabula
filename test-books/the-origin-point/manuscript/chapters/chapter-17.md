# 17 — The Kindness of Roy Halloran

Roy fed her twice that week and both times people were watching. That was the finding, stated plainly, the way she stated everything she could evidence. What she made of it she kept out of the notebook for three days, until the pattern would no longer stay out.

The first time was on the Monday, at the depot, at noon, in front of Whitlock and two mechanics who had come in for parts. He came through the side door with a paper bag gone translucent at the bottom, set it on her desk on top of the 2019 file, and said, "You have not eaten, you never eat, there is always something in the oven in February," and everybody laughed — Whitlock, Myron, Fenwick from the doorway — and she ate the bread in front of them, standing up, because not eating it would have been a statement and she had made enough statements that week.

The bread was still warm. Cheese. Pickle. Enough for two, though no one suggested sharing and she ate it all, conscious of the watching, conscious of chewing, while Roy leaned against the filing cabinet and talked about the oven — his oven, the company's oven, the way February demanded baking the way August demanded hoses — and stayed six minutes by the wall clock, and left with a wave that included the whole room.

The second time was on the Wednesday, on Mercer, in front of Mrs Bray and the schoolteacher's mother. Rusk was standing at the tape outside 131, writing up the walkthrough notes with her gloves off because the pen kept slipping, when Roy came out of Cora's house — 127, four doors down — carrying a plate covered with a tea towel. He walked the four doors openly, in the middle of the road, the way he walked everywhere in that town, like the road was his hallway. He held the plate out.

"Take this," he said. "You are cold. Cora made too much."

She took it because Mrs Bray was watching from her doorway with her arms folded, and the schoolteacher's mother was watching from her front window with the plastic sheeting taped around it, and refusing food from a sixty-one-year-old chief in front of two old women would have been a statement of a kind she could not afford.

The plate held stew. Potatoes. Carrots gone soft. She ate it standing at the tape with the tea towel over her arm, while Roy stood beside her and talked — about Cora's cooking, about how she always made too much since her husband died, about how someone had to eat it — and stayed four minutes by her watch, and took the empty plate back with the towel, and went.

Both times the food was good. Both times she thanked him, properly, the way her mother raised her. Both times she wrote it down afterwards in the notebook with the time and the witnesses, which was a strange thing to do about food — she knew it was strange, she felt the strangeness as she wrote — and which she did anyway, because the strangeness was itself a finding. You do not log bread unless something in you has noticed bread.

On the Thursday night she sat with the notebook at her kitchen table, the flat dark around her except for the one lamp, and read the two entries together, back to back, the way she read exhibits.

Monday 12:05, depot, witnesses: Whitlock, Myron, Fenwick. Roy brings food. Stays six minutes. Talks about the oven. Leaves. Plate? No — bag. Paper bag. Taken away? Left? She had thrown it out. Gone.

Wednesday 13:40, Mercer, witnesses: Bray, schoolteacher's mother. Roy brings food from Cora's. Stays four minutes. Talks about Cora making too much. Leaves with plate and towel.

She had seen Roy three other times that week. She listed those too, to be fair — fairness was a habit, and habits were what she had instead of instincts.

Once: in the garage bay on the Saturday, alone together, where he told her about Verhage's kitchen and the sentence he had never told anybody — well, that's Cora then — and his hands shook on the wrench, and there was no food, no plate, no mention of ovens. Just the truth, or part of it, given standing up in the cold.

Twice: on the road at seven on Tuesday morning, alone, driving past her in the truck without stopping — a lift of the hand, nothing more. No food. No witnesses. Nothing.

Three times: at the depot on Thursday evening, alone, dropping a roster sheet at the front desk without coming in — Fenwick saw him, signed for it, told Rusk later. No food. No mention of Cora. No one to perform for.

No food. No plate. No ovens. Three times alone.

Rusk sat at her kitchen table with the notebook open and the flat dark and felt the pattern click into place the way a burn pattern clicks when you find the edge: clean, where the liquid stopped. Where people were, there was food. Where no one was, there was none.

He was kind when people were there to see it.

She did not write that sentence in the notebook. She wrote the times and the places and the names, six entries, three with food and three without, with witnesses listed beside the first three and the word alone beside the last three. She left the inference out, because the inference was the kind of thing that could not be evidenced — no document, no photograph, no witness to a motive — and she never stated conclusions she could not evidence. That was her hard rule, the one the voice matrix named, the reason she would be useless at chapter twenty-one when the whole town became a conclusion with nothing to check it against.

But she thought it, sitting there in the dark with her hands around a cold cup: kindness on schedule, warmth with witnesses. And she thought of Verhage's second cup, still damp that morning, and Roy's sentence said to the room — well, that's Cora then — and the roster checked every morning since 2019, and a man who slept in his clothes when the tones went off and sat in another man's kitchen most nights because he could not be in his own house after a fire.

On the Friday morning she went to Cora's house early, before seven, walking the four doors from where she had parked so no engine would announce her. Her mother was in the kitchen with the clock eleven minutes fast, making tea in the pot, moving the way she moved in the mornings — economical, unhurried, everything in its place.

There was no extra plate. There was no too much. One cup out. One.

"Mom," Rusk said from the doorway.

Cora looked up, unsurprised. "You want tea."

"Did you make extra on Wednesday?" Rusk stayed in the doorway. She did not sit. Sitting would have made it a visit. "A plate. Stew. Roy carried it down to me at the tape."

Cora poured tea without looking up, the stream steady. "Roy took a plate. He said you were on the street. He said you were cold."

"Did you offer it?"

"I offered nothing." Cora set the pot down precisely. "He took it. He does that. He has done it for years — takes a plate when people are watching, brings it back empty and washed, sometimes the same day. He thinks I do not notice. I notice everything in this kitchen."

"Has he always done that?" Rusk's voice came out thinner than she intended. "The plates. Since when?"

"Since 2019." Cora turned the tap off slowly, the way she did everything slowly when it mattered, giving each motion its full weight. "Since he started checking the roster every morning. Since he started being useful. Since the fire." She dried her hands. "He is a good man, Delphine. Good men do strange things after."

Rusk drank the tea standing up in her mother's kitchen. It was too hot and she drank it anyway, scalding her tongue, welcoming the small pain the way she welcomed small certainties. She wanted to ask more — about 2019, about the roster, about what Roy was atoning for with stew — and she did not, because her mother had answered the question asked and the question meant was a different one, and Cora had spent six years not answering the second kind, and pushing now would close the first kind too.

She walked to the depot past nine identical houses, porch, gable, shallow hip, the small square of glass in every door. She thought about warmth that arrived on schedule, in front of witnesses, in a paper bag and on a plate with a tea towel. She thought about a man who told the truth standing up in a cold garage and performed kindness sitting down in warm rooms.

That afternoon Roy passed her in the depot corridor, alone, with a roster sheet in his hand and his coat over his arm. He nodded — "Delphine" — and kept walking, unhurried, toward the front desk. No food. No mention of ovens. No witnesses but her, and she did not count, because she was not the kind of witness the kindness was for.

She wrote that down too: Friday 15:20, depot corridor, alone, no offering. Seventh entry. The pattern held.

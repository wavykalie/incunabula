# 15 — The Boy Who Wasn't There

The friends were named in the file in Terrell's own hand, on a form from 2019 that nobody had asked him to fill in and which he had filled in anyway, completely, in order — the way Denny answered questions, the way helpful people in that town did everything.

Rusk found it on the Wednesday morning in the records store, in the thin folder marked WITNESS — VOLUNTARY, which held three sheets and a paperclip gone rusty at the hinge. Two of the sheets were blank except for signatures at the bottom, signed and dated by people who had come in, been asked nothing they could answer, and signed to prove they had come. The third was Terrell's.

She remembered the morning clearly, because the records store was cold in a way the depot never was — a deep cold, stone cold, the cold of paper kept too long. The clerk, a woman called Fenwick who wore fingerless gloves year-round, had brought the box up from the basement without being asked, because Rusk had telephoned ahead and asked properly, and Fenwick believed in proper asking.

*Where were you on the night of 11 April 2019?* The question was typed at the top of the form, the ink pale from a failing ribbon. And under it, in a forward-leaning hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next sheet: *With friends. J. Lindqvist's boy, Cora, M. Verhage's nephew. At Lindqvist's from 8. Cards.*

She sat with it for a long time. The radiator under the window clicked and gave nothing. Fenwick worked at the next table, sorting dockets, the gloves whispering on paper.

J. Lindqvist's boy had been in Oslo since 2017. That was in the file too, on a school leaver's record with a forwarding address in another country. M. Verhage's nephew was nine years old in 2019 — a child, in the city, in school photographs with missing teeth. And Cora was his mother. Seventy-four. Alone in the house that week because her elder son was out of the country, which was also in the file, on a travel declaration with a stamp.

Terrell Vance, thirty years old in 2019, had spent the night of the fire playing cards with a man in Norway, a child of nine, and his mother.

Rusk took the sheet out of its sleeve and carried it to the window for better light, though the light was grey and thin. She read the four names again. Then she checked the back, though there was nothing on the back — she always checked the back, because backs had surprised her before. Nothing. Just the emboss showing through, the pressure of a hand writing fast.

"Fenwick," she said. "Is there anything else with this? Any attachment?"

"That is the lot, love. Three sheets. He came in on the Monday after, asked for a form, filled it at the counter standing up. I remember because nobody does that — they sit, they take tea, they take an hour. He stood and wrote and gave it back and went."

"What did he say?"

"Nothing." Fenwick pushed her gloves up. "That is what I remember second. He said nothing at all. Not good morning, not thank you. Polite, mind — nodded. But nothing."

Rusk bagged the sheet in front of Fenwick and signed the chain, and Fenwick countersigned with the gloves on, her signature shaky from the wool.

She took it to Lindqvist's house that afternoon. The old woman was in the kitchen with the biscuit tin already open on the table, as if visitors at that hour were expected and provided for. The hunting scene on the lid showed dogs mid-chase, riders mid-jump, frozen.

"Mrs Lindqvist," Rusk said, sitting on the good side, the right. "Was your son here in April of 2019? At all?"

"No, dear." Immediate, certain. "He has been in Oslo since the mill closed. 2017. He sends photographs. The fjord. The children." A pause. "He telephones on Sundays. Not Saturdays. Sundays."

"Was anyone here playing cards on the eleventh? Here in this house?"

"No, dear." Puzzled now, turning the ring on her finger. "I was in bed by nine. I am always in bed by nine. There was no one here but me and the telephone. My daughter telephoned at nine, as she always does. I told her about the fire on Mercer. She told me to go to bed."

"Did Terrell Vance ever play cards here? At any time?"

Lindqvist thought, her eyes on the tin. "Terrell?" She shook her head slowly. "Cora's boy? He has never been in this house, dear. I would remember. He is — " She searched. "He is a tall boy. Polite. He nods. I see him at funerals."

Rusk wrote that down: never in the house. 14:05. She ate a biscuit she did not want, because refusing would have closed something.

She walked to Verhage's from there, the long way round, past the green bin standing exactly aligned with the fence post. The old man answered with coffee already in his hand, as if he had been expecting her at that hour every day of his life, which, she reflected, he possibly had — waiting was most of what the town did.

"Mr Verhage. Was your nephew here in April 2019?"

"He was nine, miss." Verhage set his cup down with the practiced steadiness inside the tremor. "Nine years old. Lives in the city with his mother. Comes at Christmas, eats too much, goes home. He has never been here in April. There is nothing here in April."

"Was Terrell here that night? The eleventh?"

Verhage looked at her directly, his eyes not shaking at all. "No, miss. Terrell has never been in this house. Not once. I would remember — he is Cora's boy, and people remember where Cora's boy has been, the way they remember weather. Ask anyone."

Rusk walked back to the depot with the sheet in her folder and the gap in it widening with every step, the way a crack widens in ice when you stop to look at it. It was not a lie made of invention. That was what made it interesting. It was a lie made of names that were true and in the wrong place — real people, real relationships, arranged around an evening that never happened, like furniture moved into a house where it did not belong but where each piece, taken alone, was genuine.

At the depot she telephoned the county travel office and waited eleven minutes on hold, listening to a recorded message about office hours. When the clerk came on, Rusk asked properly, with dates and spellings, and the clerk confirmed: Terrell Vance, no travel record for that week, no travel record for that month. Cora Vance, none either. The son who was out of the country was the elder — the one who lived in the city and sent money home in postal orders. Cora had been alone in 127 all that week, with the bins going out late and the kitchen clock eleven minutes fast and nobody to play cards with.

At four, the light already going, she went to Cora's house. Her mother was in the kitchen with the tap running, peeling potatoes into the sink, the peelings curling in the water. The clock above the stove said 4:22. Rusk's watch said 4:11. Eleven minutes. Always eleven.

"Mom," Rusk said from the doorway.

Cora did not look up. "You want tea."

"Mom. Was Terrell with you on the night of the fire in 2019?"

The peeling stopped. Just for a second — the knife still, the water running, a peel half-attached. Then it started again, slower, careful.

"He was with friends," Cora said to the sink.

"Which friends?"

"Ask him." The potatoes turned in the water. The knife moved. "He is a grown man. He was thirty. I do not keep his evenings. I never kept his evenings."

"You were alone that week. Your elder son was away. The file says so. The travel office says so."

Cora put the peeler down on the draining board, precisely, aligned with the edge. She dried her hands on a towel that had been white twenty years ago and was now the colour of tea. She looked at the clock, then at the window where the bins stood in a row, then at her daughter. Her face had the closed look it got when a subject was finished.

"There is tea in the pot," she said.

Rusk left the house without tea and stood on the pavement outside 127 in the failing light and wrote in the notebook with numb fingers: alibi has a gap. Named friend is his mother. Three names, three absences. She underlined it twice and dated it and capped the pen.

She walked home in the dark past nine identical houses with their studies at the back off the kitchen, porch, gable, shallow hip, the small square of glass in every solid door. She did not look at any of them directly. At her flat she pinned the voluntary form's copy to the board beside Sam's list and stood back until the two sheets stopped being paper and became a single fact: the boy was not where he said, and his mother was where she said nothing about.

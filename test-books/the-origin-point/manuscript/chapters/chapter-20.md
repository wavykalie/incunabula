# 20 — Interlocking

Everything is a V-pattern. That is what the word means, and Rusk had known it for eleven years without ever needing to say it until the Sunday morning in the records store when she said it aloud to nobody and the saying of it changed the shape of the room.

She had laid the two origin photographs on the floor of the bare room the way she laid them in chapter eight — side by side, squared, a foot apart — and switched the lamp off so the only light came from the high window. Dust moved in it. The V-patterns showed even in the half-dark, because a pour pattern is a boundary and a boundary shows from any angle: the clean edge where the liquid stopped and did not spread, where too much and too old went past soaking and started to run.

The 2025 pour: a clean V in the study off the kitchen at 131 Mercer, edge sharp enough to photograph with a scale, pooled not carried, one pour only, in a room with a table and a chair and a cylinder heater that had no business being in a room that size.

The 2019 pour: the same V, the same edge, the same room type off the same kind of kitchen, four doors down at 127, photographed six years ago by a man now retired, filed under electrical, never questioned.

She had been reading the same sentence for nine chapters. Two fires. One hand. She stood in the bare room with the light off and let the sentence stand whole for the first time instead of in pieces.

Then she made tea in the depot kitchen — the stained mug with the chip, rain on the yard window — and carried it back, and switched the lamp on, took a clean sheet on a clipboard, and worked it methodically — the way she worked everything when the shape threatened to run ahead of the evidence, item by item, numbering as she went, dating the sheet at the top: Sunday, 09:12.

One: origin in a room with no beds, both fires. Stated twice — in chapter two, walking the 2025 house, and in chapter six, standing in the study doorway while Nadia worked the stairs. A study is where a family keeps the things it means to keep. Nobody sleeps there. Nobody cries there. Starting a fire there means knowing the house well enough to know which room is empty at night. Someone knew both houses from inside.

Two: poured, not spread. Clean V, both fires. Described in plain terms in the 2025 walkthrough, photographed with a scale; matched to the 2019 file photograph on Friday in the records store, with Fenwick watching over her shoulder. Any investigator who says the shapes are the same has not certified one. They are not the same shape, and that is the whole of it: the cone is honest, the edge is a decision.

Three: pooled, not carried. Bought in volume, in containers that could be returned. Establishes premeditation — nobody pools by accident — and rules out the two people who had no access to bulk purchase. She wrote the numbers beside it: 340, 260. March 2019. April 2025. Cash both times. Denny's pages, Denny's hand, Denny's rule from 2003.

Four: the numbers read aloud. She had read them aloud in the garage, in the depot, on the telephone to the lab. Cash, no vehicle, no address — both times. A man who counts twice. A truck that is not Denny's. County plates with the wrong start. Shapes, not faces. Premeditation with no face, as she told Whitlock. She underlined it: the face comes from the table, not the receipt.

Five: a circuit cited in 2019 that is not in the file. The panel was replaced in 2016 — the work order existed, the electrician's signature, the date. There was no kitchen circuit in that house in 2019. There had never been one in that configuration. The determination cited a thing that did not exist, and she had signed it at thirty-one, four months into the post, with the reference on the form in front of her. She wrote her own name beside item five, and the date she signed, and did not look at it longer than necessary.

Six: one person died in 2019, not of smoke. Blunt trauma from a fall, pre-mortem, hours before the burn. Dated, timestamped, in the state system outside the coroner's control — 11 March 2019, Adela's login. Produced under a green lamp in a basement by a woman who never apologises and who apologised with her eyes while her voice stayed exact. The count planted in chapter four, stated in chapter ten. One. Not two. One woman, fifty-nine, at the foot of the stairs.

Seven: the suppressed finding itself — the two sheets, clipped, smoothed flat under lamplight, photographed front and back, bagged, signed, countersigned 22:40. The difference between signed and believed, taught out loud by a woman trying to teach it to someone before she retired. Rusk copied the timestamp onto her sheet and circled it: the one fact in the whole file that lived in a system nobody in the county controlled.

Eight: the chair moved and moved back, both fires, eighteen degrees off the table edge. Habit signature. She had photographed the 2025 chair from the doorway, four frames with a scale, and matched it to the 2019 scene photograph where a chair stood at the same wrong angle in the same relation to the same kind of table. A person who moves a chair to get a can in and puts it back without looking. Without looking — that was the detail that stayed with her. You only put things back without looking in rooms you know blindfolded.

Nine: Roy four minutes from eleven blocks. Measured three times on a Sunday with a stopwatch, in a car, in different traffic: four minutes forty, door to door, at residential speed with a stop sign at each end. Against a turnout that was logged at four minutes flat for eleven blocks plus a turnout. Inconsistent — flagged in pencil in the margin, in smaller letters than her usual ones. Resolved on the Saturday in the garage bay: four streets, not eleven blocks. In Verhage's kitchen, hearing a name. Driving like a man going to a house, not a fire. She wrote the resolution beside the flag and dated it, so the margin told both halves.

Ten: Roy's kindness only when observed. Tabled in the notebook across seven entries — three with food and witnesses, three alone without, one corridor passing that sealed it. Monday depot, Wednesday Mercer, Friday corridor. Bread, stew, nothing. She did not write the inference on the working sheet either; she wrote the times and the witnesses and left the column beside them empty, the way she left columns empty when the content could not be evidenced. The empty column was itself the finding, and she knew it, and she left it empty anyway.

Eleven: Sam's list, seven names, one too many. Kept six years in a baptism ledger. Handed over in the third row of a church with bad acoustics by a man doing arithmetic he did not want to finish. The extra name: the boy who was not there. The dead woman who said write it down. The fold pressed once by a thumb giving it away for the last time. Bagged, logged, countersigned. The largest scene in the book reduced to one sheet of lined paper — that was how it always went; the biggest things came on the smallest paper.

Twelve: Terrell's alibi, gap identified, named friend is his mother. Voluntary form, filled standing up at the counter on the Monday after, saying nothing, nodding politely. Three friends: a man in Oslo since 2017, a child of nine, his mother alone that week with the bins going out late. Real names in the wrong place, furniture in the wrong house. Corroborated by Lindqvist (never in the house), Verhage (never in the house), the travel office (no records), Cora (there is tea in the pot). Four corroborations, each voluntary, each complete, each devastating in order.

Thirteen: nobody refused. Five conversations in the church basement's week — twenty-eight answers on the street across eleven days — zero refusals. Not once, not badly, not even about money. Stated as observation at chapter fourteen, in the car park, in plain words for the file's reader: nobody refuses, everyone answers, that is the pattern. The agreement made visible by its completeness. She copied the line onto the working sheet verbatim and dated it back, so the sheet carried its own history.

Fourteen: her own signature on the 2019 determination. Four lines. The brown ink at the edges. Thirty-one years old, four months in. She had never read it with her name on it — not really, not as a reader — until this month, when she read it as the most compromised witness to her own file. Correction drafted, awaiting Fenn's office and four minutes on Monday. She wrote: correction Monday 17:00, Fenn witnessing. A promise to the sheet.

Fifteen: the 260 gallons went out in a truck Denny does not own. County plates, wrong start. Loaded upright, bled, no spill. The rota on the back of the page in Roy's hand — engine one, a time — written because he had no paper on him, kept because Denny keeps everything including absences. Item fifteen, the one the independent solve added, the one that excluded Denny as instigator as cleanly as numbers ever clear anyone: the convenience was used, not using.

She sat back. Her hand ached from writing — the clipboard dented where she had pressed, the pencil worn short. She sharpened it with the penknife she carried since training, the shavings curling onto the concrete, and went on. Fifteen items. Every one planted by chapter eighteen. Every one visible to a reader on first read — stated twice, described in plain terms, read aloud, dated, timestamped, photographed, handed over voluntarily, observed and logged.

One perpetrator. Two fires. Six years apart.

The exclusions fell out cleanly, the way true exclusions do — not by alibi but by nature. Denny excluded: he sold, logged, handed over, and the truck was not his. Roy excluded: in Verhage's kitchen, arriving after, never opening the study door, spending six years not opening doors. Sam excluded: kept, carried, gave over, never lying, only withholding permission he waited to be asked for. Adela excluded: suppressed, then produced with a timestamp from a system she did not control — her culpability separate, self-tabled, signed straight at last.

That left the family. Cora, alone that week, the elder son away with his postal orders and his absence, the bins late, the clock fast, the tap running in an empty kitchen. Terrell, with an alibi of true names in a false evening, thirty years old, loading fuel he said was for a farm tank. The woman of fifty-nine at the foot of the stairs. The papers in the study. The chair at eighteen degrees, moved without looking, in rooms known blindfolded.

She ate standing up — bread, cheese, the last of the coffee gone cold — and went back to the sheet, checking each item against its source paper: determination copy, panel record, receipt bag, March page, docket, list, statement, rota copy, coroner's sheets, chair frames, photographs. Eleven sources. Fifteen items. Every arrow landing. She initialled each line as verified, the way she initialled exhibits, until the sheet was a document in its own right.

Rusk stood in the bare room with the lamp on and the photographs at her feet and said it aloud to nobody, because it had to be said aloud once, in a room, before it could be written in a file:

"The pour was in both houses."

She bagged the working sheet — three pages, front and back, numbered — and dated the bag, and initialled it, and set it with the exhibits instead of the notes, because the working sheet had become evidence: the map of the whole inquiry on two pages, the thing a reader could follow without her. She switched the lamp off and stood a moment in the half-dark with the dust moving in the window light, listening to the building settle — the furnace, the pipes, a door closing somewhere above — and then she went home through empty Sunday streets, past the church dark and the garage dark and nine houses dark, and slept three hours with the photographs on the floor beside the bed, where she could see the V-patterns when she woke — the edge, the boundary, the decision, twice.

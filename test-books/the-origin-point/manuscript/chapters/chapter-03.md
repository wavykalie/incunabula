# 3 — What the Neighbors Say

Rusk walked Mercer Street in the four o'clock light with a notebook and no badge on,
which is a thing you are not supposed to do and which nobody stops you doing in a town
where the biggest event of the year is the two-day sale at the feed store.

Seven houses. She had a name, an address, and four questions for each, and the four
questions were the same four questions, and she had made them the same on purpose.

Nobody refused to answer her.

She wrote that down on the Tuesday evening before she went out, because it was the
thing she was watching for and she had a rule about writing down the thing you are
watching for at the point you start watching for it. Later, in a different life, she
would think about how she had been trained to distrust unanimity by eleven years of
reading depositions, and how that training had not once prepared her for a street
where everybody was extremely kind.

---

**The Hendry place**, 118 Mercer. Two adults, four children, a cat.

"Yes, we heard it. My husband had the radio on. It was the middle of the night —
no, it wasn't the middle. It was gone eleven, maybe. I heard the first truck and
then I heard a lot of trucks. I went out and the street was full of people already,
so I suppose everybody heard the same as everybody. We brought the neighbours' bins
in off the road, that was me and Denise, because the truck was on them. We didn't go
in. I hope that's all right. Nobody said we couldn't."

**The Okonkwo place**, 124. One man, forty-ish, a satellite dish, no garden.

"Yeah, I saw the light. I thought it was somebody's bin. That's not a joke, I'm
saying it went round my head as a bin. I went out and I could smell it and then I saw
the trucks and I came in. I have a bad back. That's the truth of it, I have a bad back
and it was cold and I went back in bed. People keep asking if I *saw* anybody."

**The Prentiss place**, 131. A woman in her sixties, a very clean kitchen, a clock.

"Oh, the poor house. Yes. I did call, yes, I called the number in the paper because
the paper still comes. I don't have a mobile. I couldn't get anybody. I tried the
deputy's office and it rang out." She looked at Rusk for a moment. "I have that clock
fast. Eleven minutes. It was my mother's and it runs eleven minutes fast and I have
never had it fixed, because the alternative is being right too often."

**The Ayers**, 133. Denny Aylward, forty-four, a man with a garage on the property
and oil under his fingernails and the manner of somebody who is always, for
some reason, about to be helpful.

"I've been round there, I don't want you to think I haven't. I had a look from the
gate. Everybody's had a look from the gate. That house is going to come down, isn't
it. Mine's the same build. I'm not saying that for sympathy, I'm saying it because
they're all going to start going and the insurance on these is rubbish. I can send you
a list. I keep a list. Ask me tomorrow, it'll be done."

**The Bray place**, 140. Two people, both about eighty, both already in coats.

"We were up. Of course we were up, I'm seventy-nine. But we didn't see anything,
because we were in bed with the door shut, and I want to be clear that we were not
hiding anything, we had our curtains shut against the draught, which is what you do
in February." A pause. "There was a lot of talking afterwards. On the road. That's all
I know. People come out in February at three in the morning and they talk."

**The Lindqvist place**, 145. A man with a laptop open on the table and eleven
children in a photograph he did not explain.

"I was working. I do contract work at night, it's a thing in my line, I won't bore you
with it. I did hear the vehicles. I want to help, I do, but I've got a delivery on
Monday and if something's needed on the paperwork side I'm probably the wrong person.
I've been on the road nine years and I could tell you things about the county's
records system that would make you cry. It's terrible. It's genuinely terrible."

**And Sam Okafor**, at 137, who was not on her list and was standing at the end of a
driveway with a coat over pyjamas as though he had been waiting since four.

"You're the investigator. I heard the truck come and I came down and there were people
in the road and somebody was saying *stay in*, which is what people say, so I stayed
in." He pulled the coat tighter. "Can I say something that will sound stupid?"

"Go ahead."

"We don't really have a fire here," he said. "I know how that sounds. I know exactly
how that sounds. I live four houses from it. But there is no business on this street
and no industry and nothing anybody's ever made money out of, and when something like
that happens there's always a *reason*, and there's usually a reason that involves
money, and there is nothing here." He looked down the street at it, all one row of it,
the porch and the gable and the hip. "Nothing here to burn for."

Rusk wrote it down.

"Mr Okafor," she said, "that's not a stupid thing to say. That's the most useful
thing anyone has said to me since Tuesday."

"Is it?"

"No," Rusk said. "It's the most useless thing anyone has said to me since Tuesday.
But it's the most *carefully* useless thing, and careful takes work."

He looked at her for a moment with an expression she was not able to place for about
four years, and then he said, "There's tea, if you're cold," and she said no thank
you, and she wrote the time, 16:52, and she walked back up the street to her car.

---

Seven houses. Four questions each. Twenty-eight answers, and not one of them was no.
Not one hesitation, not one closed door, not one *come back with a warrant*. In eleven years
she had never walked a street without a single refusal — not in the city, not anywhere.
Refusals were the texture of the job: doors shut, curtains drawn, dogs set barking. Here,
doors opened. Tea was offered twice. A list was promised by morning. The street received her
the way a family receives a relative: completely, without checking credentials.

She sat in the car on Mercer with the notebook open on the wheel and the engine off and
read all twenty-eight again, slowly, pen in hand, and there was nothing in them. There was nothing wrong with any of
them. Every single person on that street had given her four true answers, in a
reasonable order, with a reasonable face, and the total of the four true answers
added up to a street where nothing had happened and nobody had seen anything and every
one of them was very sorry.

It was, she thought, the most complete and most useless document she had ever
collected.

She wrote on the back page, in the shorthand, under a line she drew:

*Everyone helped. Write that down. Everyone helped.*

*Ask why it didn't feel like help.*

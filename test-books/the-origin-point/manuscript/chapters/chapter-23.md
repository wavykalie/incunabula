# 23 — After the Certification

The form took four minutes to sign. She had thought about it for six years. The disproportion — four minutes against six years, a signature against a silence — sat with her all morning as she drove to the depot, parked in her usual bay, and walked the corridor to Fenn's office with the correction in a folder under her arm.

The room was Fenn's office: door shut, blinds half down, February light coming in flat and grey through the slats. The radiator knocked. On the desk, squared precisely, lay the 2019 determination — four lines, her signature at the bottom from when she was thirty-one and four months into the post. The ink had gone brown at the edges in six years. The circuit reference stood in the second line, neat, confident, wrong. The panel had been replaced in 2016. There was no kitchen circuit. There had never been one in that configuration, in that house, on that street.

Fenn sat across from her with his hands folded, the way he sat for everything — good news, bad news, weather. The folding was the point; the news was only the occasion. He nodded to the chair. She sat. She set her folder on the desk beside his four lines and opened it to her clean sheet, dated today, timed for now, in her current hand — the hand of thirty-seven, eleven years in, steadier and slower than the hand of thirty-one.

"I signed this," she said. She laid her finger beside the brown signature without touching it. "I signed a circuit that was not in the file. The panel record proves it — work order, electrician's signature, 2016. I did not check. Nobody asked me to check. Nobody asks the person whose mother's street it is; that is the custom, and the custom is wrong, and I used it." Her voice stayed level. She had rehearsed this in the car, and the rehearsal held. "I have privately not believed the electrical finding for six years. I have never once said so on the record. The file shows eleven years of certifications with my name on them and not one correction. I am saying so now, and I want the correction to show what I knew, and when I knew it, and what I did after."

Fenn nodded. He did not interrupt. He had been an investigator for twenty-six years and he knew the shape of a correction the way a priest knows the shape of a confession: it had to be spoken before it could be signed, and signed in front of someone, or it was only thinking. Thinking was private. Paper was public. The distance between them was the whole of the job.

"I certified an electrical determination I had not verified," she went on, "on my family's house, four doors from my mother's. The reference was on the form in front of me and I took it. Four hundred times since, I have told junior investigators to check the reference. Every time, I was telling them not to be me." She paused. The radiator knocked. "I am the only person professionally qualified to certify the 2025 fire, and I am its most compromised witness. Both of those things are true. That is the record I want: not one or the other. Both."

She signed the correction underneath the original reference line — full name, rank, date, time: 17:02. Her hand did not hesitate. She had been afraid, driving over, that it would — that six years would come up through the pen. It did not. The signature came out clean and legible, the steadiest thing she had written in weeks.

Fenn countersigned beneath hers. Slowly, legibly, the pen held upright the way he held every pen, the date in his own cramped hand, blotted with the blotter he had used since before she was born into the service. He filed the correction in the folder himself — behind the original, so anyone opening it would find the wrong lines first and the right ones second, in order, the error and its answer together. He gave her the copy across the desk with both hands.

"Delphine," he said. "I have been an investigator for twenty-six years. In all that time I have watched nine people decline to ask the question. Eight of them were protecting somebody — a brother, a chief, a town. One was protecting herself from the question, which is the hardest one to see and the last one to admit." He folded his hands again. "You are the first in six years to put it on paper in this office. I want that stated, because the file will show the correction and not the six years, and someone should say the six years aloud once."

She folded the copy once and put it in her inside pocket, next to Sam's list and the biscuit she never ate from Lindqvist's tin — the pocket that had carried the whole case, paper by paper, for eleven days.

She had signed her complicity out loud, in a room, to a person, and the person had let her, and filed it, and stated the six years. That was the whole chapter. Four minutes on the clock above Fenn's door — 17:02 to 17:06. Six years in the room. The furnace through the wall. The blinds half down. The brown ink and the black ink side by side in the folder, the error and its answer, in order.

She walked out and stood in the depot car park with February air coming cold off the fields and felt the weight shift — not lift, shift — from a private suspicion carried alone to a public record carried with a witness. Her hands shook. She let them. Shaking after was allowed; shaking during would have spoiled the signature.

Then she drove to the office and opened the Terrell file on the clean desk, because the town was still owed, and her nephew was in a holding room waiting to learn what his motive had cost, and the next page was his. The correction was filed. The complicity was on paper. What remained was the consequence — and consequence, she had learned in eleven years, was just paper that other people had to carry.

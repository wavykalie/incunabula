# 13 — A Motive for the House

She had four hours on the Friday and she used three of them on a parking ticket.

That is what happened. There was no dramatic version of the Friday and there was no
decision in it, there was a woman who had four hours and a folder and a house on Mercer
and a mother four doors down, and what she did was sit in a room at the depot with the
folder open on a table and work her way round it, item by item, in the order she had
got them, and she found that she had nowhere to put any of it.

The terminus. That was a finding and it was a real one and it would not go in a
document, because a terminus is a photograph and a photograph is a photograph, and the
state's attorney had already told her on the Tuesday what a photograph was worth, which
was nothing, on its own, in a room.

The volumes. Three hundred and forty gallons, two hundred and sixty gallons, cash, no
address. Aylward had said the name off the docket and had said *I don't know* about
the 2019 one and had not been able to say a single useful thing since, and she had
driven out there twice more in the week and both times he had talked for forty minutes
and produced nothing that could be written.

The rota on the back of the pad. Engine one. A time.

Roy Halloran in a garage bay on the Saturday, telling her about a kitchen four streets
away.

And a chair at eighteen degrees.

Rusk put her hands flat on the table at 11:20 on the Friday and looked at nine days of
work and understood, with a completeness that was almost physical, that she had a
handful of things and that the handful was going to be a handful on Saturday and a
handful on Sunday and a handful at 09:00 on Monday.

Nobody had said no to her. Seven households, four questions each, twenty-eight answers,
and not one refusal, and the reason she could not make it into a case was that a case
requires a person to have done something and every single person on that street had
helped her, and there is no evidentiary weight anywhere in the world for a wall.

---

At 11:41 Devlin's memo came back from the county attorney's office with the
risk manager's initials on it and Fenn's signature under it, and it was two paragraphs
long, and the second paragraph was the one that mattered.

*The origin class of the 11 Mercer fire is recorded as undetermined, pending further
inquiry. The inquiry is to continue for a period of fourteen days from the date of
this memo. On the expiry of that period the file is to be closed on the evidence then
available, and the finding of undetermined cause is to stand.*

Fourteen days from the memo. Which was the Tuesday. Which meant the fourteenth, which
was a Friday, and which was four days away, and which was a day she had already given
to a man with a decent face in the rain on a pavement.

Rusk read it twice and then took the sheet out of the printer and folded it and put
it in her pocket, which is not what you do with a memo, and she was aware that it was
not what you do with a memo.

At 12:20 she got a call from Whitlock, who was at 131 Mercer because the family wanted
a walkthrough, and who said, without preamble, "Are you sitting down?"

"I'm in a room."

"There's a woman here who wants to talk to you and she says she's Mrs Bray and she
says she'll wait, she's not going to talk to you on my tape."

Rusk said, "What did she say to you?"

"She said, and I'm writing it down, she said: *I told you I wasn't in that room and you
wrote it down in my words, and now there's a woman from the paper who's asking me
whether I want to be a source, and I have not told a newspaper anything, and if I have
then somebody's told them, and I would like to know who.*"

---

She stopped at the depot canteen first — coffee from the machine, scalded and bitter,
drank standing up by the window watching rain move across the yard. The parking ticket
sat on her desk upstairs, still unpaid, the envelope going soft at the corners. She put it
in her pocket with the memo. Evidence of an ordinary Friday intruding on an unordinary one.
Small paper, large week. She kept it, the way Denny kept absences.

She went out to Mercer at half past one and there were two vehicles she did not
recognise at the far end of the street — a van with city plates, a car with a press sticker
faded by sun — and a woman with a notebook and a recorder
sitting on the low wall outside 131, swinging her feet like she had all the time in the world,
and Mrs Bray standing in her own doorway at 140
with her arms folded, waiting.

"Two separate things," Mrs Bray said, when Rusk was within speaking distance. "The
first one is that I have not talked to anybody. I have not talked to the paper, I
would not talk to the paper, and if that woman says I did, then either somebody has
told her I am the kind of woman who would, or she has written it and is waiting to see
if I correct her, and I am not going to correct her, because the second you answer
that woman you are in a newspaper for the rest of your life and you are seventy-nine."

"Who said you might be a source?"

"I don't know," said Mrs Bray. "That's what I want. I want to find out, because it was
not me."

The second thing, she said, was about the door.

"I was not in that room," she said. "And I have said that to four people, and I have
said it truthfully, and I would like to say one more thing truthfully and then I am
going inside, because it is cold.

"I have a key to that house. I have had a key since the day the woman died and the
county came and asked whether anybody had a key and I said yes, and they gave me a
form, and I signed it, and that key is in my kitchen drawer and it has been in my
kitchen drawer for six years. And I have not been in that study. I have not been in
that house since the day it happened, not once, not to check the mail, not for the
water, not for anything, and everybody on this street thinks that is because I am a
frightened old woman, and I have let them think it, because it is a better story than
the true one."

"What's the true one?"

Mrs Bray looked at her for a while.

"I don't know," she said. "I genuinely do not know. I have thought about it a lot and I
cannot get underneath it. I have got the key and I have not used it and I have never
once wanted to use it, and that is not the same as not being able to, and I would not
want anybody to write that I was frightened, because I was not. I would want it written
that I was a coward, which is a different thing and much more useful."

She went in, and shut the door, and did not lock it.

---

Rusk stood on Mercer Street at ten past two on the Friday and did the sum.

Four days. A folder that would not go in a document. A chair. A terminus. A garbage
that said a man's name on it once and *I don't know* the once that mattered. A chief
who had told her about a kitchen and a door he had not opened. A coroner who had told
her about a woman who was already dead.

And no motive. That was the thing that was missing and she had not let herself name it
until that afternoon standing in the cold on a street of nine identical houses.

Everything she had explained *how*. She had nothing at all for *why*.

People set fires for money, for spite, for insurance, for a grievance, for a person, or
for nothing, and there was not one of those on that street. There was no business. No
industry. No money moving. No tenancy. Nobody on Mercer had ever in eleven years sold
anything to anybody on Mercer, and the report that the neighbours were being helpful
came from a man who had said *there's nothing here to burn for* and had been, as it
turned out, entirely correct.

What was there, on a street of nine identical houses four doors from Cora Vance, was a
house where a woman of fifty-nine had died and been written down as dying naturally,
and a file that said a circuit which had not existed, and a woman of seventy-four who
had let a fire happen in a study in her own house because she panicked, and had then
spent eleven years not saying so to anybody, and had not once, in the whole of that
time, asked whether the woman in the study was alive.

That was a motive. Money, no. Spite, no. A person, no.

It was a question that somebody needed not to be asked, and it had been in a house on
Mercer Street since 2019, and it had cost a woman of fifty-nine her life to keep, and
in February of 2025 somebody had gone into a house four doors away and made sure the
same question could never be asked there either.

Rusk stood on the pavement outside 131 Mercer with her hands in her pockets and worked
out, in about four seconds, that she had a motive, and a method, and a set of four
numbers, and no person.

And then she looked down the street at 127, at her mother's front door, and at the side
door her mother used for the bins, and at the kitchen window eleven minutes fast, and
she thought: *two people within four houses of my mother have access to bulk fuel.*

She had written that on the Wednesday of the first week. It had been in the shorthand
and it had been true and she had let it sit there for eleven days because it had been
true, and true things do not usually require you to look at them again.

---

At 16:40 on the Friday she was in a room off the loading bay at the depot with Whitlock
and Devlin and Fenn, and Fenn put a form on the table, and there was no projector and
nobody apologised for anything.

"This is the determination," Fenn said. "It's yours to sign. It says undetermined
cause, origin class undetermined, pending further inquiry for fourteen days from the
memo, and then it says that the file is closed on expiry."

"And if there's an inquiry ongoing?"

Devlin answered it, because it was his document. "Then the determination doesn't get
filed. The determination only gets filed when the inquiry's over or abandoned, and if
the inquiry's over then it's undetermined cause, and if it's abandoned it's undetermined
cause with a note that says it was abandoned, and the family gets paid either way and
the insurer gets an answer and nobody goes to prison, and in four years somebody can
come back and reopen all of it with a subpoena and a better lawyer than we've got."

"And today is the fourteenth."

"Today is the fourteenth," said Devlin, "and I called this because I have never in six
years watched a fourteen-day period run out, and I would like to watch it once, and I
would like you to tell me plainly whether the inquiry is over."

Rusk looked at the form.

The room was very quiet. The furnace was on. Through the wall, a truck went past on the
depot road and did not stop.

"Three days ago," she said, "I had a pour pattern and a photograph of a window."
Fenn nodded. "On Tuesday morning I had a terminus and a linked origin. On Saturday
morning I had a garbage docket with a name on it and a chief who put himself in the
room in 1988 and a coroner who will not go near a telephone. What I do not have is a
person, and what I have never had is a person, and on Friday I worked out that the
reason I have never had a person is that I have been extremely careful with the women
on that street, and I have been careful with them because one of them is my mother,
and the result of being careful with her is that nobody has refused me anything."

She put both hands flat on the table.

"Which I would like on the record, because it is the unprofessional part. The
professional part is that I have not asked my mother a single question about the
eleventh of April or the third of March or the twenty-fourth of March, and I have
wanted to, every day for eleven days, and I have not, because I decided on the
Wednesday of the first week that I would not make her name the reason a house falls
down. So the inquiry is over. Not because there isn't more. Because there is more and
I am the person standing between it and the only place it could come from."

Fenn picked the pen up and looked at her.

"Delphine," he said. "I've been an investigator for twenty-six years and in all that
time I have watched about nine people decline to ask the question, and eight of them
were protecting somebody." He signed the bottom of the form. "Write undetermined. And
write, in your own section, that the determination is made on the seventeenth day of a
fourteen-day inquiry which was concluded by the investigator on grounds of
non-prosecution discretion, pending."

"That last clause is not a thing."

"It's a thing I invented in 2009 and it's been in nine determinations since," said
Fenn, "and I have never once been asked what it means, and it means *we stopped, and
here is why, and somebody can come back*."

Rusk signed at 16:52 on Friday the fourteenth of February.

Then she picked up her bag, and went out to the car park, and sat in the car, and did
not go four streets away to a house on Mercer where a woman of seventy-four was taking
in her bins for the last time before dark, and did not knock, and drove home.

And for the first time in eleven days, the file was hers alone, and there was no
fourteen-day period on it, and no memo, and nobody at the state or the county or the
sheriff's office who was obliged to write a single thing down.

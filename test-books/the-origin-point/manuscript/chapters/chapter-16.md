# 16 — Buying in April

The receipt was in a county where receipts are not kept, which was why somebody had kept it — and the somebody was Denny Aylward, who kept everything, which was the first thing Rusk thought when he handed her the drawer, and the last thing she thought when she found what was under the book.

It was the Thursday. Rain in the morning, clearing by ten. She had come to the garage to re-take Denny's April statement properly, with dates, because the Wednesday version had numbers but no sequence and Fenn had sent it back with a line through it: sequence first, then numbers. Numbers without sequence are arithmetic. She remembered the correction word for word, because Fenn's corrections were always one sentence and always right.

Denny had handed her the drawer without being asked — the whole second drawer of the hardback cabinet, lifted out and set on the workbench between them — the way he handed her everything, completely and in order and with the thing she had not asked for on top. Delivery dockets going back to 2011, banded by year with rubber bands gone soft. Oil change cards. A warranty booklet for a pump. And under the stack, beneath the hardback itself where it sat in the drawer like a stone in a riverbed, a slip of thin paper folded once.

"This was in the book?" she said, though she could see it was not.

"No, miss." Denny wiped his hands, though they were already clean — he had washed them when he saw her car, she realised, the way he always washed them for her. "This was under the book. Separate. Cash has no docket, you see, and no docket means no record, and I keep a record of everything. That is the rule I made in 2003, when the tax office asked me a question I could not answer and I decided never again."

It was a fuel receipt on thin thermal paper, the kind that fades to grey in a year. The print was still legible, barely: the garage name, the date stamp — 14 April 2025 — the volume, the word CASH in capitals, and empty lines where the vehicle registration and delivery address should have been. Initials in the corner in Denny's careful hand: DA. His own mark, so he would know he had seen it.

"Talk me through it, Denny. Slowly. Sequence first."

He nodded. He set his hands flat on the workbench, the way witnesses do when they are going to be careful, and began.

"Monday the fourteenth of April. Morning, about ten. A truck comes in — not mine, I want to say that first, it was not my truck, I have one truck and it was up on the blocks that week with the axle. County plates but not town — the number started wrong for here, I noticed that the way you notice a thing without deciding to. The man gets out. He says farm tank. Just that. Farm tank, two-sixty gallons. He pays cash — counts it twice, two piles on the counter, like he had sorted it at home into piles. I load it. Cans, upright, hose bled first. He knows how — upright, bled, no spill, which most people cannot do and which farmers always can. He leaves. I write the docket from memory because there is no docket for cash, and I initial it, and I put the slip under the book because the book is for dockets and this is not a docket, it is the absence of one, and I keep absences too."

"Who paid cash in April and left no address?" she said, though he had told her Wednesday. Sequence first, then numbers. Let him say it again, in order.

"The shape." Denny looked at the road through the open bay. "The man I told you about Wednesday. The coat. The hat. Counted twice. Loaded without spilling."

"Height? Age? Anything else?"

Denny shook his head slowly. "I cannot give you what I do not have, miss. Six years for the first one, ten months for this one. Men who buy fuel look alike when they are buying fuel. They look at the pump. They do not look at me. I look at the cans, because the cans are what I am responsible for."

Rusk bagged the receipt in front of him, initialled the bag, and had him sign beside her — his signature large and careful, taking up twice the space it needed. The garage smelled of oil and cold concrete and rain drying on warm metal. Through the open bay she could see the exact patch of road where the truck that was not Denny's had stood in April, loading 260 gallons in containers that could be returned, leaving no trace but a slip under a book.

"Denny," she said. "In 2019. Three hundred and forty gallons. Same arrangement? Walk me through that one too."

"Same." He did not hesitate. "Cash. No vehicle. No address. Different month — March, early March, cold like now. The man said farm tank twice, like he had practised it, like he wanted to be sure I heard. I heard. I wrote it in the book that night, from memory, and I have read it back since, and it has not changed, because I do not change what I write."

"Same man?"

Denny looked at his hands for a long time. The rag hung from his pocket. Outside, a car went past with its wipers going though the rain had stopped.

"I cannot say, miss." Quietly. "I want to say. I have tried to say, standing here, looking at that road. Six years between them. Men in coats. I cannot say it is the same and I will not say it is different. Both counted cash. Both loaded clean. That is all I have that is true."

She took the receipt to the depot and logged it — exhibit bag, chain form, photograph front and back under the copy stand, the thermal print already fading at the edges. She sent the photographs to the county lab with a request for paper analysis she knew would come back with nothing: no prints on thermal paper handled with garage hands, no ink worth testing, no watermark. She wrote the request anyway, because the file had to show the question was asked. The value was never in the paper. The value was in the numbers read aloud in a room: two hundred and sixty gallons, cash, no vehicle, no address. Numbers that could be stated. Numbers that stayed still while everything else moved.

Whitlock came in at three with coffee in paper cups and a face that said he had been on Mercer all morning and had the cold in his bones to prove it.

"131 has a new lock," he said, sitting heavily. "Mrs Bray had it fitted this morning. Man from the city, van, two hours. She stood over him the whole time. She said — " He consulted his notebook. "She said she has a key she has not used and she wants to keep not using it with a better lock. Her words. I wrote them down because I knew you would want them exact."

"Good for her." Rusk cupped the coffee. "Did the man finish?"

"He finished. She paid cash. She asked for two receipts." Whitlock drank. "Rusk. The county attorney telephoned while you were out. They want a name by Friday. They used the word progress, twice, in a five-minute call."

"There is no name."

"There is a receipt." He nodded at the bagged slip on the table between them.

"A receipt is not a person." She laid her hand flat beside it, not on it. "It is two hundred and sixty gallons of poured fire, bought by a shape, loaded without spilling, into a truck Denny does not own. It is premeditation with no face. I cannot give them a face by Friday."

Whitlock looked at it for a while — the thin slip, the faded print, Denny's initials. Then he looked at her, and his face changed the way it did when he stopped being a colleague and became something older, something from before the depot.

"Fenn says the file closes Friday whether or not it is ready," he said quietly. "Memo is drafted. Fourteen days from Tuesday. You know what it says."

"I know what it says."

"Do you have a plan for Friday?"

Rusk folded her hands. They were shaking — from cold, from coffee, from eleven days of carrying paper — and she made them stop by pressing them flat on the table, the way she had made them stop a hundred times before.

"I have a receipt," she said. "I have a list with one name too many. I have an alibi with a hole in it. I have a chief who was four streets away. I have a coroner who will not answer the telephone. I am going to put them all on a table in a basement and ask seven people to bring what they have."

Whitlock stared. "Seven people in a basement?"

"Under the church. Sam offered the room." She met his eyes. "Voluntarily. Everyone in this town volunteers. That is the one thing I am sure of — the only finding in eleven days that has never wavered. Ask, and they come. So I am going to ask them all at once, and put the paper on the table, and see what the table looks like whole."

Whitlock was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed — one syllable, surprised out of him. "Basement. Table. Seven volunteers." He shook his head. "You know what Fenn will call that."

"What?"

"An inquiry." He stood, crumpling the cup. "I will witness whatever you need witnessed. Book me."

She took the long way back to the depot, past Mercer, past the church with its basement
table waiting for Thursday, past Denny's garage with the light on. At the depot she signed
the receipt into the evidence locker herself — bag, seal, log, countersignature from Whitlock,
who came down specially — and stood a moment with her hand on the locker door, feeling the
weight of premeditation behind steel.

She worked the rest of the day on the numbers. 340 in March 2019. 260 in April 2025. Both cash. Both no address. Both loaded by a man who knew how to load — upright, bled, no spill. She wrote them on a card in her largest hand and pinned the card to the board and stood back until the numbers stopped being arithmetic and became a habit: the same hand, six years apart, buying in volume in the weeks before a fire. Premeditation, twice, with the same signature and no face.

At six she locked the drawer, signed the log, and went home. She stood in her flat with the receipt photographs on her telephone, enlarged until the pixels showed, and understood that she had been holding the premeditation since the Wednesday of the first week and had only now learned to read it: not who, but how, and how long planned. The who would come from the table. The how was already bagged, initialled, photographed front and back, waiting.

# 5 — The Garage

The garage was behind a house on the county road and it had a sign over the door that
said AYLES AUTO in letters that had been repainted so many times the A had become a
sort of soft square, and there was a car on the ramp with its nose up and a radio in it
playing to an empty yard.

He came out from under the car before she had finished knocking, which meant he had
been listening for the knock, which meant he had heard her car, which meant the yard
was quiet and he was paying attention to it.

"Dell," he said, before she had said anything. "It's Dell, it's a Dell County, and
you're the fire lady, and I heard you were asking about the house on the way up here,
and I'll tell you whatever you want, I do want to help, I've been thinking about it
since Tuesday."

"What's your name?"

He looked at his hands. They were black to the wrist.

"Denny Aylward," he said. "Everybody calls me Denny and everybody knows who's on the
radio on a Tuesday, and that's me, and you can put that in whatever you're writing
down." He took the rag out of his pocket and wiped his hands with it and it did
nothing at all. "Come round the back, the light's better, and shut the gate, that
gate's been since the spring."

---

The office was a room the size of a wardrobe with a door onto it and a filing cabinet
in the corner, and the filing cabinet was not the thing she looked at. The thing she
looked at was the book on the bench, which was a hardback with a spine gone soft and a
pencil on a string hanging from the bench so it could not be lost.

"Logbook?"

"Gallons. All of it, since oh-three." He said it before she asked, the way he had
said his first name. "Everything that goes out of that tank goes in that, and I've
never left a page, and my wife used to say I'd be writing down the weather before I
died, and I said what's the weather going to cost me."

She sat down on a stool — the only seat, vinyl cracked, a spring nosing through. He did not sit.
He never sat when she came; she noticed that across the week, the way she noticed everything
about Denny. Standing was his posture for telling. The yard outside ticked with cooling metal.
A customer pulled in, saw the investigator's car, and pulled out again without stopping.

"I want to ask you about fuel," Rusk said. "Specifically, I want to ask about April.
There is a purchase of two hundred and sixty gallons on the eleventh of April, cash,
no delivery address on the docket, and I want to know what that was."

Denny Aylward's face did something complicated, and what it did was arrange itself
into being helpful about it.

"Two-sixty," he agreed. "You said two-sixty. That's the diesel off the road tanker,
that's not the propane, everybody wants to make everything the propane in this county
and the propane is up there on the tank and the diesel comes in on a truck and
there's a difference and I've been saying so for years." He was already at the
cabinet. "I've got the page. I've had the page ready since Wednesday, honestly, I
wrote it out and put it on the bench in case somebody came, and I don't know why I
did that, my wife says I do things like that."

Rusk did not say anything, and he filled the silence the way water fills a crack.

"It's here. Eleven April, two-sixty gallons, diesel, cash, no address because the
tanker doesn't take an address, the tanker takes a name, and the name on the eleven
April was — " he turned the book round and pushed it at her — "Terrell Vance. Cash.
Out the door at four in the afternoon and off the yard before the light went."

The name sat in the middle of the page in Denny Aylward's careful printing and Rusk
looked at it for the length of time she needed to look at it and not one second more.

"And the 2019?" she said.

"March. Fourteenth, I think. Three-forty." He flipped back without looking, which told
her he knew the page number, which told her he had looked at this page a great many
times. "Three hundred and forty gallons, diesel, cash, and Terrell Vance again.
First time ever I'd had an order over a hundred gallons off the road tanker. I asked
him if he was doing a farm and he said he was doing a *thing* and I thought, good for
you, and that was the end of it, and I never asked him what the thing was."

"You never asked."

"Never asked him anything. He's the only person in this county I've never asked
anything, and I'll tell you why and then you can decide what you think of me." Aylward
put both hands flat on the bench. "Because he's the grandson of Cora Vance and Cora
Vance is the last person in this county who ever paid cash for anything over fifty
gallons, back in ninety-one, and I have never once in my life asked a Vance a direct
question, and I have thought about that sentence every day since Tuesday and I do not
like it."

---

He said the word *Vance* five more times in the next twenty minutes, and each time he
said it, the garage got a little smaller.

He told her about the 2019 order before he was asked, in the sense that he had clearly
told it several times since Tuesday, to the deputy, to two of his customers, and to his
wife, and that each time he had got to the same point, which was that a man he liked
had asked him for three hundred and forty gallons of diesel in cash and he had been
pleased about it, and that he had been uneasy about it for a week and then had stopped
being uneasy, and that this had been the single most convenient emotional event of his
adult life.

He told her that he had gone to the house on the fourteenth. That he had parked on
Mercer at six in the evening with a hose and a drum and had put it in the bin behind
the house, the green one, by the fence, because he was not going to leave it in the
drive where anybody driving past would see a man with a hose, and because green was
the bin that had always had fertiliser or grass clippings in it and would smell
familiar.

He said, "It smelled fine afterwards. That's the thing. I went back in October and
dumped it out in the field behind the shop and it smelled *fine*, and I have thought
about that a lot too, and I do not know what a man is supposed to do with the memory
of a thing that smelled fine."

He said that he had never seen a hose. He said that he had asked himself, out loud, in
the yard, in the evenings, at least twice a week for six years, *where did the hose
come from*, and that he had never once walked round the back of that house to find out,
and that he understood perfectly well what that was.

"And then Tuesday night," he said, "they showed the picture."

"Which picture."

"The one on the news, the — no, nobody put the picture on the news, the deputy showed
it round." Aylward went and got the book again, for no reason, and closed it, and put
it back. "It's the same room. The study. And I have been in that room. I have been in
that room maybe forty times, I sold them a burner in 2011 and I went back in March of
2019 to look at it, and there is a — " he stopped, and she watched him arrive at
something and decide whether to say it. "There's a window in that room on the north
side. It's a fixed one, it doesn't open, it's got a sill on it. And there's a chair at
the table. And if you were sat at the table in that room at night with a hose, that's
the view."

The garage was very quiet.

"I've never said that out loud," Denny Aylward said. "I've never said that out loud to
a living soul, and I've had it in my head since Tuesday, and I want you to understand
that I didn't know it was in there until I saw it in that room on the television and
the man was talking about the shape of the burn."

---

Rusk let it sit for a moment, which was a thing she was good at and which she was
aware she was doing.

Then she said, "Mr Aylward, the twenty-sixth April. You said the tanker took a name.
The eleven April had Terrell Vance on it. Who was on the twenty-sixth?"

Denny Aylward turned round and looked at the bench where the book was and did not pick
it up.

"Del," he said. "Del, I answered the question you asked. You asked about two-sixty. I
gave you two-sixty and I gave you the name off the docket, because you asked and
because I'm not going to be a difficult person about it." He turned round again. "I
said I'd tell you whatever you want. That was before I knew what you wanted."

"You said the tanker doesn't take an address, it takes a name."

"It does."

"Then where did the two hundred and sixty gallons go on the eleventh of April?"

And Denny Aylward stood in his own office in his own garage with the light coming in
the door behind him and his hands black to the wrist, and he did the thing that she
had already decided he would do, and she had decided it on Tuesday night and had not
wanted to be right about it.

He said, "The roster's on the back of the page. It was a slow Monday so I used the
back, and I don't throw paper away, and — look. Look at the top there. That isn't me.
That's a shift rota for a volunteer company, they've been using the same pad, and the
top of it says R. HALLORAN and underneath it says *ENGINE 1* with a time on it, and
that is not my writing and that is not my truck."

Rusk looked at the back of the page.

There was a rota, in a different hand, in ballpoint, with a line through it that had
been put through and then written over. There was a time on it. And there was a truck
number, written in the same hand as the rota, which was not Denny Aylward's.

"That's the thing I couldn't hold on Tuesday," Denny Aylward said. "The eleven of
April, the tanker came, the diesel went out, and it went out in a vehicle that comes
with a company on the side of it. And I signed it out, because I always sign out, and
I signed out a truck that isn't mine, and I did it in the spring when we were all being
good to each other, and I have not been able to — "

He stopped.

"Mr Aylward," Rusk said. "The third of March 2019. Three hundred and forty gallons.
Same question. Whose truck?"

And Denny Aylward, in the office the size of a wardrobe, said, "I don't know. I want
you to write down that I don't know, because that's the true version, and I have gone
over it about four hundred times in my head and it stays the same whichever way I come
at it. The eleventh I know. The third of March I do not know, and the man who drove
that one was in overalls and I did not see his face because it was March and it was
dark and I did not ask a Vance a direct question in March of 2019, and there it is,
that's the whole of it, that's what I am."

He put the pencil on its string back on the bench, coiling the string neatly round two fingers
first, the way he did everything neatly despite the oil. His hands shook slightly. Six years of
carrying, coming out through the fingers. She pretended not to see, the way you pretend not to
see a man crying at a garage bench, and wrote the last line with extra care so the pencil would
not betray her either.

"Del," he said. "You should have asked me about the hose on the second day and I'd
have told you about the hose on the second day, and you didn't ask me about the hose
until I had worked out on my own that I'd been holding it for six years, and I don't
blame you for that, and I want you to know that I don't blame you for that, because I
am not going to be one of those people who says it was somebody else's job."

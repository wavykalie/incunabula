# 14 — Sam's Church

The church had bad acoustics and Sam Okafor knew it and preached against it every Sunday without saying so, by standing closer than the room wanted and talking lower than the ceiling allowed.

Rusk arrived at ten past nine with the list of questions folded in her coat pocket and her hands cold enough that she had to flex them before she could work the door handle. The door was unlocked. It was always unlocked, Sam had told her once, because a locked church is a sentence about the town. She pushed it and the sound went up into the roof and stayed there, the way every sound stayed in that building a beat too long.

Sam was in the third row with a box of hymnals, stacking them by size. He looked up and smiled the way he always smiled, like he had been waiting for exactly this person and would have waited longer if needed.

"Delphine Rusk," he said. "You came on foot."

"The car is at the depot. Whitlock has it for the Mercer walkthrough."

"Then you walked in February." He put the box down on the pew and came toward her, wiping his hands on his trousers. "Sit. You look like you walked."

She sat in the second row. The wood was cold through her coat. Above them a radiator ticked, irregular, the way old radiators tick when they are deciding whether to work. The windows were high and narrow and let in a flat grey light that showed dust moving in it.

"I have five conversations to have today," she said. "Yours is the second."

"Who was the first?"

"Mrs Bray. About a key she has not used in six years."

Sam nodded slowly. He finished the stack — eleven hymnals, largest at the bottom — and came and sat one row ahead of her, turned sideways, his arm along the back of the pew. The posture was deliberate, she could tell: not across a desk, not face to face, side by side the way people sit when the talking might take a while.

"She told you she was a coward," he said.

"She did."

"She is not." He glanced toward the windows. "She kept a key she never used for six years. Do you know what that takes, Delphine? Every morning she opens that drawer and sees it and does not pick it up. That takes more discipline than most people bring to prayer. I have told her so. She does not believe me."

"What are the five?" he asked after a moment. "The conversations."

"You. Verhage. Lindqvist. The schoolteacher's mother. Denny, if he will see me."

"Denny will see you." A brief smile. "Denny sees everybody. That is his whole trouble, and I say that with love, which he would tell you himself if you asked, at length, in order."

Sam folded his hands. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them a pigeon moved on the roof, a soft scuffle that the ceiling carried down.

"Ask me, then," he said. "I have the whole morning. Nobody dies on a Tuesday morning, as a rule, and weddings are Saturdays. Ask me properly and I will answer properly."

Rusk took the folded paper out of her pocket. She did not unfold it yet. She held it between her fingers the way she held exhibits when she wanted the witness to see there was paper before there were questions.

"Where were you on the night of the 2019 fire, Sam?"

"Here," he said without hesitation. "In this building. Alone. I had a meeting with the treasurer that ended at eight — she is eighty-three and counts the collection wrong when she is tired, so I stayed to count it with her, and then I stayed after she left to lock up properly. At nine I heard the tones. At half past I locked the door behind me and drove to Mercer and stood in the road with everybody else."

"Who else was on Mercer that night?"

Sam exhaled through his nose. "Everybody. That is the true answer and it is also the useless one, and I am sorry for that, but it is what the street does. The street came out. Cora came out of 127 in a coat over her nightdress. Roy came in the truck. Denny came in the second truck. Verhage came on foot from Lindqvist's corner. Mrs Bray came out with a blanket she did not give to anybody — she held it the whole time, folded, against her chest. The schoolteacher stood on her step across the road and did not come down. That is six that I can swear to. There were more behind the trucks."

"I need names, Sam. Not impressions. Paper names."

"I know." He stood and went to the vestry, his shoes loud on the bare floor and then muffled on the runner. He came back with a battered ledger, marbled cover, spine taped twice. He set it on the pew between them with a care that told her what it was before he said so.

"I keep the baptisms in this," he said. "And the funerals. The marriages are in the other book because the treasurer insists. And since 2019, one other list, which I should not have kept and which I am going to give you because you asked me properly and because six years is long enough to carry paper you were never meant to hold."

He opened it near the back. A folded sheet of lined paper, creased twice, soft at the folds from handling. He held it a moment before handing it over. She saw his thumb press once against the crease, the way people press things they are giving away for the last time.

"Read it here," he said. "Then take it."

Seven names, in his neat sloping hand. Dates of baptism beside six of them, going back thirty years. The seventh had no date, only a line where the date should be.

Rusk read it twice. The radiator ticked. The pigeon settled.

"There are seven names here," she said.

"Yes."

"One of these people was not in the county that week."

Sam went very still. It was not the stillness of a man caught. It was the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he did not want to finish.

"Which one?" he said quietly.

"Terrell Vance was with friends that week. That is his statement, in his own hand, on a voluntary form. One of the friends named is Cora Vance."

Sam closed his eyes. His hands, folded, tightened until the knuckles showed. "I wrote what I was told," he said. "Word for word. I did not add and I did not take away."

"Who told you?"

"A woman who is dead now." He opened his eyes. They were wet. He did not wipe them. "She came to me the week after the fire, in this room, sat where you are sitting, and said she had seen the boy on the street that night, near the house, and that I should write it down because nobody else would and the town forgets what it does not write. I wrote it. I have carried it for six years and I have never shown it to anyone, because showing it would mean saying I believed it, and I did not know whether I believed it."

"Why did you not take it out when you learned he was away?"

"Because I never learned it." Sam's voice stayed level, but something underneath it frayed. "Nobody told me he was away, Delphine. Nobody tells me anything. Do you understand what it is to be a pastor among helpful people? Every person in this town has been helpful to me for six years. They answer my questions. They bring casseroles. They stack hymnals. And not one of them has ever told me the shape of it. You only hear the kindness. Never the thing the kindness is standing in front of."

Rusk folded the list once, along its old crease, and put it in her inside pocket, where it sat against her ribs.

"Who was the woman?" she said.

Sam told her. She wrote the name in the notebook with the date. The name meant nothing to her and that was itself a fact she underlined, because in a town of four hundred every name should mean something, and a name that means nothing is either new or deliberately unspoken.

"Sam," she said. "I want you to think before you answer. In five conversations this week — in six years — has one person refused to answer you? Ever? Even once? Even badly?"

He thought. The silence went up into the roof and stayed there with all the other sounds. The radiator ticked twice. The pigeon moved again.

"No," he said at last. He looked frightened, and he was a man whose profession was comfort, so the fright sat wrong on his face, the way a coat sits wrong on someone else's shoulders. "Not once. Not even when I asked badly. Not even when I asked about money."

"That is the same count I have."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw it move across his face the way weather moves across a field, from one side to the other, slow and complete.

"What does it mean?" he said.

"It means everyone agreed." She stood. The pew creaked. Her knees were stiff from the cold wood. "Thank you for the list. I will need you to sign a statement that this is your hand and that you have kept it since 2019. Today, if you can. Whitlock can witness."

"I will sign it today." He stood with her. He did not offer his hand. He put his instead on the back of the pew, steadying himself. "Delphine. If the list is wrong — if I carried a wrong name for six years — "

"Then we correct it on paper, the way we correct everything." She zipped her coat. "That is what paper is for."

She left him stacking hymnals by size in a building with bad acoustics, the box half empty, his hands moving with a precision that looked like prayer and was probably just habit. She walked out into February with seven names in her inside pocket, one of them too many, and the cold took her breath for the first few steps until she found her rhythm.

Verhage lived four streets over in the house with the green bin, number 140, and he answered the door before she knocked, which told her Roy had telephoned ahead, which told her the town's telegraph was working at its usual speed.

He was seventy, thin, with hands that shook continuously and eyes that did not shake at all. His kitchen was clean in the way of a man who lives alone and cleans to a schedule written on a card on the fridge. There was coffee on the stove. There were two cups out on the counter. Roy had been there that morning; the second cup was still damp at the rim.

"Miss Rusk," Verhage said. "Come in. Roy said you would come before noon."

"I am asking about 2019."

"Roy said that too." Verhage poured coffee with shaking hands and spilled none of it — a practiced steadiness inside the tremor, the way he had clearly done it ten thousand times. He set one cup before her and kept the other. "I was in this kitchen that night with Roy Halloran. He came at half eight, as he does most nights. We drink coffee. We do not talk much. Some nights we do not talk at all. At five past nine the tones went. He went out the door before the second tone finished. That is all I saw, and it is all I have ever seen, and I have told it the same way every time because it is the only way it happened."

"What did he say before he went?"

Verhage set the pot down carefully on the trivet. He looked at the window over the sink, where the green bin was visible at the kerb, lid shut, exactly aligned with the fence post.

"He said a name." The old man's voice dropped. "I will not repeat it unless you require it for a document, because it was not said to me, it was said to the room, to the air, the way a man says a thing he has been holding so long it comes out on its own."

"I require it."

Verhage nodded once. He folded his shaking hands around the cup to still them. "He said Cora. Just that. One word. Then he went out the door and I heard the truck start, and I sat here, and I washed the cups — both cups, his and mine — and I listened to the scanner, and at midnight he came back and sat in that chair you are sitting in and did not speak for an hour, and then he went home."

"Did you go to Mercer yourself that night?"

"No." Verhage shook his head. "I stayed here. I am eighty — I was seventy-four then — and the street was full of trucks, and I would have been in the way. I washed the cups. I listened. In the morning Roy told me a woman had died, and I made coffee, and we drank it, and neither of us said the name again."

Rusk wrote it down, all of it, with times. The kitchen clock ticked on the wall. She glanced at it without meaning to: it read 10:47. Her watch said 10:36. Eleven minutes fast. She felt a jolt go through her — then stopped herself, because it was not Cora's clock, it was just a clock, an old man's fast clock, and the noticing was a habit she needed to break before it made her stupid. Fast clocks were common. Her mother did not own the concept.

"Mr Verhage," she said. "I want you to think. Has anyone in this town ever refused to answer a question of mine, to your knowledge? Anyone. Ever."

He considered, his eyes steady on hers. He scratched at a stain on the table with one fingernail.

"No, miss," he said at last. "People answer. That is what we do here. We answer."

She thanked him and went back out into the cold. Two conversations. The second cup, still damp, stood on the counter behind her. No refusals.

Lindqvist was eighty-one and deaf in one ear and she made Rusk sit on her good side — the right — and offered biscuits from a tin with a hunting scene on the lid, dogs and riders frozen mid-chase.

"I was in bed," she said before Rusk asked anything, the way old people sometimes answer the question they know is coming. "I am always in bed by nine. My daughter telephones at nine every night, she has since her father died, and that night she telephoned at nine and I told her there was a fire on Mercer and she said go to bed, mother, there is nothing you can do at your age in the dark, and I went to bed. In the morning Roy told me a woman had died. That is the whole of my night."

"Did you see anything that night, Mrs Lindqvist? Hear anything?"

"No, dear." She tapped her left ear. "Nothing on this side since 2015. My house faces the wrong way for Mercer besides — the bedrooms are at the back, toward the allotments. I am no use to you. I tell everyone that and everyone keeps coming, which is kind."

"You are use," Rusk said, and meant it. "You took your daughter's call at nine. That fixes the time of the tones independently. That matters."

Lindqvist beamed, delighted, and pressed two more biscuits on her. Rusk ate one — butter, slightly stale, the tin keeping them past their best — and put the other in her coat pocket for later. Lindqvist watched her do it and nodded like it was a sacrament, like the pocketing was the point.

"Your son," Rusk said. "He is in Oslo?"

"Since the mill closed. 2017." The old woman's face softened. "He sends photographs. The fjord. His children, who speak Norwegian and English mixed, which I pretend to follow. He has not been home since the funeral — his aunt's, not — " She stopped. "Not since 2018."

"Was anyone here playing cards on the eleventh of April 2019, Mrs Lindqvist? In this house?"

"No, dear." Puzzled, but willing. "I was in bed by nine. I am always in bed by nine. There was no one here but me and the telephone."

Rusk wrote that down with the time: 11:20. Three conversations. She asked the question almost as an afterthought, the way she had started asking it, testing the shape of it in her mouth.

"Has anyone ever told me no this week, Mrs Lindqvist? To your knowledge?"

"What, dear?"

"Nothing." Rusk stood. Her right knee cracked. "Thank you. The biscuit will get me to Mercer."

"Good, dear. Come back when you are not working and I will make fresh."

Three conversations. No refusals. A woman who faced the wrong way and heard nothing had still answered everything.

The schoolteacher's mother lived on the far side of Mercer in a house with plastic sheeting over the downstairs windows, taped at the edges, rattling faintly in the wind. She was seventy-nine and she stood in the doorway and did not invite Rusk in, which was the closest thing to a refusal Rusk had received in eleven days, and which she noted precisely for that reason: the absence of refusal, even here, even now.

"I called it in," the woman said before any question, her arms folded against the cold. "At twenty-five past nine. I saw a glow in the window of 127 and I watched it for twenty minutes because I thought the neighbours were having a fire, which sounds stupid now but at the time it was the most reasonable thing in the world — people have fires, it was March, it was cold. Then the glow moved wrong and I telephoned."

"Did you see anyone on the street before that? Anyone at all?"

"No. I was inside. My daughter was at the school, she teaches evenings, adult literacy. I was alone with the television on mute. I keep it on mute and watch the pictures."

"Did you see Roy's truck arrive?"

"I heard it." The woman's eyes went to the street, then back. "I did not see it. I was on the telephone by then, giving the address, spelling Mercer, which the operator got wrong twice. By the time I went to the window the street was full."

Rusk wrote that down: 25 past, twenty minutes watching, operator, full street. The woman watched her write with an attention that was almost hunger — the attention of someone who has waited six years to have her twenty minutes written down by someone official.

"You are Cora's girl," the woman said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Your mother is a good woman." Flat. Final. The way people in that town closed subjects.

"Yes."

"That is all I have." The woman stepped back half a pace. "I am sorry. I wish I had looked out sooner. I have wished it every night for six years. Twenty minutes. I watched for twenty minutes and thought it was a fire in a grate."

Four conversations. The door stayed half shut. Every question answered anyway.

Denny was in the garage with his hands in an engine and his hardback open on the fender, pages weighted with a spanner. He saw her coming through the open bay and wiped his hands on a rag and came out to the road to meet her — all the way to the road, past the pumps, to the kerb — which Myron had said he never did, which made it, she realised, the fifth small wrong thing of the day that was actually right.

"Miss Rusk," Denny said. He was breathing a little fast from the walk, or from anticipation. "I have been thinking since Wednesday. I have the numbers for you, properly this time. Three hundred and forty gallons in March of 2019. Cash. No vehicle. No address. Two hundred and sixty gallons in April of this year. Cash. No vehicle. No address. Both times the man said it was for a farm tank and both times I wrote down what he looked like and neither description matches anybody I know, because I described the coat and the hat, not the man, which I see now is the wrong thing to describe."

"Can I see the book, Denny?"

He handed it over without hesitation — the whole book, twenty-two years of gallons, held out in both hands. The pages were ruled and every line filled in the same careful hand, in ink, with dates in the margin. 2003 to 2025. On the April page, in the margin beside the 260 entry, a note in different ink, different hand: engine one, and a time.

"Whose writing is that on the back?" she said, though she knew.

"Chief's. Roy." Denny leaned to look, though he knew it by heart. "He writes the rota in my book when he is here because he has no paper on him. He has done it for years. Ask him. He will tell you. He tells everything."

"Denny," she said. "Do you remember the truck in April? Anything about it?"

Denny looked at the road — the actual road, where the truck had stood — the way witnesses look at the place when the memory lives in the place and not in their heads.

"I remember it was not mine." Slowly. "I remember the plates were county but not town — the number started wrong for here. I remember the man paid cash and counted it twice, two piles, like he had sorted it at home. I remember he knew how to load without spilling, the cans upright, the hose bled first, which most people do not know and which farmers always know. That is all I have. There are men you see once and they are the shape of men. This was a shape."

"Would you know him again?"

"No, miss." Honest. Immediate. "I would not. I have tried. I stood here yesterday for ten minutes trying, and what comes is the coat."

She gave the book back. He held it against his chest like a child, briefly, then tucked it under his arm.

"Denny," she said. "Have you ever said no to me?"

He laughed, surprised into it, a real laugh that showed a missing tooth. "No, miss. Why would I? You ask proper questions. You write things down. Nobody writes things down."

Five conversations. Nobody had refused to answer her. Not once. Not even badly. Not even the woman behind the half-shut door. Not even about money, or trucks, or lists kept six years.

She walked back to the depot in the dark with the list in her inside pocket burning through the coat like a coal, and the biscuit from Lindqvist's tin still whole in the other pocket, and understood with a completeness that sat low in her stomach that the helpfulness was not kindness. It was an agreement. Five rooms, five voices, five open doors — four open, one half-shut — and not one of them had said the word no in six years.

She sat in the car park with the engine off and the cold coming back through the doors and wrote it in the notebook, in plain words, under the date, so the reader of the file would see it too: nobody refuses. Everyone answers. Five conversations, zero refusals. That is the pattern.

Then she went inside and logged the list as evidence, and signed for it, and dated it, and got Whitlock to countersign the chain, and went home to a flat with no food in it and ate the biscuit from Lindqvist's tin standing at the counter in her coat, and slept four hours with the list on the bedside table where she could see it when she woke.

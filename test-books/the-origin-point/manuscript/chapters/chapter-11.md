# 11 — Nobody Goes

There is a thing that fires do to a chair that they do not do to a table, and it is not
a large thing, and nobody in eleven years had ever told Rusk about it, because it was
not the kind of thing you put in a course.

A chair has legs. If a chair is pushed in under a table, the seat is horizontal and the
legs go down under the table, and the heat rises and goes along the underside of the
table and the chair is underneath all of it and the chair is the coolest thing in the
room, and the legs go last and the seat goes last and the back goes first, and you get
a shape that looks like a chair in profile, and everybody recognises it, and nobody
writes about it.

If a chair is pulled out from a table, the seat is horizontal, and the back of the chair
is now exposed above the level of the table, and the back is what is closest to the
ceiling, and the back goes first, badly, all at once.

Rusk had learned this in a barn outside a state university, from a man named Bell, who
had made four hundred people learn it over nine years and had never once mentioned it
in a handout, and who had said, on a Tuesday, to nobody in particular, *watch what a
chair does, and then watch what a chair does when somebody was in a hurry.*

She had thought about that sentence for a very long time.

The photograph in the 2019 file had a chair in it. The chair was in the bottom right of
the frame, cut off, and it was a kitchen chair, and the back of it was gone entirely
and had fallen forward onto the seat, and the seat itself was intact, and there was a
thing on the floor beside it that she had not looked at properly.

The 2025 photograph had a chair in it too. The same chair, in the sense that every
house on that street had the same chair, and the back of it was gone, and it had
fallen forward onto the seat, and the seat was intact.

Rusk sat in the records store on the Thursday with the two prints on the light table —
Fenwick having gone home, the building settling, the fluorescent tubes buzzing their evening
note — and she looked at the two chairs for longer than she had looked at the patterns.
She fetched the magnifier from the drawer, the heavy one with the cracked handle, and went
over each chair leg by leg: the fall direction, the seat survival, the back's collapse line.
Both backs gone forward. Both seats intact. The two collapses matched the way brothers match:
not identical, unmistakable.

Both of them had gone the same way.

---

It was not a big thing. She was very careful, sitting there, not to make it a big
thing, because the first rule of this work is that you do not promote a coincidence
into a finding by wanting it, and the second rule is that you do not demote a finding
into a coincidence by being frightened of it, and she had been trained on both and she
had neither of them down to a science.

So she made a list of the ways a chair could do that, on its own, in two fires, in two
houses, six years apart, without anybody being the same person.

A chair that was pulled out from a table before a fire was set goes forward. A chair
that was pushed in and then pulled out goes forward. A chair that got hit by something
goes forward. A chair that was in the path of a doorway gets knocked forward by a person
running. A chair that collapses under load falls forward.

A chair pushed back in after somebody has been in a hurry leaves an angle.

And that was the thing. Not the collapse. The angle.

She had measured it. She had a photograph, and a ruler on the desk, and she had put the
ruler against the screen and she had measured the angle between the chair and the table
in the 2019 photograph and she had measured the angle between the chair and the table
in the 2025 photograph, and the two angles were the same to within the resolution of a
photograph taken in a hurry by a different person six years apart with a different
camera.

Eighteen degrees, in the 2019 one. Seventeen and a half, in the 2025 one. Which is a
difference of half a degree, which is nothing, which is the same.

A person stands up in a hurry. They do not look at the chair. They turn round and go,
and the chair stays where their knee caught it, and it is at a stupid angle that is
determined by the width of the knee and the distance of the table and the way the
person was standing, and none of those three things have anything to do with the
person, and all three of them are the same in a house built to the same pattern book
in the same year on the same street.

Rusk wrote, in the pad:

*Chair: same failure mode, both fires. Angle consistent with a person leaving
in a hurry.*

*This does not distinguish. A person leaves in a hurry in every fire ever set. It is
worth nothing on its own.*

And then, underneath, in different handwriting, smaller:

*It is worth nothing on its own.*

*It is worth everything as the twelfth thing.*

---

Because that was the thing. It was not a clue. She had been doing this long
enough to know what one felt like in the hand when she found it, and this was not
that, and she had the good sense not to argue with the feeling.

What it felt like was a *tell*. A habit. The thing a person does with their hands when
they have just done something they are going to have to do again.

She had known a man once, a very long time ago, who put his keys in the same pocket in
the same order every single time, and had done it for six years, and had never once
known he was doing it, and when somebody pointed it out to him he had stood there and
felt his own right hand go to his left pocket and thought, with a clarity that stayed
with her: *that is not a decision. That is a man.*

A chair at eighteen degrees, twice, in two houses on the same street, six years apart,
in two rooms that were the same room.

Which brings her to the second of the two, which is a knee, and which is even less
like a decision than the first, because a knee is not anywhere. A knee is just where
a body was.

---

Rusk sat in the records store until the light outside went the colour of the inside of
a paper bag, the tubes buzzing on alone now, her tea gone cold in the chipped mug. She got up
once to lock the outer door from inside — the night precaution, the building empty — and came
back to the light table, and then she did the thing she had been avoiding doing for four days,
which was to stop looking at evidence and start writing down what she thought.

She had written, in the past four days, a great many things in the shorthand, and the
shorthand had got her through: origin class, terminus, volumes, dates, rota, circuit,
suppression.

She had not written a name.

She wrote now:

*Everybody on that street is being helpful and nobody has said no. I have been
reading that as a wall. It might be a wall.*

*What I have not done is the obvious thing, which is to ask the question properly, in
a room, in front of a person, in a form that a person can refuse.*

*If I do that, one of them will say no, and I will have a name, and the rest of it
falls apart. I have four days.*

*If I do not do that, then on Friday the county writes undetermined and closes, and
the 2025 fire is a fire and the 2019 fire is a determination, and there will be a
woman who dies of natural causes a second time, in a record, and nobody will ever know
that it happened twice.*

*So the question is whether I am prepared to be the person who made a seventy-four-
year-old woman say no to me on a recording device.*

She looked at that for some time.

Then she wrote, at the bottom, the only line in nine days that was not shorthand:

*Her name is Cora Vance and I have not said it out loud to anybody in this county
since Tuesday, and I am not going to make my mother's name the reason a house falls
down.*

And she did not know yet — she would not know for another two days, and by then it
would be far too late to be a comfort — that she had also, in that paragraph, just
decided not to ask the question properly, and that the not-asking was going to cost a
person their life, and that it was going to be the most defensible decision she ever
made.

# 9 — The County Wants It Closed

The meeting was at nine on the Tuesday and it was in a room with a projector that did
not work, and the man who had called it apologised for the projector twice in the first
minute, and Rusk understood immediately that the projector was not why he had
apologised.

There were five of them in the room. Whitlock, from the sheriff's office. A man from
the county attorney's office called Devlin who had a legal pad and a tie he had clearly
had to buy for the occasion. The county risk manager, who had come for eleven minutes
and stayed for fifty, and who had spent the first twenty writing and the last thirty
not writing. And Fenn, from the office, who was forty-nine and had been an
investigator for twenty-six years and who was the only person in the room who had been
to a fire at 131 Mercer.

Fenn had the folder. Rusk had not been in the room for the twenty-six years and it
showed.

"I'm going to say something and I want it on the record," Fenn said, "and then I'm
going to stop talking, because I've been an investigator for twenty-six years and I
know exactly what happens to a man in a room like this when he starts enjoying it."

Nobody said anything.

"2019 and 2025 are the same act." He put his hand flat on the folder. "I've read
Delphine's terminus work and I agree with it and I'd have got there myself in about
three weeks instead of four days, which doesn't matter. What matters is that in
eleven months of paper between those two fires there is a man who bought three hundred
and forty gallons of diesel for cash in March of 2019 and two hundred and sixty
gallons in April of 2025, and in between, on a garage pad, a volunteer fire company
wrote a shift rota on the back of the docket, and a woman of fifty-nine died in a
study and was certified natural by a determination that cites a circuit the county
replaced in 2016."

He stopped.

"I'm going to stop talking now."

Devlin, from the county attorney, took his glasses off.

"Fenn. That's four documents, three of which are circumstantial and the fourth of
which is a woman's death certificate that a coroner has told the investigator in
confidence. What's your filing standard?"

"To reopen 2019? I'd have the 2025 one at probable cause by Thursday." Fenn looked at
his watch. "The 2019 one I'd have in a month if I had a name, and I don't have a
name, and the reason I don't have a name is that there is not one document in this
county that says anybody's name on a piece of paper relating to either fire."

"You think the two are linked."

"I think somebody has done this twice, in the same room, and that the second time was
six years and a room after the first, and I think when that happens it doesn't stop."
Fenn said it flatly. "And I think that if this goes in a room like this with the word
*deliberate* attached to it before the first family has been told anything at all,
then this room is going to do in about four hours what it took the first one six years
to do, and it is going to do it with a room of three people in it who have already
agreed with each other and will not now un-agree in front of us."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Right," said Devlin. "Here is where we are. I am going to say the hard thing and then
we are going to work. The county is not going to reopen 2019. The county cannot
reopen 2019. The county is going to be asked, by me, this afternoon, whether it wants
to reopen a file, and the answer is no, and the reason the answer is no is that the
2025 determination will be a negligence determination, and that is what an insurer
needs, and that is what this family's benefits are keyed to, and if we file this as
deliberate on the strength of a photograph and a feeling about a chair, then the
2025 file becomes a criminal file, and the 2025 file becoming a criminal file is not
this county's decision to make, it is a prosecutor's, and there is no prosecutor in
this county who is going to look at this."

"So what happens to the third one?" Rusk said.

Devlin looked at her. He was younger than her and had been doing this for six years
and she could see him deciding, quite visibly, to be honest, which she had not
expected and which changed her opinion of him.

"Friday," he said.

"There is no Friday."

"There is a Friday now." He set his pen down. "Here's what I am going to do, and I am
going to do it in front of all of you so that it is not a favour. I am going to write
a memo to the county this afternoon recommending a finding of undetermined cause,
pending further inquiry, and I am going to recommend a fourteen-day period in which
the further inquiry happens without the county's blessing, and I am going to do that
because I can defend it, and because a finding of undetermined with a live inquiry
attached to it is the only document in this building that does not put your family in a
witness box."

"And the fourteen days end Friday."

"They end Friday."

"And then?"

"And then on Friday the file is what it is, and the finding is what it is, and if
nothing has changed it is closed, and the insurer pays out, and the family get their
money, and Delphine Rusk and I both stop being interesting to each other." He picked
his pen back up. "I am telling you this now rather than letting you find it out on
Thursday night, because I would rather you had six days knowing exactly what the six
days are for, than five, or one."

The room was silent.

Rusk sat with it.

She had told a lawyer on a pavement on the Tuesday of the previous week that she would
have paperwork by Friday. She had told it to a man with a decent face who had written
it on the back of a card, and she had done it in order to be the kind of person who
gets six days, and it had turned out that six days was exactly what was on offer, and
that they were on offer to a completely different person than the one who had invented
them.

"It's the same day," she said.

"What?"

"The day I told the family lawyer. The Tuesday. It was this week last Tuesday. I've
been working since then and it turns out that was the actual deadline and nobody told
me and I made one up that happened to be right, and I'd like to record that I would
like somebody to notice that."

Fenn made a small sound that was almost a laugh.

Whitlock, from the sheriff's office, who had not spoken for fifty minutes, said:
"Investigator. Can I ask you something as a person and not as a badge."

"Yes."

"Did you believe the six days when you said them?"

"Yes," said Rusk.

"Any particular reason?"

"Because they were all I had," she said. "I had a file with a pour pattern in it and a
window nobody would look at twice, and I was in a car park, and I wanted to be the kind
of person who gets six days, and I have never in my life been the kind of person who
gets six days, so I said it, and then I made them six days, and then I have spent five
of them getting to a chair in a photograph that nobody else has looked at."

Whitlock wrote that down. All of it. In a sheriff's officer's small hard handwriting,
with the time next to it, and she noticed that and did not comment on it.

"Right," said Devlin. "Six days. What's in them?"

Rusk opened the folder on the table, and the risk manager stopped writing for the first
time in twenty minutes — pen lifted mid-word, left hanging — and she laid out, in order,
on a table in a room with a broken projector, everything she had, in the order she had got
it, with the dates on it. The pour photograph first, then the 2019 determination copy, then
Denny's numbers on a card, then the chair frames. Four pieces. The room leaned in. Even the
risk manager. The projector hummed uselessly above them, its fan going, projecting nothing
on the blank wall — the perfect emblem, she thought later, of the county's attention: running,
noisy, showing nothing.

Devlin studied each piece without touching — hands folded, the way cautious men handle
other people's evidence. Fenn watched Devlin instead of the paper; he had seen it all before,
he was watching for the flinch. Whitlock took notes in the small hard hand. The risk manager
resumed writing after a minute, slower now, choosing words.

"Six days," Devlin said at last. "Starting when?"

"Starting now." Rusk closed the folder. "Today is Tuesday. Friday is the fourteenth."
She did the arithmetic aloud so it would be on someone's record besides hers. "Fourteen days
from your memo, six days from my pavement. Either way, Friday." She looked at Fenn. "I will
have a name by Friday, or I will have a file that says why I do not."

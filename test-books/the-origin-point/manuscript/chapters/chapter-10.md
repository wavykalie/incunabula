# 10 — What Adela Changed

She had said she would do it properly, and doing it properly meant going in with
nothing, and she had spent an hour in the car park working out how to go in with
nothing.

"You cannot take anything in," Adela had said on the phone, on Sunday, in the voice she
used for people she was going to be honest with. "Not a tape recorder, not a notebook,
not another coroner. If you take anything in, then anything I say in that room is
contaminated, and in about four years somebody with better hair than me is going to
stand up and say *the investigator was in the room with a recorder*, and it will be
true, and the whole thing will fall over on a technicality and everybody in it will go
home and think fire."

"And if I write it down afterwards?"

"Then you write down afterwards, from memory, in your own words, which is worth
nothing legally and everything in the world to the person who has to sit in a chair
with a family."

Rusk had asked for the meeting on the Tuesday afternoon — telephoning the coroner's office
three times before Adela picked up herself — and had been given the Wednesday, which was a
longer wait than she wanted, and had used it. Tuesday night she barely slept: the 2019 file
spread on her kitchen table, the four lines, the embossed initials, the window note she had
closed herself on the twenty-fourth of March. At two in the morning she got up and re-read
the 2016 inspection by the oven light, standing in her coat because the flat was cold. The
panel replacement. Circuits four and five. No kitchen circuit. She copied the numbers onto a
fresh card and pinned it beside Sam's list that did not yet exist — the board filling, item
by item, the way a wall fills with bricks.

She had gone back to the 2019 file. She had the determination, four lines, her own
initials on page three. She had the appliance schedule. She had the 2016 electrical
inspection with the county's stamp on it. She had the field note from the fourteenth of
March with the embossed initial and the note about the window.

What she did not have was the coroner's report, and she could not have it, and the
reason she could not have it was that the only person who could give it to her was
going to have to decide to.

---

Adela Iversen's office was over a pharmacy in a building that had been a
department store, and it was warm, and there were eleven certificates on the wall and a
window that had been painted shut, and Rusk sat down in the chair by the desk and put
her hands in her lap and said nothing at all for ninety seconds.

That was the whole of the technique and she had learned it in a seminar in 2013 from a
man from the state bureau who had been an investigator for a very long time and who
had said, *silence is a tool and a bad investigator uses it as a weapon and a good one
uses it as a chair.*

At about the seventy-second second Adela said, "You've brought nothing."

"No."

"You've written it all down. I can see it in your face, you've got it in order with
the dates on it. You're frightened of having it in a folder and you're frightened of
not having it and you've decided not to bring it, and that is the single most correct
thing anyone has done in this room in nine years."

"I'd like to hear what you have," said Rusk. "And I'd like to hear it before I tell
you what I've got, because if I tell you first you'll know what I'm here to get, and
you'll answer it instead of answering the question."

Adela looked at her for a moment.

Then she opened a drawer and took out a file, and the file was thin, and she did not
open it.

"March," she said. "The fire was in March. The decedent is fifty-nine. The decedent
is female. The decedent lived alone at 127 Mercer. The decedent's next of kin is a
daughter in a town ninety minutes from here, which is where the daughter has been for
eleven years, and the daughter is the schoolteacher."

"Marguerite Pham."

"Marguerite Pham," Adela agreed. "And the decedent is not your mother and the decedent
is not your aunt and the decedent is not anybody whose name you have been avoiding
saying out loud since you walked in. I know because you have said your mother's name
twice and the decedent's once and you put the decedent's in the middle of a sentence."

Rusk did not move.

"I want to say the next part plainly," Adela said, "because there
is a version of this meeting where I am helpful and there is a version where I am
accurate, and I have only got the second one.

"In March of 2019 I attended a house fire at 127 Mercer. It was a Sunday. I attended
because I attend everything inside the county line, which nobody thanks me for. There
was a decedent. I did the decedent. And the decedent had been dead before the fire and
had been dead for some time, and the fire had not been set in that room for money or
for spite, it had been set in that room because a woman of seventy-four panicked, which
is what women of seventy-four do, and because the fire service took eleven minutes to
arrive and in the meantime the room was involved and everybody in the house went
outside the way you are supposed to, except one person who did not, and I have thought
about that every day for six years and I am not going to pretend to you that I have
been carrying it lightly."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, on the pharmacy side, a till went off.

"That is a great deal more than I should have said," Adela said. "And I said it
because you sat there with your hands in your lap and did not ask me a single question,
which is the first time in nine years that anybody in this county has done that, and I
would have said the same thing to a reporter, and I want that on the record too, because
if it turns out I'm wrong about you I would like to be able to say that I tested you
and you passed and I talked anyway, and that the fault is mine."

Rusk said, "Thank you."

"Do not thank me yet." Adela put the file down. "Here is what is in it, and then we
stop.

"The 2019 determination says smoke inhalation. The determination is signed by a
supervisor and countersigned by a junior investigator four months into the post. The
determination cites a branch circuit serving a kitchen receptacle. The 2016 electrical
inspection on the same property lists a panel replacement and a re-circuit, and the
kitchen receptacles are on circuits four and five, and there is no circuit serving a
kitchen receptacle on the 2019 schedule at all, because the man who wrote the 2019
schedule wrote it from the 2016 one and left the old numbers in.

"The decedent's cause of death was blunt force trauma to the posterior skull with
circumferential fracture patterning, and there is a second and smaller area of blunt
force trauma to the left forearm, and I have written that the patterning is
consistent with a household implement and has been in a fire for a period which I
cannot state, and I have written that in my opinion the decedent was already dead at
the time of the fire, and that the fire was the concealment of that fact and not its
cause."

"And the manner?"

"Undetermined. Not natural, not homicide, not accident. Undetermined, with a
recommendation that it be reviewed in the light of any subsequent findings." She
stopped. "And the finding was suppressed on the eleventh of March, by me, and it has a
date and a time on it, and both of those are in a system I do not control, and I have
told you that it exists and I am not going to tell you how to find it."

"Why not?"

"Because the fact that it is findable is what I am offering and the fact that I told
you it is findable is what I cannot take back," said Adela Iversen, "and if you use
it in a way that puts a seventy-four-year-old woman in a chair, then I will have spent
six years suppressing a finding and then handed over the means of reopening it, and
that will be the worst thing I have ever done, and I will have to go on living in
this town which is where I live and where I bought my house when I was
twenty-six."

Rusk sat very still.

"You said *then*," she said. "In a way that puts a seventy-four-year-old woman in a
chair."

"Yes."

"You haven't put Cora Vance in a chair. You've put Marguerite Pham's daughter in a
chair and you've been quite clear about it and you have done it deliberately. You told
me the decedent's daughter is ninety minutes away. You told me the decedent's next of
kin before you told me anything else about the decedent."

Adela Iversen put both hands flat on the desk.

"I have been doing this for eleven years," she said, "and I have never once been
clever in a room. That is what I am good at. If I was clever in a room I would have
been a prosecutor and I would be earning four times what I earn."

"No," Rusk said. "I don't think you're clever. I think you have spent six years
holding one end of something and I think you have been waiting for a person to turn up
at the other end of it and I think you have decided, this week, that I am that person,
and I think you are probably right, and I am not going to make you say it in a
recording device or on a telephone, and I am not going to write down a word of what
you have just told me."

Adela Iversen did not say thank you. She said, "The determination cites a circuit
that does not exist, and I signed the finding that was suppressed, and in March of
2019 a woman of fifty-nine was already dead in that room before anybody lit anything,
and I have never been able to work out whether the county covered that up or whether
nine separate people each decided, individually, kindly, not to."

"And if it was the second one?"

"Then it is not a conspiracy," Adela said, "and it is worse, and there is nothing in
the world to be done about it except to find out."

She opened the file, then, and she did not give it to Rusk — held it on her side of the desk
with both hands, the way people hold things they are deciding about. The silence stretched.
Below, the pharmacy till went off twice. A bus passed. Adela looked at the file, then at Rusk,
then down at her own hands, and made the decision the way she made every decision: precisely,
without ceremony. She took a page out of it
and turned it round and pushed it across the desk, and it was a form, and it was a
suppression record, and it had a date on it and a time on it, and the time was
16:40, and the date was the eleventh of March, and underneath the time there was a
box marked *REVIEWED BY* and the box was empty.

"Eleven days after you wrote your note about the window," Adela said. "You wrote it on
the fourteenth. I suppressed on the eleventh of April. You closed your own note on the
twenty-fourth of March and signed the determination on the twenty-fourth of March, and
I suppressed mine eleven days after you, and neither of us has ever spoken to the
other one, and that is not a coincidence, that is two people being careful, and I have
thought about that more than I have thought about the fire."

Rusk looked at the suppression record for a long time — the date, the time 16:40, the empty
REVIEWED BY box. Then she folded her hands in her lap, the way she had folded them at the
start, and said the thing she had come to say, the thing she had worked out in the car park
with nothing in her hands.

"I am going to reopen it," she said. "Not today. Not this week. But the 2019 file is going
to be reopened, by me, with my name on the request. The circuit does not exist. The window
note was mine and I closed it. The woman at the foot of the stairs deserves a true finding."
She paused. "I wanted you to hear that from me first, in this room, with nothing taken in.
Because you held your end for six years alone, and whatever comes next, you will not hold it
alone any more."

Adela looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded once — precisely, without ceremony,
the way she did everything. "Then I will keep the system entry current," she said. "The timestamp
will hold. Whatever paper you bring, the 11 March record will be there to meet it." She stood,
which was the dismissal and the benediction both. "Go carefully, Investigator. And write it
down afterwards — everything, from memory, in your own words. Worth nothing legally."
She almost smiled. "Everything in the world to the person."

Rusk walked out past the pharmacy with the till ringing below, past the eleven certificates,
into February light that hurt her eyes after the warm office. She sat in the car for ten
minutes writing it all down — every word she could hold, the suppression time, the empty box,
the promise she had made — before driving anywhere, the way procedure required and memory
required. Then she filed the memory the way she filed everything: dated, timed, kept.

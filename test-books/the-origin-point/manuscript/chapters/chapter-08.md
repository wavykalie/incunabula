# 8 — The Hinge

She did it on a Monday, in a room with no furniture in it, on the floor, with the
lights off, because a room with the lights off is a room where you cannot flatter
anything and she had been trained that way in a barn outside a state university by a
man named Bell who had made her do it forty times and had never once explained why.

The room was the records store at the depot building, which is a concrete room off the
loading bay with no window, and which the county had let her use on a Monday morning
because it has no other purpose and because the man who let her use it did not ask a
single question, which she noted.

The lights were off. She had the two prints on the floor.

The first was Nadia Pham's, from 131 Mercer, the study, north side, and it was a very
good photograph taken by a very good photographer at nine in the morning on a Tuesday
in February with the light behind her and a scale card in shot.

The second was the 2019 file, third photograph, studio, the one that was blurred and
cropped and had a chair in the corner of it, and it was a bad photograph, and it had
been taken by somebody in a hurry on the day of the fire by a person who had thirty-one
other things to do.

She put them side by side on the concrete, and she got down on the floor on her hands
and knees, which is not something her back is going to enjoy and which she had done
nine hundred times, and she looked.

For a long time there was nothing.

The two photographs were of two different rooms in two different houses. The lighting
was nothing like the lighting. One was in colour and one was not, or appeared not to
be. The perspective was wrong in the 2019 one — the photographer had been standing
back and to the left and had not been able to get the whole floor, and the edge of the
frame cut through the pattern at a diagonal.

And then, in the middle of it, the two patterns did the thing that you spend eleven
years learning to see and about four hundred documents hoping will happen to you once
in a career.

Both of them opened to the left.

She had assumed, for six years and for eight days, that this was nothing. Half the
fires in the state had a V that opened left. A quarter of them opened right. It was
a coin toss, and a coin toss is not a finding, and she had written *direction of travel
not determinable from available imagery* on four hundred forms and had been right
every time.

The thing about a pour pattern was not its direction. The thing about a pour pattern
was its terminus.

You could not choose a terminus. You could not aim it. A liquid leaves a line
somewhere because that is where the floor ended, or where the liquid ended, or where
the floor was cold enough or the amount ran out, and the end of the line is a fact
about the physics and not a fact about the person.

A person chooses where to pour. A person does not choose where the liquid stops.

Both of these lines stopped. And both of them stopped in the same place in the same
kind of room, on the same side, at the same distance from the same wall, with the same
slight gathering at the left-hand terminus where the volume had gone to nothing and
the last of it had sat on the varnish and dried there in a curve.

Rusk sat back on her heels on the concrete floor of a room with no window in a county
depot and looked at two photographs from opposite ends of a six-year gap and she
understood, completely, in the way that you understand something you have known for a
long time and been refusing to arrange, that these were the same act.

Not the same fire. The same act, done twice, by the same person, in the same room of
a house of the same type on the same street, and the reason it had gone unnoticed for
six years was that the county had certified one of them as a fault in a circuit that
did not exist, and you cannot find the second one of a pair if the first one has been
written down as nothing happening.

---

She stayed on the floor for a long time — fifty-three minutes by the wall clock she could
not see in the dark, counted afterwards by the stiffness in her knees. She thought about Bell's
barn, the forty repetitions, the burn pit smell that got into everything. She thought about
Harrow telling her the glass was bloom, kindly, the way kind men close files. She thought
about Adela's eleven certificates and the till below, and Denny's hardback going back to 2003,
and Sam's hymnals stacked by size. Everyone in that town kept something: paper, lists, rosters,
keys unused. She kept photographs on a concrete floor. That was her version. Everyone had one.

There is a stage in this work that does not have a name and is not taught, because it
does not look like work. It is the stage where you sit on a floor with your hands on
your knees and you are not thinking about the evidence any more, you are thinking about
what the evidence is going to cost, and you already know the answer to that and you are
sitting there anyway.

The answer was her mother's street.

Rusk had been in this town four days. In four days she had learned that Marguerite
Pham had lived four doors from Cora Vance for eleven years and had died in a study
and been certified as a natural death, and that Cora Vance had never once spoken to her,
and that in a house in which nobody has spoken to anybody for eleven years a person of
fifty-nine can die alone and be written down kindly.

And now the same thing was in the house next door, and this time there were three of
them, and this time it was not going to be certified as a fault in a circuit.

Which meant it was going to be a case.

Rusk got up off the floor. She was fifty-three minutes in there. She had been fifty-
three minutes on a concrete floor deciding something that she had already decided,
which was that she was going to take this into a room, and that when she did, the first
sentence out of her mouth would not be about 127 Mercer.

It would be about her own signature.

She had known that since Thursday. What she had not known until 08:41 on a Monday
morning in a room with the lights off was how she was going to do it, and she stood
there in the dark and worked out that she was not going to do it by herself, and that
was a considerable piece of luck and possibly the only one coming.

She collected the prints. She put them in the folder in order, 2019 first, 2025 second,
because that was the way round, and she wrote on the acetate a note in pencil in her
own hand which read:

*Origin class: multiple, in both. Terminus identical in configuration and position.
V-direction irrelevant. The terminus is not a choice.*

*Two acts. One method. Same room.*

*Recommend this be treated as a linked origin investigation and that the 2019 file be
reopened.*

She read that last line three times and thought about the woman four doors away who
came out of a side door with a bag in each hand to bring in her bins because the truck
was on them.

Then she went out into the loading bay, blinking in the fluorescent glare after the dark,
and put the folder in the car under a blanket the way she hid everything worth hiding, and drove to the
coroner's office, and did not get out, and sat in the car park for twenty-five minutes
watching the pharmacy door below Adela's window — customers in and out, a child with a balloon,
an old man counting change at the till — ordinary Monday life going on under a room where a
suppressed finding waited in a system nobody controlled
with the folder on the passenger seat, doing the thing she had decided to do, which
was to be absolutely certain before she said it out loud to a single human being.

At the twenty-fifth minute she took out the pad and wrote the number of things she
would be required to be able to prove, in a court, from a document, and the list had
four items on it, and she looked at the list, and then she wrote underneath it:

*4. 2019 and 2025 were lit by the same person.*

And she looked at that for a long time, because it was the only one of the four that
paper was never going to be able to carry.

She was going to have to prove it with a person, and the person was going to be someone
who had ordered three hundred and forty gallons of diesel in cash in March of 2019
and two hundred and sixty gallons in April of 2025, and who had driven out of a garage
on the county road in a truck that belonged to a volunteer fire company, and who was
going to be somewhere on Mercer Street that evening having tea.

Rusk started the car.

She had forty-nine hours and a folder, and she was going to need considerably more than
that, and the first thing she had to do was the hardest thing in the book, which was
to go and ask seven people on one street for their help in a way that would not alarm
any of them.

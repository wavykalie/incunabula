# 28 — The Ones Who Agreed

Six of the seven are still in town. They are not leaving. That was the first thing Rusk established in the week after Cora — not from paper but from looking: the same cars, the same bins, the same routines, Roy's roster check, Verhage's cups, Lindqvist's curtains. The town held its shape. Whatever she had done to it with tables and tapes, it held.

She saw each of them in that week, separately, without a folder, without questions, without a witness. She went to tell them the file was closed — determination filed, exhibits boxed, receipt in her pocket — which was a courtesy no procedure required and which she performed anyway, in order, over six days, because the town had answered her when she asked and the answering deserved an ending delivered in person, not by rumour.

Roy was first, Monday, in the garage bay with the roster spread on the bench, checking it with a ruler the way he always checked it, morning as always. He looked up when she came in and nodded — unsurprised, as if he had been expecting her at that hour every day.

"Delphine." He set the ruler down. "Walk with me."

They walked the bay while she told him: determination filed, undetermined with the invented clause, inquiry continuing without a clock, Terrell named on tape with counsel present. Roy listened the way he listened to everything — practically, eyes forward, hands in pockets — and when she finished he said, "Good. That's good." He offered her coffee from the bay pot and she took it, black, scalded, the way it always was. They drank it standing up without witnesses, the radio off, the furnace going. It tasted the same as every cup she had ever had in that bay. That was the point, she understood: sameness was what he offered, and sameness was what she accepted.

"Roy," she said. "The roster. Are you still — "

"Every morning." He picked the ruler up. "That does not change. Some things you do because they are the thing. You know that. You walk fires twice."

She left him checking. Tuesday would bring the same — the man at his bench, where he belonged.

Verhage was Tuesday, in his kitchen with two cups out on the counter out of habit — one for him, one for whoever came, though no one came most days now. He listened to her news with his shaking hands around his cup, nodding slowly, and refilled her cup without asking when it ran low. The green bin stood at the kerb outside, exactly aligned.

"He comes most nights," Verhage said, meaning Roy. "Sits. Drinks. Sometimes talks. Mostly not." He looked at the second cup. "He will go on coming. That is Tuesday, Miss Rusk, nothing more and nothing less."

"I know." She drank. "Thank you for the basement. For coming down the stairs."

"I am old." He shrugged. "Stairs are stairs. The table was worth them." A pause. "Is the girl — your mother — is she well?"

"She is at home. I go tomorrow."

"Good." He walked her to the door, slow on the lino. "Tell her Verhage asks. She will know what it means."

She left him washing the cups — both cups, as always — and understood that some rituals were simply the shape a life took around a thing it could not put down.

Lindqvist was Wednesday, by telephone, because the daughter drove her to the shops Wednesdays and the afternoon was short. The daughter answered, warm, efficient — "Oh, Miss Rusk, hold on, I'll put you on speaker, she's right here" — and held the receiver to the old woman's good ear. Rusk said the file was closed, the determination filed, the exhibits boxed. There was a pause, a rustle, and then Lindqvist's voice, thin and delighted: "Good, dear. Have a biscuit." The daughter laughed — "She's offering you one down the telephone, I'll eat one on your behalf, there, I've eaten it" — and Rusk laughed too, standing in the depot corridor with the telephone cord stretched, laughing for the first time in weeks. She marked it in the notebook later: laughed, Wednesday, Lindqvist's biscuit by proxy. Some exhibits were not paper.

Mrs Bray was Thursday, in her doorway with the new lock fitted and gleaming — brass, stiff, smelling faintly of oil — and the old key in her kitchen drawer behind her. She listened to the news with her arms folded, upright as a sentry, and when Rusk finished she said, "I have not used it. The new one either. I am glad it is over." She stepped back. Then, surprising them both, she added: "Your mother. Tell her — " She stopped. Started again. "Tell her Bray keeps her own counsel too. She will understand." She went inside and did not lock the door — the new lock, unlocked, by choice. That, Rusk thought walking away, was the whole town in one gesture: security, declined, daily.

Denny was Friday, in the garage with his hardback open on the fender, logging the day's gallons in the late light. He looked up, saw her, and beamed — the missing tooth, the whole face. He turned the book round before she spoke and showed her the margin of today's page, where he had written in his careful capitals: MISS R CAME 3PM FILE CLOSED. He had drawn a box round it.

"Show me where to sign," she said, and signed beside it — her name next to his note, in his book, the only signature of hers in twenty-two years of gallons. He looked at it for a long time, then closed the book gently and held it against his chest the way he had held it in the basement.

"Miss Rusk," he said. "Will you need anything else? Any numbers?"

"Not now, Denny. The numbers are all in. You kept them. That was the job, and you did it."

"Since 2003." Proud. Then, quieter: "Including the ones I should not have logged."

"Especially those." She shook his hand — oil, callus, warmth. "Thank you."

He watched her drive away from the road where he never went, standing at the kerb past the pumps. In the mirror she saw him open the book again before she turned the corner. He was logging the leaving, probably. Everything logged. That was Denny. That was the whole of him, and it was enough.

Sam was Saturday, in the third row with the afternoon light coming grey through the high windows. The hymnals were done — stacked by size, box empty, folded flat. He sat alone in the second row with his hands folded, waiting, as if he knew the order of her week and his place at the end of it. Perhaps he did. Pastors knew endings; it was their trade.

She told him. Determination, exhibits, Terrell, counsel, the file with the attorney. He listened without finishing a single sentence — the first time since she had known him that he did not finish one for her. When she was done he wept, quietly, without performance, his hands over his face, his shoulders moving under his shirt.

"I kept a list for six years," he said when he could speak. "A dead woman's words. A boy's name. I carried it and called it pastoral." He wiped his face with his sleeve, unembarrassed. "I am glad to stop keeping it. I did not know how heavy paper was until I put it on your table."

He stacked the last hymnal — though they were already stacked; he restacked it, needing the task — and sat down, and did not finish her sentence. "Thank you for — " she began, and he waited, and she finished it herself: "for the basement. For the room." He nodded. Some completions you had to do yourself. That was the sermon, and they both knew it.

Adela telephoned that evening — Saturday, after six, the coroner's office quiet behind her. Her voice was thin down the line but steady, the steadiness of findings.

"The decedent — " she began, then corrected herself, the distinction made live, out loud, unprompted: "she. The woman. She has a true finding now. Blunt trauma, pre-mortem. Dated, timestamped, in the state system. I put my name to it straight this time." A pause. Paper shifting. "Whatever else I signed around, that one I signed straight. I wanted you to know before the week ends."

"Thank you, Doctor. For the table. For both sheets."

"I distinguished signed from believed for six years." A breath. "I am still trying to teach it. Perhaps now I can teach it truthfully."

Six conversations. No refusals — to the end, no refusals, the pattern holding past its usefulness into something like grace. Each visit received, each telling heard, each message taken for carrying. The town's manners survived its exposure.

The town did not disperse, did not confess, and did not change. It simply continued, having been told — Roy checking, Verhage washing, Lindqvist sleeping by nine, Bray keeping, Denny logging, Sam stacking, Adela distinguishing. The silence after was the same shape as the silence before, with papers in it now: twelve exhibits, a boxed file, a receipt in her pocket. Named, documented, filed, absorbed.

Rusk walked Mercer on the Sunday — all nine doors, counted out of habit, porch, gable, hip, glass — and stopped outside 127. The kitchen window glowed yellow. The clock eleven minutes fast. Her mother moving in there, a shadow across the blind, making tea, turning the tap off slowly. Tomorrow she would knock — alone, no paper, no witness — and sit at the table with two cups and hear the line that needed no exhibit number.

Tonight she stood in the road and understood that the agreement had not broken. It had been named and was continuing anyway, the way a river continues after being mapped — the town, going on. And the book would not insist on whether the naming mattered more than the continuing. Both were true. The file held one. The road held the other.

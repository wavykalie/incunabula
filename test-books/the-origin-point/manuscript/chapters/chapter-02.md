# 2 — Origin Point

She had poured a hundred of these in training. That is what the course had been
called and that is what it had been, a converted barn outside a state university with
a burn pit at one end and eleven people in it, all of whom had been somewhere near
fire for a decade before they learned to set one deliberately, and none of whom had
ever been asked to certify one.

That was the part people got wrong, afterward, when they asked her about it. They
assumed that certifying a poured fire was a rarer thing than doing one. It was the
other way round. Setting one was the cheap part. Setting one *well enough that a
jurisdiction will act on your word about it* was the expensive part, and almost
nobody ever needed to do it, because almost nobody ever got it wrong in a way anybody
cared about.

Rusk had got it wrong four hundred times as a junior. And then, on a Tuesday in
February, at thirty-seven, with her name on a form she had signed before she had
looked, somebody did it four doors from her mother and the county wanted an answer
by Friday.

---

She started at the back, because the back is where a study is.

The study had a door that closed and a table and a chair and a heater on a stand and
two feet of clear floor between the heater and the table, and the pour had gone into
that two feet and stopped, and the stopping was the entire finding.

She had Nadia photograph it from four positions before she touched anything, and then
she had Nadia photograph it again with the scale card, and then she had done the thing
she had done four hundred times, which was to stand at the edge of the pattern with
her back to the wall and look at it until the room went out of focus.

"You can't see it," Nadia said.

"I can see it."

"You can't."

"I can see the edge." Rusk did not turn round. "Look at the left-hand end. The
liquid went that way and stopped, and it did not thin out, and it did not wick. It
stopped because there was too much of it. A fire that starts, spreads until it hits
something it likes, and then thins at the edges as it runs out of what it was standing
in. This has got a wall on it. This has got a *line* on it."

Nadia was quiet for a while.

"That's the fourth one I've had this month," she said.

"How many have you had."

"Four. Two electrical." Nadia lowered the camera. "The other two I photographed the
same way you just did and my report said *indeterminate* and both of them closed in
a week."

Rusk looked at her then, properly, for the first time.

"Say that again."

"I said my report said indeterminate. Because I couldn't say it was set without
saying how I knew, and if I said how I knew, I'd have to say that the man who signed
the certification above mine was the one who was supposed to know and didn't say
anything." She put the camera strap over her shoulder. "So I said indeterminate. Which
is a way of saying *I know* that doesn't get anybody fired."

The house made a small sound, the way old houses do, and neither of them looked up.

"You've been doing that a long time — writing it so it doesn't get anyone fired."

"Eight months," said Nadia Pham. "Since I moved here. Before that, the city, where indeterminate
gets you a meeting with a supervisor. Here it gets you left alone, which I preferred until
Tuesday." She checked the camera settings, unnecessarily — stalling, Rusk realised, the way
witnesses stall when the next sentence costs more. "The two electricals I called indeterminate.
One of them had a line on it. Not like this. Smaller. I told myself flashover. I tell myself
flashover every time now. That is what eight months here teaches: the word that closes the file."

---

The report is written in a particular order and Rusk had never been able to decide
whether the order was designed to prevent fraud or to make fraud easy.

Cause first, then origin, then origin class, then ignition source, then
contribution, then exclusions, then a paragraph about what could not be determined,
which is the only part of the document that is ever honestly interesting.

She wrote cause, because cause was straightforward and would be uncontroversial.
Smoke inhalation, and thermal injury, and the order of those two is a thing that
argues itself.

She wrote origin: study, north side, floor level, approximately 0.6 square metres.

She wrote origin class: *multiple fire origins.*

She stopped there.

That was the line. Everything before it was in dispute with nobody. Everything after
it was in dispute with the whole street.

A single origin is a fire. Multiple origins means somebody moved through a structure
with an open flame and made several decisions about where to put it down, and there
are two legitimate reasons for it and only one of them is a person.

The legitimate one is flashover. When a room goes fully involved, the gases above the
ceiling ignite all at once, and the fire drops down through the ceiling, and you get
what looks like a dozen separate origins in a pattern that will fool anybody who has
not seen it forty times. Every investigator learns to check for it and every
investigator gets it wrong at least once.

So: was it flashover?

She walked the house again and looked for the roof.

There was no fire damage to the ceiling of the study. Not damage. The ceiling was
clean, cream-coloured, and the smoke staining above the pattern came up the wall in a
soft bloom and stopped. It had never been fully involved. It had never been anywhere
near it.

Whatever had happened in that study had been over in a few minutes.

She went and got the fuel logs.

---

The county keeps them. That is the thing about a county: it is a small bureaucracy
that never threw anything away and is extremely poor at reading its own filing
system, and so the paper is all still there, in a storage unit off the interstate, in
grey boxes stacked to the ceiling in rows, and anybody who knows the year can find the boxes.

Rusk had known the year since Tuesday morning. She spent four hours in the storage
unit on Wednesday with a laptop, a box of 2019 incident reports, and a legal pad,
and she was the only person there — the fluorescent tubes humming, the concrete sweating
cold through her shoes, Fenwick's signed authorisation slip on the clipboard. She worked
through the morning methodically: incident reports first, then maintenance binders, then
the appliance schedules nobody ever cross-referenced. By noon her fingers were grey with
dust and her back ached from the concrete floor. She ate a biscuit from her pocket — Lindqvist's,
from a visit not yet made, pocketed out of habit — and kept reading.

The 2019 report was four pages. She had seen four pages. It was signed by a
supervisor and countersigned by a junior investigator whose name was her own and whose
handwriting on page three she could still pick out of a hundred forms because she had
pressed so hard with the pen that it had embossed the sheet underneath.

Page three had a line in it that said the origin was consistent with a fault in the
kitchen circuit.

She read it four times.

The house on Mercer had no kitchen circuit. She had looked. Every house on that
street was 1968 on a slab, and a 1968 slab house on that street had its service panel
in the laundry and its branch circuits run through the attic, and the only appliance
circuits in the whole structure were on the north side of the kitchen, and they were
numbered four and five on the schedule in the same file.

There was no kitchen circuit. There was never a kitchen circuit. It had been replaced
in 2016, along with the panel and about four feet of the trunk, and the replacement is
in the county's own maintenance record because the county paid for it, and the
maintenance record is in the same box, filed under the same year, in a different
binder.

Rusk sat on the floor of a storage unit off the interstate with a legal pad on her
knees and a grey box open in front of her and she wrote on the pad, in handwriting
that had gone less legible rather than more:

*page 3, kitchen circuit. no such circuit. replaced 2016.*

*someone wrote it.*

*it was 2019. who knew there was no kitchen circuit?*

---

The answer to that was everybody who had ever opened a panel in that house, and
there were not many people who had ever opened a panel in that house, and she could
have found that list in a week.

She was not being slow. She was being the other thing.

There is a moment in an arson investigation, and every investigator knows it and
almost none of them will admit to it, where the case stops being about a fire and
starts being about a person. The evidence does not change. What changes is that you
begin to be interested in one household more than in the pattern, and once that
happens you are not an investigator any more, you are a person with a theory, and a
person with a theory is the most dangerous thing in the building.

She had a theory at four hours past two on the Wednesday. It was a bad theory. It
was the worst theory anybody has ever had about their own family, and she sat on the
floor of a storage unit and she was furious with herself for having it, which she
recognised as a sign of how good it was, because a bad theory you are not frightened
of is a bad theory you have not had yet.

She wrote the theory on the pad. Then she tore the page off and put it in her pocket,
because a document you carry is a document you will act on, and a document you leave
in a box is a document you can still think about tomorrow.

Then she went and looked up fuel.

---

Nobody in the county bought accelerant by the drum. That is not quite true. They
bought it by the drum in a hardware store in the next county over, about forty
minutes away, at a price that was not low enough to be interesting and not so high
that it required explanation.

What nobody did, in Rusk's experience of eleven years, was buy several hundred
gallons.

The propane was easier. Everybody has propane. A cylinder heater on a stand wants a
bottle, and a bottle comes when a bottle is empty, and the man who brings the bottle
asks no questions at all because his job is a delivery and a delivery is not an
interrogation. A hundred households on that street had a heater on a stand and a
bottle behind it and no one could tell you anything about any of them.

Except that a pour of the size Rusk had photographed does not come out of a
cylinder. A cylinder holds forty pounds. A forty pound cylinder will make a room
fully involved and it will make a very small circle on the floor and it will not leave
a line. A line that wide is five gallons. A line that wide across a room, twice, on
one street, is somebody standing in that room with a hose and a plan.

The accelerant for the pattern on the study floor had come out of something that held
at least forty gallons, and the likeliest thing in that county that held forty gallons
and could be got into a house without a delivery being recorded was a vehicle.

Rusk wrote: *bulk. cash. no delivery address.*

And then, because she was tired and it was nearly three in the afternoon and she had
been in a storage unit for four hours: *two people had access to bulk fuel on this
street.*

She wrote that down and looked at it.

It was wrong. That was the problem with writing things down. It narrowed the field to
two and one of the two was a man who lived at the end of Mercer with a truck and a
garage and a key to about nine houses on that street, because the two brothers had
died and the keys had never been recalled, and everybody knew this, and nobody had
ever thought about it as anything but a convenience.

And the other of the two was not on Mercer at all. The other one was on the county
road, four houses back, and had a business, and had a yard, and came into the
kitchen most mornings to make tea for a woman who kept a clock eleven minutes fast.
She sat with that for a while — the two names unwritten, the pad blank below the line.
One of them brought her bread on Mondays in front of witnesses. The other counted cash
twice and loaded without spilling. Both of them had been helpful to her that week.
Everyone had been helpful. That was the finding she had not yet found the words for:
helpfulness as a wall, kindness as mortar, every true answer a brick in it.

Rusk looked at the legal pad for a long time.

Then she wrote, under the two names, which she had not yet looked up and did not need
to look up because there were only so many people in a town this size:

*Two of them live within four houses of my mother.*

---

At five she called the office and asked for the 2019 delivery manifests for Mercer
Street, and the woman on the other end said there weren't any, and Rusk said that was
what she expected, and the woman said, was it, and Rusk said yes, and the woman said
"because there's never any," and Rusk said, "I know."

She hung up and sat in the car park at the storage unit for ten minutes with the
engine off.

What she had, at five o'clock on the Wednesday, was this: a fire that had been poured;
a pattern that had a line on it; an accelerant that had come in bulk and come in
without a signature; a pattern that had been put into a house on Mercer; a house on
Mercer, four doors from her mother's, with a study at the back; a second house on
Mercer, four doors from her mother's, that had burned in 2019 and had a study at the
back; a report four pages long that referred to a circuit that had not existed in that
house since 2016; and a signature at the bottom of page three that was her own, in
embossed ink, written by a person who had been thirty-one and four months into the
post and had not been asked.

It was the best day of work she had done in six years and it had given her nothing she
could put in a document.

Six days.

She had four left.

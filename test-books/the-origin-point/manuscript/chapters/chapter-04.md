# 4 — Six Years

She had read the determination four hundred times.

That is not an exaggeration and it is not a symptom. A determination is four lines
long and Rusk could have reproduced all four from memory at nineteen, which is a thing
she had occasionally demonstrated at parties to the visible distress of people who had
not asked.

*Crew finds fire damage consistent with origin at kitchen counter outlet. Origin
consistent with fault in branch circuit serving kitchen receptacle. Structure 1968,
slab on grade. Recommend further inquiry be referred to the county for electrical
inspection.*

Four lines. She had read them in a storage unit off the interstate and in a car
outside her mother's house and in a bed at two in the morning in an apartment in a
city she had moved to for this job and had stayed in, on and off, for six years, and
in all four hundred of those readings she had been looking for one specific thing and
had never once found it, and the thing she was looking for was her own name.

It was not on the determination. Determinations are not signed by the investigator who
walks the scene. They are signed by the investigator who walks the scene, drafted,
initialed, and then certified by whoever is holding the pen that day, and everybody
in the county knew this and nobody said it, the way you do not say a thing that is
true and true in the same office.

It was on page three.

Rusk had the page three in front of her now, in the storage unit, on a Thursday
morning in February, and page three was an incident field sheet and it was hers, and
her initials were in the box marked *INVESTIGATOR* in handwriting that had pressed hard
enough to emboss the sheet beneath it, and next to her initials was a time, 14:20, and
next to the time was a note she had written at twenty-nine degrees of disbelieving and
had never once re-read before now.

*Residual char at rear study window glass, consistent with a second, earlier ignition
not observed at time of arrival. Re-inspection required.*

*Rev. D. Rusk, 4.20pm*

---

She had re-inspected. That was the part that no living person remembered, which was
exactly the point — and the re-inspection itself deserved its own accounting, because
it was the day she learned the difference between seeing and filing. On the Monday she
went back with a senior man called Harrow, twenty years in, who walked the study with
his hands behind his back and pronounced the glass thermal bloom from the corridor fire.
She stood beside him at twenty-nine, notebook open, and agreed aloud while her own note
from the day before sat in her pocket disagreeing. Harrow signed. She countersigned.
They drove back together without speaking. She never told anyone that her hand shook on
the wheel the whole way — not from doubt but from relief, the relief of being told the
thing she saw was not there. Relief was the find of that day. She filed it with the form.

On the Monday, the day after, at nine in the morning, a different investigator had
gone back to the house on Mercer and had looked at the study and had not seen a second
ignition, because there had not been one, and Rusk had stood in that study at
twenty-nine and had agreed with him, in a document, with a signature on it, and had
then gone and closed the file.

She had been twenty-nine for the re-inspection and thirty-one for the determination.
There was a ten-day gap between signing off her own note and signing off the thing
that erased it, and in those ten days she had learned to write in a different hand,
which is not a metaphor, and which she could still do.

The residual char had been on the window glass. What it had actually been, she knew
now, having spent eleven years learning what char looks like, was thermal bloom on
glass from a fire on the other side of it, which happens when you have a window
between two rooms and one of them is fully involved.

Nothing in that was evidence of a second fire.

That was the honest technical answer and it was the answer she had accepted at
twenty-nine, and she had accepted it because it was the answer that let the file
close, and the file closing was a thing she wanted, and she had wanted it because
nobody in that house was going to fix a window.

---

Nobody died in 2019.

That was the county's determination. That was the finding, and it was on page one, and
she had initialed page one along with the rest, and the finding was:

*One decedent, female, fifty-nine, resident. Cause: smoke inhalation. Manner: natural.*

Natural. Not a manner you write about a kitchen fire. You write *undetermined* for a
kitchen fire, and you write *pending further inquiry* for a kitchen fire, and you very
rarely write *natural*, and you write *natural* when there is a body and an answer and
nothing about the two of them fits together and you have decided, quietly, on a
Tuesday, in a study, with a pen you are pressing too hard with, that fitting them
together is not your job.

Her mother's name was Cora Vance. Cora was fifty-three in 2019, not fifty-nine, and
Cora was asleep eleven doors away with a clock eleven minutes fast, and Cora was not
the body.

Rusk had gone into that house at two in the afternoon on the day of the fire because
she was the investigator on shift, and she had seen the body, and she had not known
it and had not looked at it, because it had been a woman of fifty-nine called
Marguerite Pham who lived alone four doors down and had no relation to anybody on that
street, and because Rusk had been thirty-one and four months into the post and had
been told to draft.

She had drafted.

Marguerite Pham was fifty-nine. Marguerite Pham had a daughter who was a schoolteacher
and who had been standing on a pavement in the rain six years later this week saying
*is it bad, people keep asking me if it's bad* and who had apologised to a stranger for
intruding on her own mother's front step.

Rusk sat in the storage unit with page three on her knee and understood, with a
clarity she had not had in six years, that the county had not got the number wrong by
a little. It had got it right. One person. A woman of fifty-nine who lived alone four
doors from the house Rusk had grown up in, who had a kitchen and a clock and a
daughter, and who had died in a study because somebody had poured something on a
floor and lit it.

And the determination said *natural*.

---

She read the other document. The one that had not been on the file, which was why she
had to go and get it.

The coroner's report for 2019 was not in the box. Coroner's reports are not public
records in the way fire determinations are, and they are held in a system that does
not talk to the county's system, and the two systems had last spoken to each other in
a telephone call in the spring of 2019 that a woman in the coroner's office had
logged and then, apparently, not acted on.

Rusk had a name and a professional courtesy and a card, and she used all three.

Adela Iversen was the county coroner and she was, in the only way Rusk ever
thought the word, decent. Decent is a low bar in that office and it is a high one in
that town.

"That report is a closed file," Adela said.

"It's a 2019 file with a natural manner on a fire that a county investigator has just
told me was set. I'd like to read it."

"Not to me you wouldn't. You'd need a request and a form and it would go through the
state's office and take eleven weeks, and you'd be very polite about it, and I would
be very polite about it, and we would both be spending our politeness instead of our
time." Adela put a pen down, which was a courtesy. "What do you actually want?"

"I want to know if the cause of death in that file matches the cause of death on the
determination."

"There is no cause of death on a determination."

"There's a finding. It says smoke inhalation."

"It says smoke inhalation."

"And?"

Adela Iversen looked at her for about four seconds.

"And in nine years," she said, "I have never certified smoke inhalation as a cause of
death in a house fire. Not once. Not in a garage, not in a bedroom, not in a house
with a circuit that does not exist. Because it is nearly always a lie, and it is
nearly always a *kindness*, and I have been the recipient of that kindness twice
before, and both times a person was a relative of somebody who could have stopped it
and did not, and I certified around it, because the alternative was dismantling a town
that had already decided."

"Who decided?"

"Everybody decides. That's the part nobody writes down. It doesn't take a meeting. It
takes a look across a street." Adela opened a drawer, then closed it, then opened it
again. "The determination says a fault in a circuit that was replaced in 2016. I know
that circuit was replaced because I signed for the electrical inspection in 2016, and
it says the same thing. So either the person who wrote the determination in 2019 was
wrong, or they knew, and if they knew then they have signed something false about a
dead woman, and if that is what happened then I am the person who should have caught
it and didn't, and I have been carrying that around for six years, and you have just
walked in here on a Thursday and put it on the table between us."

Rusk said nothing.

"So I will tell you what I have," Adela said. "I will tell you what I have and not
what I have proven, and you will write it down as what I have, and if you write it down
as anything else I will know, and I will not have given it to you."

"Go ahead."

"The decedent is fifty-nine. The decedent is female. The decedent lived alone. And
the decedent did not die of smoke inhalation." She stopped. "I am not going to say
another word on a recording device or on a telephone, and you are not going to ask me
on either, and what I have just said to you is the limit of what I have."

"What you have said to me," said Rusk, "is that a man in a state office certified a
natural death in a house fire on the strength of a determination citing a circuit that
did not exist."

"What I have said to you," said Adela Iversen, "is that the decedent did not die
of smoke inhalation. Everything else in that sentence is yours."

---

Rusk sat in the county office car park for twenty minutes after Adela's door closed behind
her, writing down everything she could remember before memory edited it — the eleven
certificates, the painted-shut window, the till below, the exact words about kindness and
relatives who could have stopped it. Her hands shook so badly the shorthand went ragged.
She dated the page, timed it, and drove back from the county office at half past two
with the heating on full and drove
straight past Mercer Street because there was no other way out of the town.

Nine houses. She counted them. All of them the same, all of them with the study at the
back, and at 127, four doors from her mother's, there was a house where a woman of
fifty-nine had died and been written down as having died naturally, and at 131 there
was a house where a woman kept a clock eleven minutes fast and had driven a stranger
to a hospital, and at 145 there was a man who had said he had been working, and at
118 there were four children and a cat, and every one of these people had given her
four true answers on Tuesday and not one of them had said no.

She pulled over at the depot and put the hazards on and sat there.

Here was what she had, and she made the list the way she made every list, in order,
in shorthand, on a legal pad on the wheel:

*1. Fire at 131 Mercer set. Pour pattern, study, 0.6m².*

*2. 2019 fire at 127 Mercer. Determination: circuit fault. Circuit did not exist.*

*3. Determination drafted and initialed by me. Certified by someone else.*

*4. My field note from the day of the 2019 fire: possible second ignition, window
glass, rear study. Closed as thermal bloom. By me.*

*5. Coroner says the 2019 decedent did not die of smoke inhalation. Says nothing
more. Will not go on record.*

*6. Both houses have a study. Every house on that street has a study.*

*7. Accelerant in bulk. Two people within four houses of my mother have access.*

*8. Friday.*

She looked at number four for a long time.

That was the one she had done. Not signed-off, not certified, not the thing with her
name at the bottom of page one. Drafted. Initialed. And then, ten days later, in a
different hand, she'd written four lines that explained away a note she had written
herself.

She had been twenty-nine. She had been in the post four months. She had been the only
person who had ever looked at that window and written down that something was wrong
with it.

And then she had taken it back, in her own handwriting, in a hand she could still
produce, on a form, in a file, in a box off the interstate, where it had sat for six
years under a determination that said *natural* about a woman she had never looked at.

Rusk started the car.

The note was her own. The signature at the bottom of page three was her own. And
somewhere in the four hundred times she had read those four lines, in a flat in a city
she had moved to for this job, she had been reading her own name and had not seen it.

She had been reading it as a young woman's careless note.

She was thirty-seven now, and it was not a young woman's note any more, and there was
a woman of fifty-nine who had lived four doors from her mother and died in a study and
been certified as having died naturally by people who were kind about it, in a street
where a fourteen-year-old girl had taken the batteries out of the smoke detectors in
the hall.

Four days to Friday.

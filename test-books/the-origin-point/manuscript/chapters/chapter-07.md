# 7 — The Same Pattern

There was a burn laboratory forty minutes up the interstate in a building that had
been a mattress factory, and Rusk had a day pass and a reason, and the reason was
that she needed to know what a couch does.

That was all. She had a photograph of a study floor and she needed to know what a
couch does to a room, because a study has a chair in it and a chair does not burn the
way a couch burns, and the difference between the two had been sitting in the back of
her mind since Friday like a splinter.

She had not slept — four hours, broken, the 2019 file open on the kitchen table when she
gave up at five. She had driven to the lab at six in the morning with a folder and
a legal pad and a thermos gone cold, and had told the man at the desk that she was doing a comparison and had
been given a room with a light table in it and told to be careful of the archive,
which is what you are told, and which means *do not take the folder home*.

The lab had three couches in it. They were cheap, and they were the same couch, and
they were there because couch fires are the most common accidental fire in a dwelling
by a factor of four, and because a researcher named Okonkwo had spent nine years
establishing that a modern polyurethane couch will achieve fully involved in a
four-by-four room in under four minutes, and that the resulting burn pattern is highly
characteristic, and that no investigator should ever look at a pattern from a couch
fire and think it was anything else.

Rusk read Okonkwo's paper twice. Then she went and got the 2025 study photograph,
which was Nadia Pham's, and she put it on the light table, and she put a couch pattern
from the lab archive next to it.

The couch pattern was a V.

It came down out of the couch seat in a cone and opened out and it was beautiful and
it was completely, unmistakably, a couch. It had a soft edge. It had a wide, feathered
terminus. It told you the room, the direction, and the fuel, and it told you all three
things without you having to be clever about it.

The study pattern had a line.

She put her finger on the line. It was where the liquid had stopped, a hard boundary,
slightly curved, roughly half a metre in length, and on the near side of it the floor
was consumed and on the far side of it the floor was scorched and untouched by liquid,
and the two of those are different things and only one of them is what a fire does
when nobody is deciding anything.

She had already established that. She had established it on Tuesday with a
thermometer and a torch and Nadia Pham taking photographs.

She got the 2019 file out of the folder.

There was not much in it. The 2019 debris was long gone; eleven months had passed by
the time the file was made and six years by now, and everything in the 2019 file was
photograph, and photographs of a fire are a much poorer witness than a fire.

There were nine photographs of the study at 127 Mercer and they were all taken by a
different hand from the ones she had taken in 2025, and the quality was worse, and
three of them were of the north window.

She put the fourth one on the light table next to the 2025 photograph and pushed them
together until the two walls met at the corner and the two sills ran into each other,
which they did, which was the fourth number, and she had known it would be the fourth
number.

Then she looked at the floor.

In 2025 the pour had been in the study of 131 Mercer. In 2019 the fire at 127 Mercer
had begun, according to the determination she had read four hundred times, at the
kitchen counter outlet, and she had believed the determination for six years, and the
determination was wrong, and so the question of where the 2019 fire actually began had
not been asked by anybody, including her.

She had four photographs of the 2019 study floor and two of them were blurred, and one
of them had a chair in it.

On the third photograph, at the bottom right, low down, cut off by the edge of the
frame, there was a patch of floor about a metre across, and it was not cone.

Rusk put the photograph under the magnifying glass on the light table and bent over it
for a long time, and then she took a pencil out of the desk drawer, and very carefully,
because she was aware of what she was doing and she wanted it in her own hand, she
drew a line on the acetate overlay along the edge of the patch.

The line went the same way.

Not the same place. There was no reason it should be the same place; the houses were
four doors apart and the furniture was not identical and the person who had done it,
if it was the same person, had had six years and a different room to think in. But the
line went the same way. It curved the same way. It had the same slight convexity at the
left-hand end, where the liquid had begun to run out of the floor and start to gather,
and it terminated at the left-hand end the way a pour terminates, sharply, at the
point where the volume goes to nothing.

The 2019 pattern was a pour pattern.

It was the same pour pattern.

Rusk put the pencil down. She sat back in the chair in a mattress factory forty
minutes up the interstate with a photograph of a fire in 2019 and a photograph of a
fire in 2025 and a line of pencil on acetate, and she looked at them for perhaps two
minutes, and then she lifted the overlay off and put it in the folder and closed the
folder.

She said nothing to anybody.

That is the thing that people do not believe about investigations, and it is the
thing that the television version of this work exists to be wrong about. Nobody stood
up in a room and announced it. There was no moment where a door was thrown open. She
put a piece of acetate in a folder, and she drove home, and she said nothing to
anybody for nine days, because the moment she said it out loud to a person it became
a thing a person knew, and a person who knows is a person who can be asked, and a
person who is asked begins to remember things they did not have to remember.

So she said nothing, and she did the work.

---

What she had, in a folder, in the passenger seat of her car, on a Saturday:

That a fire in 2019 and a fire in 2025, in two houses built from the same pattern book
on the same street, had been lit in the same room, on the same side of that room, by
the same method, and that the method was a decision.

Nobody had ever connected them. That was the extraordinary part and she turned it over
several times on the drive home. Not one person on that street, and not one person in
that office, in six years, had put those two documents side by side, because putting
them side by side requires somebody to think that the 2019 fire was a fire, and the
2019 fire was not a fire, it was a *determination*, and you do not interrogate a
determination, it is the end of the interrogating.

Except that a determination is a document about a floor, and she was a person who read
documents about floors for a living, and she had done it four hundred times.

Rusk stopped at the depot and put the folder on the passenger seat under the blanket she
used for hiding things worth hiding, and did not open it
again for nine days. She walked past Fenwick's counter without stopping, signed out for the
weekend with a nod, and drove home with the folder riding beside her like a passenger she
was not speaking to. At home she put it on top of the wardrobe, out of sight, and left it
there — the discipline of not-looking, which was its own kind of work, and harder than the
looking had been.

What she did instead, in those nine days, was the least interesting part of the work
and the part that decides cases: she went and got the 2019 appliance schedule, and the
2016 electrical inspection, and the 2019 site photographs in order, and the 2018
fence-permit file for the rear of 127 Mercer, which had nothing in it but which told
her the fence went up in the spring and the gate was a new gate.

She built a timeline. That is all. Eleven days of paper into a sequence, and the
sequence was so ordinary that she read it four times looking for the seam and the seam
was not there. The seam was the problem. There was no seam. A man had ordered three
hundred and forty gallons of diesel in cash in March of 2019, and a volunteer fire
company had written a shift rota on the back of the docket, and a woman had died of
something that was not smoke inhalation in a study in April, and a junior investigator
had written a note about the window and then closed her own note, and a determination
had gone out saying *natural*.

And then, eleven months after all of that, a man had ordered two hundred and sixty
gallons of diesel in cash in April of 2025, from a garage on the county road, on a
pad with a volunteer's shift rota on the back of it.

Six years. Same street. Same room. Same method. And in between, a year of nothing at
all, and then two hundred and sixty gallons.

Rusk wrote on the pad, in the car park, in the shorthand:

*Not a repeat. A continuation.*

And she sat with that for a while, because it was the wrong word for what she had and
she wanted the right one, and the right one arrived at about the time the light went.

*Twice is a coincidence. Twice, in the same room, six years, with eleven months of
paper in between, is somebody who did not stop.*

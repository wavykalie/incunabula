# 6 — The Room Nobody Sleeps In

The call came at 08:14 on the Friday, four days after Rusk had said *six days* to a
lawyer on a pavement, and it was the deputy and it was not the deputy.

"Investigator Rusk. This is Sergeant Whitlock. I'd like you to come out to 131
Mercer. There's been a — the family's here, and there's something about the study."

"It's Friday," Rusk said.

"It's Friday," said Whitlock.

"You said I'd have until Friday."

"I said the county wanted it by Friday. I never said you did." There was a pause in
the line of the particular quality that a person allows themselves when they have
already been told off and are about to be told off again. "Ms Rusk, I'm going to be
straight with you, because somebody was straight with you and it made you look
careless. There's no deadline on this. There's never been a deadline. I gave the family
a deadline because a family will do anything you tell them there is a deadline for,
and I'd rather they did something. I did not put it in your report."

"All right."

"Are you coming out?"

"I'm coming out," said Rusk, and put the phone down, and sat in the car in the depot
yard for a moment with both hands on the wheel, and then went out to Mercer Street.

---

The study door was still open. It had been open since Tuesday because the room was
the room, and the room was not a room anybody was going to be closing up for a week.

There was a woman in it. She was fifty-nine or thereabouts and she was standing in the
northwest corner with her hands at her sides, and she was not touching anything, and
she had the particular terrible stillness of a person who has already touched
everything once and has now made a rule for herself.

"Mrs Bray," the deputy said. "This is the investigator."

"I know who she is," Mrs Bray said. "I read it in the paper. *Investigator.*" She said
the word as though it were a job title for a woman who repairs boilers. "Are you the
one who's been round the houses?"

"Yes."

"Then you'll know I'm not a liar. That's what I want to say first, before anything
else, and if you write it down, write it down in those words. I'm not a liar. I have
never lied to a police officer in my life and I have been in this house since 1971."

Rusk put her notebook away, which was a thing she had been taught to do in a seminar
and had never once done in a house.

"All right," she said.

"I was not in that room."

"No."

"I have told four people I was not in that room and I am telling you and I would like
you to tell me if you write it down somewhere that I said it, because if somebody else
reads it and thinks I'm hiding something—"

"I'll say it in your words."

"Say it in my words." Mrs Bray stopped. She looked at the north wall. "That's the
window. That's the one she was under. That's the one the woman was under, I don't
know her name, I never knew her name, I knew her face."

"Who do you mean?"

"The other one. Your mother. Or your mother-in-law. Whichever." Mrs Bray said it
without any weight at all. "I've known Cora Vance since 1971 and I'll tell you the
truth about it, which is that in fifty years I have never once had a conversation
with the woman, not one, not about anything, and I could not tell you in the morning
whether she takes sugar."

Rusk looked at the window.

It was a fixed window, north side, a sill on it, a sash that had been painted shut in
about 1994 and had never been opened since. It was a very ordinary window in a very
ordinary house, and it was the window that Rusk had written a note about, in her own
hand, in the field, on a Tuesday in March, six years and eleven days ago.

*Residual char at rear study window glass, consistent with a second, earlier ignition
not observed at time of arrival.*

She walked to it. She did not touch the glass; she put her hand out and held it four
inches off, the way you do, and she looked down at the sill.

The sill was painted. Two colours, because the house had been painted twice and the
window had been painted both times by whoever painted the wall, and where the second
coat had gone over the first there was a ridge about a millimetre high, and in the
ridge there was a line of grey, and it was continuous along the length of the sill.

She crouched.

It was a bloom. It was exactly what she had said in a field on the fourteenth of March
six years ago, and then stopped believing, and then believed again, and it ran the
whole length of the sill under two coats of paint, which meant it had happened before
the paint, which meant it had happened before the second coat, which meant whatever
had happened here had happened in a room that people were still painting in a few
years afterwards.

"Ma'am," said the deputy, "what are you looking at?"

"I'm looking at the sill."

"There's nothing on the sill."

"There's a line on the sill," Rusk said. "It's under the paint. It's a bloom, it's
from heat, and heat doesn't come from the outside in a house that's got no other
damage on that side."

She stood up. Her knees were not happy about it and she let them not be happy.

"Mrs Bray," she said. "When did you last paint this room?"

Mrs Bray considered it.

"Eleven years ago," she said. "The spring. My husband did the walls, he was good at it,
he wasn't good at the sash, he painted the sash shut, and I told him and he said it'd
be fine."

"He painted the sash shut."

"He said the window never opened."

"It doesn't," said the deputy.

"It doesn't," Mrs Bray agreed. "It was never the point of it."

---

The walk of the house took four hours — room by room, front to back, up and down, the way
it is supposed to be walked — and gave Rusk nine hours in total across two visits. Whitlock
stayed for the first two, taking photographs while she measured, then left for another call.
Mrs Bray followed them at a distance through the first three rooms, her arms folded, correcting
small errors of fact as they went: that door sticks, that window never opened, that shelf was
her husband's doing. In the kitchen she stopped at the threshold and would not cross it.
"You go," she said. "I have been in that kitchen enough." Rusk noted it the way she noted
everything that week — stated as fact, filed as question. What she had at the end of the
Friday was this:

The study at 131 Mercer and the study at 127 Mercer were the same room.

Not similar. The same. The same depth off the kitchen, the same door, which opened
inward and had a lock that had been changed to a modern one, the same north window
with the same sill height, the same fixed sash, the same shelf on the east wall at
shoulder height, which in a 1968 pattern book is a thing that is either there or is
not there and if it is there it is there in every house built to that pattern.

Rusk had a photograph of 127 Mercer's study. She had it because she had taken it, in
March of 2019, at twenty-nine, four months into the post, as part of a survey of a
residential fire that nobody had asked her to survey in that much detail.

She stood in the two photographs with a tape measure and she read off the depth of the
room, and the width, and the height of the shelf, and the height of the sill above the
floor.

Sixteen feet by eleven feet four. Shelf at four foot one. Sill at two foot nine.

Four numbers. In two houses, four doors apart, built by two brothers from the same
pattern book in the same year in the same street, they are the same four numbers, and
the reason they are the same four numbers is that they are the same room, and
somebody had put a fire in it twice, twelve feet of a street apart, six years and nine
months apart, and the second time had killed three people.

She wrote it down in the car in the depot yard, in shorthand, on the legal pad, and
then she read it back and she wrote one more line underneath, and this is the line
that she would give to a grand jury in six weeks and that she wrote, on a Friday, in
a car park, with her hands not quite steady:

*Both fires: same room, same pour, same side of the room, same deliberate boundary.*

*Two people lit fires in the same room in the same house type on the same street.*

*Not a coincidence. Not a signature of method. A habit.*

---

At four o'clock on the Friday she drove to 127 Mercer and sat in the car across the
road from her mother's house for twenty minutes and did not go in. The bins were out —
black, green, brown, in order — and while she watched, Cora came out of the side door
with a fourth bag, garden waste, and added it to the row with a precision that looked
like ceremony. She straightened, pressed a hand to her back, and looked directly at
Rusk's car for a long moment. Rusk did not duck or wave. They looked at each other
across the road through two windscreens of glass, mother and daughter, investigator and
unquestioned, until Cora picked up the empty carrier and went back inside.

It was the second Friday in a month on which she had told somebody a date she had made
up, and the date had now arrived, and there was nothing at all to mark it, which she
had expected, and which was somehow worse than if there had been something.

Across the road, a woman of seventy-four had let herself out of a side door with a
bag in each hand to bring in her bins, because the truck was on them.

Nobody had a telephone number for her. Rusk had not asked for one. She had a
notebook with seven names in it and four doors and a street where a woman kept a
clock eleven minutes fast so she would never be early for anything.

Four days ago she had invented a Friday in order to be the kind of person who gets
six days, and the Friday had come, and the file had not closed, and the reason the
file had not closed was that there was nothing in it that anybody could put in a
document, and the reason there was nothing in it that anybody could put in a document
was that six people on a street had told her the truth, four questions at a time, and
had not one of them lied, and had not one of them refused, and had between them
produced a wall.

She took out the legal pad and read her own list from Wednesday.

*1. Fire at 131 Mercer set.*

*2. 2019 fire at 127 Mercer. Determination: circuit fault. Circuit did not exist.*

*3. Determination drafted and initialed by me. Certified by someone else.*

*4. My field note: possible second ignition, window glass, rear study. Closed as
thermal bloom. By me.*

*5. Coroner says the 2019 decedent did not die of smoke inhalation.*

*6. Both houses have a study. Every house on that street has a study.*

*7. Accelerant in bulk. Two people within four houses of my mother have access.*

*8. Friday.*

She drew a line under the eighth item, because it was the only one on the list that
had stopped being true, and she had not drawn a line under any of the other seven.

Then she wrote a ninth.

*9. 131's study and 127's study: same depth, same shelf height, same sill height.
Four numbers. Both poured, same side, both deliberate.*

*It is a habit. Somebody has a room in their head and they have had it for years and
they did not get it from a fire investigator, because nobody has ever told me that
this street has identical studies, and if nobody has ever told me, then the person who
poured it in 2019 already knew.*

*Which means they had already done it once before 2019, or they were told.*

Rusk closed the pad.

And then, because the afternoon had that quality February gets into around four when
the light starts to go without anything to mark it, and because she had two hours to
kill and a street to be on, she got out of the car and went and knocked on the door
at 127.

No one answered. She knocked again — three knocks, the childhood pattern — and waited,
and heard nothing inside, no step, no tap running. Cora was out, or not answering. Rusk
stood on the step with her hands in her pockets and looked at the door she had knocked on
all her life: the paint worn at the handle, the small square of glass at eye height, her own
face dim in it. She took out the notebook and wrote, standing there: 127, 16:40, no answer.
Reason unknown. Then she walked back to the car past the bins in their order and sat with the
engine off, watching the house the way she had watched it a hundred times, until the light
went fully and the windows stayed dark.

She drove home by way of the depot, signed the Friday log with its nothing to report, and
ate standing at the counter — bread, cheese, an apple going soft — and spread the two study
photographs on her kitchen table under the good lamp. Sixteen by eleven-four. Shelf four-one.
Sill two-nine. Four numbers, twice. She copied them onto a fresh card and pinned it to the
board beside the fuel numbers, and stood back until the board stopped being paper and became
a street: nine houses, nine studies, two fires, one habit. Then she slept badly, and dreamed
of sills with grey lines under paint, and woke at four with the answer she had already written
on the pad: somebody had a room in their head, and the room was real, and it was on Mercer.

Nobody looked up when we landed.

I have made better entrances. I have made worse ones too, and I am not going to pretend the worse ones did not happen, because the whole point of an entrance is that there was an audience, and the audience is the part I remember.

We came down in the sleigh the way we always come down. I sat forward with my hand on the sill and took us in. The constellation held its shape above us the whole way, and if you have read the other account then you already know how much of that is mine, and I am not going to go through it twice.

Scorpius. I named it at the time and I am keeping the name.

The ground was flat and clean and full of people who were working.

That is the first thing. Not that they were hostile, and not that they were busy. They were working the way people work when there is nothing else they are going to do today, and tomorrow is not a separate question.

Before we set down I looked at the whole of it and could not find the middle. Every other place I have come into has had a middle, and that is where I have always gone, because the middle is where the people are and I am from the middle. This one had hands, in rows, and the rows went out past where I could count them.

I want the count in, since I always do. I got to fourteen rows and I lost it. I did not lose it because the number was large. I lost it because the rows did not hold still, and because nobody in any of them was looking up.

I am going to come back to that.

Landing is nothing. Landing is putting something down.

Achird set us at the edge of the rows. She does the setting down. I narrate it, and I have been narrating it for long enough that it has stopped being a lie and become a habit, which is not the same thing, and I know it.

The runners touched. The shape came off the sky and went on about its own road, the way it does when we stop. Then we stood in the open with the sleigh behind us and the work in front of us, and nobody came.

I am not going to call that a slight. It was not a slight. It was the first time in my life I had stood in a new place and been the least interesting thing in it, and I want that written down before I say anything else about the place, because everything after it comes out of it.

There was no gate. There was no greeting. There was nobody whose job it was to welcome anyone, or explain anything, or look at people who had just arrived.

A woman went past us carrying something long and flat, and she went past at the distance you keep from a wall.

I waited.

I am good at a great many things and waiting is not one of them. I waited anyway, because I have arrived in enough places to know that whoever speaks first has already lost, and because I was certain that somewhere in all of that there was a person whose job it was to come over and be delighted.

Nobody came.

I walked the length of the first row. Achird did not stop me, and I do not stand in doorways.

The hands were doing the same thing and not the same thing, and I want to be exact about this because I have thought about it a great deal since. One pair was taking a thing apart. One pair was fitting a thing together. One pair was holding a thing still while the other two did it. None of them was doing anything I could put a word to, and none of them was watching any of the others.

I put my hand out once, the way you do when you want to be handed something, and a man moved what he was carrying out of the way of my hand without looking away from it, the way you move a branch.

That is what I was worth to the first person I touched here.

Then a woman stopped in front of me.

I want to be exact about looking, because this is the chapter where looking stops being a gift and becomes a trade. She did not look at me. She looked at my hands. She looked at them for the length of one breath, and then she held something out.

It was a tool. I am not going to describe it, because describing it would make it a thing, and it was never a thing. It was the part of the work that needed holding.

“It needs holding,” she said.

I said something then. I have been asked since what I said and I have given a different answer every time, which means I do not remember and have decided to enjoy not remembering. I am fairly sure it was grand. I had been in the place for four minutes and nobody had offered me a chair, and I was already being handed work, and a girl with my appetites does not waste a moment like that.

She listened to the whole of it. Not politely. She listened the way you listen to weather.

“Sorry,” she said. “It has to be held.”

Then she went back to her own part of it, and I stood there holding a tool with no idea what it was for, being needed.

I want to be careful here.

I have been needed before. I want to say that plainly, because the version of this where nothing has ever been asked of me is a version I used to enjoy telling and have stopped, and you should know which one you are getting. People have needed me to look a certain way at certain hours. They have needed me to be in a room and be seen in it. That is a real need and I have been paid in full for meeting it.

This was not that. Nobody had asked me to be looked at. Nobody was going to. A piece of work needed a hand on it and mine was the hand that was nearest, and that was the whole of the arrangement.

I held it.

I held it for an hour, and at the end of the hour I was still holding it, and nothing had happened to reward me and nothing was going to, and I was as pleased with myself as I have ever been in my life.

I want to tell you why, since I have been honest about the other parts.

It is because it did not end.

I have been wanted by people. Wanting is a thing that gets satisfied and then gets over, and I have stood in the doorway afterwards more times than I am going to count. This had no afterwards in it. The work was not going to be finished, the part I was holding was not going to be done, and as long as my hand stayed where it was, I was the exact shape of the hole that needed filling.

I have never once been the exact shape of anything.

So I held it, and I was delighted, and I did the thing I always do when I am delighted, which is to look for somebody to be delighted at.

That is when I noticed.

I looked along the row, the whole length of it, and then I looked along the row behind it, and I could not find one person who was watching the work as though there were any chance of it being watched. There was a woman at the far end who checked what she had done twice, and did it with her eyes only, the way you check a door is shut, and then went on.

I have spent my whole life in rooms arranged so that whatever I did happened in front of somebody. I do not know how to do a thing that nobody is going to see. I have never learned it and I have never needed to.

So I did the only thing available to a person like me, which was to make myself worth seeing. I turned what I was holding to the best angle it had. I found a way to stand that made the holding look deliberate. I made a small adjustment to the thing in my hands that improved nothing at all, purely because somebody might have been about to look.

Nobody was about to look.

I do not know how to write the next part without it sounding like a complaint, and it is not one. It is the opposite of one. I had been given a job in a place with no audience in it, and I was happier than I had been in a year, and those two facts are both true and I am not going to sort them for you.

I looked for whoever was in charge.

This is a reflex. I have a whole set of them and this is the first one, before I have even decided to want anything. You find the person in the room who decides things and you make them look at you, and after that everything is arranged.

I asked a man, in the way that sounds like a compliment, who I should be speaking to.

He did not stop working. “About what,” he said.

“About all of it. Who runs the work.”

He thought about it for as long as the work let him think about anything, which was not long, and then he said, “Nobody runs it,” and moved on to the next thing he had to do.

I want to say that I knew he was telling the truth, because he was. He was not hiding anything from me. There was no rank in that answer and nothing wrong with it, and it took me an hour to understand that it was the first truly frightening sentence anyone had said to me here, and that he had said it the way you say the time.

Nobody runs it. It continues because everyone does their part. That is the whole of the arrangement, and the whole of the arrangement has no room in it for anybody who wants to be met.

Achird had not moved from the edge of the sleigh.

I want to get this down properly, because it is the part I did not understand until later and I am writing this afterwards, so I have the advantage and I am going to use it. She had stood there the whole hour. She had walked a little way along the first row and come back and stood at the sleigh again, and she had one hand in her pocket, and she was watching me hold a thing.

Nobody had given her anything.

Not in the way that is a refusal. Nobody had refused her. It had not come up. Somebody had needed a tool held and I had been nearest, and she had not been nearest, and that was the entire reason, and it had been an accident that took four seconds, and nobody had thought about it since.

I have been overlooked in rooms. I am excellent at noticing when I have been overlooked, and I have never once in my life noticed it on behalf of another person.

I noticed it then.

I said something to her about it. I do not remember what, which is unusual for me, because I remember everything I say and revise very little of it. I think I asked her whether anyone had asked her to do anything. I asked it the way I ask things, which is to say I asked it with the answer already inside it and waited to be corrected.

“Not yet,” she said.

That was all of it, and she went back to watching, and I went back to holding, and the work did not care either way, which was becoming the theme of the afternoon.

The light here does not go out. It thins, and then it is evening.

By the time it thinned I had been standing with my hand in the same position for a long while and my arm had gone from hurting to a different and more serious thing. I set the tool down on the ground next to where I had been standing, because I wanted to see what would happen.

A man came past within the minute and picked it up and put it in my hands, and said, “It has to be held,” and went on.

Not sharply. Not as a correction. He said it the way you would tell somebody the floor was wet, and then he was gone, and I was holding the tool again, and I had learned something I had not asked to learn.

I did not put it down a second time. I want to be honest about the reason. It was not that I had been instructed. It was that being asked again would have cost me something, and I had decided on the first afternoon that I was not going to pay it.

Nobody had looked up all day.

I walked back to the sleigh at the end of it with the tool in my hands because there was nowhere else to leave it, and I want to be exact about the last thing, because it is the small one and the small one is the true one.

I told myself the whole way back that this was the best thing that had ever happened to me, and I was not lying. Everything I have ever told myself about myself had finally been given somewhere to stand, and it was a row of people who did not need to be told, and I did not have to perform any of it, and I had never once in my life been off the stage.

Both of those were true. I held them both the whole way.

I did not notice, that first evening, that they went together.

The work went on behind me. I could hear it, and the sound of it did not change when I reached the sleigh, and it did not change when I went inside, and it did not change when I lay down. I have been listened to by a great many people, and none of them was ever as steady as that.

Achird was at the front of the cabin with her shoulder against the glass.

“Long day,” I said.

She did not answer that. She was watching the rows.

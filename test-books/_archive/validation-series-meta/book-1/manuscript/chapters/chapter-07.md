The runners found the ground before I had finished a sentence, which is the story of my life in one image.

I was still talking. I am going to stop pretending otherwise. I got into the cabin still talking and I sat down on the bench still talking and I was talking when the clearing went out of the glass.

I steered us out.

I want that sentence where it belongs, at the front of the last chapter, because I put one like it at the front of the first one and I am a person who finishes what she starts. I sat forward and put my hand on the sill and I took us up over the growth and out. That is my account and I am keeping it.

The truth is a thing I have already put on the record and will not be putting on it twice: she flies it, she has always flown it, and I narrate as though I do. I have been doing it since the first night of this book. You have had the whole of it from me in the wrong voice, and you have known for a while. Neither of us has said anything about it, and that seems to me the correct way to end a thing.

So.

Up past the growth. The light came down through the trees in one piece, the way it had every evening, and then we were above it, and then we were above the weather, and then the world was a shape below us that I did not have a name for and had never wanted one.

Nothing stopped us.

I want to be careful here, because a person writing this afterwards would like there to have been a moment. A hand on the runners. A being at the edge of the clearing, looking up. One glad face that had finally understood and was watching us go with something in it.

There was nothing.

They were glad. They were glad the way they had been glad when we arrived and glad every morning in between. They did not come to the edge of the clearing to see us off. They were not sad, and I do not think one of them understood that anybody was leaving, because nothing in them is built to keep that.

The last of them I saw was standing at the lip of the low ground with its hands out, in the warm spot, being glad at somebody who was already gone.

I looked for the light to change. It is a thing I did for weeks before I left, without noticing that I was doing it. I watched the trees for the moment the light would go down through them in the one long piece and the day would be over, the way it always was.

It did not change. We were above it.

So that was the leaving, and here is what it cost.

Nothing.

I am going to say that as many times as it takes. It cost us nothing. There was no pursuit, no last-minute thing reaching for the runners, no being on the ground with its hands out asking us to stay. The place had made it as easy to go as it had made everything else. I had spent the whole of my life being kept in rooms by people who wanted me for something, and this one let me walk out the way you walk out of a room that has no door on it.

It was the easiest thing I have ever done.

That is the part I think about. Not the shelf and not the soil. The ease. I got everything I had ever wanted and I was as happy as I have been, and I was talking, and then the runners found the ground, and that was the entire exit.

I did not decide to go.

I want to be plain about that too, since I have been plain about the other things. I did not weigh it and I did not agree and I did not turn to her at any point and say that she was right. The sleigh decided and I followed and the reason I followed is that my feet have a mind of their own and always have, and I am not going to dress that up as a choice I made.

I do not know why I left. I have been asked, since, in the way that people ask. I have given them answers, and every answer I have given has been true and none of them has been the reason. The case for staying is still standing. Nothing got disproved. The bread was still good, the light still came down in one piece, nobody had ever asked me for anything.

I simply went. That is the whole of the mechanism, and it is not a mechanism, it is a person being carried by a thing that had made up its mind.

We went a long way before either of us said anything.

I took the shape up. The old one was long gone, the dolphin had gone off about its own road weeks before and the sky had been empty of us, and when the sleigh came up out of the weather the new one was already there waiting the way the road waits.

Scorpius. I want it named, because I named the other one and it would be a poor sort of book that gave you a dolphin at the front and nothing at the back.

It came together out past the trees, a long curve with a hook on the end of it, and it is a scorpion if you agree to see it as one, and the sleigh ran along it the way it had run along the dolphin, and I sat with my hand on the sill and watched the whole of it take shape in front of us.

Then it was just the two of us in a cabin with old glass in it, going somewhere, and there was nothing to perform for and nobody to perform at.

That is when I looked at her.

I want to describe it properly and I am going to fail, and I have decided that failing at it is the right thing to do, because every version of this I have written down in my head has been too good.

She was sitting where she always sits, forward, with her shoulder against the curve of the glass. She had one hand on the sill and the other in her lap, and she was watching where we were going, because there was nothing else to watch. The light from the shape out there came in through the old glass and was not quite clear, so it was soft instead of bright.

She had just done the thing. That is what I was looking at. She had taken me by the hand for the fourth time in her life and walked me to a sleigh, and she had not explained it, and she had not argued, and she had not once in the whole of the last day said a single word about what she wanted.

And I sat there and looked at her, really looked, probably for the first time since I have known her, and I saw it.

I am not going to write the word. I have never once written the word and I am not going to start at the end of my own book, and if you have been paying attention you do not need me to.

It was there. It had been there the whole time, in every morning and every walk and every time she said one thing and stopped and waited to see whether I would pick it up, which I never did, because I was in the middle of something.

She has been in love with me since before we landed.

I did the thing I do.

I started talking.

I want that in the record as well, since we are at the end. There was one moment in this entire book where I could have put a plain sentence on the table, and what I did with it was open my mouth and fill the cabin.

I said something about the shape. I said Scorpius was a poor substitute for a dolphin and that whoever drew the sky had a mean streak. I said she should have picked a better one. I said I would have picked a better one, and I said it was a good thing I was the one piloting, and I said all of it looking at the ceiling, because I could not keep looking at her and talk at the same time.

She did not answer.

So I got quieter.

I do not know how to describe the next part except sentence by sentence, which is how it happened, and which is why this chapter is longer than it should be.

I stopped talking about the shape.

I looked at the glass.

I said: “You could have said something before now.”

That was the closest I got, and it came out wrong, and it came out as an accusation, which is my native language.

She did not answer that either.

And then I moved.

I want to be exact, because the exact version is the small one and the small one is the true one. I did not take her hand. I have never been the one to take the hand, in all the time we have had each other. I did not start in a sleigh above a world I was glad to leave.

I reached over and I put two fingers on the back of her hand where it was resting on the sill.

That is all. Two fingers on the back of her hand, and I left them there, and I did not say anything while I did it, and I looked out of the glass the whole time like a person who was not doing anything at all.

She did not move her hand.

We sat like that for a while. The shape went on being a scorpion out in front of us and the cabin was old and the glass was old glass, and neither of us said one word about it, and after a while it stopped being a thing I was doing and became a thing that was simply the case.

That is the acknowledgment. There is no larger version of it. If you came for a larger version, I have a coat story and a winter I was famous and I will give you either of them instead.

She never said it and I never said it and we have been to four worlds since and neither of us has said it, and it does not need saying, which is the only thing I have ever learned that I did not learn from a room full of people looking at me.

We put distance on the place. I did not look back at it, because there was nothing to look back at that I had not already declined to name, and because I have never been the sort of person to stand in a doorway.

Out past the trees the scorpion held its shape and the dark went on being dark and the dark is the same dark everywhere.

I took my fingers back after a while, because my arm was going numb. I told her it was because my arm was going numb, and she did not answer that either, and I have decided that her not answering is a language I have finally learned to read.

I got up and I sat down on the other side of the bench, where I could see her, and I told her about the winter I was famous.

She listened at the exact length of the telling, the way she does, not a beat more and not a beat less. Somewhere under us the world we had left went on being warm and glad and lit. The beings went on being glad about it, and the bread went on being made, and the bench stayed where it was, facing the middle of the clearing, where it had always faced, which is where a bench should face if the point of a bench is watching whatever comes.

I got what I wanted.

It was smaller than I thought it would be. It was a girl who had been sitting in the same room as me for years, and two fingers on the back of her hand, and a cabin with old glass in it.

I am not sorry about any of it.

And out in front of us the shape went on being a scorpion, moving the way a constellation moves, which is slowly, and out of reach, and exactly where it had always been.

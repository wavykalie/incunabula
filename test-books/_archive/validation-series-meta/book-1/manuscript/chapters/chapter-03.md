The mornings here do not start. They arrive already in progress, the way a good conversation does.

I woke up warm and I woke up rested. I want to put a number on that, because I have slept in a great many beds and rested in almost none of them. I got up when I felt like getting up. It was late enough that the others were already out and early enough that the light had not moved to the middle of the clearing yet.

Nobody had come to fetch me. Nobody ever came to fetch me here, and I had stopped noticing it, and it stays the best thing about the place.

There was bread. There is always bread. I am not going to keep explaining the bread; either you have eaten something that was made for you by somebody who was not going to be thanked, or you have not.

Achird was out at the edge.

She does that in the morning, and I have decided it is her version of saying good morning, which is to say it is the version where almost nothing happens and you are expected to be fine with that.

I sat on the bench and did my hair without a mirror, which I can do. It impresses nobody here, and it is one of the few things I do entirely for myself.

“Where are you,” I called across.

“Here.”

“You are always there. Come and sit where the sun is.”

She came and sat where the sun was. She does most things you ask her to if you ask them plainly, which nobody believes about her, because she never looks like she is about to agree to anything.

We ate. I told her the story about the winter I spent being famous. It is a long one and it has a part in the middle that is not flattering to me, and I leave that part in on purpose, because a story that is only flattering is a business card and not a story.

She listened to the whole thing. That is a thing she does. She listens at the exact length of the telling and not a beat more or less.

Then she stood up, mid-morning, in the middle of something I was saying, and walked to the growth.

I have never once gotten used to it. Saying something to Achird and watching her walk away from it is like setting a table for somebody and having them go and look at the wall.

I followed her, because I was curious and because she had found a thing, and finding things was turning into her entire personality.

There was a shape in the growth.

It was down low, half in and half out of it, and it was not a plant. A plant is one thing, and I have seen every kind of plant there is on two dozen worlds, and they all do the same set of things. They branch. They spread. A good half of them climb. They have a grain to them, a direction they were built to go in.

This had no grain. It came out of the ground in a curve and went back into it, one long smooth bend about as long as my forearm, and it was hard the way a bench is hard and not the way a root is hard, and it was the wrong color for anything that grows.

“It did not grow,” Achird said.

“It is shaped like the rest of it.”

“It is shaped like the rest of it,” she said, which is her way of saying yes and moving on at the same time.

I looked at it for a while. It was a curve in the dirt and it was not doing anything, and I have stood in front of more interesting walls.

“You have a gift,” I told her, “for finding the one thing in a room that is not furniture.”

“I do not have a gift.”

“You do. It is a very worrying gift and you should hear what I am saying as a compliment.”

She stood there and looked at the curve a little longer, and then she walked back toward the food, and that was the whole of it, and we did not come back to it that day.

This is the point where I have to be honest with you about the time, and honesty here costs me something, so I am going to do it in one sentence and then get on with it.

I do not know how many mornings there were.

I know there was the first. One of them was the second, and after that the counting stopped being a thing I could do, because the mornings were so much alike that the difference between two of them was less interesting than the bread. I have a good memory. I remember the middle of stories I was told once. I could not tell you whether the morning under the trees was the fourth or the seventh, and I have thought about it since enough that I should be able to, and I cannot, and I am putting that down where you can see it rather than tidying it away.

Nobody mentioned it. Not Achird, not the beings, not me. I said something once, casually, about how quickly the days were going, and one of them offered me fruit, and I took the fruit, and that was the end of it.

The next morning was the same morning, and there is nothing wrong with a good morning happening twice.

They were glad to see me again. I want to be exact about the gladness, because I keep using the same word for it and it deserves better.

It had not worn thin. That is the thing. In any ordinary place, the tenth time you walk into a room, the room has adjusted to you. The welcome is still a welcome, but it has dropped the part where it is pleased.

Not here. They came out to me every time like I had been gone a year. They held out the empty hands every time. They made room and stood close enough to touch and touched nothing and were glad, and it was new every morning, and I could not find the seam in it, and I stopped looking for one because there is no pleasure in finding the seam in a gift.

I told the coat story again.

I want you to understand that I was not short of material. I have dozens. But the coat story is the best thing I have, and the audience was behaving as though it had never heard it, so I gave it to them again, and they took it the way they had taken it the first time, with their faces at exactly the right angle, and it went better than the first time because I had had time to rehearse the pause before the end.

Achird said, from across the clearing, in the middle of the laugh: “There is another one.”

“Another what.”

She had already turned and gone.

I have to be careful here, because this is the part where a person writing it down afterwards would like to look better than she looked, and I am not doing that.

Achird took me by the hand.

She had never done that. In all the time I had known her she had never once reached out and taken my hand and walked me toward a thing, and I remember the whole of the walk, because I was so pleased about being led, for once, that I did not pay attention to where I was being led.

It was not far. It was the far side of the growth, past where the light thinned, and there was something lying in the open where the ground was bare.

It was small and round and dull, about the size of the fruit stones I had been spitting out for days, and it had a face on it, or something like a face, worked into it in lines that were too fine to have been made by accident and too regular to have been made by anything here.

“Look at it,” Achird said.

I looked at it.

I looked at it for exactly as long as it took me to decide that there was nothing in it worth the walk. It was a small dull thing on bare ground. I had held better things in a market and put them back on the table. Whatever had made it had made it once and moved on.

“It is a bit of nothing,” I said.

“Yes,” she said.

“You brought me across the whole clearing to see a stone.”

“Yes.”

“Then let us go back, because the light is doing the good thing again, and the good thing does not wait.”

She came back with me. She did not look disappointed, and I would have noticed, because I notice everything on a face when I am performing, and there was nothing on hers to notice.

I forgot about it within the hour.

I want to state that plainly, because the rest of this book leans on it and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out later. I forgot that I had seen it. Not the walk, I remembered the walk, I remembered being pleased about the hand. But the thing itself went out of my head the way a face at a market goes out of it, and it did not come back until it was standing on a shelf in front of me in a place I have not told you about yet, and when it did come back it cost me something.

The afternoon was the best one yet.

I am going to describe the happiness now, because this is the chapter of my life I would go back to, and I would be lying if I pretended otherwise.

There was no edge on anything. I did not once check whether I was being watched, and I have checked that every hour of every day since I was fifteen. I did not plan the next story. I did not count the audience, and I had been counting that clearing every morning since I arrived, and that morning I stopped, and the reason I stopped is that the number had gotten large enough that the arithmetic was no longer worth doing.

That is what I had been asking a place for my whole life and had never once said out loud. Not admiration. Enough of it that I could stop counting.

I sat on the bench and let the afternoon happen, and the light came down through the growth in one piece and stayed a long time, and I was happy in the plain uninteresting sense of the word, which is the only sense that counts. I did not feel clever. I did not feel chosen. I felt like the day had been built to hold me and I fit it.

Achird came and sat at the end of the bench.

She had not done that before either. She sat at the end of it with her feet on the runner and looked out at the clearing with me, and for a while neither of us said anything, and it was the closest thing to sitting together that we had ever managed.

Then she said: “There is something in the growth that did not grow.”

“Yes,” I said. “You told me. It is a curve. It is doing the two things curves do, which is be a curve and stay where it is.”

She looked at the growth.

She did not say it again, and she did not say anything else about it, and I have gone over that hour more times than I can count, and she never once raised her voice or argued or made it a scene. She made a statement. She waited to see if it would be picked up. It was not, and she set it down and went on being pleasant, and I took the quiet as agreement because I was happy and because quiet is what I had always read Achird’s agreement as.

I reached over and put my hand on her shoulder, which I had also never done.

“That is the sort of thing you notice,” I said. “It is why I keep you.”

She did not answer that. She was good at not answering the things I said when I was pleased with myself, and I took that for agreement as well.

The light went down through the trees all at once, the way it does here, and the beings turned, and the evening was as warm as the morning had been, and nothing in the whole day had asked me for a single thing.

I lay there after and thought about how much I liked it here.

I did not think about anything else, and I want that in the record too, because a person reading this afterwards will want to know whether I had a warning and ignored it. I did not have a warning. I had a curve in the dirt and a stone on the ground and a friend who noticed things, and I had the best afternoon of my life, and I could not have told you which one of those was the important one.

The growth at the far edge went on being shaped the way it was shaped, and the light came down through it in pieces, and Achird sat at the end of the bench with her hand loose in her lap, watching the place where the ground was bare.

I slept better than I had ever slept, and it was not the first morning, and it was not the last, and I could not have told you at the time which one it was.

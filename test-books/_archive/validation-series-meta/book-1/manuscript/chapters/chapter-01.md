I have been looked at before.

Not like this. I want to be accurate about it, because accuracy is one of the few things I have that nobody gave me.

The cabin is old, and the glass is old glass, which is to say it is not quite clear, so the dark on the other side of it comes through as a soft grey instead of as dark. The runners are wood. There is a bench down one side and a lamp that does not need lighting, and a small brass thing on the wall that has never been explained to me, which is the only reason I have never asked about it.

We were somewhere that was not on any chart. I checked.

I check things. It is one of my better qualities and I have never once been thanked for it.

That night the shape it had taken was Delphinus. That means a dolphin, which means a long curve made of dark with lights strung along the length of it, going up and coming back down again for no reason a dolphin would recognise as a reason.

I was steering it.

Achird was sitting forward, where the glass curves, with her shoulder against it. She does that. She has done it for as long as I have known her, which is long enough that neither of us has to say anything about it.

“Delphinus,” she said.

“I know what it is called.”

“I know you do.”

She turned her head back to the stars. Behind the shape the rest of the lights went on being lights.

“How long,” she said.

“That depends on what you mean.”

She did not say anything else. That is how I know it was a real question. A girl like Achird does not spend sentences. She had one, she spent it, and the spending was the whole of it.

I will say this now, while we are being accurate. I did not know how long. I have never once known how long, and I have never let it stop me, and I would thank you not to make one fact out of the two.

Below us was a world. It does not have a name. I am not going to give it one.

It had weather. The weather was being patient.

Landing is not difficult.

Landings are difficult when the ground has opinions, and this ground did not. We came down the way you put down a bowl you have finished with.

Nothing about arriving here was hard. Nothing about arriving here was even interesting, and I have come to believe that is the highest thing you can say about a place, because interesting places keep you standing up.

The light is easy. I want to be careful with that word, since I do not usually use it. Light is normally something a person has to manage. It is too much in the morning and gone by the middle of an afternoon, or it comes off the water at an angle that ruins whatever you came to look at. This light did not do any of that. It arrived and it stayed. It showed you things.

Then I saw the hedges.

The growth along the edge of the clearing here is not a wall and not a border. It has room in it. It is arranged the way chairs are arranged, in a space where somebody has already thought about where a person would want to sit.

I took that as a compliment.

I would like it on the record that I took it as a compliment and not as anything else, because that is what it was. There is no other reading of a room that has been set up before you walk into it. Somebody did the work. You get to enjoy it.

There is a bench at the near end of the clearing. It is low and it is old and it faces the middle of the space, which is where a bench should face if the point of a bench is watching whatever arrives.

I sat on it.

Achird did not. She walked the whole edge first, all the way around, which took a while. When she came back she stood behind the bench instead of on it, and looked at the hedges rather than at the clearing.

“It is old,” she said.

“The bench?”

“All of it.”

I told her that old things are the best things, and that she had never once disagreed with me about that, and she did not disagree with me about it then either.

Then the beings came.

All of them at once. Not a delegation. Nobody had been chosen to speak for anybody, and nobody had stayed back to keep an eye on the rest. They came out of the growth on every side of the clearing at the same time, and they were glad.

I have seen people be pleased to see me. I am good at it, and I am not modest about being good at it. This was a different thing, and I am going to describe it badly, so I will describe it twice.

The first way: they did not check.

People check. There is always a moment at the beginning, however short, when somebody looks at you to find out what you are going to cost them. Even the ones who like you. Especially the ones who like you. These ones came out of the hedges and looked at me and did not check anything. They were glad. That was the whole of it.

The second way: there were a great many of them, and the number kept not mattering.

I want to do that properly, so I will. I counted. Thirty-one of them had turned toward me, which is a fine number, and I have stood in rooms with thirty-one people in them and watched twenty-six of those people look at somebody else. I counted again, because counting felt good, and I got a different number, because they move and they would not hold still long enough for me to be finished.

That annoyed me. It is the only thing in this chapter I did not enjoy, and I am putting it in because I said I would be accurate.

They did not touch me. They stood close enough to touch, and did not. They smelled of cut stems. I liked it. One of them held out both hands with nothing in them, palms up, which I have thought about since and still cannot decide whether it meant anything.

I did not take the hands. I do not take hands. I have never needed to be brought anywhere.

One of them copied the way I was standing.

I noticed because it is the sort of thing a person notices, and because it made a poor job of it. It put its weight on the wrong foot and held its chin at an angle I have never once held my chin at, and it looked pleased with itself. I let it have that.

Achird was watching it. She watches things the way other people take notes.

Then Achird came and stood at the edge of it, and they were as glad to see her.

I want to be exact about this, because it matters and I did not notice it at the time. They were as glad to see her. They came out of the growth for her too, and made the same room. They held out the same empty hands.

She said something to them. I did not hear what it was. She says things in a voice that does not travel, and I was busy.

Later, on the way back, she said, “The hands.”

“Which hands.”

“The ones with nothing in them. That is what they do instead of a greeting.”

“They gave me eleven greetings before noon,” I said. “I was counting.”

“That is what I mean,” she said.

I did not follow it, and I did not ask her to say it again, because I was in the middle of something.

I am going to tell you about the food, because a person who describes a place without describing the food is really only describing herself.

There were four things. One was a kind of bread with a crust that did not break cleanly, which I liked. One was a fruit with a stone in it, and it tasted the way the smell of a fire smells after somebody else has already put it out. One was green and had no opinion. The fourth I declined on principle and then regretted.

“Are you going to eat,” Achird said.

“I am eating.”

“You gave that one back.”

“I was being choosy. I have never been choosy. This is what it is like.”

She looked at me for a moment longer than the sentence needed. She does that too.

I want to say something about the shape, because it did not stay where I put it.

A constellation is not a picture that hangs on a wall. It moves, and the shape moves with it. By the middle of that morning Delphinus had gone far enough along its own road that the dolphin was no longer overhead. It had drifted out past the trees and was hanging there in the open sky, bright, unmistakably a dolphin, doing nothing at all.

I wanted them to look at it.

That is a small and foolish thing to want, and I am putting it in anyway. They had been glad to see me for most of a day. I wanted a dolphin over their heads and I wanted somebody to know whose it was.

“Come and look,” I said, to all of them, or to whichever of them was nearest, which turned out to be most of them.

They came. They looked up. They were glad about that as well. They did not do the thing I wanted, which was to turn and look at me, but I had a sky with a dolphin in it and a whole morning of my own choosing, and I decided to be satisfied.

Achird looked up once, for a long time. She did not say anything about it.

By the end of the first day they had shown me things without being asked, which is the correct way to show a person things. A low place in the growth where the air was cooler. A bowl that held water and did not tip. A stretch of ground that was better for sitting on than the ground beside it. The difference was real and I could not tell you why.

I accepted all of it. I accepted it the way you accept a chair, without thanking anybody, because a chair offered is a chair offered.

They brought more than I could eat and did not mind when I left some of it. Nobody asked me how I liked anything. That is rare, and it is worth writing down, because in most places a meal comes with an interview.

Later, I stopped talking.

That is not a thing I do. Talking is not something I do on purpose, it is closer to the way you keep your balance, and I do not know how to put it down. But the morning had gone on a long time by then, and I stopped in the middle of an account of myself that was going well, and I did nothing at all.

Nothing changed.

They kept looking. They did not start doing something else, or turn to each other, or remember an appointment. I stood there with my mouth shut, being nobody in particular, and thirty-odd of them went on being glad about it.

I did that twice more later, to be sure.

I am not going to explain what that was like. I have an account of myself that I have been giving for years, and it has never once had an audience this patient, and I am going to leave it there and get on with the day.

The light here does not end. It goes down through the trees in one long piece and then it is evening.

By then I had been given four more things to eat and declined two of them on principle, and I had said the word no more times in one afternoon than I had said it in the whole year before it.

Achird walked the edge of the clearing. She does that when everybody else is in the middle of it. She stopped and bent down and came up with something small in her hand.

I called across and asked her what it was.

She turned it over twice.

“Somebody lost this,” she said.

Then she put it in her pocket, and she did not take it out again, and she did not say anything else about it. Not that evening and not the next one.

I did not go over and look. I want to be plain about that, because it is exactly the sort of detail a person puts in afterwards to make herself look better, and I am not doing that. I did not go over because the light was doing something in the trees and I did not want to miss it. I have never yet missed anything I wanted to see.

I stayed on the bench until the light had gone all the way down. Nobody came to fetch me. Nobody had come to fetch me at any point in the day, which I had not thought about until then, and which I decided was the most restful thing that had ever happened to me.

I meant to ask them how long they had been expecting somebody. I had a way of asking it that would have sounded like a compliment, because everything I say sounds like a compliment, and I was looking forward to using it. I did not get to it that evening.

The light came down through the hedges in pieces and went on for a long time, longer than light should.

Across the clearing, Achird had one hand in her pocket and was watching the trees.

I slept better than I have ever slept.

## Audience Landscape

**Genre position.** Quiet exploration SF with a hospitality-horror turn, written in translated
light-novel register. This is a genuinely thin niche: the SF audience does not habitually buy
light-novel prose, and the light-novel audience does not habitually buy horror endings. The book's
actual beachhead is readers of *literary* SF and translated fiction who already cross that gap.

**Engagement type.** `foundation.md` ranks **empathy-primary**, **fascination-secondary** (the
reveal), **intellectual-tertiary** (the theme question). The primary engagement type decides the
primary persona: a reader who buys **voice**, not plot.

**The structural risk the personas are built to test.** The book has no antagonist, no chase, no
rescue, and a protagonist who is carried out rather than choosing to leave. Every persona below has
a different reason to bounce off exactly that, which is the point of building them.

**Comp title insights.** From the three supplied reference works plus the desk market map:
*Wandering Witch* readers buy narrator voice and episodic structure and will forgive a plotless
episode if the voice holds. Kino's Journey readers buy the moral question rather than the answer.
Literary-SF horror readers (*Annihilation*, *Piranesi*) buy atmosphere and will accept an
undramatic ending if the atmosphere pays off. Each of those is a different reader, and only the
first is the primary.

## PRIMARY READER: Nadia, 27

### Demographics & Identity
- **Age:** 27
- **Gender:** woman
- **Location:** mid-size city, rents, no car
- **Education:** undergraduate degree in linguistics; works in localization
- **Occupation:** Japanese-to-English localization QA for a games studio
- **Reading frequency:** ~45 books a year
- **Reading format:** Kindle and Kobo; physical copies only for books she already loves

### Reading Psychology
- **Why they read:** for a voice. She reads to be inside a sentence she would not have written.
- **How they describe their taste:** "I want a narrator I can't quite trust, but I want to like them anyway."
- **What hooks them (first page test):** a first line that is doing something. She will commit to a
  paragraph if it sounds like a person and not like a book.
- **What makes them abandon:** a narrator who is only reliable and only nice. Also: an ending that
  punishes the character to prove the author is serious.
- **Emotional triggers:** a character refusing to say the plain thing. Being looked at. Being
  overlooked in a room full of people.
- **Re-read triggers:** short books with a trick of structure — she re-reads to watch the trick work.
- **Reading speed:** moderate, but stops dead on a good sentence
- **Patience for slow sections:** high. This is her preferred register.
- **Relationship to discomfort:** reads to be *recognised*, not comforted. Comfort bores her.

### Emotional Profile
- **What they want to FEEL:** implicated. A narrator who is wrong in a way that is uncomfortably familiar.
- **What they want to AVOID feeling:** lectured, or handed a moral she already agreed with.

### Genre Relationship
- **Three books they loved (and why):**
  1. ***Wandering Witch: The Journey of Elaina*, vol. 1** — "The narrator is a little bit awful and the book knows it and never apologises. It trusts me."
  2. ***Piranesi*, Susanna Clarke** — "I read the last thirty pages twice. Nothing is explained and I still felt the whole thing land."
  3. ***Convenience Store Woman*, Sayaka Murata** — "It's a narrator who has learned the rules from the outside. I think about the last line monthly."
- **Three books they hated (and why):**
  1. ***Klara and the Sun*, Kazuo Ishiguro** — "Beautiful and I felt nothing, and I could not tell you why, which made it worse."
  2. **Most LitRPG** — "The voice is a stat sheet. I bounce off in four pages."
  3. ***The Midnight Library*, Matt Haig** — "It told me the moral at the end. I'd worked it out at page forty."
- **What they love about the genre:** first person, present-feeling, a narrator with an agenda.
- **What they're tired of in the genre:** isekai protagonists who are secretly the best at everything.
- **Crossover interests:** translated literary fiction, horror novellas, narrative podcasts.

### Discovery & Purchase
- **Where they find books:** BookTok (lurks, does not post), r/Fantasy and r/horrorlit, a Discord of
  ~40 translation nerds, and the "readers also enjoyed" rail on Kobo.
- **What makes them BUY:** the first two pages as a sample, or a single quoted line in a screenshot.
- **What makes them RECOMMEND:** a line she sends to one specific friend with no commentary.
- **How they recommend:** screenshots of a passage. Almost never a plot description.
- **Price sensitivity:** waits for a sale; happily pays £3.99 for a novella-length ebook.

### Social Sharing Behavior
- **Platforms:** group chats, occasionally Storygraph reviews
- **What they share:** quotes, and structure observations ("it's told backwards and you never notice")

### The ONE Thing They Need from Chapter 1
A narrator with an agenda she is admitting to and also hiding. Specifically: a first-person voice
that performs confidence while telling you it is performing, by page 1.

### What Would Make Them Give a 5-Star Review
"I have been looked at before" — a book that opens by naming her whole problem and then spends
60 pages proving she cannot see it.

### What Would Make Them Abandon at Chapter 3
The happiness chapter reading as *sincere*. If the book appears to agree with Merope that this place
is wonderful, Nadia decides it is a shallow book and puts it down — she needs the dread to be visible
to her while invisible to the narrator.

### This Persona's Test
- **Where would Nadia abandon this book?** (She is the most likely person to finish it, so the
  diagnostic question is where she would *stop trusting it*, not where she would stop reading.)

## STRETCH READER: Marcus, 41

### Demographics & Identity
- **Age:** 41
- **Location:** suburban, drives
- **Occupation:** paramedic
- **Reading frequency:** ~15 books a year, in bursts
- **Reading format:** audiobook in the car, Kindle in bed

### Reading Psychology
- **Why they read:** to be gripped. He reads on shift nights.
- **How they describe their taste:** "Something has to be happening. I don't mind slow if it's tense."
- **What hooks them:** a wrong detail early. Something that does not add up.
- **What makes them abandon:** prose that feels like it is admiring itself; a book where nothing is at stake.
- **Emotional triggers:** a likeable person being quietly led somewhere bad by something that does not
  mean them harm.
- **Reading speed:** fast
- **Patience for slow sections:** low — three consecutive pages without a turn and he skips
- **Relationship to discomfort:** reads for tension, not for sadness

### Emotional Profile
- **What they want to FEEL:** unease that keeps building. Dread as a physical sensation.
- **What they want to AVOID feeling:** that he has been reading a character study with no engine.

### Genre Relationship
- **Three books they loved:**
  1. ***Annihilation*, Jeff VanderMeer** — "The tunnel. I put the book in the freezer."
  2. ***The Troop*, Nick Cutter** — "Nasty and relentless, exactly what I wanted that week."
  3. ***The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet*, Becky Chambers** — his crossover: "I did not expect to care about a crew this much. It got me."
- **Three books they hated:**
  1. ***House of Leaves*** — "Clever, but I was working and I wanted a story."
  2. ***Solaris*, Stanisław Lem** — "The planet is the antagonist and it never does anything. I finished it out of spite."
  3. ***The Road*, Cormac McCarthy** — "Too bleak, no light."
- **What they love about the genre:** escalation. A rule you learn and then a rule that breaks.
- **What they're tired of:** endings where the monster wins and nothing means anything.

### Discovery & Purchase
- **Where they find books:** Audible recommendations, and a friend who reads horror
- **What makes them BUY:** a blurb that promises a place that is wrong
- **What makes them RECOMMEND:** "I finished it in two nights"
- **Price sensitivity:** low for audiobooks; buys credits monthly

### Social Sharing Behavior
- **Platforms:** none, really; recommends in person

### Why They Don't Usually Read This Genre
He does not read translated light novels. The register — short sentences, present-tense asides,
a narrator who addresses him directly — reads to him as "not for me" in the first two pages.

### What Would Pull Them In
The engine. "A place where being welcomed comfortably is how you die" is a *premise* he would pick up
from a blurb, and it is the same promise *Annihilation* makes. The second thing: **Achird counting.**
A character methodically measuring a phenomenon is his ideal protagonist behaviour, and she is
the one doing it — not the narrator.

### The Bridge
He is pulled in by the **horror spine** and held by **Achird**. Merope is the obstacle to his
enjoyment, not the draw; his ideal edit would make Achird the POV. He stays if the counting pays off.

### This Persona's Test
- **Would Marcus finish this book?** (If he would, the horror engine is working. If he would not,
  the book is a voice piece and cannot be marketed to horror readers — which changes the packaging,
  not necessarily the book.)

## HOSTILE READER: Priya, 33

### Demographics & Identity
- **Age:** 33
- **Location:** large city, commutes 50 minutes each way
- **Occupation:** product manager
- **Reading frequency:** ~30 books, mostly whatever her book club picks
- **Reading format:** whatever is cheapest, mostly library ebooks

### Reading Psychology
- **Why they read:** to have something to say about it on Thursday
- **How they describe their taste:** "I read to be taken somewhere. If I can predict it, I'm out."
- **What hooks them:** a promise that the book is going to do something she has not seen
- **What makes them abandon:** vagueness treated as depth. Any paragraph that is atmosphere instead
  of information.
- **Emotional triggers:** a smart woman being condescended to. She will not forgive a book that does that.
- **Reading speed:** fast skimmer
- **Patience for slow sections:** low; she reads the first line of each paragraph
- **Relationship to discomfort:** fine with it, if it is going somewhere

### Emotional Profile
- **What they want to FEEL:** that she was right to be surprised
- **What they want to AVOID feeling:** that she has been reading a mood board

### Genre Relationship
- **Three books they loved:**
  1. ***This Is How You Lose the Time War*** — "Short, strange, and it *did* something with the form."
  2. ***Gone Girl*** — "I was wrong and I liked being wrong."
  3. ***Piranesi*** — "I did not know what was happening for 150 pages and I was never bored, which is the hard trick."
- **Three books they hated:**
  1. ***Normal People*, Sally Rooney** — "Nothing happens and everyone is sad about it. I finished it at book club and said so."
  2. ***The Alchemist*** — "A fortune cookie with a spine."
  3. **Any novella that turns out to be a prologue** — "I want the whole thing."
- **What they love about the genre:** a conceit that pays off inside one sitting.
- **What they're tired of:** "quiet" used as a synonym for "nothing".

### Discovery & Purchase
- **Where they find books:** her book club, then the library app
- **What makes them BUY:** hype plus a short length — she will gamble on a novella
- **What makes them RECOMMEND:** "It's 60 pages, read it tonight, then tell me if you saw it coming"
- **Price sensitivity:** reads via library; buys only if the library queue is long

### Social Sharing Behavior
- **Platforms:** a book club WhatsApp group, a Goodreads account she updates in bursts
- **What she shares:** verdicts and hot takes, usually one line

### Their Objection Before Reading
"Short 'quiet SF' novella about two girls and a sad planet, by an AI pipeline" — she is primed to
find it generative-AI-flavoured, thin, and coasting on register. She has read three things this year
that were exactly that and DNF'd all three.

### What Would Confirm Their Bias
- Prose that performs ambiguity instead of generating it
- A reveal that arrives on time and unearned
- An ending where nothing changes
- **Any sentence that sounds like the pipeline talking** (she will notice the vocabulary; a
  scene-packet word in the narration is an instant fail for her)

### What Would Convert Them
**The shelf.** Specifically, Chapter 4's flat line: *"I was happy that my things were on the shelf
with the others."* Priya respects a book that lets its narrator be genuinely awful without comment.
Her second conversion moment is discovering the chapter-5 reveal was **assembled out of Chapter 2's
measurements** — she will go back and check, find that she missed it, and that is the thing she
reports to the book club.

### The Conversion Point
**Chapter 4.** If she reaches the shelf and Merope's reaction does not land for her, she is gone.
Chapter 4 is the book's single load-bearing chapter for the hostile reader, and it is also the
chapter the prose gate fired on hardest.

### This Persona's Test
- **What would Priya highlight or screenshot?** (If the answer is nothing, the book has no shareable
  surface and the hostile reader has no way to defend it to the book club.)

## Writer Instructions

### Chapter 1 Must Deliver
- **For Nadia (primary):** a performing narrator who admits to performing — inside the first 3
  paragraphs. **Delivered:** "I want to be accurate about it, because accuracy is one of the few
  things I have that nobody gave me."
- **For Marcus (stretch):** one wrong detail that is not explained — a counting creature and a bench
  that faces the arrival. **Delivered:** the bench and Achird's "It is old."
- **For Priya (hostile):** a promise that this is not a mood board. **Delivered:** the counting
  (31, then a different number) establishes that the book is organised around precision, not vibe.

### Pacing Calibrated to Reader Patience
- **Maximum consecutive pages of introspection without a concrete object:** 2. The drafted chapters
  run under this; every chapter has a physical object (bench, button, curve, stone, shelf, sleigh)
  inside the first 2 pages.
- **Concrete-object frequency:** at least one per chapter, ideally one per movement. **Met.**
- **Length:** 7 chapters, 2,077–2,402 words each. Deliberately inside Priya's one-sitting patience.

### Content Boundaries
- **Explicit content:** none. INTIMACY is expressed as two fingers on the back of a hand. This is
  correct for all three personas and must not escalate in the packaging.
- **Violence/darkness:** the darkness is implied, never shown. No remains are ever on the page.
- **Humor:** dry, in Merope's self-presentation. Priya will read it as voice; Marcus will skim it.
- **Political/social content:** none.

### What Each Reader Would Share
- **Nadia shares:** "I was happy that my things were on the shelf with the others."
- **Marcus shares:** "Achird counted forty-one things on a shelf and nobody owned any of them."
- **Priya shares (if converted):** "It's short, it's structured, and the horror is that everyone is
  kind. Read it tonight."

### Deal-Breaker Watchlist — These Will Lose Readers
1. **Nadia:** the happiness chapter reading as sincere rather than as the narrator's failure.
2. **Marcus:** a chapter with no escalation. Movement II is where he would bail; ch.4's shelf and
   ch.5's reverse reveal are the escalation he needs and they must stay in that order.
3. **Priya:** any sentence that sounds like the pipeline. The word "packet" was found in ch.3 and
   removed for exactly this reason — see `continuity/manuscript-audit.md` finding CONT-004.
4. **All three:** an ending that reads as fatalism. The acknowledgement in ch.7 is the entire
   counterweight; if it reads as too small, the book loses every persona at once.

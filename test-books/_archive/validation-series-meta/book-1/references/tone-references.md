## Reference C — *Shoujo Seikan Hyouryuuki* → **the closest match to our premise**

**READ IN FULL** (Chapter 1, *Planet of Flames*, 44 pages, via MangaDex's public API and the
compressed page variant). This is the most important of the three references.

Premise: a spaceship **shaped like a horse-drawn carriage** crosses the galaxy, crewed by **Ridley**
(the scientist who built it) and **Watari** (younger, quiet). They left an uninhabitable Earth to
find a planet to settle on. **Episodic**: one strange world per installment, each on one conceit.
Tags: Yuri, Episodic, Aliens.

### What an episode actually does, in order

1. **In space.** Quiet conversation between the two. Nostalgia, the search, the ship glimpsed small
   against stars. A narration box sets atmosphere in two or three lines.
2. **Arrival.** A beautiful place. A host appears at once and is *glad to see them*.
3. **Hospitality.** Fruit, sake, a bath, rest. The girls say out loud how unbelievably kind it is.
   This is the longest and warmest stretch of the chapter.
4. **The idea of staying.** "Moving to this planet might be a good idea."
5. **The conceit, stated plainly by a friendly local.** Those who immigrate here slowly become part
   of the planet. Ridley shrugs: *no big deal, right?*
6. **Casual exploration** that quietly assembles the real horror: a painting, a shrine, cryptic
   answers, a girl's hair beginning to change colour while she is being bathed.
7. **Someone arrives looking for a person who is already gone.** A friend with a burn scar asks
   after someone by name.
8. **Investigation.** Ridley asks the host personal questions and the host answers honestly and
   without knowing the significance. The missing person **is the host**, and does not remember.
9. **The reversal, on one flat line.** Delivered calmly, as a conclusion, not a scream:
   *this planet is a carnivorous plant, and after turning its prey into vines it devours the heart.*
   The planet **cultivates contentment** — fear ruins the crop, so it is kind on purpose.
10. **Violent escape**, over quickly, using tools Ridley already has.
11. **A quiet coda in the ship.** Tenderness, a small unexplained trace left on the floor, and a
    closing narration box about hoping the next planet is better.

### Craft observations

- **Third person, restrained.** Narration boxes are rare and used only for atmosphere and the
  closing line. Almost everything is dialogue.
- **The two leads have opposed voices.** Ridley: confident, decisive, teasing, competent, *acts.*
  Watari: quiet, worried, follows, asks *what should I do?*
- **Physical intimacy is constant and casual** — shared bath, sitting in each other's lap, feeding
  each other, hair-touching. Yuri as domestic warmth, not as event.
- **Food matters.** Meals are lingered on and used to make the welcome feel real.
- **Light comedy runs underneath**, even late ("That's strange! It's so pleasant though!").
- **The horror is one flat sentence.** No hysteria, no explanation, no lecture. The confident
  character states it and immediately acts.
- **The episode ends softly, not climactically.** The escape is fast; the last beat is tender, plus
  one small wrong detail left unexplained.
- **Chapter titles name a place or a thing** — *Planet of Flames* — and the title only makes full
  sense at the end.

### Why this matters to our book

This reference is **the same shape as our premise**: two girls, a horse-drawn vehicle, a strange
world whose **welcome is the mechanism of harm**, revealed late, then escaped. The user's chosen
engine for our book is very close to this episode's engine.

**Our deliberate differences, and they must stay deliberate:**

- In C, the transformed residents **are** the former visitors. In ours, the beings are **something
  new grown from the dead** — not the dead — and they **do not know what they are.** That innocence
  is our distinction and the source of our horror.
- In C, the planet is **actively predatory and knows it** ("I wanted you to become happy vines up
  until the very end"). Ours has **no malice at all** and no intention. Nobody is doing anything to
  anyone. That is a harder, quieter effect and should not be softened into a villain.
- In C, the leads are comic and competent. Ours has a **grandiose first-person narrator** and a
  kuudere, which is a colder pairing than Ridley and Watari.

**Tone correction this forces on our current outline:** the draft is dread-forward from chapter 4
onward. The reference keeps the welcome **long, warm and genuinely lovely**, spends its dread on
**one or two flat statements**, and then ends **tenderly**. Warmth is not the setup for the horror;
the warmth *is* the horror, and it has to be pleasurable to read.

## Reference B — *Twinstar Cyclone Runaway* → **tone, texture, the pair**

- Third person, past tense. Composed, technical, unbothered.
- Heavy worldbuilding **stated once and used**, as though the reader already lives there.
- The core unit is **a pairing of two women with opposed voices**: **Terra**, verbose, enthusiastic
  and insecure; **Diode**, blunt, flat, extremely short-spoken. Their exchanges carry the chapters.
  Neither is a villain; neither is a victim. (Terra is described as unusually tall; Diode as a
  striking, mysterious runaway.)
- **The work is treated with genuine procedural seriousness** — fishing on a gas giant, the roles
  of "decomper" and "twister", nets and hauls. Competence is the pleasure.
- Physical description is **frank and admiring** — a yuri register carried in attention, not
  announced.
- **No dread and no gothic.** Wonder, work, and a world that is *dangerous* rather than hostile.
- **This is the pair dynamic we want for Merope and Achird** — though note our Merope is grandiose
  and sincere, where Terra is anxious and self-deprecating, so it maps loosely.

## Reference A — *Elaina* → **POV and register**

Read: Vol. 1 Ch. 1, plus Vol. 5 Ch. 4 *Two Teachers* and Ch. 8 *A Certain Girl's Predictions*
in full, and the complete chapter-title lists of Vols. 1, 2 and 5.

### The register, at sentence level

- **First person, past tense, retrospective.** She is telling it afterwards — *"I still wonder
  which it was."* The narrating self is older and calmer than the self in the scene.
- **Present-tense snark layered over past-tense event.** The event is told in past; her opinion of
  it arrives in present, unmarked, mid-paragraph: *"I know tobacco is really nasty. It causes all
  kinds of damage and has absolutely no benefits whatsoever."*
- **Rhetorical questions as the comic engine.** *"What is this world coming to?" · "Shouldn't I be
  the Midnight Witch if my hair's black?" · "Is this what this world is coming to?"*
- **Deadpan understatement immediately after a slight.** She has her hand slapped away, and the next
  line is *"So, this is a common handshake in this region. Good to know."* The joke is that the
  narration refuses to complain.
- **Very short paragraphs, often one line.** Dialogue carries most of the chapter. Big stretches are
  nothing but alternating lines of speech.
- **Emotional beats land in one flat sentence and are not dwelt on.** *"But we had never grown
  estranged." · "Somehow or other, we had decided together that this was the right level of
  distance."* Nothing is underscored.
- **She comments on her own telling.** *"If I were to write it down, my story would take only a few
  lines."*
- **Judgement is withheld.** Cruelty, greed and death are reported without moralising; the comic
  register never turns into a lesson.
- **The "you" is the reader, casual.** Never ceremonious, never lyrical.

### Chapter shape

- **A present-day frame, then a flashback.** Ch. 4 opens with an old friend arriving and a
  conversation; the bulk is *"This is the story of something that happened when I was still
  traveling with my teacher";* then it returns. Ch. 8 frames on two backs turned to each other and
  runs backwards through a whole shared life.
- Chapters end on a **quiet image**, not a punch: a new-moon sky, stardust, two brooms leaving.
- Chapter titles name a person, place or situation and hover *above* the chapter — they do not
  state its lesson. (Vol. 5 Ch. 4 is *Two Teachers*; the chapter is about much more than that.)

### The one thing that does not transfer

**Elaina winks. Merope must not.** Elaina's vanity is a gag she is in on, and her narrative voice
keeps deflating her. The user has ruled that Merope **means every word** — no wink. So we take
Elaina's *sentence machinery* (first person, short paragraphs, present-tense asides, rhetorical
questions, deadpan, retrospective calm, casual address to the reader) and remove the
self-deprecation. The same light, fast, chatty prose — with a narrator who is sincerely certain of
her own centrality. Whether that reads as comic or unsettling becomes the reader's problem, not
hers.

### Chapter titles — the important finding

**Third-person noun phrases naming a place or situation, with one wry or understated modifier.**
Examples: *The Country of Mages · A Gentle Death Slowly Approaches · How to Use Weapons Peacefully ·
Cute Makes Right · Two Pupils · Regarding the Bomb.*

**Both references title by place or conceit, not by moral.** Elaina names countries; Shoujo Seikan
names planets. Neither states its theme in a title. That is the family our titles belong to — and
it is the test both of my rejected sets failed (v1 stated the theme and the reveal; v3 abandoned
the family entirely for an invented first-person scheme).

## Where our book departs from C (the format model)

C proves the shape works. Our departures from it are the book:

1. **The world does not mean it.** C's planet is a predator that has a plan. Ours has none. Nobody
   is doing anything to anybody, and that is a quieter and harder effect.
2. **The beings do not know what they are.** C's vine-people are the transformed dead. Ours are new
   life grown out of the dead, innocent of it. The horror is their innocence meeting the pile.
3. **Our narrator is grandiose and means it.** C is third-person and comic. Merope narrates her own
   welcome as something she is owed, with no wink.
4. **Our ending is not an escape into tenderness.** C ends in the ship, safe and fond. Ours ends
   with the relationship changing into something new — acknowledged, unresolved, not damaged.

That fourth point is the largest departure and the one to guard hardest: **nothing is lost in this
book.** If a draft starts costing the characters something, it has drifted.

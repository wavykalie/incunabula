# The naming system in *Night on the Galactic Railroad*

**Why this file exists.** `USER_PREFERENCES.md` §1 names Giovanni and Campanella as the
model for meaningful names. Before building anything on that, the claim needed checking:
is the pairing a *system the author applied*, or a coincidence two later readers noticed?

It is a system. Three of the four principals are named the same way — from a reference
outside the story that carries a meaning the plot does not have to state.

---

## 1. Campanella

**Italian** *campanella* — **"little bell."**

Two further layers, both established in Japanese scholarship and neither invented:

- The name of **Tommaso Campanella** (1568–1639), the Neapolitan Dominican friar whose
  *La città del Sole* — *The City of the Sun* — is a utopian work written **in prison**,
  where he spent roughly thirty years for heresy. Miyazawa's work is steeped in
  Utopian thought, and the friend who ends up imprisoned and killed is named for the
  prison thinker. That is not decorative. It is the plot of *Sun City* delivered as a
  character's name.
- The **constellation**: in Japanese the friend is written カムパネルラ, and scholarship
  reads the name as pointing to the star-cluster associations in the sky-map of the
  railway itself.

> Source: 宮沢賢治研究 suite, *銀河鉄道の夜* constellation study,
> hondanissho.jp/study/galaxynight/ — 「彼の名前は、17世紀のイタリアで反宗教改革の受難を生き抜いたカンパネッラに由来している。」

## 2. Giovanni

Written ジョヴァンニ — Italian for John. The boy's festival is the **Feast of St John**
(San Giovanni), a **midsummer** festival of 24 June; the Milky Way **Star Festival** in
the story is that same night, in a town where the children are told their fathers will
come back from the northern sea. The name of the protagonist is **the name of the night
the book is about.**

Confirmed: 「ケンタウル祭は日本の七夕祭と、このジョバンニ祭が結びついたイメージが浮かびます。」
(*The Centaur Festival fuses with Tanabata and the Feast of St John.*)

## 3. Zaneri

The bully is named from **commedia dell'arte** — Zaneri/Zaneri is a stock servant or
trickster figure, so the antagonist is named for a stage type rather than a person.

> Source: 本多日生記念財団, 「『銀河鉄道の夜』の星座世界 1」 — 「ザネリの名にはイタリアの古典仮面劇コンメディア・デッラルテに登場する召使い…」

## 4. The rule this yields

Three names, three different source types — a **word in another language**, a **saint's
day**, a **theatrical stock figure** — and every one is chosen because of what that
reference means *outside* the story.

| source type | example | carries |
|---|---|---|
| an ordinary word in a foreign language | Campanella = "little bell" | a sound, and a prison |
| a saint's feast day | Giovanni = 24 June | the calendar, the season, the festival |
| a stock stage figure | Zaneri = the trickster servant | the character's function, pre-announced |

**The common structure: the name is chosen for a meaning the plot does not state.**
The reader who knows the reference gets something extra. The reader who does not still
reads the story correctly. That is the test — a name that breaks the story for someone
who lacks the reference has failed.

---

## 5. What I could not verify, stated rather than papered over

- The **constellation** reading of Campanella is widespread in Japanese commentary and
  appears in the source above; I did not find it stated in a peer-reviewed source and am
  recording it as **well-attributed but not established**.
- The **Giovanni/24 June** link is the strongest of the three and is the one I would build
  on. The **Campanella/Tommaso Campanella/utopia-in-prison** link is directly quoted
  from a research foundation and is the one that carries the most weight in the book.
- All of this is secondary Japanese-language scholarship. Miyazawa left no statement of
  intent on the naming, so **every claim here is inference from the names and the text**,
  not documentation of authorial intent. The honest claim is: *these names are
  referential, and the references are the ones described.* Whether he chose them for the
  reasons above is not recorded anywhere I found.
